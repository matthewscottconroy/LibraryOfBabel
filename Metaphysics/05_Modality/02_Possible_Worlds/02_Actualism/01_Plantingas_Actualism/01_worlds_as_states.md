# Plantinga's Actualism: Worlds as Maximal States of Affairs

Alvin Plantinga shares with Lewis the conviction that possible worlds are indispensable for modal metaphysics. He departs from Lewis on what they are. For Plantinga, possible worlds are not concrete realities but abstract entities — maximally comprehensive possible states of affairs. The framework, developed in *The Nature of Necessity* (1974) and subsequent work, is designed to provide a full account of modal truth without positing any non-actual objects.

## States of Affairs

A **state of affairs** is Plantinga's name for an abstract entity that either obtains or fails to obtain. "Socrates being wise" is a state of affairs that obtains. "Socrates being a rock star" is a state of affairs that does not obtain but could have. The key point is that states of affairs exist necessarily as abstract entities — they are always available as objects of reference and quantification — even when they fail to obtain.

This distinction between *existing* and *obtaining* is crucial. "Socrates being a rock star" exists necessarily as an abstract object; it fails to obtain because Socrates was not a rock star. The actual abstract entity is present in our ontology; only its obtaining is absent. This allows Plantinga to maintain actualism — all existing entities are actual — while accommodating unrealized possibilities: the abstract state of affairs "Socrates being a rock star" actually exists, even though the situation it represents does not obtain.

## Possible Worlds as Maximal States of Affairs

A **possible world** is a maximal state of affairs that possibly obtains. Formally:

- A state of affairs S includes T iff necessarily, if S obtains then T obtains: □(S obtains → T obtains).
- A state of affairs S precludes T iff necessarily, if S obtains then T does not: □(S obtains → ¬T obtains).
- A state of affairs W is a **possible world** iff W possibly obtains and for every state of affairs T, either W includes T or W precludes T.

The actual world is the unique possible world that actually obtains. Other possible worlds exist as abstract objects — they are maximally specific abstract descriptions of how things could be — but do not obtain.

This account is formally adequate for possible-worlds semantics: modal operators are defined in terms of truth at all or some possible worlds, and the maximality condition ensures that every question is settled. □P is true iff every possible world includes [P]; ◇P is true iff some possible world includes [P]. Plantinga holds that metaphysical modality is governed by S5: what is necessary is necessarily necessary, what is possible is necessarily possible.

## The Contrast with Lewis

The contrast is fundamental. For Lewis, possible worlds are concrete spatiotemporal systems; for Plantinga, they are abstract states of affairs. For Lewis, actuality is indexical — "actual" picks out our world, but all worlds are equally real. For Plantinga, actuality is absolute — the actual world has a metaphysically special status in that it *obtains*, and obtaining is not a mere indexical label. For Lewis, the individuals in other worlds are concrete realities; for Plantinga, only actual individuals exist, with abstract haecceities representing possibilities for non-actual individuals.

| Feature | Plantinga | Lewis |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| Nature of worlds | Abstract states of affairs | Concrete spatiotemporal systems |
| Ontological status | Necessarily existing abstract objects | Concrete realities, equally real |
| Actuality | Absolute: the actual world obtains | Indexical: "actual" picks out speaker's world |
| Individuals | Only actual individuals exist | Inhabitants of all worlds equally real |

For Plantinga, the actual world is metaphysically special: it is the unique maximal state of affairs that actually obtains. "Obtaining" is not an indexical concept but an absolute one. Other possible worlds exist but are metaphysically inferior to the actual world in not obtaining.

## The Role of Abstract Objects

Plantinga's account relies heavily on abstract objects — states of affairs, propositions, properties, and haecceities — all of which exist necessarily. This is not seen as a defect: Plantinga is a committed Platonist who holds that abstract entities are necessary beings. Numbers, sets, propositions, and properties all exist necessarily as abstract entities.

Critics from a nominalist background argue that this Platonic ontology is itself costly — abstract objects are philosophically mysterious, their existence is difficult to explain, and our knowledge of them raises its own epistemological challenges. Plantinga's response is that the mystery of abstract objects is familiar from the philosophy of mathematics, and the cost of positing abstract objects is less radical than the cost of Lewis's infinitely many concrete universes.

The account faces the question of what makes a state of affairs possibly obtain. Plantinga's answer is broadly: a state of affairs possibly obtains iff its obtaining involves no contradiction — iff it is logically consistent. This is a logical criterion. Whether logical consistency tracks metaphysical possibility exactly, or whether there are metaphysical constraints beyond logical consistency (as Fine and Kripke suggest), is a question Plantinga's framework must address.
