# Individual Essences and Haecceities

Plantinga's modal metaphysics requires a way to represent de re possibilities about specific individuals across possible worlds — including individuals who might not have existed, and individuals who do not actually exist. His solution is the doctrine of haecceities or individual essences: for each individual, there is a property that is essential to that individual alone, that exists necessarily as an abstract object, and that could be instantiated only by that individual.

## Definition and Role

A **haecceity** (from the Latin *haec*, meaning "this") is an individual essence: a property P such that:

1. Necessarily, if Socrates exists, he has P: □(∃y(y = Socrates) → P(Socrates))
2. Necessarily, nothing other than Socrates has P: □(∀x)(P(x) → x = Socrates)
3. P is a purely individual property — it does not involve other individuals essentially.

The haecceity of Socrates is the property of being identical to Socrates, or more naturally: the property of being Socrates himself. This is a non-qualitative property: it characterizes Socrates not in terms of how he is qualitatively, but in terms of who he is numerically.

Haecceities serve a crucial function in Plantinga's actualism. In Lewis's realism, questions about whether an individual exists in another world are answered by the existence of the individual (or a counterpart) in that world. In Plantinga's actualism, there are no non-actual individuals — but possible worlds can still represent possibilities about individuals through their individual essences. The possible world where Socrates exists but is a mathematician contains the haecceity of Socrates co-instantiated with the property of being a mathematician. The haecceity exists (as an abstract object) in every world; in that world, it is co-exemplified with mathematician-hood; in the actual world, it is exemplified by the concrete Socrates.

## Haecceitism and Anti-Haecceitism

Plantinga is a strong haecceitist: individuals are individuated by their haecceities rather than by their qualitative properties, and there can be purely haecceitistic differences between worlds — two worlds can be exactly qualitatively alike yet differ in which individuals they contain.

Lewis is the leading anti-haecceitist: there are no irreducible haecceities; modal and individual facts supervene on the distribution of qualitative properties. Two qualitatively identical worlds are identical; haecceitistic differences between worlds are illusory.

This is a fundamental disagreement. For Plantinga, the individuality of an individual is not exhausted by their qualitative nature — there is a primitive thisness, a property of being this very individual, that exists necessarily and is not further analyzable. For Lewis, the counterpart relation captures everything that de re modal claims require, and it does so entirely in terms of qualitative similarity.

## Merely Possible Individuals

The most important application of haecceities is to merely possible individuals — individuals like Sherlock Holmes who don't exist actually but might have. How can Plantinga make sense of "Holmes might have existed" without positing a non-actual Holmes?

Plantinga's answer: there is a Holmesian haecceity — a property that is such that necessarily, if anything has it, it is Holmes. This haecceity exists necessarily as an abstract object. The possible world where Holmes exists is a maximal state of affairs that includes the instantiation of the Holmesian haecceity. Holmes "exists" in that world in the sense that his haecceity is exemplified there.

This raises the circularity worry. The Holmesian haecceity is "the property of being Holmes." But if Holmes doesn't exist, what is the content of this property? The haecceity seems to presuppose the existence of Holmes to be defined, but Holmes doesn't exist to define it.

Plantinga's response: the haecceity is not defined descriptively as "the property that Holmes has." It is a primitive abstract property that exists necessarily, whose essential feature is that it could only be instantiated by a specific individual. Its content is not descriptive but purely "thisness" — the individual-constituting property, existing as an abstract entity even though Holmes does not concretely exist. Whether this response is fully satisfying — whether we can genuinely grasp an individual essence of a non-existent individual — is a further question that critics press.

## Transworld Identity Grounded in Haecceities

Haecceities provide Plantinga's account of transworld identity. When we say "Socrates might have been a mathematician," we mean: the possible world where the Socratic haecceity co-obtains with mathematician-hood is a genuine possible world. The "Socrates" in that world is not a counterpart but the very same Socrates — the individual whose haecceity is exemplified there.

Transworld identity, on Plantinga's view, is grounded in haecceitistic identity: x in world w₁ is the same individual as y in world w₂ iff x and y instantiate the same haecceity. This gives a precise criterion for transworld identity without appealing to qualitative similarity (counterpart theory) or primitive numerical identity across worlds (as if individuals persisted through worlds the way they persist through time on the endurantist view).

Two objections deserve mention. The *content objection*: what is the content of a non-qualitative property? If a haecceity has no qualitative specification, it seems to have no content. Plantinga's response is that content in the sense of qualitative characterization is not necessary for properties to play individuating roles. Haecceities are sui generis properties whose "content" is their role as individual-constitutors. The *bootstrapping objection*: the haecceity of Socrates is individuated by Socrates, and Socrates is individuated by his haecceity. This circle is at least not obviously vicious: haecceity and individual may be correlative concepts, each helping to fix the other in a mutually explanatory rather than asymmetrically grounding relation.
