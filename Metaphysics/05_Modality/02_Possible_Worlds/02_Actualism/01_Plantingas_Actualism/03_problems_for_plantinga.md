# Problems for Plantinga's Actualism

Plantinga's actualism is sophisticated and internally coherent, and it successfully maintains the core actualist commitment — all existing entities are actual — while providing a framework adequate for possible-worlds semantics. But it faces serious challenges. These are not merely technical difficulties; they reveal genuine pressure points in the project of giving an actualist account of modality that is both ontologically parsimonious and modally adequate.

## The Problem of Alien Properties

Are there possible properties that no actual thing has — properties that might have been instantiated but never are? If there could be a world with a new fundamental force, or a new kind of color quale, these would be alien properties: not exemplified in the actual world and not constructible from actual properties.

Plantinga's abstract states of affairs must include states of affairs involving such alien properties. But what are these properties? If they are abstract entities that actually exist, they are part of the actual world's ontology — which inflates the actual abstract domain dramatically. The question: does Plantinga's account allow for worlds with genuinely alien properties — properties that don't exist even as abstract entities in the actual world? If so, what grounds the existence of these abstract properties? If not, the space of possible worlds seems too restricted.

Plantinga typically takes the permissive option: all properties exist necessarily as abstract objects, including properties no concrete entity has ever had. This avoids alien property worries but requires an enormous (in fact, uncountable) Platonic ontology of properties. This is not technically inconsistent, but it is ontologically very demanding. The nominalist will find it profligate; even Platonists may find uncountably many uninstantiated properties hard to accept without further explanation.

## The Problem of Non-Existent Individuals

For every merely possible individual — every person, creature, or object that doesn't actually exist but might have — there must be a haecceity that actually exists as an abstract object. If Holmes might have existed, there is a Holmesian haecceity in the actual abstract ontology. If the number of merely possible individuals is uncountably infinite, there are uncountably many haecceities of non-existent individuals.

The circularity worry presses hardest here. The Holmesian haecceity is individuated by Holmes — it is the property of being Holmes. But if Holmes doesn't exist, what individuates the haecceity? Plantinga's response — that haecceities are primitive properties that exist necessarily regardless of whether their potential instantiators exist — is coherent, but it asks us to accept a very large primitive ontology of individual essences without any independent motivation beyond the theoretical need for them.

## The Ontological Proliferation Objection

The combination of abstract states of affairs and haecceities multiplies abstract entities in staggering numbers. For each possible world (and there are uncountably many), there is a distinct abstract state of affairs — a maximal consistent abstract complex. For each possible individual, there is a distinct haecceity.

Critics, especially nominalists, find this ontological proliferation excessive. If the motivation for avoiding Lewis's concrete worlds was parsimony, Plantinga's abstract worlds pay a different but equally significant ontological price — not in concrete entities but in abstract ones.

Plantinga's reply: the ontological cost of abstract entities is different in kind from the cost of concrete entities. Abstract entities are causally inert, spaceless, and timeless; they do not add to the causal complexity of the world. The proliferation is theoretically harmless in a way that the proliferation of concrete worlds is not. Whether this reply is satisfying depends on one's view of abstract ontology — whether mere causal inertness is sufficient to defuse the cost, or whether any large, inexplicable ontological posit incurs a genuine cost regardless of whether it is concrete.

## The Explanatory Gap

What explains the truth of modal claims on Plantinga's view? A state of affairs possibly obtains because there is no contradiction in its obtaining — but this is a logical criterion, not a metaphysical ground. The deeper question: what makes certain combinations of states of affairs consistent, and others not? Plantinga's account identifies possible worlds with maximal consistent states of affairs and invokes consistency as the ground of possibility, but does not explain what makes some abstract complexes consistent and others not. The account redescribes without explaining.

Compare the essentialist answer: it is possible for water to boil because of the nature of water and heat — the boiling point follows from the essential natures of these substances. Plantinga lacks this explanatory resource unless he supplements his account with an account of essence — which he does, through individual essences — but then the individual essences do the explanatory work, and possible worlds become derivative.

## The Collapse Problem

A fourth problem: the distinction between actual and merely possible threatens to collapse under scrutiny. Plantinga says possible worlds exist (as abstract objects) but only one — the actual world — obtains. But what does "obtaining" add to existence? If abstract objects exist necessarily, all possible worlds exist necessarily; the difference between actual and merely possible worlds is just a further fact about which one "obtains." If "obtains" is a primitive notion, the framework has a primitive modal notion at its core — no less mysterious than Lewis's concrete worlds, and lacking the explicit theoretical payoff of Lewis's analyses.

These challenges are not decisive refutations of Plantinga's actualism — they are genuine pressures that any adequate actualist framework must address. They motivate the search for alternative actualist accounts, including combinatorialism and ersatzism, each of which tries to capture the advantages of possible-worlds semantics with different ontological commitments and different responses to these difficulties.
