# Armstrong's Combinatorialism

David Armstrong's combinatorialism, developed in *A Combinatorial Theory of Possibility* (1989), represents the most thoroughgoing actualist approach to modality. Where Plantinga inflates the actual abstract ontology with haecceities and states of affairs, Armstrong stays close to the actual world's concrete furniture: the individuals and universals (properties and relations) that natural science reveals. Possible worlds, on Armstrong's view, are just recombinations of these actual building blocks.

## The Core Idea

The inspiration is Wittgenstein's *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*: the world is the totality of facts, facts are combinations of objects and properties, and possible worlds are alternative arrangements of the same objects and properties. Any consistent recombination of actual individuals and actual properties represents a genuine possibility.

Armstrong's ontology is sparse. Only actual things exist — no abstract Platonic objects, no necessarily existing states of affairs, no merely possible individuals. The world consists of concrete particulars (individuals) bearing universals (properties and relations). This is not Platonism about universals but immanent realism: universals exist only insofar as they are instantiated in the actual world.

The fundamental principle: **any arrangement of actual individuals and actual universals that involves no formal contradiction is a genuine possibility**. Possibility is exhausted by logical consistency relative to the actual building blocks.

## Formal Structure

Let I = {a, b, c, ...} be the set of actual individuals and U = {F, G, H, ...} the set of actual universals. A possible state of affairs is any combination — (a, F), (b, G), (a, R, b) — consistent with the others. A possible world is a maximal consistent combination. The principle of **free recombination** says: for any individual x and property F (both actual), there is a possible world where x has F; for any two actual individuals, there is a world where they coexist and one where they don't; for any actual individual, there is a world where it doesn't exist.

This yields three basic types of possibilities: **subtraction** (worlds with fewer individuals than the actual), **addition** (worlds where actual individuals have properties they actually lack), and **rearrangement** (worlds where actual relations between individuals are different).

## Actualist Advantages

The advantages are clear. All ingredients of possible worlds are actual — they are the very individuals and properties that exist in the actual world. There are no non-actual possibilia, no abstract states of affairs, no necessarily existing Platonic entities. The ontology is as sparse as any actualist account can be, and it has a strong naturalistic flavor: modal truth reduces to the combinatorial possibilities of actual natural particulars.

## The Alien Property Problem

A significant challenge: if possible worlds are restricted to recombinations of actual properties, then properties that no actual thing has — alien properties — are impossible. But this seems too restrictive. Physics has discovered fundamental properties (charge, spin, color charge) that were previously unknown. It seems epistemically open, at least, that there could be further fundamental properties not instantiated in our world.

Armstrong's responses were several. First, he allowed structural novelty: properties that are novel combinations of actual simple properties along actual dimensions. A novel color might occupy a new position in the structural space defined by actual color properties — not strictly alien but not yet realized. Second, he challenged the intuition that alien properties are genuinely possible rather than merely epistemically open: our ability to imagine language for alien properties may outstrip genuine metaphysical possibility. The imagination, Armstrong argues, is not a reliable guide to possibility at the level of fundamental properties.

These responses have not fully satisfied critics. The first response concedes that strict combinatorialism cannot accommodate truly novel fundamentals. The second — challenging the intuition — seems ad hoc unless there is a principled reason why actual universals exhaust the possible ones.

## The Formal Criterion

For Armstrong, possibility is identified with logical consistency: a combination of actual individuals and properties is possible iff it involves no contradiction. The criterion is purely formal: the only barrier is formal inconsistency. This has the virtue of providing a clear, non-circular account of what makes something possible.

The challenge: formal consistency may not track metaphysical possibility. "Being made of water and not being made of H₂O" is formally consistent — no logical contradiction — but metaphysically impossible. Combinatorialism seems to count this as possible. Armstrong must either deny the essentiality of composition or supplement his formal criterion with essentialist constraints, at which point the account is no longer purely combinatorial.

## Legacy

Armstrong's combinatorialism has not attracted many adherents, but it represents a philosophically important position: the most parsimonious actualist account available, reducing modal truth entirely to facts about actual individuals and universals. Its limitations — particularly with alien properties and individuals — reveal the genuine constraints that actualism places on the space of possibility. Every actualist account faces some version of these limitations; combinatorialism makes them maximally vivid.
