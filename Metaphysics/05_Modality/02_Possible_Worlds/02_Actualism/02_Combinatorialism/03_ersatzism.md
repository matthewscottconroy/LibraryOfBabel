# Ersatzism About Possible Worlds

Lewis coined the term "ersatz" — German for "substitute" — to describe views that treat possible worlds as abstract objects rather than concrete realities. Ersatzist views accept the theoretical framework of possible worlds while rejecting Lewis's ontological extravagance. The central commitment: possible worlds are abstract representations of how things could be, not genuine concrete alternatives to the actual world.

## Linguistic Ersatzism

The most natural ersatzist view is linguistic: possible worlds are maximal consistent sets of sentences in an ideal language. A possible world is a complete description of a way the world could be. The actual world is the complete true description; other possible worlds are complete false-but-consistent descriptions.

Formally, let L be a fully expressive language with names for every actual individual, predicates for every actual property, and logical and modal vocabulary. A maximal consistent set of L-sentences is a set S such that S is consistent (no contradiction follows from S by logic) and S is maximal (for every sentence φ of L, either φ ∈ S or ¬φ ∈ S). A possible world (linguistic ersatz) is any such set. "Necessarily P" means P is in every maximal consistent set; "Possibly P" means P is in some such set.

The advantage: language is uncontroversially part of the actual world, so possible worlds require no exotic ontology beyond actual linguistic and logical objects. Linguistic ersatzism is maximally actualist about concrete entities.

The disadvantage: the language must represent all possibilities, including alien ones. If a world contains a genuinely alien property P*, we need a predicate in our language for P*. But if P* is alien, we have no actual referent for the predicate. Introducing "P*" as a primitive with no actual referent is philosophically mysterious — we cannot explain what the term means if there is nothing actual it refers to.

## The Representation Problem

Lewis's central objection to all ersatzist views is the **representation problem**: abstract entities represent possibilities only if there is an account of what makes them representations. A set of sentences represents a possible world only if there is a connection between the linguistic items and what they represent. But specifying this connection requires either:

1. Using possible worlds in the explanation (circularity): "The sentence 'There are dragons' represents the possibility of dragons because there is a possible world where dragons exist." This uses possible worlds to explain representation — exactly what ersatzism was supposed to avoid.

2. Appealing to semantic rules and conventions: "The sentence 'There are dragons' is about dragons in virtue of the semantic rules of our language." But semantic rules are themselves specified in modal terms (what a word would refer to in various circumstances) — more circularity.

Concrete possible worlds avoid this problem. When we say "the world where dragons exist is a concrete world containing dragons," no further representational account is needed. The dragons are there, not merely represented. Lewis argues that the theoretical elegance of concrete worlds lies precisely in their avoiding the representation relation entirely — they instantiate possibilities rather than describing them.

## Pictorial Ersatzism and Stalnaker's Primitivism

Pictorial ersatzism, associated with Peter Forrest, holds that possible worlds are structured sets of properties and relations — abstract objects that "picture" how things could be. Rather than linguistic descriptions, possible worlds are abstract structural representations. This avoids linguistic complications but faces the same representation problem in a different form: abstract structures represent only if there is an account of the picturing relation.

Robert Stalnaker (*Our Knowledge of the Internal World*, 2008) has defended a moderate position that treats the notion of a "way the world might be" as a theoretical primitive. Stalnaker is not a Lewisian realist — he does not think possible worlds are concrete universes — but he does not reduce possible worlds to sets of sentences or states of affairs either. Instead, possible worlds are the theoretical entities that best systematize our modal discourse, and the modality governing them is taken as primitive. This is sometimes called **modal primitivism**: modality is a fundamental, irreducible feature of the world, and possible worlds are the constructions that best represent it.

The primitiveness of modality is not a defect on this view — it reflects the fact that modal concepts are fundamental and not further reducible to non-modal vocabulary. The theoretical entities (possible worlds) do real work in systematizing modal discourse without the commitment that they are concrete realities.

## Epistemological Advantage

One advantage of ersatzism that receives less attention than it deserves: if possible worlds are abstract objects that actually exist (as part of the actual abstract ontology), then we can know about them through the same faculties by which we know about abstract objects generally — logical and conceptual reasoning. We do not need causal access to other possible worlds to know modal facts; we need only understand the relevant abstract structures.

This advantage is real but partial: the question of how we know about abstract objects in general remains contested. The Platonist about abstract objects (Plantinga, Lowe) will find this advantage substantial; the nominalist will not. And it is unclear that the problem of modal knowledge is really solved rather than relocated.

## Assessment

Ersatzism represents a family of positions united by the commitment that possible worlds are abstract rather than concrete. No single ersatzist account is without difficulties. Collectively, they represent a serious alternative to Lewis's modal realism — one that trades the extreme ontological cost of concrete worlds for more modest ontological commitments, at the price of either taking modality as primitive or accepting abstract entities as the ground of modal truth. Whether the trade-off is favorable depends on prior commitments about abstract ontology, modal primitivism, and the purpose of possible-worlds theorizing.
