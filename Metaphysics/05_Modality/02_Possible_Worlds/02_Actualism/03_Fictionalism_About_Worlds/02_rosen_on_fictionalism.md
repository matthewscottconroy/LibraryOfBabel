# Rosen on Modal Fictionalism

Gideon Rosen identified the problem with his own view almost as clearly as his critics did. The prefix fictionalist says that "possibly there are dragons" means "according to the Lewisian fiction, there is a world where dragons exist." But what makes the Lewisian fiction the right one to use? And if the truth of modal claims depends on the content of a text, have we not reduced genuine modal knowledge to a kind of textual scholarship?

## The Original Proposal and Its Difficulties

Rosen's 1990 proposal was prefix fictionalism at its most explicit: for any modal claim M, the fictionalist paraphrase is "According to PLW [Lewis's *On the Plurality of Worlds*], M." This yields the following systematic translations:

| Modal claim | Fictionalist paraphrase |
|-------------|------------------------|
| ◇P | "According to PLW, ∃w(P is true at w)" |
| □P | "According to PLW, ∀w(P is true at w)" |
| P □→ Q | "According to PLW, Q is true at the closest P-world" |

The inferential structure is preserved. We reason within the fiction using Lewis's full analytical machinery, and the results are systematically related to our modal intuitions. So far so good.

The difficulty is what we might call the collapse objection. If "possibly, there are dragons" is true just because Lewis's book entails that dragons are possible, then modal truth is a fact about a contingent text. Three consequences follow that should trouble us. First, if Lewis had never written the book, there would be no Lewisian fiction, and modal claims would be truth-valueless or false — which makes the existence of modal truth depend on a philosopher's publishing history. Second, determining whether a modal claim is true becomes, in principle, a matter of checking what Lewis's theory entails — something knowable a priori from analysis of the text, which gets the epistemology of modal knowledge backward. Third, and most sharply, a person who believes that round squares are possible makes an error that is, at bottom, a textual error about what Lewis's theory entails. This cannot be right.

There is also a problem about why the Lewisian fiction rather than some other fiction. Why not use a Plantingan fiction of abstract states of affairs, or a combinatorialist fiction of recombined actual universals? The natural answer is that Lewis's fiction is the most theoretically elegant — it provides the most systematic and unified analyses. But if theoretical elegance is our guide to which fiction to use, we are in danger of sliding from "we use Lewis's fiction because it's most elegant" to "Lewis's theory is most likely true because it's most elegant" — which abandons fictionalism and concedes that the best theoretical systematization of modal discourse is a genuine epistemic guide to modal truth.

## Rosen's Refinements

Rosen responded by proposing that the relevant fiction is not Lewis's actual book but an idealized Lewisian fiction — the best, most complete, most consistent version of the modal realist theory, defined by its theoretical role rather than its physical realization in ink and paper. This move detaches the fictionalist account from dependence on Lewis's actual writings. Modal truth is not a fact about what a philosopher wrote; it is a fact about what the idealized best version of the modal realist framework entails.

The cost is that the idealized fiction is now a theoretical object of unclear status. What is an idealized fiction? It is not a book, not a set of sentences in a contingent language, not a state of affairs. If it is an abstract object of some kind, the fictionalist has reintroduced abstract entities through the back door. The maneuver saves the view from textual contingency but at the price of a new ontological commitment.

Rosen later moved toward a more structurally illuminating distinction. There are, he suggests, two quite different uses of possible-worlds talk. In the semantic use, possible worlds are model-theoretic entities invoked to give truth conditions for modal sentences — they are tools of formal semantics, no more requiring genuine existence than the models in formal logic require their domains to be real. In the metaphysical use, possible worlds are invoked to explain what makes modal claims true — to ground the fact that dragons are possible in something about reality's structure. Fictionalism is appropriate for the metaphysical use. When someone asks what grounds the truth of "possibly there are dragons," the fictionalist answers: nothing grounds it beyond what the best systematizing fiction entails, and this is all the grounding modal claims require or admit of. The semantic use — using possible worlds as model-theoretic posits to characterize inferential structure — requires no metaphysical commitment at all.

## Where Fictionalism Stands

We can put the dialectical situation directly. Rosen identified genuine problems with modal realism and proposed a way of capturing its theoretical benefits without its ontological costs. The parity problem — that fictionalism makes necessary mathematical truths depend on a contingent fiction — has not been fully answered. The collapse objection — that modal truth reduces to textual content — is partially addressed by the idealization move but at some ontological cost. And the problem of grounding the fiction — why this fiction rather than another — either slides toward modal realism or requires an independent account of what makes a systematization of modal discourse adequate, which presupposes precisely the modal truths the fiction was supposed to explain.

The most defensible position in the fictionalist spirit may be a form of agnosticism: use possible worlds as theoretical tools, remain uncommitted about their ultimate ontological status, and resist the demand to give a reductive account of what makes modal claims true. This is less a solution to the parity problem than a refusal to accept its framing. Whether that refusal is principled or evasive is a question that continues to divide philosophers of modality.
