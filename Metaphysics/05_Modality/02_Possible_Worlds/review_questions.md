# Review Questions — Possible Worlds

1. Lewis argues that modal realism is justified by its theoretical payoff: unified analyses of propositions, properties, counterfactuals, causation, dispositions, and laws. Evaluate the strength of this "inference to the best analysis." Does it follow that if each analysis faces serious objections, the cumulative case collapses — or can the argument survive partial failures?

2. The incredulous stare is not a formal argument, yet Lewis takes it seriously as evidence against his view. What is its evidential status? Lewis compares theoretical revision in philosophy to theoretical revision in science, but notes a disanalogy: scientific revisions are supported by vast empirical evidence, whereas modal realism cannot be empirically tested. Is this disanalogy decisive?

3. Lewis's isolation objection to ersatzism charges that abstract possible worlds must represent possibilities without any account of what makes them representations, generating circularity or regress. Ersatzists might respond that concrete worlds face the analogous Benacerraf-style problem: no causal contact, so no knowledge. Which form of the problem is harder to answer, and why?

4. Counterpart theory dissolves the contradiction in attributing incompatible properties to the same individual across worlds by replacing transworld identity with inter-world similarity. But Kripke objects that Humphrey cares about himself winning, not about a counterpart winning. Lewis distinguishes truth conditions from subject matter in response. Is this distinction principled, or does it look ad hoc when compared to how truth conditions and subject matter normally relate?

5. Plantinga's actualism replaces concrete possible worlds with necessarily existing abstract states of affairs, and merely possible individuals with their necessarily existing haecceities. The four problems raised against Plantinga — alien properties, non-existent individuals, ontological proliferation, and the collapse problem — each press in a different direction. Which of these is most damaging to the view, and does any of them apply equally to Lewis's modal realism?

6. Armstrong's combinatorialism is the most parsimonious actualist view: possible worlds are just recombinations of actual individuals and actual universals. The alien property problem reveals a systematic limitation. What does this limitation tell us about the broader constraint that any actualist theory faces: that it must derive the full space of genuine possibilities from the resources of the actual world?

7. Modal fictionalism treats possible-worlds talk as useful without commitment to worlds' existence. Rosen's parity problem is that making "necessarily, 2+2=4" true only relative to Lewis's fiction makes mathematical necessity depend on a contingent text. How does Rosen's refinement — replacing Lewis's actual book with an idealized theoretical fiction — address this problem, and does it successfully avoid introducing a new abstract ontological commitment?

8. Two-dimensional semantics assigns expressions both a primary intension (what they pick out given any world as actual) and a secondary intension (what they pick out in counterfactual circumstances given the actual world fixed). How does this framework explain both the necessary a posteriori and the contingent a priori in a unified way? And why do physicalists argue that even if Chalmers's zombie world is primarily possible, this does not establish the secondary metaphysical possibility that matters for debates about consciousness?
