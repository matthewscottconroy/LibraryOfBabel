# Essence and Essentialism

Is it essential to Aristotle that he be a philosopher? Could he have been a fisherman instead? Is it essential to a tiger that it be a mammal? Could there be a tiger made of silicon? Is it essential to water that it be H₂O? Could there be a watery substance — colorless, odorless, thirst-quenching — that is not H₂O? These questions ask about essence: the properties that something must have in order to be the thing it is, as opposed to the properties it merely happens to have while being that thing.

The concept of essence is ancient — it goes back to Aristotle's notion of the *to ti ēn einai*, the "what it was to be" for a thing — but its contemporary formulation was decisively shaped by Kripke's work in *Naming and Necessity*. Kripke argued that proper names are rigid designators (they pick out the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists) and that natural kind terms like "water" and "tiger" rigidly designate the natural kind they actually refer to. This has modal consequences: water is essentially H₂O because H₂O is what water actually is, and identity statements between rigid designators are necessarily true if true at all.

Kit Fine's subsequent challenge to the modal account of essence has deepened the discussion considerably. Fine argued that essence cannot be identified with necessary truth. Consider: it is necessarily true that Socrates is a member of the singleton set {Socrates}. But it is not part of Socrates's essence to be a member of that set — that fact is about the set, not about Socrates. The notion of essence must be understood in terms of real definition (what a thing is by its nature) rather than in purely modal terms (what is necessarily true of a thing).

Anti-essentialists challenge the coherence or utility of the essential/accidental distinction, arguing that all properties of a thing are equally properties of it and that talk of what is "essential" to a kind depends on context and interest rather than some mind-independent structure in things. This chapter develops these positions and their mutual challenges, asking what essentialism can deliver and whether the distinction between essence and accident is as fundamental as it seems.
