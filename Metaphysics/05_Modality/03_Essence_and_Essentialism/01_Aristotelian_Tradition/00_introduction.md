# Aristotelian Essentialism

Some properties of a thing are essential to it — it could not exist without them. Other properties are merely accidental — it has them, but it could easily have lacked them. Aristotle was a philosopher; he might have been a sailor. He was human; he could not have been a frog. The distinction between essential and accidental properties is among the most fundamental in Aristotle's metaphysics, and it has proven remarkably durable — philosophically productive even in contexts far removed from Aristotle's own framework.

The Aristotelian tradition ties essence to kind membership. The essential properties of a thing are those it has in virtue of being the kind of thing it is. Since Aristotle is a human being, the essential properties of human beings — being rational, being animal — are essential to him as well. Accidental properties are those that vary from member to member of the kind: that Aristotle was a philosopher, lived in Athens, and tutored Alexander are accidental to him because other human beings exist without these features.

This kind-relativization of essence creates a hierarchical picture: Aristotle has essential properties at every level of the taxonomic hierarchy — as an animal, as a mammal, as a primate, as a human — plus further accidental properties that distinguish him from other humans. What is essential at a lower level (being mammalian) is accidental at a higher level (being mammalian is an accidental feature of Aristotle qua animal). This hierarchical structure requires careful handling to avoid both collapsing all properties into the essential and making the distinction between essential and accidental trivial.

Medieval discussions of essence — especially the debates about whether essence precedes existence or vice versa — extended Aristotle's framework in ways that bear on the metaphysics of modal properties, divine nature, and the individuation of finite substances. This section traces the Aristotelian tradition in essentialism from its ancient roots to its medieval development, establishing the historical context for contemporary discussions.
