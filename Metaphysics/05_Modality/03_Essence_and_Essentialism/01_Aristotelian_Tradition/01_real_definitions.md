# Real Definitions and Essential Natures

There is a difference between knowing what the word "water" means and knowing what water is. Someone who has grown up near rivers and drunk from streams has the former knowledge in full — they know how to apply the word correctly, can recognize water when they see it, know that it quenches thirst and flows downhill. But knowing what water is — knowing its fundamental nature — took the development of modern chemistry. Aristotle saw this distinction clearly, and made it the foundation of his theory of scientific knowledge.

## Nominal and Real Definition

Aristotle's distinction in the *Posterior Analytics* is between nominal definitions, which give the meaning of a term, and real definitions (horismós), which give the essence or nature of the thing itself. A nominal definition of "triangle" gives what the word means: a closed plane figure with three sides. A real definition would give the nature of triangles as such — what makes them the kinds of things they are.

This is not merely a linguistic distinction. It reflects a deeper metaphysical commitment: things have natures that can be captured in definitions, natures that are not our inventions but features of the world itself. The real definition is not about how we use words but about what things fundamentally are. And genuine scientific knowledge — what Aristotle calls episteme, demonstrative knowledge — consists in knowledge of real definitions and the essential connections they ground.

The vehicle of such knowledge is the demonstrative syllogism:

- P1: Every blocking of reflected sunlight by an interposing body is a lunar eclipse.
- P2: The earth is currently blocking the reflected sunlight from the moon.
- C: The moon is currently eclipsed.

What makes this more than a deduction from observed correlations is that P1 expresses an essential connection. Eclipses *are* blockings of sunlight — the syllogism demonstrates from the real definition, not merely from an observed pattern. We understand why eclipses occur because we grasp their nature.

A real definition for Aristotle has a specific logical structure: genus plus differentia. The genus gives the broader category; the differentia specifies what distinguishes the kind within that genus. Human: rational animal. Equilateral triangle: plane figure with three equal sides. But not just any genus and differentia will do. The genuine differentia is the one that expresses what is most fundamental to the species within its genus — rationality is the correct differentia for human beings because it is the essential capacity that distinguishes humans from other animals, not because it is the property we happen to use most often when sorting people from horses.

Real definitions must satisfy several constraints. They must be convertible: the definition must be co-extensive with the term defined, so that everything human is a rational animal and every rational animal is human. They must involve per se predication: the properties in the real definition must hold of the subject because of what it is, not contingently. And they must be primary: reaching down to the irreducibly basic features of the kind, beyond which no further differentia applies.

## Fine's Revival

Kit Fine's "Essence and Modality" (1994) revived Aristotelian real definition as the primary vehicle for expressing essential truths. Fine's central claim is that essence is not to be analyzed in terms of modality — not in terms of properties an object has in all possible worlds — but in terms of real definitions: what it is to be x.

The difference matters. The set {Socrates} necessarily contains Socrates wherever both exist. But it is not part of Socrates's real definition that he belongs to any set. The essential truth flows from the real definition of Socrates; the set-membership truth flows from the real definition of sets. Both are necessary, but only the first is essential to Socrates. The modal account of essence, which identifies essential properties with necessary properties, cannot make this distinction. Fine's account, grounding essence in real definitions, can.

Fine distinguishes constitutive essence from consequential essence. The constitutive essence of Socrates is what makes Socrates what he is — being human, being this particular individual. The consequential essence includes everything that follows necessarily from the constitutive essence: being a mammal follows from being human, and so enters Socrates's consequential essence, even if it is not part of his constitutive real definition. Belonging to {Socrates} follows necessarily but flows from the real definition of sets, not Socrates — so it is no part of his essence at all, even his consequential essence.

## Real Definitions and Scientific Investigation

The connection between real definitions and scientific explanation is what gives this picture its reach beyond technical philosophy. Chemistry gives us the real definition of water (H₂O), gold (element with atomic number 79), ethanol (C₂H₅OH). These are not stipulative definitions. Lavoisier did not decide that water would be H₂O; he discovered what water is. This means that scientific investigation is, in part, investigation of real definitions. To discover the real definition of a natural kind is to discover what kind of thing it is at the most fundamental level.

This picture is deeply anti-deflationary. There are genuine facts about what things fundamentally are — discoverable through empirical investigation, not through conceptual analysis of our terms. The program of natural kind essentialism that Kripke and Putnam developed in the twentieth century is fundamentally a defense of real definitions as discoverable truths about the world. What makes their work a vindication of the Aristotelian tradition is not that they read Aristotle but that they arrived, by entirely different routes, at the same conclusion: meaning is not in the head, and the natures of things are not fixed by our stipulations.
