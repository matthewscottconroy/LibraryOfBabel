# Essential Versus Accidental Properties

Socrates is sitting. He is also human. Both are facts about Socrates, but they are facts of a different kind. He could have been standing — the fact of sitting is contingent, accidental, something that holds but might easily not have. Could he have been non-human? Here something changes. Socrates could have been bald or bearded, thin or fat, in Athens or in Corinth. But if we try to imagine a Socrates who is not human — who is, say, a prime number, or a geranium, or a cloud — it begins to seem that we are no longer imagining Socrates but something else entirely. This is the intuition that drives the distinction between essential and accidental properties. And its philosophical development turns out to be surprisingly complex.

## The Aristotelian Framework

Aristotle drew the distinction systematically. In the *Topics* and *Categories*, he identifies four modes of predication that span the essential and the accidental. Definition gives the real nature — "rational animal" predicated of human. Genus gives the broader category — "animal" predicated of human. Property (idion) gives what is necessarily co-extensive but not definitional — "capable of grammar" is necessarily true of humans but is not part of the definition. Accident gives what merely happens to hold — "pale" and "sitting" are true of Socrates but not necessarily.

The division that does the most work is between definition-plus-genus (essential predication) and accident. Essential properties are those that hold of a thing because of what it is; accidental properties hold of it though it might have been otherwise. Socrates's humanity is essential because being human is what Socrates is; his pallor is accidental because it is merely how he happens to be.

## The Modal Account and Fine's Critique

The most natural modern account translates this directly into possible-worlds terms. Property F is essential to individual x just in case: □(x exists → Fx) — necessarily, if x exists, x has F. Property G is accidental to x just in case: x has G but ◇(x exists ∧ ¬Gx) — x has G, but there is a world where x exists without it.

This account connects essence to the formal apparatus of modal logic and possible-worlds semantics. "Socrates is essentially human" means that at every world where Socrates exists, Socrates is human. "Socrates is accidentally pale" means that there is a world where Socrates exists and is not pale. The machinery is clean and formally precise.

Kit Fine's "Essence and Modality" (1994) argued that this account is both too broad and explanatorily backward. Too broad: the modal account counts as essential to Socrates every property he has necessarily. But Socrates necessarily belongs to {Socrates} — in every world where Socrates exists, the set containing exactly Socrates exists and contains him. Socrates is necessarily distinct from the Eiffel Tower. Socrates is necessarily such that 2+2=4. None of these seem to be parts of Socrates's essence. They are necessary properties, but they are not what Socrates is. Fine's diagnosis is that the modal account conflates necessary truth with essential truth, and the conflation runs deep.

The explanatory direction is also wrong. The correct account is:

"Water is H₂O" is necessarily true *because* it is part of the essence of water to be H₂O.

Not: being H₂O is essential to water *because* water is H₂O in all possible worlds.

Essence explains necessity; necessity does not explain essence. The modal account inverts the order of explanation, treating necessity as the ground of essence when it should be the other way around. Essential truths are those that flow from a thing's real definition — from what the thing is — and necessary truths include all of those plus a vast range of others that have nothing to do with what any particular thing is.

## Kripke on Origin and Constitution

Kripke developed two specific claims about essential properties of individuals, both grounding essence in what makes an individual the particular individual it is.

The essentiality of origin: it is essential to each person that they originate from the particular gametes they did. Consider a world where different sperm and egg combine to produce an individual qualitatively very similar to Humphrey. Is that individual Humphrey? Kripke argues that it is not — the resulting person, however similar, would be a numerically distinct person. Their origin determines their identity, and a different origin is a different individual. Formally: □(Humphrey exists → Humphrey originated from gametes G₁ and G₂).

The essentiality of constitution: a table that was actually made from a certain block of wood could not have been made from a completely different block of wood from the outset. Its initial material constitution is essential. A table made from different wood would be a numerically different table, however qualitatively similar. This connects to the broader claim that what makes an individual the individual it is cannot be entirely divorced from what that individual is made of and how it came to be.

These claims are contested. Origin essentialism implies that the very existence of a person depends on a specific reproductive event — a consequence that four-dimensionalists and psychological continuity theorists have pushed back against, arguing that what matters for personal identity is not origin but continuity of psychological organization. But the essentiality of origin has proven remarkably resilient as an intuition even under pressure.

## Essential Properties and Identity

We can put the connection between essence and identity more sharply. The essential properties of x are those whose absence would entail that we are dealing with a different individual. The accidental properties are those that can vary while Socrates remains Socrates — he can stand or sit, age, travel, change his mind — without any of this threatening his identity across time or across worlds. The interesting essential claims are thus not trivial necessities (Socrates is necessarily not a prime number) but those that distinguish Socrates from other possible individuals who might have occupied similar social, biological, and psychological roles.

This connection between essence and identity runs through philosophy of language, metaphysics of personal identity, philosophy of biology, and ethics. What terms rigidly designate is tied to what essential properties their referents have. What makes a person the same person over time involves which of their properties are essential. Whether species have essential properties bears on how membership criteria for biological kinds work. And discussions of moral standing often turn, implicitly, on which properties are essential to being a person rather than merely accidental features of some persons. The distinction between essential and accidental is not a technical curiosity but one of the load-bearing joints of our conceptual architecture.
