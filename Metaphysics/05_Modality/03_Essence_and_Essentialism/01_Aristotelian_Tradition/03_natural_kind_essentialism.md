# Natural Kind Essentialism

The question whether gold could have had a different atomic number feels, at first, like a philosopher's fancy. Of course gold has atomic number 79 — that is what gold is. But then: is it necessarily what gold is, or is it merely a contingent fact about the element that happens to populate our world's periodic table? Kripke and Putnam argued, with rather different emphases, that the necessity is genuine and metaphysical. The consequences extend from philosophy of language all the way to philosophy of biology and the metaphysics of natural law.

## The Classic Arguments

Kripke's argument in *Naming and Necessity* (1980) begins with semantics. "Gold" is a rigid designator — it picks out the same substance in all possible worlds, not whatever is yellow and malleable in a given world, but the specific element that gold actually is. Since "gold" and "the element with atomic number 79" are both rigid designators that actually co-refer, the identity between them is necessary:

- P1: "Gold" rigidly designates the element gold.
- P2: Gold is (in fact) the element with atomic number 79.
- P3: If two rigid designators co-refer, the identity is necessarily true.
- C: □(Gold = the element with atomic number 79).

An element with all the superficial properties of gold — yellow, malleable, chemically inert — but lacking atomic number 79 is not gold; it is a gold-impostor. We discovered empirically (beginning with Lavoisier's work on chemical composition) that gold has this atomic number. The discovery is a posteriori. The connection, once discovered, is necessary.

Putnam's argument in "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975) approaches the same conclusion from the direction of semantic theory. Imagine Twin Earth: a planet exactly like Earth except that what its inhabitants call "water" has chemical structure XYZ rather than H₂O. XYZ is functionally identical to H₂O — it tastes the same, quenches thirst, fills the oceans, falls as rain. When Twin Earthers say "water," do they refer to H₂O or XYZ? They refer to XYZ. "Water" as used on Twin Earth picks out whatever the watery stuff is on their planet. This shows that the extension of a natural kind term is determined partly by the actual nature of the stuff referred to, not solely by the speaker's internal concepts or "meaning in the head." Meaning, Putnam concluded, is not in the head — it is partly constituted by the world.

The formal content of natural kind essentialism can be put simply. For any natural kind K with essential property P:

□(∀x)(x ∈ K → Px)

In every possible world, anything that belongs to natural kind K has property P. For water specifically: in every possible world, anything that is water is composed of H₂O molecules. There is no possible world where water lacks this molecular structure. The necessity is metaphysical, discovered a posteriori.

## The Biological Species Problem

Natural kind essentialism faces its sharpest challenge not in chemistry but in biology. If species are natural kinds with essences, there should be essential intrinsic properties shared by all and only members of each species. But biological species do not behave like this. Homo sapiens includes individuals with enormously diverse physical traits, genetic makeups, and capacities. There is no single morphological or genetic property that all and only humans share. The anti-essentialist argument from biology runs directly:

- P1: If species were natural kinds with essences, each species would have essential intrinsic properties shared by all and only members.
- P2: No species has such properties.
- C: Species are not natural kinds with essences.

Two essentialist responses are worth distinguishing. Historical essentialism — developed paradoxically by the same biologists (Ghiselin, Hull) who attacked essentialism — holds that species are not natural kinds at all but individuals. The essence of membership in Homo sapiens is historical and relational: to be human is to be part of the evolutionary lineage of Homo sapiens, descended from the relevant ancestral population. This is a relational property, not an intrinsic one, and it is compatible with virtually unlimited intrinsic diversity.

The Homeostatic Property Cluster (HPC) theory developed by Richard Boyd takes a different approach. Natural kinds, on this view, are not defined by necessary and sufficient intrinsic conditions but by clusters of properties that tend to co-occur because they causally support each other. Species are HPC kinds: the cluster of biological properties that tend to co-occur in a species is maintained by natural selection and reproductive isolation. There is no single essential property, but membership in a species is a matter of being connected to the right causal-homeostatic cluster. This preserves the idea that species membership is not arbitrary while abandoning the demand for classical essential properties.

## Scientific Essentialism

Brian Ellis and Caroline Lierse extended the essentialist program to natural properties and natural laws themselves. The laws of nature, on this view, are not contingent regularities but necessary connections grounded in the essential natures of the properties involved. Charge is essentially such that things with charge attract or repel other charged things according to Coulomb's law. The law is not a contingent pattern observed to hold — it is constitutive of what charge is. In any possible world where charge exists, Coulomb's law holds.

This makes natural necessity considerably stronger than mere physical necessity: the laws of nature are metaphysically necessary given the natures of the properties they involve. The obvious objection is that we seem to be able to conceive of worlds with different laws. Scientific essentialism's reply follows the standard pattern for the necessary a posteriori: conceivability of different laws reflects epistemic possibility — we do not know a priori what the laws are — not metaphysical possibility. We can describe scenarios where different regularities hold, but if the actual laws flow from the essential natures of the actual properties, then no world containing those properties could have different laws. Whether there are worlds containing different properties, with different essential natures, is a further question.

The stakes extend further than they might seem. If natural laws are metaphysically necessary, then physical determinism, if true, is necessarily true — there is no possible world with the same laws where a physically identical situation evolves differently. This closes off certain modal escape routes that compatibilist accounts of free will sometimes rely upon. The question of natural kind essentialism is thus not merely taxonomic but bears on some of the deepest questions about the structure of reality and the limits of what is genuinely possible.
