# Kripke, Fine, and the Revival of Essentialism

The analytic tradition's resurgence of essentialism was sparked by Kripke's Naming and Necessity, which argued that proper names are rigid designators and that the identity statements they figure in are, if true, necessarily true. The broader consequence was that many apparently contingent identities turned out to be necessary: Hesperus is necessarily Phosphorus; water is necessarily H₂O; human beings are necessarily Homo sapiens (or whatever biological kind they are). These necessities are not known a priori — they are discovered empirically — but they are genuine metaphysical necessities, not mere definitional truths.

Kripke's essentialism was primarily concerned with natural kind terms and with the origin of particular individuals. A table that was made of wood could not have been made of ice — its material origin is essential to it. A person with a particular genetic make-up could not have had a fundamentally different genetic make-up — biological origin is essential in some respects. These claims are grounded in the intuition that origin plays a constitutive role in individual identity: to change the origin is to change the individual.

Kit Fine's influential critique of modal essentialism — the identification of essence with necessary properties — opened a different approach. Fine argued that essence cannot be analyzed in terms of necessity: it is not necessary but essential to Socrates to be a member of the singleton {Socrates}; it is necessary but not essential to {Socrates} that Socrates exists. The direction of explanation runs from essence to necessity, not from necessity to essence. What is essential to a thing is what it is to be that thing — a real definition — and modal facts are consequences of essences rather than constitutive of them.

Fine's proposal restored the Aristotelian priority of real definition over modal characterization, giving essence an explanatory priority that the modal account could not capture. This section examines Kripke's modal essentialism and Fine's critique, assessing their respective contributions to our understanding of essence.
