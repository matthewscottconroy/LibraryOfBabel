# Kripke on Essence: Naming and Necessity

Before Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* — three lectures delivered at Princeton in 1970 and published in 1980 — the prevailing view in analytic philosophy was broadly Fregean and descriptivist: names abbreviate descriptions, and modal truth is either analytic (grounded in meaning) or empirically contingent. Kripke overturned both claims at once. He showed that names work differently from descriptions, that this difference has deep modal consequences, and that some truths are necessary without being analytic — knowable only through empirical investigation, but necessary nonetheless.

## Rigid Designation and Its Consequences

A term is a rigid designator if it picks out the same object in all possible worlds where that object exists. Names are rigid; definite descriptions typically are not. "Aristotle" picks out the person Aristotle at every world where he exists, not whoever plays Aristotle's role in a given world — not whoever is the most famous student of Plato at that world, since that might be someone else. "The teacher of Alexander," by contrast, picks out at each world whoever is Alexander's teacher at that world — a different individual, perhaps, in a world where Aristotle died young.

The philosophical significance follows quickly. If two names are co-referential rigid designators — if "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" both rigidly pick out Venus — then the identity statement "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessarily true if true at all. The proof:

1. ∀x□(x = x) — every individual is necessarily self-identical. (Logical truth)
2. a = b — assumed.
3. □(a = a) — from 1, instantiated.
4. By Leibniz's Law: if a = b, then whatever is true of a is true of b. Apply this to the property λx.□(x = a).
5. □(b = a) — from 2, 3, 4.
6. Therefore □(a = b).

"Hesperus is Phosphorus" is therefore necessary. Yet we did not know a priori that Hesperus is Phosphorus — the Babylonian astronomers discovered this empirically, by tracking the morning and evening star and recognizing that they coincide. So here is a necessary truth known a posteriori: the paradigm of the necessary a posteriori, a category that the Fregean picture had no room for.

## Natural Kind Essentialism

The same argument extends to natural kind terms. "Gold" rigidly designates the element gold; "the element with atomic number 79" rigidly designates that element. If they co-refer — which chemistry has established — the identity is necessary:

- P1: "Gold" rigidly designates the element gold.
- P2: Gold is in fact the element with atomic number 79.
- P3: If two rigid designators co-refer, the identity is necessarily true.
- C: □(Gold = the element with atomic number 79).

An element with all the superficial properties of gold — yellow, malleable, rare, chemically inert — but lacking atomic number 79 is not gold. It is a gold-impostor, or fool's gold of a more fundamental kind. We discovered empirically what gold is; the connection, once discovered, is necessary. Similarly for water: "water is H₂O" is necessarily true if true, and it was discovered empirically to be true. If what we call "water" turned out not to be H₂O, we would say either that our discovery was wrong or that the stuff we have been drinking is not water — not that water is merely contingently H₂O.

The standard objection: we can imagine gold being a different element — imagine a world where what is called "gold" has atomic number 78. So the connection is not necessary. Kripke's reply is careful. We can imagine a world where something that plays the gold-role in everyday life — yellow, malleable, used for jewelry and currency — has atomic number 78. But that imagined substance is not gold; it is some other element. "Gold" rigidly designates our actual gold, and our actual gold has atomic number 79 in every world where it exists. The imaginative scenario is a scenario where a different element plays the superficial gold-role, not a scenario where gold itself has a different atomic structure.

## The Essentiality of Origin

Kripke extended the essentialist picture to individuals through a different kind of argument. Consider this table. It was actually made from a certain block of wood. Could it have been made from a completely different block of wood? Kripke argues: no. Could it have been made of ice from the outset? No. The table's origin from a specific piece of wood is essential to it. A table made from different wood would be a numerically different table, however qualitatively indistinguishable.

For persons: given that Elizabeth II is in fact the daughter of George VI, could she have been the daughter of Harry Truman? No — the individual who would have been born of those parents would not have been Elizabeth II but someone else. □(Elizabeth II exists → Elizabeth II has parents P₁ and P₂). Different parents, different person.

The underlying principle is that origin is constitutive of the identity of particulars. What makes an individual the individual it is cannot be divorced from how it came to be. This is a metaphysical claim, and it is contested — psychological continuity theorists hold that what matters for personal identity is not origin but the continuity of psychological organization, and they resist Kripke's claim that the Truman-descendant could not have been Elizabeth II. But origin essentialism has proven remarkably resilient as an intuition, and Kripke's formulation of it was precise enough to make the debate genuinely tractable.

## Legacy

The transformation Kripke brought about was not merely technical. He established that the space of modal metaphysics was much richer than either the Kantian tradition (necessity = a priori = analytic) or the logical empiricist tradition (necessity = analytic truth of a formalism) had recognized. Genuine de re necessity — necessity concerning objects and kinds, not just linguistic conventions — was back on the philosophical table with a rigorous semantic foundation. The debates about natural kind essentialism, origin essentialism, and the necessary a posteriori that Kripke initiated continue to structure the central issues in modal metaphysics, philosophy of language, and the metaphysics of science.
