# Quine's Scepticism About Essentialism

The same object can appear to be both necessarily and contingently F, depending on how it is described. The number of planets is 9. The number 9 is necessarily odd. If we substitute the co-referring expression, we seem to get: the number of planets is necessarily odd. But this is false — there could have been eight planets, or ten. Quine took this puzzle, developed across "Reference and Modality" (1953) and other papers, to show that de re modal attributions are incoherent. The diagnosis cuts deep: if necessity attaches to objects only via their descriptions, and objects can be described in ways that yield contradictory modal attributions, then "necessarily F" predicated of an object as such is meaningless.

## The Puzzle and What It Reveals

The structure of Quine's challenge is precise. In ordinary extensional contexts, co-referring terms can always be substituted for one another without affecting truth value. "9 is odd" and "the number of planets is odd" are both true, and substitution of one for the other preserves truth in any extensional statement. But modal contexts are intensional: "□(9 is odd)" is true, while "□(the number of planets is odd)" is false, even though "9 = the number of planets." The modal operator creates a context where co-referential substitution fails.

This is not merely a logical curiosity. Quine's point is that quantified modal logic — QML, which allows formulas of the form ∃x□Fx — presupposes that objects have their properties essentially or accidentally independently of how they are described. It presupposes what Quine called "Aristotelian essentialism": the view that among the properties an object has, some are genuinely essential (part of what it is) and others merely accidental. But if modal attributions are always description-relative, there is no such thing as being essentially F as such. The very notion of de re necessity — necessity predicable of objects rather than of descriptions — is without foundation.

Quine articulated three grades of modal involvement in "Three Grades of Modal Involvement" (1953). Modality predicated of sentence types (logical necessity) is acceptable: "necessarily, all bachelors are unmarried" expresses a fact about the sentence, not about bachelors as such. Modality as a sentential operator (de dicto modality) is problematic but less severe. Quantification into modal contexts — de re modality — is "wholly incoherent." The incoherence lies precisely in the requirement that objects have their properties essentially as objects, independent of the descriptions under which we access them.

Quine's deeper commitment here was ontological relativity. What objects exist and what properties they have is always relative to a background theory or conceptual scheme. There is no theory-independent perspective from which to determine which of an object's properties are essential. The Quinean diagnosis extends even to the analytic/synthetic distinction: if "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) is right that this distinction is not well-defined, then there is no principled basis for distinguishing truths that hold in virtue of meaning (necessary) from truths that hold in virtue of fact (contingent) — and the essential/accidental distinction collapses along with it.

## Kripke's Response

Kripke's response in *Naming and Necessity* is that Quine's puzzle confuses two different kinds of expressions with importantly different behavior in modal contexts. "9" is a rigid designator — it picks out the number 9 in every possible world. "The number of planets" is a non-rigid description — it picks out, at each world, whatever is the number of things that are planets at that world. When we say "necessarily, 9 is odd," we use "9" rigidly and make a de re claim about the number 9 that holds at every world where 9 exists. When we say "necessarily, the number of planets is odd," we use the description non-rigidly and make a claim that would require the planet count to be odd in every world — which is false.

The formal contrast:

- "9 is necessarily odd" = ∃x(x = 9 ∧ □(x is odd)): there is something identical to 9 that is necessarily odd. True.
- "□(the number of planets is odd)": in every world, whatever the planet count is, it is odd. False.

No incoherence arises once we correctly understand that the two expressions do not pick out their referent in the same way. De re necessity predicable of an object as such is coherent when it is grounded in a rigid designator. "9 is necessarily odd" and "9 is contingently the number of planets" are both true, and they do not contradict each other — the first uses "9" rigidly; the second notes a contingent fact about the planet count.

The broader lesson is that Quine's puzzle does not show essentialism is incoherent; it shows that modal operators are intensional. The intensionality was already known. What Kripke adds is the distinction between rigid and non-rigid designation, which dissolves the appearance of contradiction without abandoning de re modality.

## The Quinean Legacy

Quine's scepticism dominated modal epistemology from the 1950s through the early 1970s, and most post-Kripkean philosophers accept that the puzzle is solved. But Quinean echoes persist. The question of what principled criterion determines which properties are essential — rather than merely necessary, or necessary relative to a description — has not been fully answered, and Fine's work was in part motivated by the sense that even the Kripkean defense needs supplementation. What Quine's challenge did was force essentialists to be precise about their commitments in a way that pre-Quinean essentialists had not been. That precision has proven productive even for those who reject the sceptical conclusion.
