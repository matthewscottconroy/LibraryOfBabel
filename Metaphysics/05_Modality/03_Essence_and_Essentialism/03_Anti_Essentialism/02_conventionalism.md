# Conventionalism About Necessity

The logical positivists had a clean story about necessity. Necessary truths are analytic truths — true in virtue of meaning. "All bachelors are unmarried" is necessary because of what "bachelor" means. "2+2=4" is necessary because of the rules governing mathematical notation. No mysterious realm of essential natures, no Platonic objects, no metaphysical necessity beyond what our conventions generate. Everything necessary is, at bottom, a decision we made about how to use language.

This picture has a genuine intellectual appeal. It demystifies necessity without eliminating it. It explains why logical and mathematical truths can be known a priori — because they are grounded in our own conventions, accessible without looking at the world. And it fits naturally with an empiricist commitment to the view that all substantive knowledge is empirical: if necessary truths are merely analytic, they carry no factual information about the world, and there is no embarrassing a priori knowledge of extra-logical reality to explain.

## The Internal Collapse: Quine's Challenge

The trouble with this picture is not that it is obviously false but that it relies on a distinction — the analytic/synthetic distinction — that Quine argued in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) cannot be drawn in a principled way. To say that "all bachelors are unmarried" is analytic because "bachelor" means unmarried male is to invoke the notion of meaning in explaining analyticity. But what makes something a truth of meaning rather than a contingent generalization? Being true by definition — but definitions are also a matter of meaning, and the explanation turns in a circle.

The deeper problem is holism. Our beliefs face experience as a corporate body. Any belief can be held true in the face of recalcitrant experience by adjusting other beliefs; conversely, even logical truths can in principle be revised if revision sufficiently simplifies our overall theory. There is no principled distinction between beliefs held in virtue of meaning and beliefs held in virtue of fact — they are all part of the same revisable network. If this is right, then there is no distinguished class of analytic truths, and the conventionalist account of necessity has no foundation to stand on.

Wittgenstein approached the same territory from a different direction in *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics*. Mathematical truths are "grammatical" statements — expressions of our linguistic practices rather than descriptions of an independent mathematical reality. To say "necessarily 2+2=4" is to express a commitment to counting in a certain way, to hold fast to a rule. We hold this belief not because it tracks an abstract mathematical fact but because we are committed to this practice, and to revise it would be to change the game rather than to discover a new truth.

This picture avoids Platonic commitment, but the applicability problem is severe. If mathematical necessity is just the necessity of a rule we follow, why does counting in accordance with "2+2=4" yield the right answer when we count physical objects? The rule seems to apply to reality — apples, planets, particles — in a way that a mere linguistic convention would not obviously do. Some explanation of this applicability must be given, and conventionalism has no obvious account to offer.

## The Decisive Counterexample

Kripke's argument for the necessary a posteriori is, for most philosophers, decisive against conventionalism as a complete account of necessity. Consider:

- P1: "Water is H₂O" is necessarily true — there is no possible world where water lacks this molecular structure.
- P2: "Water is H₂O" is not known by conceptual analysis; it required empirical investigation through chemistry.
- C: Not all necessary truths are necessary in virtue of meaning or convention.

The conventionalist response is available: once we discover empirically that water is H₂O, we make a semantic decision to use "water" as a rigid designator of H₂O, making the connection necessary by convention thereafter. The necessity is the result of how we decide to use "water," not of anything about water's intrinsic nature.

But this response requires reading the necessity of "water is H₂O" as the product of a semantic decision rather than as a discovery about what water is. And this reading seems phenomenologically wrong. When chemists established the molecular composition of water, they did not feel they were making a decision about how to use a word; they felt they were discovering what water is. The necessity of "water is H₂O" does not feel like the necessity of "all bachelors are unmarried" — it feels like a necessary truth about a substance, grounded in that substance's nature, not in our linguistic choices.

## Neo-Conventionalism

Contemporary conventionalists have developed more sophisticated positions. Alan Sidelle argues that modal truths are grounded in our conceptual practices — in which persistence conditions we build into our concepts. We build water's H₂O-ness into the concept of water, making the connection necessary by convention; but we could have adopted different concepts with different persistence conditions. Eli Hirsch's quantifier variance holds that different conceptual schemes employ different quantifiers and different identity criteria, and that what counts as existing or as the same object is scheme-relative. On this view, metaphysical debates about essence and necessity express differences in conceptual frameworks rather than discoveries about an independent reality.

These positions are philosophically serious, but they face the same challenge that confronted logical positivism: if the necessity of "water is H₂O" is entirely conventional, we must be able to describe, in principle, the alternative convention under which water could have had a different molecular structure. And this description seems to require imagining a scenario that is not merely a different linguistic choice but a different physical reality — which is precisely what the conventionalist denies we can coherently do. Whether this objection can be answered depends on how seriously one takes the intuition that some necessities are grounded in the natures of things rather than in our conventions about language — and that intuition is not easy to legislate away.
