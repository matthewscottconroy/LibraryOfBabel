# Essentialism Defended: Responses to Scepticism

It is worth pausing to notice what the anti-essentialist challenges actually established and what they did not. Quine showed that quantified modal logic requires a principled distinction between essential and accidental properties, and questioned whether any such distinction could be drawn without presupposing the very essentialist commitments in question. Conventionalists showed that the appearance of necessity can be generated by semantic decisions, and asked whether there is any remainder — any genuine metaphysical necessity — once the conventions are factored out. These are real points. What they do not establish is that essentialism is incoherent or that no defensible version of it is available. The defenses that have been developed show not only that essentialism survives these challenges, but that it emerges with a clearer and more precise articulation than it had before.

## Kripke's Semantic Defense

Quine's puzzle about the number of planets and the number 9 rests on treating all co-referring expressions as interchangeable in modal contexts. Kripke's response is that names and definite descriptions behave differently. "9" rigidly designates the number 9 in every possible world. "The number of planets" non-rigidly picks out, at each world, whatever happens to be the planet count. When we say "necessarily, 9 is odd," we use "9" rigidly to make a de re claim about the number 9 — true at every world where 9 exists. When we say "necessarily, the number of planets is odd," we use the description non-rigidly to make a claim that would require the planet count to be necessarily odd — false, since the count is contingent.

Formally: "9 is necessarily odd" reads as ∃x(x = 9 ∧ □(x is odd)) — there is something identical to 9 that is necessarily odd. True. "□(the number of planets is odd)" says that in every world, whatever the planet count is, it is odd. False. No contradiction arises between these — they are different claims about different things, and one of them is de re while the other is de dicto.

The defense extends directly to natural kinds: "Water is necessarily H₂O" is true because "water" rigidly designates H₂O — the substance water actually is. This is a de re claim about the substance, not about the word. The necessity attaches to the substance as such, and the description-relativity that worried Quine is dissolved by the distinction between rigid and non-rigid designation.

## Scientific Practice and the Empirical Case

The second line of defense moves from semantics to practice. Scientists make essentialist claims as a matter of course. Gold essentially has atomic number 79; the electron essentially has negative unit charge; water essentially has molecular structure H₂O. These are not treated as conventional definitions that could be otherwise — they are treated as discoveries about what these substances and particles fundamentally are. Revising them would not be a change in linguistic convention but a revision of our understanding of nature.

The argument:

- P1: Our best scientific theories make essentialist claims about natural kinds.
- P2: These claims are subject to empirical confirmation and disconfirmation.
- P3: They have been empirically confirmed — chemistry confirms water is H₂O; particle physics confirms the charge of the electron.
- C: Essentialist claims are empirically confirmable truths, not mere conventions.

The conventionalist will protest that scientists may use essentialist language while the necessity is generated by their semantic decisions about natural kind terms. But this requires reading scientists as doing something other than what they intend — as making semantic choices when they believe they are making discoveries. The conventionalist gloss on scientific practice is revisionary in a way that demands justification, and the burden of proof seems to fall on the revisionist.

## Fine's Conceptual Defense

Fine's contribution is conceptual rather than semantic. We have, he argues, a robust and independently intelligible concept of what makes something what it is — its nature or real definition — that is not equivalent to the concept of necessary property. The set {Socrates} necessarily contains Socrates wherever both exist, but belonging to {Socrates} is not part of what Socrates is. Being distinct from the Eiffel Tower is a necessary property of Socrates, but no part of his essence. These distinctions track a genuine difference in our conceptual apparatus: the concept of essence picks out what lies at the heart of a thing's being, not merely what it happens to have in every world.

If this concept is coherent — and Fine's arguments strongly suggest it is — then essentialism is not eliminable by reduction to necessary property attributions. The conceptual argument:

- P1: We have a robust concept of what makes things what they are — their real definition or essential nature.
- P2: This concept is not equivalent to the concept of necessary property.
- P3: It is indispensable for understanding constitution, dependence, individuation, and scientific classification.
- C: Essentialism is not eliminable, and its basic commitments are defensible.

## Biological Essentialism and the Species Problem

The challenge from biology — that species have no essential intrinsic properties, given the enormous diversity among members of any species — has prompted essentialists to relocate the essence to relational and historical properties. Historical essentialism holds that what it is to be a member of Homo sapiens is not to have any particular intrinsic physical or genetic feature but to be part of the evolutionary lineage of Homo sapiens — to stand in the right historical and reproductive relationships to the founding population. This is an essential property, but it is relational rather than intrinsic: □(∀x)(x is human → x is descended from the founding population H₀). Intrinsic diversity is accommodated because the essential property is historical.

The Homeostatic Property Cluster (HPC) theory developed by Richard Boyd pursues a different strategy, allowing natural kinds to have essences that are clusters of co-occurring properties maintained by causal mechanisms, rather than single necessary conditions. The essence of a species is the homeostatic cluster — membership is a matter of having enough of the relevant properties and being causally connected to the mechanism that sustains their co-occurrence. This preserves the idea that species membership is not arbitrary or entirely conventional while accommodating the variability that biology describes.

The net result is that the challenges to essentialism, taken together, have been more productive than destructive. They forced essentialists to distinguish modal from essential properties, to ground natural kind essentialism in rigid designation and empirical discovery, to extend essentialist commitments to relational and historical properties, and to develop precise formal frameworks for essential predication. Essentialism after these challenges is a more precise and better-defended view than it was before them — and the challenges from Quine and conventionalism are best understood not as refutations but as provocations to clarity.
