# Review Questions — Essence and Essentialism

1. Fine's central objection to the modal account of essence is that it is both too broad and explanatorily backward. Too broad because necessary properties are not always essential (Socrates necessarily belongs to {Socrates}, but this is no part of his essence). Backward because essence should explain necessity, not the reverse. Are these two complaints independent, or does resolving one dissolve the other?

2. Aristotle's real definition specifies genus and differentia and must be convertible, per se, and primary. Fine revives this framework but distinguishes constitutive from consequential essence. How does this distinction block the counterexamples that plague the modal account — specifically the set-membership case — in a way that simply adding "directly essential" does not?

3. Kripke argues that both natural kind terms ("gold," "water") and proper names ("Hesperus," "Phosphorus") are rigid designators, and that co-referential rigid designators yield necessary identities. What is the common semantic mechanism behind both cases, and why does Kripke think the mind-body case ("pain = C-fiber firing"), even if true and necessary, faces a distinctive explanatory burden that natural kind identities do not?

4. The essentiality of origin thesis holds that an individual's origin from specific gametes is essential to it: a numerically distinct gamete combination yields a numerically distinct individual. Psychological continuity theorists resist this, holding that what matters for personal identity is continuity of psychological organization, not origin. What is at stake in this disagreement for how we individuate persons across possible worlds?

5. Quine's scepticism about de re modality turned on the apparent context-relativity of modal attributions — the number 9 is necessarily odd, but "the number of planets" picks out 9 only contingently, so substituting one for the other within a necessity operator yields contradictory results. Kripke dissolves the puzzle by distinguishing rigid from non-rigid designation. Does this fully answer Quine, or does a residual worry remain about what principled criterion identifies which of an object's properties are essential?

6. Natural kind essentialism succeeds for chemical kinds (water = H₂O, gold = atomic number 79) but faces a sharp challenge from biology: species have no intrinsic essential properties shared by all and only members. Historical essentialism and Homeostatic Property Cluster theory respond differently. Do these responses show that natural kind essentialism is unified across the sciences, or do they show that "natural kind essentialism" names a family of quite different views?

7. Scientific essentialism (Ellis, Bird) holds that natural laws are metaphysically necessary because they are grounded in the essential natures of the properties they concern. This entails that there are no possible worlds containing charge where Coulomb's law fails. What are the consequences of this view for the conceivability of worlds with different laws, and how does it interact with the debate about whether metaphysical possibility is more restrictive than logical possibility?

8. The chapter traces a movement from Quinean scepticism through Kripkean semantics to Fine's essentialist grounding of modality. How should this progression be understood: as successive refinements of a single essentialist program, or as a shift from a semantic defense of essentialism (Kripke) to a genuinely different metaphysical framework (Fine) in which possible worlds are no longer foundational? What hangs on which reading is correct for the approach to modal epistemology taken in the next chapter?
