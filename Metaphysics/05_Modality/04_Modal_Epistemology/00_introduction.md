# Modal Epistemology

We know that it is impossible for a bachelor to be married — this follows from the meaning of "bachelor." We know that it is impossible for there to be a round square — the shapes are geometrically inconsistent. We know that it is possible for there to be a mammal that can fly — bats exist. We know that it is possible for gold to be a different color — but wait: if Kripke is right and the essence of gold is its atomic number, then the possibility of differently colored gold is really the possibility of something that looks like gold but isn't. Modal knowledge seems ordinary enough for many cases and deeply puzzling for others.

The puzzle sharpens when we ask about metaphysical necessity and possibility in cases where our modal intuitions are not straightforwardly grounded in conceptual analysis or observation. Is it possible for consciousness to be realized in silicon? Is it possible for there to be a zombie — a being physically identical to a human but with no inner experience? Is it metaphysically possible for water to lack hydrogen? These are not questions we can answer by reflecting on meanings or performing experiments. They require an account of how we access modal facts at all.

The dominant approach invokes *conceivability* as a guide to possibility: if a scenario is conceivable — if one can coherently imagine it — then it is possible. But conceivability and possibility come apart in instructive ways. It was once conceivable (and seemed consistent) that water might not be H₂O; it turned out to be metaphysically impossible. So conceivability cannot be a reliable guide to possibility. Yablo's work on conceivability attempts to rehabilitate the connection; others have turned to rational intuition, modal perception, or inference from known essences.

This chapter develops the epistemology of modality — how we come to know what is possible and what is necessary — and examines its applications: to the conceivability arguments in philosophy of mind, to mathematical knowledge, and to the methodology of philosophy itself, which relies heavily on thought experiments and modal intuitions. Modal epistemology connects the abstract machinery of possible worlds to the human practices of philosophical inquiry.
