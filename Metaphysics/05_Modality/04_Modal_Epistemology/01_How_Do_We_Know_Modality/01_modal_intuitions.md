# Modal Intuitions

We are confident that round squares are impossible. We are confident that dragons are possible, even though none exist. We are confident that it is necessary that anything red is colored. These are modal judgments, and we make them before any philosophical theorizing begins — they are, in some sense, the data for the theory of modality. The question is what epistemological status they have. Are they reliable? What grounds them? And what should we do when they conflict or mislead?

## Intuitions as Evidence

Philosophers treat modal intuitions as evidence for modal claims in a way analogous to how perceptual experiences are treated as evidence for empirical claims. A strong intuition that P is possible is prima facie evidence that P is possible — defeasible, but not weightless. Thought experiments, which are the methodological currency of analytic philosophy, are designed precisely to elicit precise modal intuitions in carefully controlled circumstances.

The background epistemological framework is reflective equilibrium. We begin with pre-theoretic intuitions and theoretical principles. We adjust both until they cohere: revising intuitions in light of theoretical considerations, revising theories in light of intuitions that prove robust under pressure. Modal intuitions enter this process as inputs that carry presumptive evidential weight but are not infallible.

The sources of modal intuition are diverse. Some derive from conceptual competence: we know that round squares are impossible because we understand "round" and "square" and recognize their incompatibility. Some derive from imagination: "dragons might exist" is supported by our ability to coherently conceive a world with dragons. Some are informed by scientific knowledge: that water must be H₂O and tigers must belong to Panthera tigris reflects the integration of natural-kind knowledge into modal judgment. And some are shaped by evolutionary history and individual experience — our brains evolved to represent possibilities relevant to survival, and this fact raises the question of whether our modal intuitions are reliable guides to objective modal facts or merely adaptive heuristics with a limited scope of reliability.

## Failures and Their Significance

There are well-known cases where initially compelling modal intuitions turn out to be mistaken. The conceivability of water not being H₂O initially suggests it is possible; Kripke showed this intuition confuses epistemic conceivability with metaphysical possibility. The apparent conceivability of Goldbach's Conjecture being false might suggest it is contingently true or false; but if mathematical truths are necessary, this conceivability generates no real modal possibility at all. These failures are not merely isolated anomalies — they indicate that the inference from "S can intuit that P is possible" to "P is possible" is genuinely defeasible, and that calibration of modal intuitions is needed, not just collection of them.

Experimental philosophy has sharpened this concern empirically. Work by Stephen Stich, Jonathan Weinberg, Shaun Nichols, and colleagues has shown that philosophical intuitions about thought experiments vary systematically across cultures and demographic groups. If modal intuitions vary in this way, their use as evidence for objective modal claims requires justification, not assumption.

The defenders of modal intuitions as evidence offer three related responses. Defeasibility is not the same as unreliability: perceptual experiences are fallible too — illusions occur — but perception remains evidence, and the appropriate response to fallibility is calibration, not abandonment. Not all intuitions carry equal weight: those that survive sustained reflection, are widely shared among careful thinkers, and cohere with theoretical considerations deserve more credence than fragile, idiosyncratic, or theory-dependent judgments. And the relevant standard for modal evidence is normative rather than sociological: what matters is what ideally rational agents under conditions of full information would intuit, not what any particular demographic actually does intuit. Cultural variation in intuitions may reflect non-ideal conditions — incomplete information, unreflective responses — rather than genuine variation in the modal facts.

## The Central Epistemological Question

The question left standing after these defenses is the deepest one: what epistemic mechanism connects our intuitive judgments about possibility and necessity to the actual modal facts? For empirical knowledge, the connection is causal — the world causes our perceptions, and this causal connection grounds reliability. For modal intuition, no such causal story is available: modal facts do not cause our intuitions in any physical sense.

Three accounts are on the table. Inferentialism holds that modal intuitions are derived from recognized inferential connections — we know that P is necessary because we can demonstrate it from principles we already know, and the intuition registers this connection. Rationalism holds that rational intuitions provide non-inferential justification for modal beliefs, tracking necessary truths through a faculty that, when functioning properly under conditions of rational clarity, is constitutively connected to the truths it registers. Conceivability-based accounts hold that the exercise of coherent imagination provides evidence about genuine possibility, with the refinements needed to handle the necessary a posteriori cases. Each faces challenges that the others illuminate, and the epistemology of modality remains a genuinely open question — but not one that philosophers are without resources to address.
