# Conceivability and Possibility

Descartes argued in the Sixth Meditation that he could clearly and distinctly conceive of his mind existing without his body — a purely thinking thing, with no extension. From this, he concluded that mind and body could exist separately, and therefore that they are distinct substances. The argument turns on a principle that has been central to modal epistemology ever since: if something is conceivable, it is possible. Call this the conceivability-to-possibility (CP) inference:

(CP) If it is conceivable that P, then it is possible that P.

Or formally: if P is epistemically possible (◇ₑP), then P is metaphysically possible (◇P).

The principle has obvious intuitive support. If I can coherently conceive of a talking dog — a scenario with no apparent internal contradiction — this seems to tell us something about the genuine possibility of talking dogs. If I cannot conceive of a round square without arriving at contradiction, this seems to tell us something about its genuine impossibility. Conceivability is our main handle on the modal domain, and the CP inference is the instrument by which we use it.

## Why the Naive Inference Fails

Kripke diagnosed the failure in Descartes's argument by distinguishing two quite different things that might be meant by "conceivability." In one sense, Descartes's scenario is conceivable: for all we know, mind and body might be separate. This is epistemic conceivability — the absence of a known contradiction. But it does not follow that mind and body are metaphysically possibly separate. If mind-body identity is necessary (as the identity theorist holds), then conceiving of them as separate is conceiving of a genuinely impossible scenario. The conceivability is real, but it is not a guide to metaphysical possibility.

The water case makes this vivid. We can conceive — in the epistemic sense — of water not being H₂O. Before the development of chemistry, no one knew that water was H₂O, and it was epistemically open whether water might turn out to be a simple element. But water is necessarily H₂O; there is no possible world where water lacks this molecular structure. The epistemic conceivability of water not being H₂O does not establish the metaphysical possibility of this. The Goldbach case is similar: we can conceive of Goldbach's Conjecture being false — imagine some even number that has no prime decomposition — but if the conjecture is true, it is necessarily true (mathematical truths are necessary), and our conceivability of its falsity tracks only our ignorance, not any genuine modal possibility.

These failures are not marginal; they concern the very cases — natural kind identities and mathematical truths — that have been most philosophically important. The naive CP inference needs fundamental refinement.

## Chalmers's Refinement

David Chalmers's "Does Conceivability Entail Possibility?" (2002) provides the most systematic attempt at a refined CP principle. Chalmers distinguishes two axes of conceivability.

The first is prima facie versus ideal conceivability. A scenario is prima facie conceivable if it seems conceivable on the surface, without sustained reflection. It is ideally conceivable if it remains conceivable after all relevant a priori knowledge has been brought to bear — if even under ideal rational reflection, no a priori contradiction emerges. The necessary a posteriori cases (water not being H₂O) are prima facie conceivable but not ideally conceivable: once we understand rigid designation and the necessity of identity, we recognize that the scenario involves a modal impossibility, and the conceivability dissolves under rational pressure.

The second is positive versus negative conceivability. A scenario S is positively conceivable if we can form a coherent positive conception — a mental model that represents the scenario in some detail. It is negatively conceivable if we cannot derive a contradiction from S on a priori grounds — if S is not ruled out a priori, even if we cannot positively represent it.

Chalmers's refined principle: **ideal positive conceivability entails primary metaphysical possibility.** This is a two-dimensional claim. Primary possibility is possibility at the level of primary intensions — possibility that is not ruled out by any a priori conceptual relationship. The zombie world (beings physically identical to us but lacking phenomenal consciousness) is ideally positively conceivable because no a priori contradiction follows from the concept of a world where physical and functional roles are filled but phenomenal character is absent. Whether this entails secondary metaphysical possibility — possibility given the actual essences of the physical and phenomenal — is a further question that the refined CP principle deliberately leaves open.

## The Zombie Argument and Its Discontents

The most consequential contemporary application of the CP inference is Chalmers's argument against physicalism:

- P1: Zombies — beings physically identical to humans but lacking phenomenal consciousness — are ideally positively conceivable.
- P2: Ideal positive conceivability entails primary metaphysical possibility.
- C1: Zombies are primarily possible.
- P3: If zombies are primarily possible, phenomenal consciousness is not logically supervenient on the physical.
- C2: Physicalism (specifically, versions that claim phenomenal facts are a priori entailed by physical facts) is false.

Physicalist responses take several forms. The phenomenal concepts reply, associated with Brian Loar and Joseph Levine, holds that phenomenal concepts pick out physical states via a distinctive mode of presentation — the phenomenal mode. This explains why we can conceive of zombies without contradiction at the level of concepts (phenomenal and physical concepts present the same state in different ways) without establishing that zombies are genuinely possible. The conceivability is explained by the peculiar structure of phenomenal reference, not by any genuine modal fact. The illusionism reply, associated with Keith Frankish, holds that what seems to be phenomenal consciousness in the philosophically loaded sense is itself a kind of cognitive representation, and that once we understand what phenomenal consciousness actually is, the conceivability of zombies evaporates.

Both replies concede that there is something to explain — that the conceivability of zombies is real at some level — and locate the explanation in the structure of our concepts rather than in the modal facts. Whether this is satisfying depends on whether one thinks the phenomenal concepts strategy can bear the weight placed on it, and the debate continues to generate serious philosophical work.

## The Survivability Assessment

The naive CP inference fails decisively: the counterexamples from necessary a posteriori truths are too well-established to dismiss. The refined inference — ideal positive conceivability to primary possibility — is more defensible. What remains contested is how much work primary possibility does: whether establishing that a scenario is primarily possible is sufficient for the philosophical conclusions that conceivability arguments are designed to support. The zombie argument, the Cartesian conceivability argument, and analogous arguments in other domains all require that primary possibility translates into the right kind of metaphysical possibility — and whether it does is precisely what the physicalist disputes. The CP inference, even in its refined form, is not a knock-down argument but a framework for pressing a genuine philosophical question about the relationship between conceptual structure and modal truth.
