# Modal Knowledge in Practice

Modal knowledge is not confined to abstract philosophical argument. We exercise it constantly in practical reasoning, scientific theorizing, and ethical deliberation. To plan for the future is to consider what might happen and what would be necessary to bring about what we want. To explain a phenomenon scientifically is often to identify what must have been the case given what was. To assess a moral claim is sometimes to ask whether a situation could have been otherwise, and whether the agent could have acted differently.

In science, modal knowledge appears in the form of counterfactual reasoning and necessity claims. When physicists say that conservation of energy is a law, they are saying more than that energy has always been conserved in observed cases — they are saying that it must be conserved, that any process in which energy is not conserved is physically impossible. When biologists explain the structure of the heart in terms of its function (pumping blood), they are invoking the modal claim that a heart that did not pump blood could not, in the relevant counterfactual sense, be a heart at all.

In ethics, modal knowledge appears in judgments of moral responsibility and in the assessment of principles. The Kantian categorical imperative is explicitly modal: "Act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." The assessment of whether a maxim is universalizable is a modal assessment. Assessments of moral responsibility require counterfactual reasoning: was the agent able to do otherwise? Could the harm have been prevented?

How reliable is our practical modal reasoning? The psychology of modal cognition suggests that we are not perfectly reliable modal reasoners — we are subject to systematic biases and errors in counterfactual thinking, tend to conflate epistemic and metaphysical possibility, and have difficulty reasoning about distant possibilities. This section examines modal knowledge in practical and scientific contexts, assessing its reliability and the factors that distort it.
