# Explaining Modal Error

If we have a faculty for modal knowledge, we also have a faculty for modal error. Understanding how modal error arises is not merely of defensive value — it illuminates what the faculty for modal knowledge is actually tracking, and helps calibrate our confidence in specific modal judgments.

## The Central Error: Confusing Epistemic and Metaphysical Modality

The most philosophically consequential source of modal error is the conflation of epistemic and metaphysical modality. Something can be epistemically possible — for all we know, it might be true — without being metaphysically possible — genuinely realizable in some world. The canonical case is "water might not be H₂O": epistemically open before chemistry was developed, and still epistemically easy to imagine, but metaphysically impossible, since water necessarily is H₂O.

Kripke's analysis of this error is precise. When we say "I can imagine water not being H₂O," we are accessing one of two distinct scenarios. In the epistemic scenario, we imagine a world where we discover that "water" has been referring to a substance with a different molecular structure — where our scientific theories turned out to be wrong, or where the substance we called "water" had been mislabeled. This scenario is genuinely conceivable; it is consistent with everything knowable a priori. In the metaphysical scenario, water itself — the actual substance, H₂O — would have a different molecular structure. This scenario is not conceivable under ideal rational reflection, because water just is H₂O. The error is that we use the epistemic scenario as evidence for the metaphysical one.

Formally, the invalid inference is:

- ◇ₑ¬(water = H₂O) — it is epistemically possible that water ≠ H₂O.
- Therefore ◇¬(water = H₂O) — it is metaphysically possible that water ≠ H₂O.

The premise is true; the inference is invalid. And the general pattern extends widely. For any necessary a posteriori identity A = B, we can imagine a world where the term "A" or "B" turns out to refer to something different — a world where linguistic practice goes otherwise. But this variation in what words refer to does not generate a metaphysical possibility that the actual A ≠ B. The necessity is of the thing, not of the name.

## Imagining Scenarios with Hidden Inconsistencies

A second source of modal error is imagining scenarios that appear consistent but are subtly inconsistent at a level our imagination does not reach. We represent the scenario at a high level of abstraction and fail to notice the contradiction because it lies in the details we have not filled in.

Consider the case of tachyons — particles traveling faster than light. One can form some kind of imaginative representation of such particles. But special relativity entails that accelerating any massive particle to lightspeed would require infinite energy, making faster-than-light travel physically impossible given the actual laws. The scenario seems imaginable because our imagination operates at a higher level than the physics. Similarly, "a world where water boils at room temperature under normal atmospheric pressure" may seem coherent on first hearing, but the boiling point of H₂O at standard pressure is determined by the molecular structure, and a scenario where that changes while water remains water involves a hidden inconsistency.

The lesson is that imagination has limited resolution. We imagine "a flying horse" without modeling the aerodynamics, and we imagine "time travel" without resolving the grandfather paradox. The absence of noticed inconsistency in our imaginative representation is evidence of conceivability, but only defeasible evidence — it can be overridden by more careful theoretical analysis that reveals inconsistencies invisible to imagination.

## Mathematical Error and Epistemic Possibility

Mathematical truths, if necessary, generate a specific form of modal error: we treat epistemic open questions about mathematics as genuine modal possibilities. Goldbach's Conjecture — every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two primes — is unproven. We can form a coherent-seeming supposition of its falsity. But if the conjecture is true, it is necessarily true, and our epistemic open-ness to its falsity does not correspond to any metaphysical possibility.

This is not a remote theoretical concern. Before the proof that √2 is irrational, it was epistemically open whether rational numbers adequately represent all magnitudes — one could form a coherent mathematical assumption that they do. The proof showed that this epistemic possibility was not a metaphysical one. Mathematical proofs by contradiction work precisely by recognizing this: we assume a statement is possibly true, derive a contradiction, and conclude it is impossible (necessarily false). The "assumption of possibility" in such proofs is always provisional and epistemic — it is not a claim about metaphysical modal space.

## Context-Dependence and Hidden Conditionality

A further source of modal error is treating context-relative modal claims as absolute necessities. "Necessarily, water boils at 100°C" is true relative to standard atmospheric pressure — a background condition that we hold fixed without making it explicit. At sufficiently low pressure, water boils at room temperature; at sufficiently high altitude, it boils below 100°C. Neglecting this implicit conditionality generates modal errors by reading context-fixed truths as absolute necessities.

The methodological lesson is cumulative. Always distinguish epistemic from metaphysical modality. Check whether an apparent possibility is genuinely metaphysical or merely epistemic. Examine whether implicit conditions are being held fixed. Distinguish de dicto readings (where the modal operator governs a description) from de re readings (where it governs an object). Be cautious about treating imaginability as proof of possibility, especially for scenarios whose consistency depends on details the imagination does not represent. And apply the Kripke lesson: for natural kind terms, epistemic scenarios involving alternative discoveries do not generate metaphysical possibilities. These are not counsel to distrust modal reasoning — they are the tools by which modal reasoning becomes more reliable.
