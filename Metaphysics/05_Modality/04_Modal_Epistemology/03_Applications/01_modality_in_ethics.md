# Modality in Ethics

Moral discourse is saturated with modal claims. Moral obligations require the possible: we do not hold people responsible for what they could not have done, and we do not say they were obligated to perform an impossible act. Some moral wrongs are presented as necessary — torturing innocents for amusement is wrong not just in this world but in any world where the relevant facts obtain. Virtuous character is defined not by what an agent actually does in actual circumstances but by what they would do across a range of possible situations. The modal dimension of ethics is not a technical addendum; it is built into the structure of moral thought.

## "Ought Implies Can"

The central modal principle in ethics is Kant's "ought implies can" — ultra posse nemo obligatur, no one is obligated beyond what they can do:

(OIC): OA → ◇A

If a person is morally obligated to do A, then it must be possible for them to do A. Obligations cannot require the impossible.

The principle connects directly to debates about free will and moral responsibility. If determinism is true, then given the past facts and the laws of nature, every action is physically necessitated — it is not possible for the agent to have acted otherwise. Combined with OIC:

If the agent could not have done otherwise, then the agent could not have been obligated to do otherwise.

This is van Inwagen's Consequence Argument applied to moral responsibility, and it creates pressure toward incompatibilism: if determinism leaves no genuine alternative possibilities, and if OIC requires genuine alternative possibilities for obligation, then determinism undermines moral responsibility.

The compatibilist response is to offer a different reading of the "can" in "ought implies can." The relevant possibility is not physical possibility in the categorical sense — it is not whether some other sequence of physical events was genuinely open — but what we might call agent possibility: whether the agent would have acted otherwise had they chosen differently, or had their deliberation gone otherwise. "Could have done otherwise" means "would have done otherwise under the right conditions" — a conditional, dispositional modality rather than a categorical one. This compatibilist move is philosophically contested but not obviously wrong; the question is whether the conditional reading adequately captures what obligation requires.

## Modal Structure of Ethical Theories

Consequentialism evaluates actions by their possible outcomes and the probabilities of those outcomes. Expected utility theory is inherently modal: the value of a choice is determined by a probability-weighted sum of utilities across possible outcomes. What one ought to do is calculated by ranging over possibilities — making "ought" a function of modal structure.

Deontological ethics makes the modal force even more explicit. The Kantian prohibition on treating persons merely as means is not offered as a claim that this tendency generally produces bad outcomes; it is offered as a necessary moral constraint: □(¬(treat persons merely as means)). The necessity is essential to the deontological character of the claim. A prohibition that holds only when convenient, or only when circumstances are favorable, is not a deontological prohibition at all.

Virtue ethics characterizes virtuous dispositions modally: courage is not any action that happens to be brave in some actual situation, but a stable disposition to act bravely across all situations that call for it. The virtuous agent acts rightly not just actually but counterfactually — they would act rightly in a range of possible circumstances. Aristotle's practical wisdom (phronesis) is precisely a capacity to perceive what is required across the full range of circumstances one might encounter, not just the circumstances one actually faces.

## Moral Realism and Modal Status

Debates in meta-ethics turn partly on modal questions about the status of moral truths. The supervenience of moral on natural properties is standardly formulated as a necessary claim:

□(∀w₁∀w₂)[(w₁ and w₂ agree on all natural facts) → (w₁ and w₂ agree on all moral facts)]

Any two worlds exactly alike in all natural respects are exactly alike in all moral respects. Moral properties supervene necessarily on natural ones. This necessity is compatible with non-naturalism — the claim that moral properties are not identical to natural properties — but it constrains the modal relationships between them.

A stronger question is whether specific moral truths are contingent or necessary. "Torturing innocents for amusement is wrong" seems not merely to describe what happens to be wrong in our world but to express a truth that would hold in any world where the relevant facts about suffering, interests, and rational agency obtain. Arguments for necessary moral truths appeal to the natures of persons — the fact that persons can suffer, have interests, are rational agents — as generating necessary moral connections. On this view, the wrongness of torture tracks features of what it is to be a person, and these features are necessary features.

## Possible-Worlds Semantics for "Ought"

The semantics of "ought" in natural language connects moral normativity to possible-worlds frameworks directly. Kratzer-style semantics analyzes "ought" as a modal operator with a modal base (the set of worlds compatible with the contextually relevant facts) and an ordering source (a ranking of possible worlds by how closely they conform to moral ideals). "You ought to help your neighbor" is true at a world w just in case, among the worlds accessible from w given the modal base, the worlds most highly ranked by the ordering source — those that best conform to moral ideals — are worlds where you help your neighbor.

This framework allows systematic representation of conditional obligations, defeasible norms, and the interaction of moral and non-moral constraints. It also connects the formal analysis of "ought" to the broader metaphysics of modality: the accessibility relation governing moral "ought" is determined by moral theory, and the ordering source encodes the content of moral requirements. Whether one finds this framework illuminating or reductive depends on whether the possible-worlds representation of moral ideals captures what moral norms actually are — a question that connects the technical semantics back to normative ethics.
