# Modality and Mathematics

"2+2=4" could not have been false. There is no possible world in which two pairs of apples sum to anything other than four apples, no world in which the arithmetic relations among numbers work differently. This is the starting intuition: mathematical truths are necessary. But understanding what this necessity amounts to — whether it is analytic, synthetic, grounded in abstract objects, or traceable to modal structure itself — turns out to be one of the most difficult questions in the philosophy of mathematics.

## Mathematical Necessity and Its Sources

The claim that mathematical truths are necessarily true is almost universally accepted. □(2+2=4) — it is necessary that 2+2=4. This necessity is not merely nomological or physical; it seems to outrun any contingent features of the world. The laws of physics are perhaps contingent — they could have been different — but arithmetic seems necessary across all possible worlds, including ones with different physical laws. Mathematical knowledge is therefore, in principle, a priori: we do not need to conduct experiments to confirm that 2+2=4, because it holds in every possible world accessible through rational reflection.

The question is what grounds this necessity. The logicist program, associated with Frege and Russell, proposed that mathematical necessity is logical necessity: arithmetic truths follow by logic from purely logical or conceptual principles. Frege's theorem shows that arithmetic can be derived from Hume's Principle — the number of Fs equals the number of Gs if and only if F and G can be put in one-to-one correspondence — together with second-order logic. Neo-logicists Crispin Wright and Bob Hale have revived this program, arguing that Hume's Principle is analytic — true in virtue of the concept of number — and that arithmetic's necessity is therefore conceptual necessity.

Whether Hume's Principle is genuinely analytic is contested. If it is not a conceptual truth but a substantial mathematical principle, the logicist reduction leaves the source of necessity unexplained. And Quine's challenge, from a different direction, raised the question of whether the distinction between conceptual necessity and empirical contingency can be maintained at all. On Quine's holistic picture, mathematical and physical claims face experience together, and the immunity of mathematical claims to empirical revision is a matter of degree, not of kind. Mathematical truths are so deeply embedded in our theorizing that revising them would be extraordinarily disruptive — but this does not make them necessary in any absolute sense.

## Fictionalism and Modalism

Field's fictionalism in *Science Without Numbers* (1980) takes a different approach. Mathematical objects do not exist; consequently, mathematical claims are systematically false (or rather, true only within a fiction). But the fiction is consistent — there are no possible models in which the axioms hold and "2+2=4" fails — and this consistency grounds the modal claim: necessarily, any model of arithmetic satisfies arithmetic truths.

Fictionalism struggles precisely with the modal dimension it must invoke. If "2+2=4" is merely fictionally true, why does it seem genuinely necessary rather than necessary-relative-to-a-fiction? The intuition of absolute mathematical necessity is stronger than the intuition that our arithmetic fiction is simply a useful one.

Geoffrey Hellman's modalism in *Mathematics Without Numbers* (1989) makes the modal commitments explicit. Mathematical truth is analyzed as: any structure that satisfies the axioms of arithmetic also satisfies the relevant theorems. "There are infinitely many primes" is true just in case necessarily, any model of arithmetic has infinitely many primes:

□(∀M)(M models arithmetic → M contains infinitely many primes)

This analysis preserves the necessity of mathematics without commitment to actually existing abstract objects. The ontological commitment is to possible structures rather than actual mathematical entities. Arithmetic is about what any possible model of arithmetic is like — a genuinely modal rather than Platonistic commitment.

The challenge for modalism is that characterizing possible structures without already employing mathematics appears impossible. We describe possible models using mathematical vocabulary, and the modal space of possible models must be characterized with resources that may themselves presuppose mathematical commitment.

## Independence, Set Theory, and Modal Contestation

The foundations of mathematics in set theory introduce specific modal questions that go beyond elementary arithmetic. The Axiom of Choice (AC) is independent of ZF set theory — Gödel established its consistency with ZF, and Cohen established the consistency of its negation. Neither AC nor ¬AC is provable from the remaining axioms. The question of what modal status this independence has is philosophically significant.

Platonists who believe in a unique mathematical universe — Gödel was one — hold that AC is either true or false of that universe, and therefore either necessarily true or necessarily false, even though we cannot prove which. The mathematical reality settles the question independently of our proof systems. Modal structuralists hold that both ZF+AC and ZF+¬AC describe possible mathematical structures — that there is no single mathematical reality to settle the question, only a plurality of possible structures, each internally consistent.

Similar questions arise for large cardinal axioms. Do claims about measurable cardinals or supercompact cardinals describe necessary truths about the mathematical universe, or possible features of some mathematical structures but not others? Mathematical necessity, at the level of advanced set theory, becomes modally contested in a way that elementary arithmetic does not.

## Mathematical Knowledge as Modal Knowledge

Chalmers has argued that mathematical facts are a priori necessary: they follow from the a priori conceptual structure of mathematical concepts. On the two-dimensional framework, mathematical truths are true at every world considered as actual — the primary intension of "2+2=4" is true at every context, because in any world where the concepts of arithmetic are instantiated, the truth holds by those concepts' very structure. Mathematical knowledge is paradigmatic modal knowledge: we know mathematical truths by grasping mathematical concepts, and this grasp reveals their necessary truth.

This view connects mathematical epistemology to rational intuition. How do we come to know the axioms? Not by surveying mathematical objects (the causal access problem is severe for Platonism) but by recognizing conceptual connections — by seeing, through rational reflection, that the axioms express necessary features of the mathematical concepts. Whether this "seeing" is a mysterious faculty or a mundane exercise of conceptual competence is precisely the question that divides rationalists from naturalists in the epistemology of mathematics, and it is the same question that divides them in the epistemology of modality more broadly. The two domains are connected at the root.
