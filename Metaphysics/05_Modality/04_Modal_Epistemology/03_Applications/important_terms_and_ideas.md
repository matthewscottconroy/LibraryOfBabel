# Important Terms and Ideas: Applications of Modal Epistemology

**Zombie Argument**
Chalmers's argument from the conceivability of philosophical zombies (beings physically identical to us but lacking phenomenal consciousness) to the possibility of consciousness without physical realization, and hence to the falsity of physicalism. The argument has generated extensive debate about whether conceivability entails possibility in this case.

**Modal Ontological Argument**
Plantinga's argument: it is possible that a maximally great being exists (this is conceivable and seems coherent); by S5, if it is possible that a necessary being exists, then it necessarily exists; therefore God exists. The argument relies on the S5 axiom and raises questions about what grounds the premise of possibility.

**Thought Experiment Methodology**
The practice of using carefully constructed hypothetical cases to generate modal knowledge. In ethics, thought experiments like the trolley problem probe which features of a situation are morally necessary or sufficient; in personal identity, they probe what is essential to persons; in philosophy of mind, they probe the modal relationship between physical and phenomenal properties.

**Modal Illusion**
Cases where our modal intuitions are systematically mistaken—where we judge something to be possible (or impossible) when in fact it is not. Examples include the intuition that we could have had different parents (which Kripke denies) and the intuition that Hesperus could have been distinct from Phosphorus (which is false, given they are necessarily identical).

**Nomological vs. Metaphysical Modality**
A distinction relevant to applications: some things are nomologically impossible (impossible given actual laws of nature) but metaphysically possible (conceivable in a world with different laws). Philosophy of science and ethics often appeal to nomological modality; metaphysics requires the stronger metaphysical sense.
