# Review Questions — Modal Epistemology

1. Modal intuitions function as evidence for modal claims in the way perceptual experiences function as evidence for empirical claims. But unlike perception, modal intuitions have no causal connection to the modal facts they purportedly track. What are the three accounts — inferentialism, rationalism, and conceivability-based accounts — offered to bridge this gap, and what distinct problem does each face?

2. The naive conceivability-to-possibility inference (CP) fails for cases of the necessary a posteriori: "water is not H₂O" is conceivable in the epistemic sense but not metaphysically possible. Chalmers's refined principle restricts the inference to ideal positive conceivability and primary metaphysical possibility. Does the refined principle do enough work to vindicate conceivability as a guide to metaphysical possibility, or does it preserve conceivability's evidential role only by substantially weakening what "possible" means?

3. Williamson's anti-exceptionalist account treats modal knowledge as a species of counterfactual reasoning — a cognitive capacity we exercise routinely in everyday life. One challenge is that counterfactual reasoning about, say, whether a glass would break if dropped is continuous with empirical causal knowledge, whereas reasoning about whether water could fail to be H₂O seems to require grasping water's nature across all possible worlds. Is this challenge decisive against the anti-exceptionalist, or can the account be developed to meet it?

4. Thought experiments are the methodological currency of analytic philosophy; their philosophical force derives from modal structure. Brown argues that some thought experiments (Galileo's falling bodies) yield a priori knowledge of necessary truths — genuine platonic thought experiments. Sorensen argues that thought experiments are epistemologically continuous with ordinary empirical reasoning. What is at stake in this disagreement for how we understand the epistemology of philosophy itself?

5. The most common source of modal error identified in the chapter is the conflation of epistemic and metaphysical modality: treating "for all we know, P might be true" as evidence that P is genuinely metaphysically possible. How does the two-dimensional framework from Chapter 2 help diagnose this error systematically, and are there types of modal error it cannot capture?

6. Van Inwagen's targeted modal skepticism accepts that we know basic logical and mathematical necessities but doubts that we have adequate grounds for ambitious claims — that zombies are possible, that Cartesian dualism is coherent, that slightly different laws are metaphysically possible. The calibrated position the chapter recommends suggests our confidence should track the type of modal claim being made. Does this calibration respond to van Inwagen's worry, or does it concede his main point?

7. If Fine's essentialist program (from Chapter 3) is correct, modal knowledge is grounded in knowledge of essences — we know why necessary truths hold by grasping the relevant natures. How does this essentialist epistemology compare with possible-worlds approaches to modal knowledge in terms of what it makes tractable and what new problems it introduces? Does grounding modal knowledge in essence merely relocate the epistemological difficulty rather than solving it?

8. Modality appears as a load-bearing concept in ethics (ought implies can; the necessity of moral prohibitions), in the analysis of causation (counterfactual dependence; causal powers), and in the foundations of mathematics (necessity of arithmetic; independence results in set theory). What does this ubiquity suggest about the methodological priority of modal epistemology within philosophy — and does modal skepticism, if vindicated, threaten these dependent domains equally, or does each domain have its own independent resources for grounding the modal claims it requires?
