# Domain Survey: Time and Space

## Overview

Time and space form the scaffold of physical reality. Every event occurs at a time; every object occupies a region of space (or spacetime). Yet the nature of time and space — whether they are real or ideal, absolute or relational, and whether time flows or is merely static — is profoundly contested. The philosophical investigation of time and space must engage both the formal structure of physical theories (Newtonian mechanics, special and general relativity) and the independent metaphysical questions about temporal ontology, the direction of time, and the apparent "flow" of time that seems so central to conscious experience.

The debate about the **ontology of time** asks: do only present things exist (presentism), do past and future things exist alongside present things (eternalism, the block universe), or does reality grow by accretion of new present moments (the growing block view)? This question is not merely verbal — it determines whether the past is genuinely gone, whether our concern for future persons is rationally grounded in their present existence, and whether tense is a fundamental feature of reality or a perspectival distortion. A-theorists (who hold that tense is metaphysically fundamental, that there is an objective distinction between past, present, and future) confront the challenge that modern physics — especially special relativity — seems to undermine the absolute simultaneity required for a unique objective present.

The debate about **spacetime** tracks the development of physics. Newton postulated absolute space and time — containers in which objects exist and events occur, independent of any material content. Leibniz objected that absolute space violates the Principle of Sufficient Reason and the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles: if the entire universe were shifted two miles to the east, nothing would be different, so there could be no reason for its position; but then the universe's position is a fact without a ground, which is unacceptable. Mach proposed that inertia is defined relative to the mass distribution of the universe; absolute space should be replaced by relative configuration space. Einstein's special and general relativity transformed the debate: special relativity unified space and time into spacetime with relativistic simultaneity; general relativity made spacetime geometry dynamic, curved by the distribution of mass-energy.

**The direction of time** presents a deep puzzle: the fundamental laws of physics are (approximately) time-symmetric, yet the world exhibits a pervasive asymmetry between past and future — we remember the past, not the future; entropy increases from past to future; causes precede effects. What grounds this asymmetry? The most influential answer (Boltzmann, Price, Carroll) is statistical: the low entropy of the early universe (the "Past Hypothesis") explains why entropy increases toward the future. But this raises the question of why the universe began in such an extraordinarily low-entropy state.

## Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

### The Metaphysics of Temporal Ontology

**Presentism**: Only the present exists. Past entities no longer exist; future entities do not yet exist. The present moment is ontologically privileged.
- *Advantage*: respects ordinary intuition that the past is gone and the future is not yet real.
- *Problem*: makes cross-temporal relations impossible. If Napoleon no longer exists, how can I now stand in the relation *admiring* to Napoleon? Relations require that both relata exist.
- *Problem with relativity*: Special relativity implies that simultaneity is relative to a reference frame; there is no absolute present. If presentism requires a unique present moment, it is in tension with special relativity.

**Eternalism** (block universe / B-theory): Past, present, and future entities all exist equally. The temporal distinction between past, present, and future is analogous to the spatial distinction between here and there — merely perspectival. The block universe is a four-dimensional manifold in which all events exist timelessly.
- *Advantage*: compatible with special relativity; makes cross-temporal relations straightforward.
- *Problem*: seems to eliminate the "flow" of time — the felt sense that the present is moving forward. On the block universe, nothing changes; the moving present is an illusion.

**Growing block view** (C. D. Broad, Michael Tooley, Peter Forrest): The past and present exist, but the future does not yet. Reality grows as new present moments become past. This preserves the intuition that the future is not yet real while making cross-temporal relations available for the past.
- *Problem*: why is the present *now*? On the growing block view, there is a "edge" of the growing block — but all past times would feel like the present from "inside" the block; there is no principled account of what makes the present moment privileged.

**Moving spotlight theory** (C. D. Broad, J. M. E. McTaggart in a reconstructed version): All times exist (like the block universe), but there is a further fact — the present moves through time, illuminating each moment in turn. This is A-theory combined with four-dimensionalism.
- *Problem*: what does the "moving" of the present consist in? If the spotlight moves through time, it seems to require a hypertime in which the movement occurs — generating an infinite regress.

### The A-Theory / B-Theory Distinction

**A-theory** (McTaggart's A-series): Temporal positions are essentially ordered by the properties of being *past*, *present*, and *future*. These A-properties are intrinsic, non-relational, and constantly change (what was present becomes past). A-theorists hold that tense is metaphysically fundamental and irreducible to tenseless B-relations.

**B-theory** (McTaggart's B-series): Temporal positions are essentially ordered by the relation *earlier than*, which is permanent and unchanging. "The battle of Waterloo is past" means only "The battle of Waterloo occurs earlier than now." All temporal facts are reducible to tenseless B-facts.
- McTaggart's argument: The A-series is essential to time because change requires it (in the B-series, nothing ever changes — events just occupy their positions permanently). But the A-series is self-contradictory: every event is past, present, and future; these are incompatible properties. The response — events have them at different times — reintroduces the A-series. Infinite regress.
- *B-theory response*: The A-series contradiction is avoided by analyzing A-properties as B-relational. "The battle is past" means "The battle is earlier than this utterance." No contradiction.

### Absolute vs. Relational Space

**Absolutism** (Newton): Space (and time) are substances — real containers that exist independently of any material objects. Position, velocity, and acceleration are defined absolutely with respect to this container.
- *Newton's bucket argument*: A bucket of water spins; the concave surface of the water reveals rotation. But rotation relative to what? Not relative to the bucket (the surface is concave even when bucket and water spin together); not relative to other matter alone (the surface is concave even if the only matter is the bucket and water). So rotation must be relative to absolute space.

**Relationalism** (Leibniz, Mach): Space (and time) are not real entities but are constituted by relations among material objects. There is no space "behind" or "beneath" the relations. Position is defined only relative to other things; absolute position is a meaningless notion.
- *Leibniz's objection*: If absolute space is real, God could have placed the universe two miles to the east; but no difference would result; so there is no sufficient reason for the universe's being in one position rather than another — violating PSR.
- *Mach's principle*: Inertia is due to the total mass distribution of the universe; what Newton called "rotation relative to absolute space" is really rotation relative to the average mass distribution of the distant stars.

**Substantivalism about spacetime** (post-Einstein): Modern spacetime physics (Minkowski, Riemann, Einstein) suggests that spacetime is a genuine entity — it curves, waves (gravitational waves), and has a dynamic structure. Substantivalists argue that spacetime points or regions are real entities.
- *Hole argument* (Earman and Norton): In general relativity, diffeomorphism invariance implies that if any model is physically possible, so is any smooth permutation of its content across the spacetime manifold. If spacetime points are real, this seems to generate indeterminism (which point is "really" occupied?). Substantivalists respond with *sophisticated substantivalism* — the view that spacetime points have their identity only through their relational structure (Belot, Earman).

**Relationism about spacetime**: Hartry Field, John Earman, and others have developed relational approaches to spacetime using purely qualitative spacetime relations without commitment to a spacetime substance.

## Key Arguments and Counterarguments

### McTaggart's Argument for the Unreality of Time

**P1.** For time to be real, there must be genuine change.
**P2.** Genuine change requires the A-series (events having the properties of being past, present, future, and these properties changing).
**P3.** The A-series generates a contradiction: every event must have all three properties (past, present, future), but these are mutually inconsistent.
**P4.** The response — events have these properties *at different times* — is circular, requiring a prior A-series to analyze.
**C.** The A-series is contradictory; the B-series alone does not constitute time; therefore time is unreal.

**B-theorist response**: P2 is false. Change consists in the B-relational fact that an event/object has one property at one time and a different property at another time. The A-series is not required. McTaggart's contradiction is dissolved by analyzing A-properties as disguised relational properties.

**A-theorist response**: P3 is not a genuine contradiction because A-properties are had at different times — this is not circular but is simply the form that tensed predication takes. The regress McTaggart cites is harmless.

### The Argument for the Block Universe from Special Relativity

**P1.** Special relativity implies that simultaneity is frame-relative; no frame is objectively privileged.
**P2.** Presentism requires an objectively privileged present moment (absolute simultaneity).
**C.** Presentism is incompatible with special relativity.

**Presentist responses**: (i) *Neo-Lorentzian presentism* (Craig): there is a privileged frame that corresponds to objective simultaneity; Lorentz transformations are empirically equivalent but the Lorentzian interpretation posits an objectively preferred frame. (ii) *Privileged-foliation presentism*: in general relativity, certain globally defined simultaneity foliations (e.g., the cosmic time of the cosmological standard model) may be physically preferred. (iii) *Revisionary presentism*: accept that special relativity shows no absolute present, and revise presentism accordingly — perhaps the present is frame-relative.

### The Puzzle of Temporal Experience

We experience time as "flowing" — the present seems to move forward, the future approaches, the past recedes. But if eternalism is correct, the block universe contains no moving present. So either:
(a) our experience of temporal flow is systematically illusory, or
(b) eternalism is false.

**Eliminativist response** (Price, Callender): The experience of temporal flow is an artifact of the brain's processing — a way of organizing B-theoretic temporal information, not a veridical perception of objective flow. The "moving present" is anthropogenic.

**Passage theory**: Passage (the flow of time) is a genuine objective feature of reality, not reducible to the B-series. The A-theory captures this. (Broad, Zimmerman)

## Key Papers and Books

1. **McTaggart, J. M. E., "The Unreality of Time" (1908)** — The defining argument for both the A/B distinction and the unreality of time; still the reference point for all metaphysics of time.

2. **Lewis, David, "The Paradoxes of Time Travel" (1976)** — Develops a four-dimensionalist treatment of time travel using the personal time / external time distinction; connects temporal metaphysics to identity and possibility.

3. **Price, Huw, *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point* (1996)** — The definitive treatment of the direction of time from an eliminativist perspective; argues for a view from nowhen and explains the asymmetry via the Past Hypothesis.

4. **Sider, Theodore, *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001), chapters 1–3** — Defends perdurantism and the block universe; relates temporal ontology to the debate about persistence.

5. **Skow, Bradford, *Objective Becoming* (2015)** — Defends the moving spotlight view; the most careful recent treatment of how passage can be real.

6. **Earman, John, *World Enough and Space-Time* (1989)** — Standard treatment of the substantivalism/relationism debate in relation to spacetime physics; introduces the hole argument.

7. **Callender, Craig, *What Makes Time Special?* (2017)** — Defends a broadly B-theoretic approach; argues that temporal asymmetry is explained by thermodynamics and cosmological initial conditions rather than fundamental metaphysics.

8. **Craig, William Lane, *The Tensed Theory of Time* (2000)** — Defends A-theory and presentism; argues for a neo-Lorentzian interpretation of special relativity.

## Live Debates and Open Questions

1. **Time in quantum gravity**: Both loop quantum gravity and string theory suggest that the smooth spacetime manifold of general relativity is emergent rather than fundamental. If spacetime is not fundamental, what are the primitive ontological resources, and how do spatial and temporal relations emerge?

2. **Growing block and passage**: The growing block view requires a distinguished present that is not accessible from within the block. Some philosophers argue this is an epistemic rather than an ontological problem; others treat it as fatal.

3. **Directionality without thermodynamics**: Can time's arrow be grounded in something other than the thermodynamic entropy gradient? Proposals include quantum decoherence, causal asymmetry, and cosmological expansion.

4. **Tensed properties and change**: Even if B-theory is correct, there seems to be a phenomenological difference between past and future that needs explaining. Is this difference fully explicable by the cognitive state of the temporal agent, or does it require an objective asymmetry?

5. **Relativistic presentism**: Is there a version of presentism compatible with special relativity that is not merely neo-Lorentzian ad hocery? Philosophers including Lombard, Wüthrich, and Callender continue to explore this.

## Connections to Other Domains

**Time/Space and Identity/Persistence (Domain 03)**: The endurantism/perdurantism debate is directly a debate about temporal ontology — whether objects have temporal parts. The block universe supports perdurantism; presentism fits more naturally with endurantism. The puzzle of temporary intrinsics arises from the B-theory of time combined with endurantism.

**Time/Space and Causation/Laws (Domain 04)**: The direction of time is tightly connected to the direction of causation — causes precede effects. Causal theories of time (Reichenbach, Grünbaum) analyze temporal priority in terms of causal priority; the asymmetry of causation is then explained by the thermodynamic arrow of time.

**Time/Space and Modality (Domain 05)**: Possible worlds are typically conceived as spatiotemporally self-contained. The block universe is a four-dimensional entity existing at all times; possible worlds are other four-dimensional entities. Lewis's realism about worlds and his perdurantism about objects within worlds are deeply connected theses.

**Time/Space and Metaphysics of Science (Domain 10)**: General relativity dynamizes spacetime — it is a field, not a mere background. This forces a revision of the absolute/relational debate. Quantum field theory in curved spacetime raises further questions about the ontology of spacetime at the Planck scale.

**Time/Space and Free Will (Domain 08)**: Causal determinism is formulated as a thesis about what the laws and prior states determine; the temporal ordering of determination requires a prior metaphysics of time. On the block universe, all events are equally real, which raises the question of whether deliberation and choice are anything more than later stages determined by earlier stages.
