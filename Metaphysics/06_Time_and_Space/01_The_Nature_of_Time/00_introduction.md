# The Nature of Time

John McTaggart Ellis McTaggart published his argument for the unreality of time in 1908, and its influence has not diminished. McTaggart distinguished two ways of ordering events in time. The A-series orders events as past, present, and future — properties that change: what is now future becomes present and then past. The B-series orders events as earlier than, simultaneous with, and later than — relations that are permanent: if the Battle of Waterloo is earlier than the Moon landing, this is always true, in every time and from every perspective. McTaggart argued that the A-series is essential to the passage of time, that the A-series is internally contradictory, and therefore that time itself is unreal.

The argument can be resisted at every step, but its lasting contribution is the A-series/B-series distinction, which has organized the philosophy of time ever since. A-theorists hold that tense is real and that the passage of time — the fact that things genuinely change from future to present to past — is an objective feature of the world. B-theorists hold that tense is perspectival and that all events exist equally in a static four-dimensional block; "now" picks out the time of the utterance, not an objectively privileged moment.

This debate is not merely semantic. The A-theory aligns with our ordinary experience of temporal flow: time really does pass; the future is genuinely open; the present moment has a special ontological status. The B-theory aligns with the picture of time suggested by special relativity: simultaneity is frame-relative, so there is no frame-independent "now"; the four-dimensional manifold just is, without passage. Presentism — the A-theoretic view that only the present moment is real — struggles with the relativity of simultaneity; eternalism — the B-theoretic view that past, present, and future are all equally real — struggles to explain why we experience time as flowing.

This chapter develops the McTaggart argument, the A-theory and B-theory responses to it, and the debate between presentism and eternalism. The reader who understands these debates will understand why time is genuinely philosophically puzzling rather than merely complicated.
