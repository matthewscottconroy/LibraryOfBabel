# McTaggart and the Unreality of Time

J.M.E. McTaggart's 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time" is one of the most widely discussed and most influential papers in the history of metaphysics. McTaggart did not merely offer a theory of time; he argued that time does not exist at all — that what we experience as temporal passage is appearance rather than reality. The argument is intricate, but its central structure has proved enormously fertile, giving rise to the distinction between the A-series and the B-series that has organized the metaphysics of time ever since.

McTaggart distinguished two ways of ordering events in time. The B-series orders events by the permanent relations of "earlier than" and "later than": the Battle of Hastings is permanently earlier than the Battle of Waterloo, regardless of when the ordering is made. The A-series orders events by the properties of being past, present, and future — properties that genuinely change: what is now present was future and will be past. McTaggart argued that the A-series is more fundamental than the B-series, because only A-series properties capture the genuine passage of time.

He then argued that the A-series is incoherent: every event has all three A-series properties (past, present, future), which are mutually incompatible. The obvious response — that an event has these properties at different times — generates a regress: we need a second-order temporal series to explain how events can have different A-properties at different times, and this second-order series either requires a third-order series (generating an infinite regress) or must itself be understood in B-series terms (betraying the premise that the A-series is fundamental).

Whether McTaggart's argument succeeds is contested. But its legacy is unquestionable: the A-series/B-series distinction remains the organizing framework for contemporary debates between presentists (who accept the ontological primacy of the present), B-theorists (who deny that there is any ontologically privileged present), and growing block theorists.

This section examines McTaggart's argument carefully, assessing its premises and its consequences for the metaphysics of time.
