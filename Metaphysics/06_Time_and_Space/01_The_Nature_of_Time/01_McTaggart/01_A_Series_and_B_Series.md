# The A-Series and B-Series of Time

Two clocks sit on a shelf. The one on the left is an ordinary clock: it has a face with hands that move, and at any given moment the hands are at a particular position. But this clock also shows something else: a continuously updated inscription that reads "now." The hands show a time; the inscription shows the present. According to J.M.E. McTaggart, these two clocks represent two fundamentally different ways of ordering events in time — and they are not reducible to each other.

The B-series is the ordering of events by the relations "earlier than," "simultaneous with," and "later than." The signing of the Magna Carta is earlier than the First World War. This relation is permanent and unchanging — it held before either event occurred (so to speak), it held during them, and it holds now. B-series relations are eternal: no event changes its B-series position. The B-series is like the left-hand clock — it tells you where events stand relative to one another.

The A-series is the ordering of events by the properties "past," "present," and "future." But unlike B-series relations, A-series properties change: what is future becomes present and then recedes into the past. The present moment is not a fixed position but a moving one. The A-series is like the "now" inscription on the right-hand clock — it always says the same thing, but it always refers to something different.

McTaggart's argument in "The Unreality of Time" (1908) is that the A-series is essential to genuine time (change requires that events have genuinely different A-series properties), yet the A-series is contradictory (every event has all three A-series properties, since every event was future, is or will be present, and was or is or will be past). The attempted resolution by saying events have these properties "at different times" reintroduces the A-series, generating a vicious regress. Time is therefore unreal.

The conclusion has not been widely accepted. But the distinction itself has been extraordinarily productive. It grounds the debate between A-theorists, who hold that the A-series is an irreducible and fundamental feature of temporal reality, and B-theorists, who hold that the A-series is reducible to or derivative from the B-series — that "past," "present," and "future" are not metaphysically primitive properties but perspectival descriptions of B-series positions from a temporally located viewpoint. This debate, its formal development through Prior's tense logic, and its implications for the nature of temporal passage are the subject of the files in this section.

McTaggart also introduced a third notion — the C-series, a permanent ordering of events that lacks temporal direction — which anticipates later discussions of the topology of time and the question of whether the past-future asymmetry is fundamental or derived from the thermodynamic structure of the world. The C-series provides the "skeleton" of temporal order; the A-series, on McTaggart's view, provides the direction. Whether that direction is real and fundamental, or emergent and perspectival, is the central question that divides the A-theory from the B-theory.
