# McTaggart's Distinction

Think for a moment about the difference between two ways of describing the same event. We can say that the Battle of Hastings is earlier than the French Revolution — and this will be true tomorrow, was true yesterday, and will remain true in a thousand years. Or we can say that the Battle of Hastings is past — and while this is true now, it was once false, and it was once not past but present, and before that, future. Both descriptions locate the same battle in time. Yet they seem to be doing something quite different.

This is the observation J.M.E. McTaggart pressed in his 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time." He called the first kind of ordering the B-series and the second the A-series, and the distinction he drew has structured the philosophy of time ever since.

The B-series orders events by the permanent, relational properties Earlier(E₁, E₂), Simult(E₁, E₂), and Later(E₁, E₂). These relations are dyadic and unchanging: the Caesar event is earlier than the Waterloo event is simply and timelessly true; no further indexing is needed. The A-series orders events by the monadic properties Past(E), Present(E), and Future(E). These change their truth values: the Waterloo event was once future, was present on June 18, 1815, and is now past. A-series predicates are irreducibly tensed in a way B-series relations are not.

So far, this looks like a pair of useful descriptive tools. But McTaggart goes further: he claims the A-series is more fundamental than the B-series — that the B-series, without the A-series, does not constitute genuine time at all. A series ordered by "earlier than" is a static ordering, no different in principle from a spatial ordering. Books on a shelf have a left-to-right ordering, but no one thinks the books are flowing anywhere. If time is to involve genuine flow — genuine change, genuine passage — something more than the B-series is needed. What makes one event earlier than another, McTaggart argues, is that the earlier event is further in the past relative to any present moment; the very direction of the B-series is parasitic on the A-series.

This priority claim is contested. B-theorists following Bertrand Russell hold that "earlier than" is a primitive relation, and A-series talk is reducible to B-series relations plus temporal indexicals. But we should not move too quickly: McTaggart's argument that time requires genuine passage, and that passage requires an A-series, motivates the entire debate that follows. We return to it throughout this module.

## The Formal Structure

Let us sharpen the distinction. In the B-series, the fundamental temporal facts are dyadic relations among events, and they are permanent: it was always true and will always be true that one event stands in these relations to another. In the A-series, the fundamental temporal facts are monadic — a property of single events — and they change. What was future becomes present, then past.

McTaggart then argues that both series are necessary for genuine time, and then argues that the A-series is contradictory. Every event has all three A-properties: the Waterloo event was future (before 1815), present (on June 18, 1815), and is now past. But Past, Present, and Future are mutually exclusive — nothing can be both past and present. The natural reply is that the event is past-at-one-time, present-at-another, future-at-a-third. But this introduces a second A-series for the reference times: each reference time is itself past, present, or future relative to further times. The regress has no termination, and the original contradiction is never resolved.

Most philosophers have rejected the conclusion that time is unreal. But the structure of McTaggart's problem — the tension between the static relational facts of the B-series and the dynamic, perspectival facts of the A-series — is the organizing question for the philosophy of time. His paper launched a debate that occupies us still.

## Connections Across the Module

The A/B distinction is not merely terminological housekeeping. It connects to questions across metaphysics with surprising directness. If the B-series is fundamental and the A-series merely indexical, then persisting objects most naturally exist by having temporal parts — the "worm" view developed by Lewis and Sider. The object-at-time-t is a temporal stage, and what we call an "object" is a temporally extended mereological sum of such stages. The object does not "move through" time; it simply has different temporal parts at different locations.

If the A-series is fundamental, a quite different picture beckons. Objects persist through time by being wholly present at each time at which they exist — the endurantist picture — genuinely gaining and losing properties as they move from future to present to past. A leaf does not merely have a green-stage and a red-stage spread across a four-dimensional block; it is fully the same leaf, and it genuinely changes color.

The A/B distinction thus reaches into the metaphysics of material objects, and far beyond the philosophy of time as a narrow specialty. McTaggart's 1908 paper — written in the service of absolute idealism, with the conclusion that time is unreal — has outlasted the idealist framework that produced it and become the unavoidable starting point for any serious work in this area.
