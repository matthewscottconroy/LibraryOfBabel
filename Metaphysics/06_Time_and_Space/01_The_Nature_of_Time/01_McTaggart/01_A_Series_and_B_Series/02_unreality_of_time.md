# McTaggart's Argument for the Unreality of Time

Caesar's death was once future. It was then present. It is now past. This much seems uncontroversial. But notice: being future, being present, and being past are mutually exclusive — nothing can be all three at once. And yet, by McTaggart's lights, every event must be all three. The Battle of Hastings was future before 1066, present in 1066, and has been past ever since. So it has all three A-properties. And this, McTaggart claims, is a contradiction.

The natural response is immediate: the event is not all three at once — it is future at earlier times, present at its own time, and past at later times. We relativize the properties to times, and the contradiction dissolves. But McTaggart presses: this relativization reintroduces the A-series for the reference times themselves. Each reference time is future, then present, then past — and we need a second-level A-series to explain this. Then a third level for those times, and so on without termination. We never reach a point at which the A-properties are explained without reintroducing them. The regress is, McTaggart insists, vicious.

His conclusion: the A-series is internally contradictory and cannot be consistently maintained. Since genuine time — time as more than a static B-series ordering — requires the A-series, time itself is unreal.

The argument formalized:

- P1. Genuine time requires both a B-series (ordering by earlier/later) and an A-series (ordering by past/present/future).
- P2. The A-series is essential because time involves genuine change and passage; a mere B-series ordering is static and does not constitute time.
- P3. Every event possesses all three A-series properties: any event that is now past was once present and before that future.
- P4. The properties Past, Present, and Future are mutually exclusive: nothing can be both past and present, both present and future, or both past and future, at once.
- P5. Therefore there is an apparent contradiction: every event has all three properties, but no event can have all three at once.
- P6. The natural resolution is to relativize: E is past-at-t₁, present-at-t₂, future-at-t₃ (where t₁ > t₂ > t₃ in the B-series).
- P7. But "past-at-t₁" presupposes that t₁ is a time with A-series properties: t₁ is past, present, or future relative to other times.
- P8. Resolving that presupposition requires a further relativization, which in turn raises the same presupposition.
- P9. This regress is vicious: we never reach a level where the A-series properties are explained without reintroducing them.

Conclusion: The A-series cannot be consistently maintained. Since genuine time requires the A-series, time is unreal.

## Is the Regress Vicious?

The key philosophical question is whether the regress at P9 is genuinely vicious. A regress is vicious when each step fails to accomplish what it was introduced to accomplish, so that the original problem is never actually solved. McTaggart maintains that the regress is vicious because at each level we must attribute A-series properties to times, and this attribution is exactly what needed explaining without circularity.

Two important challenges have been pressed. The B-theorist's response — the most influential — holds that the regress never arises because A-properties are not genuine properties at all. "Caesar's death is past" does not attribute an intrinsic property to the event; it expresses a relational fact between the event and the speaker's temporal location. On this view, P2 is false: a pure B-series suffices for genuine time, and the A-series is a feature of our temporal language and cognition, not of temporal reality. The regress is dissolved rather than answered.

A.N. Prior responded that this move begs the question. It assumes the conclusion — that time is fundamentally B-series — and uses it to dissolve the problem. Prior argued that tensed expressions like "it is now the case that P" are irreducibly first-personal and cannot be captured by any B-series paraphrase. Consider: when I am relieved that a painful experience is over, what am I relieved about? Not the B-series fact that the experience is earlier than my relief — I knew that fact throughout the experience without being relieved. The relief is precisely that it is now past. This "now" is not reducible to any temporal indexical in disguise; it points to something the B-theory cannot capture.

## Dummett's Defense and Prior's Formal Response

Michael Dummett took McTaggart's side in a celebrated 1960 paper ("A Defense of McTaggart's Proof of the Unreality of Time"). Dummett argued that the regress is vicious because the task at each level — explaining how a time can have conflicting A-properties — is not accomplished; it is merely deferred. The only consistent position, he concluded, is to deny that there are A-properties at all and to accept a pure B-series conception of time.

Prior's most significant contribution was constructive rather than critical: rather than arguing that the regress is harmless, he developed tense logic as a formal system for A-theoretic claims. His system introduces operators:

- **P**: "It was the case that..."
- **F**: "It will be the case that..."
- **H**: "It has always been the case that..."
- **G**: "It will always be the case that..."

These operators are treated as primitive logical constants, not abbreviations for "at some earlier time" or "at some later time." The resulting system is a modal logic of time. For Prior, the regress is blocked because the operators P and F are defined with respect to the present moment — the moment of evaluation — and "the present" is not a further A-property requiring analysis. The hierarchy of A-properties collapses into a series of operator applications, each of which is perfectly intelligible. This tense-logical framework has since become a standard tool in formal logic, linguistics, and computer science.

## The Broader Stakes

McTaggart's argument was written in the context of British absolute idealism — he aimed to show that time is unreal, a form of misrepresentation of an atemporal spiritual reality. Subsequent analytic philosophy set aside the idealist context and extracted the logical core: a puzzle about the analysis of temporal language and ontology. The A/B debate inaugurated by his paper is now one of the most active in analytic metaphysics, far outlasting the framework that produced it. And whether his regress is vicious or not is a question that still divides philosophers, because it connects directly to whether temporal passage is a feature of reality or a feature of how we think about it.
