# A-Theory Versus B-Theory of Time

The debate between A-theory and B-theory of time is, at its core, a debate about what kind of thing the present is. For the B-theorist, "now" is an indexical like "here": it picks out the time at which it is used, but there is nothing metaphysically special about that time. The time at which you are reading this sentence is no more real, no more fundamental, and no more privileged than any other time in the four-dimensional block of events. For the A-theorist, this analysis leaves something out — something that cannot be captured by any amount of B-series information, however complete. The present is not merely "the time the observer happens to be at"; it is the time at which things are really happening, the moment of genuine becoming, the locus of existence itself.

This disagreement has several dimensions that are worth keeping separate. The ontological question is about which times exist: only the present (presentism), the past and present (growing block), or all times equally (eternalism)? The semantic question is about whether tensed sentences — sentences that say something is happening now, or was the case, or will be — can be analyzed in B-series terms or whether tense is semantically primitive. The physical question is whether modern physics, especially special relativity with its relativity of simultaneity, decisively favors the B-theory.

A-theorists disagree among themselves about the nature of temporal "flow." Some identify it with the constant becoming of new events, holding that only present events exist at all. Others hold that both past and future events exist but the present is additionally marked — the moving spotlight view. Some hold that the structure of reality is genuinely fragmented across times, with different times containing genuinely incompatible facts. What unites them is the conviction that the B-series description, however accurate, leaves out what is most important about time: the fact that things are genuinely happening, that the present is not just a structural position but the point of real existence.

The B-theory responds that what A-theorists feel is missing is explained, not by adding primitive A-series properties to the world, but by attending to the temporal situatedness of temporal thinkers. We are beings embedded in the B-series: we access events in temporal order, form memories of earlier events, make plans for later ones. The "specialness" of the present is a feature of our perspective — of what it is like to be a temporally located agent — not a feature of the block universe. D.H. Mellor's systematic B-theoretic treatment (*Real Time*, 1981, *Real Time II*, 1998) and Ted Sider's four-dimensionalism (*Four-Dimensionalism*, 2001) represent the most thorough recent defenses of this view. Bradford Skow's *Objective Becoming* (2015) offers the most thorough recent defense of the moving spotlight A-theory.

The files in this section examine A-theory and B-theory in detail, explore the main arguments on each side, and attend to the connections with special relativity and with the semantics of tense.
