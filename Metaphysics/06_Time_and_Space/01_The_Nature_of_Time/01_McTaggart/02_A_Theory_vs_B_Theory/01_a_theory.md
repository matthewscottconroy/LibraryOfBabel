# The A-Theory of Time

Here is the thing we are trying to explain. The present moment feels different from past and future moments. Not just different as the spot I am standing on feels different from spots I am not — a merely indexical difference — but different in a way that seems to reflect something real about the structure of time itself. When a painful experience ends, I am relieved that it is now past. When I anticipate a future pleasure, my anticipation is directed at something not yet real. This asymmetry between past, present, and future is not merely how time seems; the A-theorist insists it is how time is.

The A-theory holds that the distinction between past, present, and future is an objective, fundamental feature of reality. There is a privileged present moment — the now — that is not merely the time at which one happens to be located but a genuine metaphysical fact about the world. Time "flows" because the present moves through the temporal series, converting future events into present ones and then relegating them to the past.

A.N. Prior was the philosophical champion of this view in the modern period. He developed tense logic as a formal system in which "It is now the case that P" is a primitive operator that cannot be analyzed in B-series terms. His defense of the irreducibility of tense was grounded in both formal logic and metaphysics, and it set the terms for much of the subsequent debate.

## The Argument from Experience

Prior's "Thank Goodness That's Over" (1959) gives the A-theory's most direct philosophical motivation. The argument has the structure of a phenomenological datum that the B-theory cannot explain. When I am relieved that a painful dental procedure is over, the object of my relief is not the B-series fact that the procedure is earlier than my utterance — I knew that fact throughout the procedure without being relieved. What I am relieved about is that the procedure is *now* past — that the present moment is on the far side of the experience. No sentence of the form "event E₁ is earlier than event E₂" captures this. The A-theory is motivated by the irreducible indexicality of the present: not as a perspective-relative indexical like "here," but as a marker of a genuine metaphysical asymmetry.

The B-theorist replies that the phenomenology can be explained by our cognitive architecture. We have different access to past and future — we remember the past and can act on the future, but not the reverse — and these functional asymmetries explain why the present feels special without requiring an objective metaphysical privilege of the present. The A-theorist finds this explanation circular: it assumes the B-theory's conclusion (there is no objective present) and uses it to explain away the evidence that there is one.

## The Argument from Genuine Change

A second major argument concerns intrinsic change. On the B-theory, an object that changes properties — a leaf that turns from green to red — simply has "green at t₁" and "red at t₂" as permanent features of a four-dimensional block. David Lewis called these "temporary intrinsic properties," and he recognized that accommodating them without the A-series is a genuine difficulty. The B-theorist has two options: treat the properties as relations to times ("the leaf is green-at-t₁"), which makes them relational rather than intrinsic; or adopt temporal parts, in which the leaf-at-t₁ is green intrinsically and the leaf-at-t₂ is red intrinsically, but they are numerically distinct entities.

The A-theorist insists that neither option captures genuine intrinsic change. If the leaf is green-at-t₁ and red-at-t₂ permanently and from eternity, the leaf does not genuinely change; it merely has different temporal profiles. For genuine change, there must be a single object — wholly present — that actually loses the property green and gains red. This happening-in-the-present, this real loss and gain, is what makes change real rather than merely a distribution of properties across a four-dimensional block.

The B-theorist's counter: the demand for genuine change of a persisting substance is a conceptual holdover that the temporal-parts ontology makes unnecessary. Change, properly understood, is variation in properties across time. The four-dimensionalist captures this perfectly. But the A-theorist will insist that this reply changes the subject.

## A-Theory Variants

A-theories differ primarily on what exists. Presentism holds that only present entities exist — the past is literally nothing, the future is literally nothing. This is the most radical form and faces hardest questions about truthmakers for past-tense statements. The Growing Block, defended by C.D. Broad in *Scientific Thought* (1923) and developed by Michael Tooley and Trenton Merricks, holds that the past and present exist but the future does not; reality grows as new events are added at the leading edge of the present. The Moving Spotlight, considered by Broad and recently defended by Bradford Skow in *Objective Becoming* (2015), holds that all times exist as in the B-theory's block universe, but one time is additionally illuminated by the property of "nowness" that moves through the series. Fragmentalism, Kit Fine's proposal, holds that the world is not a single consistent whole but a collection of fragments each centered on a different time; temporal becoming is the process by which new facts come to obtain absolutely.

What all these views share is the rejection of the B-theory claim that "past," "present," and "future" can be analyzed in purely B-theoretic terms. For the A-theorist, tense is irreducible: to say something "is happening now" makes a claim that cannot be captured by any eternalist, non-tensed sentence.

## A-Theory and Physics

The A-theory's central challenge comes from special relativity. Special relativity denies that there is a frame-independent notion of simultaneity, and without an objective simultaneity relation, there is no frame-independent "present." The A-theory requires such a present. Different inertial observers "slice" Minkowski spacetime into different simultaneity surfaces; there is no privileged foliation that the A-theorist can identify with the objective now.

A-theorists have developed several strategies. Presentists like Quentin Smith and Thomas Crisp have argued that the relevant notion of simultaneity for metaphysical purposes is not the relativistic operational notion but an absolute metaphysical simultaneity that coincides with some privileged foliation of spacetime. Dean Zimmerman has proposed that quantum mechanics may restore an absolute foliation not recognized by classical special relativity. Others argue that special relativity is a theory about the behavior of rods and clocks in idealized conditions, and that the metaphysical present is a real feature of the world even if our physical theories do not encode it explicitly.

None of these responses has been universally accepted. The tension between the A-theory and special relativity remains one of the central unsolved problems in the philosophy of time, and any A-theorist who takes physics seriously must have an account of it.
