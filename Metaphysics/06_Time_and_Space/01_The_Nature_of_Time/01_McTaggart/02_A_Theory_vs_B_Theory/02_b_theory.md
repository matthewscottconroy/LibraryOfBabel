# The B-Theory of Time

Consider the word "here." It picks out the location of the speaker; any location can be "here" from the perspective of someone standing there. We do not think "here" names an intrinsically special place in space — a place that is special regardless of who stands at it. "Here" is indexical, context-dependent, perspectival. The B-theory holds that "now" is analogous. It picks out the time of the speaker's utterance or experience. Any time can be "now" from the perspective of someone located there. The appearance of a metaphysically privileged present is an artifact of the indexicality of "now," not a feature of the world.

This is the B-theory's central philosophical move. The only fundamental temporal relations are B-series relations: earlier than, simultaneous with, and later than. The A-series properties — past, present, future — are not additional, objective features of events but are indexical descriptions that reflect the temporal perspective of the speaker or agent. All temporal facts are B-series facts. Time is a dimension analogous to space, and the block universe is the appropriate picture: all events exist at their spacetime locations, equally real, and the present is simply the time at which one happens to be located.

The view was championed in the twentieth century by Bertrand Russell, D.C. Williams ("The Myth of Passage," 1951), and J.J.C. Smart. Its more recent defenders include D.H. Mellor (*Real Time*, 1981; *Real Time II*, 1998) and L. Nathan Oaklander. Its main advantages are well known: it is consistent with the block universe picture supported by special relativity, it avoids McTaggart's regress, and it provides a parsimonious ontology — no special "nowness" needs to be explained or analyzed.

## The Token-Reflexive Analysis

The most technically developed B-theoretic semantics is the token-reflexive account, worked out by Reichenbach and systematized by Mellor. The key distinction: tensed sentence-types cannot be translated into non-tensed sentence-types (since "now" shifts its reference across tokens), but the truth condition of any particular token can be stated in B-series terms plus the identity of that token. A token utterance of "It is raining now" is true iff it is raining at the time of that utterance. A token utterance of "It was raining yesterday" is true iff it was raining approximately twenty-four hours before the utterance.

This is not the naïve B-theory of the early twentieth century, which tried to translate tensed sentence-types into non-tensed ones and ran into obvious problems (what is the fixed time t₀ that "It is raining" translates to, and why does the translation vary?). The New B-Theory, developed by Mellor, Horwich, and others in the 1980s and 1990s, avoids this by insisting that truth is fundamentally a property of tokens, not types, and that no type-level translation is required.

Mellor further argues that his analysis captures the full cognitive significance of tensed beliefs. The special urgency that "it is happening now" has for action — the way present-tense beliefs motivate immediate response — is explained by the token-reflexive account: such beliefs refer to the time at which they are formed, and that is the time at which action is possible. There is no irreducible "nowness" needed.

## Williams on the Myth of Passage

D.C. Williams's "The Myth of Passage" (1951) is the locus classicus of B-theoretic criticism of the A-theory. Williams argues that the concept of temporal passage — the "moving now" — is not a genuine feature of the world but a philosophical confusion.

The block universe contains all temporal events at their spacetime positions. There is no additional fact about these events "flowing" or "moving" through time. Motion requires that a thing be at different places at different times. If time "moves," it must be at different "times" at different "times" — which requires a second temporal dimension. But if temporal passage requires a meta-time, we are not explaining time but positing a second temporal dimension that raises the same questions.

The experience of temporal passage is real, Williams acknowledges. But it arises because we are temporally extended beings who experience our cognitive states in temporal sequence. Our memories and anticipations create a sense of "movement" that is really just the experience of being embedded in a four-dimensional structure. We do not think that space "flows" from left to right, even though we experience spatial sequences as we move through them. The experience of temporal passage is similarly an artifact of our embedded perspective, not a feature of the block universe itself.

## Challenges for the B-Theory

The most persistent challenge is the epistemic argument. Suppose I know all the B-series facts about the world: I know that it is 3:00 PM on a particular day, and I know my position in the B-series. Do I thereby know what time it is *now*? The B-theorist says yes — knowing my temporal location in the B-series is all that "knowing the present time" amounts to. But there seems to be something distinctive about knowing that it is *now* 3:00 PM, as opposed to knowing timelessly that 3:00 PM has a particular position in the B-series. This "essential indexical" character of temporal self-location seems to be something the B-theory cannot accommodate without appealing to a notion of "nowness" that goes beyond the B-series.

This argument, pressed by Casati and Torrengo among others and connected to Kaplan's work on indexicals and Perry's work on essential indexicals, remains the most philosophically interesting challenge to the B-theory's claim to have fully reduced A-series facts to B-series facts plus indexicals.

A second challenge concerns causal asymmetry. The B-series ordering is symmetric: "earlier than" is just the converse of "later than," and neither has any intrinsic direction. But causation is asymmetric: causes precede effects. The B-theory must explain this asymmetry without appeal to the A-series direction from past to future. Lewis's answer appeals to the thermodynamic arrow; critics argue that this makes temporal asymmetry contingent in ways that seem wrong — that time would flow in this direction even if the initial conditions of the universe had been otherwise, whereas the Lewis account implies the direction would reverse.

These challenges do not refute the B-theory, but they show that the view is not as cost-free as its parsimony might suggest.
