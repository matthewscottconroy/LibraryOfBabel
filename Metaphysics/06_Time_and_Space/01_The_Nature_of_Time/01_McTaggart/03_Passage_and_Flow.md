# Temporal Passage and the Flow of Time

Ask someone what is most distinctive about time compared to space, and the most common answer will be some version of this: time flows. The present moment is not static; it moves. Events approach from the future, arrive in the present, and recede into the past. This movement is not merely apparent — it is among the most vivid features of experience. We feel time passing. We wait, we anticipate, we remember. The present is not just where we happen to be; it is where things are happening.

The philosophical difficulty is that this seemingly obvious observation resists coherent analysis. If time flows, it flows at some rate — and the obvious candidate, "one second per second," is trivially true and gives no information. If we try to measure the rate by reference to some other temporal scale, we need that other scale to have its own flow, and a regress threatens. D.C. Williams's "The Myth of Passage" (1951) pressed this objection: the concept of temporal passage, taken literally as involving motion of the present through time, is either unintelligible or requires an infinite regress of meta-times. The apparent vividness of the passage experience is real but misleading — it reflects the asymmetric temporal structure of our cognitive architecture, not any objective movement in reality.

The moving spotlight view attempts to preserve the reality of passage while avoiding the regress: all times exist in the block, but there is an objectively present time — the spotlight — that is not merely identified by its structural position but by a primitive nowness property. The challenge is to characterize this moving spotlight in a way that does not either collapse into B-theory (by making nowness just a structural position) or regress into meta-time (by treating the spotlight's movement as a process occurring in a further temporal dimension).

The files in this section examine the moving spotlight view in detail, the problems for temporal passage that Williams and others have identified, and the relationship between passage and physics — in particular, how the thermodynamic and causal arrows of time might account for the features of temporal experience that A-theorists attribute to genuine passage. The central question running through this section is whether the felt flow of time tracks something real in the metaphysical structure of time or whether it is, as the B-theorist maintains, an artifact of what it is like to be a temporally embedded being in a universe with a specific thermodynamic history.
