# Problems with Temporal Passage

We feel time passing. Events arrive, happen, and recede. The present moves. This experience is so fundamental to our sense of what time is that it can seem nearly inconceivable that it might be a systematic illusion. And yet, when philosophers try to say what temporal passage actually is — what it would mean for time to literally flow — they run into a cluster of serious problems. These problems are among the most important arguments for the static, B-theoretic picture of the block universe.

## The Rate Problem

If time flows, at what rate does it flow? Velocity is displacement per unit time: a car travels at 60 miles per hour. By analogy, temporal passage would be displacement in time per unit time — but displacement in time per unit time is simply one second per second, which gives no information about rate. The answer "time flows at one second per second" is trivially true and tells us nothing.

Could there be a more informative rate? Only if there were an independent measure of time against which to evaluate the passage of the object-time. This would require a second time dimension — a meta-time — and temporal passage at the meta-level would again face the same rate question. Huw Price, in *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point* (1996), formulated this point with particular clarity. The regress is structurally the same as McTaggart's.

One might respond that the rate question is a pseudo-question: asking how fast time flows is like asking where space is located. Spatial position is always position relative to some spatial framework; asking where space itself is located is a category mistake. Similarly, temporal rate is always rate relative to some temporal framework; asking how fast time flows is a category mistake when applied to time itself. Defenders of passage accept this response and insist that passage is a sui generis feature of time — not reducible to ordinary motion, not subject to rate questions. But critics find this concession significant: if passage cannot be understood on the model of ordinary motion, the "flow" metaphor may be doing no real explanatory work.

## The Direction Problem

The fundamental laws of physics are almost completely time-symmetric: if a process is allowed by the laws, so is its temporal reverse. The exception is the weak nuclear force, which violates time-reversal symmetry in certain meson decays, but this is believed to have no macroscopic significance. At the level of classical mechanics, electromagnetism, and quantum mechanics (apart from the weak force), there is no physically privileged direction of time.

Yet we observe a strong asymmetry: eggs break but do not spontaneously unbreak; heat flows from hot to cold but not from cold to hot; the universe evolved from a low-entropy initial state toward higher entropy. These asymmetries are thermodynamic and cosmological, not fundamental at the microscopic level.

If the fundamental laws do not prefer one temporal direction, and if temporal passage is a fundamental feature of reality, then the direction of passage cannot be derived from the laws. The most common suggestion is that direction is determined by the thermodynamic arrow — but this makes the direction of temporal passage contingent on the initial state of the universe. We feel as though time would flow in this direction even if the initial conditions had been otherwise. A-theorists respond that the direction of temporal passage is primitive and not reducible to thermodynamics: the A-series has a built-in direction — events become present and then past, not the reverse — and this direction is not derived from the laws but is a fundamental feature of temporal becoming.

## Williams and the Myth of Passage

D.C. Williams's "The Myth of Passage" (1951) is the classic B-theoretic critique. Williams argues that the "picture" of time flowing or moving is not coherent when taken literally. His key argument: motion requires that a thing be at different places at different times. If time "moves," it must be at different "times" at different "times" — which requires a second temporal dimension. Since no such dimension is on offer, there is no literal motion, and the "myth" is a poetic or emotional response to the fact that we are temporally extended and sequentially experience events.

We "feel" time passing because our experiences occur in temporal sequence, and our perspective changes as different events become present to us. But this trivial "travel" is just the fact that we are temporally extended beings, not an indication of any additional motion of time itself. The myth is seductive because we are, in a trivial sense, "traveling through time" — we have experiences at different times — but this does not indicate anything beyond the structure of the block universe.

## A Defense of the Phenomenology

Defenders of the reality of temporal passage have challenged Williams's dismissal. Craig Callender ("Shedding Light on Time," 2000) argues that even if temporal passage is not a fundamental feature of physics, it might still be a significant emergent phenomenon — like temperature, which is not fundamental at the level of particle physics but is real at the macroscopic level. The experience of temporal passage might track a genuine, though non-fundamental, feature of reality: the thermodynamic asymmetry that makes the past and future cognitively asymmetric for beings like us.

Jenann Ismael (*How Physics Makes Us Free*, 2016) argues that temporal passage is "thick" — it involves the full texture of temporal experience, including memory, anticipation, and the sense of being embedded in an ongoing causal process. This experience cannot be dismissed as mere illusion; it is the form in which we engage with the temporal world. Understanding how physics generates this experience is an important scientific and philosophical project. Whether physics "generates" the experience or merely correlates with it is, of course, precisely the question at issue.
