# Temporal Passage and Physics

One of the most powerful arguments for the B-theory is the apparent tension between the A-theory's "flowing present" and the physics of special relativity. If the present is the set of all events simultaneous with a given moment, and if simultaneity is frame-relative, then the present is also frame-relative. What counts as "now" depends on the observer's state of motion. This makes it very difficult to identify the present with an objective, universal, metaphysically privileged class of events — which is what the A-theory requires.

A-theorists have responded in several ways. Some accept that special relativity is incompatible with presentism but argue that there is a privileged reference frame — perhaps determined by the cosmic background radiation or by quantum non-locality — relative to which true simultaneity can be defined. This is neo-Newtonian presentism: it revives an absolute frame for temporal purposes while accepting special relativity's local structure. Others, like Dean Zimmerman, argue that special relativity is a local theory about the structure of spacetime in the absence of quantum gravity; quantum gravity may restore a preferred foliation that special relativity does not acknowledge. Huw Price and Craig Callender take the more deflationary line: the conflict between the A-theory and special relativity is not merely technical but reflects a deeper incompatibility, and the felt passage of time is a psychological phenomenon, not a physical one.

## The Challenge from Minkowski Spacetime

Minkowski's 1908 reformulation of special relativity as a four-dimensional geometry makes the challenge to temporal passage precise. In Minkowski spacetime, the structure of the world is captured by the spacetime interval between events:

s² = c²(Δt)² - (Δx)² - (Δy)² - (Δz)²

This interval is invariant — it has the same value in every inertial frame. The decomposition of the interval into temporal and spatial parts is not invariant: two observers moving relative to each other will disagree about the spatial distance and the temporal separation between a given pair of events, though they agree on the spacetime interval. This means there is no frame-independent notion of "which events are simultaneous with event E" — different observers partition spacetime into different simultaneity surfaces. The A-theory requires a unique, objective simultaneity surface — the "plane of the present" — but Minkowski spacetime contains none.

## The Rietdijk-Putnam Argument

The argument from special relativity against presentism was formulated most sharply by Rietdijk (1966) and Putnam (1967):

- P1. In special relativity, there is no absolute simultaneity: whether two events are simultaneous depends on the reference frame.
- P2. If presentism is true, only present events are real.
- P3. Therefore, on presentism, which events are real depends on the reference frame — existence is frame-relative.
- P4. Existence cannot coherently be frame-relative.
- Conclusion: Presentism is inconsistent with special relativity.

The argument is powerful. Several presentists deny P4, holding that frame-relative existence is unfamiliar but not incoherent. Others deny that P1 applies to the metaphysically relevant notion of simultaneity — distinguishing operational simultaneity (Einstein's light-signal convention) from metaphysical or absolute simultaneity.

Neo-Newtonian presentism, advocated in various forms by Thomas Crisp, Michael Rea, and Ned Markosian, accepts that metaphysical presentism requires an absolute present and defends this by positing a privileged reference frame not recognized in special relativity. This might be identified with the rest frame of the cosmic microwave background radiation (CMB) — physically motivated but physically undetectable by local experiments. The objection is sharp: Einstein's principle of relativity holds that all inertial frames are equally valid for physics. Positing a privileged frame that is physically undetectable conflicts with the motivating spirit of relativity. The presentist can reply that metaphysics and physics have different standards of justification; physics requires empirical testability, metaphysics may posit features of reality that, while physically undetectable, are metaphysically necessary to ground our intuitions about the nature of time.

## General Relativity and the Arrow of Time

General relativity complicates the picture further. In GR, spacetime is curved and there is no globally defined inertial frame. Some solutions to Einstein's field equations — the Gödel universe, certain Kerr spacetimes — contain no global time function at all; there is no way to foliate the spacetime into a sequence of "nows" without contradiction. Lawrence Sklar's *Space, Time, and Spacetime* (1974) provides a thorough analysis: the dynamic character of GR spacetime makes it implausible to think of temporal passage as a feature of the metric itself. Passage, if real, would have to be an additional structure imposed on the GR metric, but there is no principled basis for choosing which structure.

## The Thermodynamic Arrow

A physically grounded discussion of temporal passage must engage with the thermodynamic arrow. The second law of thermodynamics states that entropy does not spontaneously decrease in closed systems. Philosophers like Craig Callender and Huw Price argue that the apparent asymmetry of temporal passage — the felt difference between past and future — is entirely explained by the thermodynamic arrow and does not require the A-series. We remember the past (not the future) because memory formation is a low-entropy-increasing process running in the direction of thermodynamic time. We feel as if time "passes" from past to future because our cognitive processes are aligned with the thermodynamic arrow.

The "Past Hypothesis" (David Albert, *Time and Chance*, 2000) provides the foundation: the thermodynamic arrow is explained by the extremely low-entropy initial state of the universe. Given this initial condition, the overwhelmingly most probable trajectory of any macroscopic system is toward higher entropy. This explanation, if successful, grounds the apparent passage of time in a contingent fact about the initial conditions of the universe — not in any fundamental directedness of temporal becoming.

A-theorists respond that this explanation addresses the causal and epistemic asymmetries between past and future but does not address the metaphysical question: even in a universe with purely symmetric physics and no thermodynamic arrow, there would still be a genuine present, past, and future. The thermodynamic arrow explains the *content* of our temporal experience but not the *form* of temporal becoming — the categorial difference between past, present, and future, not merely the asymmetric distribution of our information about them.
