# Presentism vs. Eternalism

Is the past real? Are there dinosaurs — not as historical facts represented by fossil bones, but as actual existing entities — somewhere in the ontological inventory of the universe? Presentism says no: only what is present exists. The past has ceased to be, the future has not yet come to be, and the only things with genuine being are those that exist right now. Eternalism (or "four-dimensionalism" in one sense) says yes: the past, present, and future are all equally real parts of the four-dimensional spacetime manifold, and "now" marks our position in this manifold, not an ontologically privileged stratum.

The debate between presentism and eternalism is not merely abstract. It has implications for how we understand causation (can the past cause the present if the past no longer exists?), for the analysis of modal claims (do merely possible things exist in merely possible worlds, analogously to the way past things exist in the past?), and for personal identity (if the past is not real, what grounds the continuity of persons over time?).

Presentism is intuitively attractive: we do say that Julius Caesar no longer exists, that what will happen tomorrow does not yet exist. But presentism faces serious problems from physics. Special relativity suggests that there is no frame-independent notion of "now" — what counts as simultaneous depends on the reference frame. If presentism is true and only what is present exists, and if "present" is frame-relative, then what exists is frame-relative — a conclusion that many find metaphysically intolerable.

Eternalism is friendlier to special relativity: if all spacetime regions are equally real, then the relativity of simultaneity does not generate the problem that it does for presentism. But eternalism struggles to capture the phenomenology of temporal passage: if the future is as real as the present, in what sense is there "becoming" — in what sense does the future unfold into the present and recede into the past?

This section examines presentism and eternalism alongside the intermediate "growing block" view, assessing the theoretical costs and benefits of each.
