# Presentism

The most natural response to the question "what exists?" is to point at what is in front of you. And what is in front of you is what is happening now — not what happened an hour ago, not what will happen tomorrow. The past is gone; the future is not yet here. Presentism takes this common-sense observation and treats it as a serious metaphysical thesis: the domain of existing things is exhausted by what is present.

This is not merely the claim that present things are more accessible, or that we know more about them, or that they are more practically important. It is the claim that past and future things simply do not exist — not in any qualified sense, not as "eternally located" items in a block, not as "subsisting" entities at their respective times. The present is all there is. When Caesar crossed the Rubicon, that event and all its participants existed; they do not exist now. The Rubicon crossing is a past event, and past events are not events at all — they are former events, things that used to exist and no longer do.

Presentism is the most thoroughgoing form of A-theory. It takes seriously the A-series intuition that time involves genuine change in what exists, not merely change in which existing things are "present." The changing of a thing's A-series properties — its moving from future to present to past — is, on the presentist's account, literally a change in whether it exists at all.

The central challenge for presentism is not phenomenological but metaphysical and physical. If only present things exist, what grounds the truth of past-tense sentences? What makes it true now that Caesar crossed the Rubicon if Caesar and his crossing no longer exist? And what do we make of special relativity's demonstration that simultaneity — and hence the present — is frame-relative? If the present is what exists, and different frames define different presents, does existence vary across reference frames? These challenges, and the various presentist responses to them, are the subject of the files in this section.

Key defenders of presentism in contemporary analytic philosophy include A.N. Prior, who provided the formal tense-logical framework; Ned Markosian, who has systematically defended the view against objections; Trenton Merricks, who has pressed the truthmaker challenge; and Dean Zimmerman, who has worked on reconciling presentism with special relativity. The debate they have conducted over the past three decades represents some of the most technically demanding work in the metaphysics of time.
