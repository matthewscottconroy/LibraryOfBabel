# What Is Presentism?

Consider how we ordinarily talk about the past. We say the dinosaurs *existed* — past tense, because they exist no longer. We say Caesar crossed the Rubicon — but Caesar himself is gone, the crossing is gone, and there is nothing before us now to which we could point and say "there it is." The past is genuinely gone. And the future is genuinely not yet here: it will come, but it does not exist now. Reality is exhausted by what is happening at this moment.

This common-sense picture is presentism. Presentism is the view that only present entities and events exist. More precisely: necessarily, for any object x, x exists if and only if x is present. This makes "present" and "exists" coextensive — things that are past or future do not merely fail to be in certain locations; they do not exist at all. The past is not a region of reality populated by entities that are no longer accessible; it is nothing. The future is not a region that will contain entities; it is nothing.

Presentism aligns with the A-theory: if only the present is real, the present is metaphysically privileged in the strongest possible sense. It is the only time that is real at all. Ned Markosian, Trenton Merricks, and Dean Zimmerman are among its leading contemporary defenders. They argue that presentism captures the ordinary understanding of temporal change (things genuinely gain and lose properties), the phenomenology of the present (the feeling that now is special), and the grammatical structure of natural language (which distinguishes present, past, and future tense).

## Presentism and Common Sense

Arthur Prior pressed the case that ordinary temporal discourse is best understood as presentist. When we anticipate a future event, we do not think the event already exists; it will exist. The use of future tense in natural language tracks the view that the future is genuinely open — not yet settled, not yet real. Presentism vindicates both of these common-sense attitudes by making the present the only real time.

The B-theorist's strategy of "analyzing away" tense into non-tensed, B-series language does violence to the ordinary meaning of temporal expressions, Prior insisted. When I say "Thank goodness that's over," I am not saying "thank goodness that event stands in an earlier-than relation to this utterance." I am saying something about the present moment, and presentism takes that at face value.

## The Truthmaker Problem

Presentism faces a structural difficulty about truth. "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" is true. But if Caesar does not exist and the crossing does not exist, what is this truth *about*? The truthmaking principle — the principle that for every true proposition, there is something in virtue of which it is true — demands an answer.

Several strategies are available. The Primitive-Fact Response (Crisp, Markosian) holds that some past-tense truths are brute facts — not made true by any currently existing entity but simply true about the world's temporal history. "It was the case that Caesar crossed the Rubicon" is a primitive, irreducible tensed fact. The objection: this abandons the truthmaking principle for an important class of truths, which seems ad hoc, and leaves unexplained why the world contains these particular primitive facts rather than others.

The Property-Instantiation Response holds that the world currently has certain abstract properties — something like "being such that Caesar crossed the Rubicon" — that encode information about the past. But this seems to smuggle in past entities under a different description: specifying what it is for the world to have such a "historical" property will ultimately appeal to what actually happened, making the account circular.

The Traces Response (Bigelow) holds that current traces, memories, and records are the truthmakers for past-tense statements. The objection is instructive: consider a world in which Caesar crossed the Rubicon but in which all traces of the event were immediately and completely destroyed. In such a world, "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" seems true — the event occurred — but there are no traces to serve as truthmakers. The traces account is extensionally inadequate.

## Historical Lineage

Presentism has roots in medieval philosophy and was given systematic form in Leibniz's monadology. In contemporary analytic philosophy, it was explicitly formulated and defended by A.N. Prior in the 1960s and has been developed by Markosian ("A Defense of Presentism," 2004), Merricks (*Objects and Persons*, 2001), Zimmerman (numerous papers on presentism and special relativity), and Crisp (several papers in the 2000s on presentism, truthmakers, and the grounding problem).

The most pressing challenge facing contemporary presentism is the interaction with special relativity, to which we turn in the next section. If simultaneity is relative, what counts as "present" is frame-dependent, which seems to undermine the presentist's claim that there is a unique, objective present. The presentist must either posit a metaphysically privileged frame beyond what physics recognizes, or reconceive what "presentness" amounts to in a relativistic world. Neither move is comfortable, but both have been attempted with genuine philosophical care.
