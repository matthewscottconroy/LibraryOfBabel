# Arguments for Presentism

What do we mean when we say that the apple "changed" from green to red? One natural answer: there is a single object — the apple — that had the property green and now has the property red. Not a green-stage of the apple and a separate red-stage, but the same apple, successively green and then red. This is what presentists call genuine intrinsic change, and they argue that only presentism can accommodate it.

The B-theorist faces what David Lewis called "the problem of temporary intrinsic properties." On the block universe view, an apple that changes from green to red simply has "green at t₁" and "red at t₂" as permanent features of a four-dimensional structure. The apple "always" had both properties. The B-theorist has three options. First, temporal parts: the apple-at-t₁ is green intrinsically and the apple-at-t₂ is red intrinsically, and they are distinct entities. Second, relational properties: "green" at t₁ means "green-at-t₁," a relation between the apple and a time, not an intrinsic property at all. Third, presentism: the apple only exists in the present; when it was green, it had the intrinsic property green simpliciter; now it is red, and has the intrinsic property red simpliciter.

Lewis himself preferred temporal parts. Many A-theorists find both options 1 and 2 unsatisfying: option 1 makes "change" a feature of the distribution of properties across stages, not genuine change of a substance; option 2 makes "green" and "red" relational rather than intrinsic, which seems to misrepresent their nature. Presentism offers what these views cannot: genuine intrinsic change of a genuinely persisting entity.

## The Argument from the Open Future

When I deliberate about what to do, I take myself to be choosing between genuinely open alternatives. If my future actions already exist in the block universe — if the future is "already there" — then my deliberation seems to be a mere "reading" of what is already determined, not a genuine choice-making process. The feeling of deliberating is epiphenomenal on the eternalist view.

Presentism accommodates genuine deliberation by making the future genuinely non-existent: the future is not yet real, so my actions are not yet real, so I am genuinely choosing between alternative futures. The openness of the future is metaphysically real, not merely epistemic.

The B-theorist's response is worth taking seriously: the block universe does not entail determinism. Indeterminism about future events is compatible with their being real once they occur — which is all the block universe requires. The future could be genuinely open (indeterministic) even if future events, once they occur, are permanently part of the block. The argument from agency conflates the metaphysical question (are future events real now?) with the modal question (are future events determined?). The presentist's reply: even if the block universe is compatible with indeterminism, the sense of genuine openness that deliberation requires is not merely indeterminism but something like the future being "not yet there." Presentism captures this sense; eternalism does not, even if eternalism is compatible with indeterminism.

## Prior's Argument from Relief

Arthur Prior's "Thank Goodness That's Over" argument has special force for presentism. The relief I feel when my painful experience is over is relief that the experience is *now past* — that the present moment is on the far side of the experience. This cannot be expressed in B-series terms. The B-theorist says I am relieved that the painful experience is earlier than my relief. But this was true during the experience — the experience would be earlier than any subsequent relief-state, and I knew this while experiencing the pain. Yet I was not relieved then.

For the presentist, this is natural: the painful experience was real (it was present); it is now absent (past, that is, non-existent). The relief is that the painful experience is now no more. This is a straightforwardly correct description of what has changed: the experience has moved from existence to non-existence. The "now" that the B-theorist cannot capture is precisely the presentist's "now" — the only real time.

## Parsimony and Common Sense Combined

A final argument appeals to parsimony combined with fidelity to common sense. Common sense says that the past is gone and the future is not yet here. This view requires positing only one domain of existing entities — presently existing things — rather than three (past, present, and future things). Presentism is more parsimonious than eternalism while also being more commonsensically natural.

Ted Sider (*Four-Dimensionalism*, 2001) argues for eternalism partly on grounds of theoretical parsimony: the block universe provides a simple, uniform ontology. But the presentist can respond that "simplicity" depends on how we count. Presentism posits fewer kinds of entities (only present entities), while eternalism posits a vast four-dimensional manifold of entities most of which cannot be observed or interacted with from the present. Whether this parsimony argument succeeds depends on contested background assumptions about how to count ontological commitments — but it at least shows that the parsimony case is not straightforwardly on the eternalist's side.
