# Problems for Presentism

The intuitive appeal of presentism is real, but so are its problems. Three challenges in particular have driven many philosophers to prefer eternalism despite its apparent counterintuitiveness: the grounding problem, the relativity problem, and the problem of cross-temporal relations. Each reveals a structural difficulty that presentism must solve before it can be regarded as a fully worked-out metaphysical view.

## The Grounding Problem

The grounding problem is widely regarded as presentism's most serious challenge. It proceeds as follows:

- P1. For every true proposition, there is something in virtue of which it is true (the truthmaking principle).
- P2. "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" is true.
- P3. If presentism is true, no past entities or events exist.
- P4. A past event — Caesar's crossing — would be the natural truthmaker for P2.
- P5. But if no past entities exist, the natural truthmaker does not exist.
- Conclusion: On presentism, the true proposition "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" has no truthmaker.

The responses developed by presentists are various. The Primitive-Fact Response (Crisp, Markosian) holds that some past-tense truths are primitive — they are not made true by any currently existing entity but are simply brute facts about the world's temporal history. The truthmaking principle applies to present-tensed truths but not necessarily to past-tensed ones. The objection: this abandons a general principle for an important class of truths, which seems ad hoc.

The Property-Instantiation Response holds that there are currently instantiated structural properties that encode the past — the world currently has the property "being such that Caesar crossed the Rubicon." The objection: this seems to smuggle in past entities under a different description. What is it for the world to have such a "historical" property? Explaining it will ultimately appeal to what actually happened, making the account circular.

The Traces Response (Bigelow) holds that current traces, memories, and records are the truthmakers for past-tense statements. The isolation case is decisive against this: consider a world in which Caesar crossed the Rubicon but in which all traces of the event were immediately destroyed. In that world, "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" seems true (the event occurred), but there are no traces to serve as truthmakers. Traces are evidence for past facts, not grounds of past facts.

## The Relativity Problem

The Rietdijk-Putnam argument has the following structure. Consider two observers, Alice and Bob, in relative motion. Alice finds that events E₁ and E₂ are simultaneous with the current moment in her frame. Bob, moving relative to Alice, finds that E₁ is simultaneous with his current moment but E₂ is in his relative future. According to special relativity, both assignments of simultaneity are equally valid — there is no privileged "true" simultaneity.

On presentism, only present entities exist. For Alice, E₁ and E₂ both exist. For Bob, E₁ exists but E₂ does not yet exist. But whether E₂ exists should not depend on the reference frame — existence is not frame-relative.

The presentist responses are two. Privileged Frame Presentism holds that there is a physically undetected but metaphysically real privileged frame of reference. True simultaneity is simultaneity in this frame. The challenge: this postulates a metaphysical structure that is empirically indistinguishable from the predictions of standard special relativity. Einstein's principle of relativity rules out such a privileged frame in a physical sense. The presentist must accept that their metaphysics goes beyond what physics empirically warrants.

Relativistic Presentism modifies the notion of "the present" to accommodate relativistic spacetime — perhaps the present is the set of events on the observer's past light cone boundary. The challenge: the light cone boundary is not a simultaneity surface but a surface of "just having become causally accessible." This generates anomalous results: some events that have occurred are never present for any observer and never exist, which contradicts the basic presentist idea that existence coincides with the present.

## The Problem of Cross-Temporal Relations

Consider: "Socrates was shorter than Plato." This seems to express a relation between Socrates and Plato. But on presentism, Socrates and Plato do not currently exist, so they cannot currently stand in any relation.

Some presentists respond that cross-temporal relational claims are disguised claims about the world's history: "It was the case that (Socrates exists and Plato exists and Socrates is shorter than Plato)." This makes the cross-temporal "relation" an iterated past-tensed statement, not a genuine relation between non-existing objects. But this response faces the grounding problem in turn: what makes it true that "it was the case that Socrates was shorter than Plato" if Socrates and Plato do not exist? The regress points back to the truthmaker problem.

An alternative is to invoke "ersatz" objects — abstract representations of past individuals that are currently existing and can stand in relations to each other and to present objects. Markosian has developed a version of this view, but it requires accepting that abstract objects can represent concrete past particulars without reducing to them, which raises its own philosophical difficulties.

## Mereology and Persistence

These problems connect to the mereology of temporal parts. Eternalism typically goes with four-dimensionalism: objects persist by having distinct temporal parts at different times. Presentism is naturally allied with three-dimensionalism: objects persist by being wholly present at each time at which they exist, not by having temporal parts.

The presentist can say: the apple is wholly present now, is red, and is numerically identical to the apple that was green yesterday. There are no distinct temporal stages to identify; there is one apple, fully present, genuinely changing its properties. This is the most natural account of persistence given presentist ontology, and it has the virtue of accommodating genuine intrinsic change.

The central tension in presentism is that its most intuitive features — its account of change, its handling of deliberation, its fidelity to common sense — are underwritten by the very metaphysical structure that creates the grounding problem and the relativity problem. Presentism captures what it feels like to be in time; making that feeling into a precise and defensible metaphysical view is harder than it looks.
