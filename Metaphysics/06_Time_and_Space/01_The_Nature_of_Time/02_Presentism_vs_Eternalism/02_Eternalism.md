# Eternalism and the Block Universe

In 1908, Hermann Minkowski announced that henceforth space by itself and time by itself were doomed to fade away into mere shadows, and only a kind of union of the two would preserve an independent reality. The philosophical import of this announcement, which Minkowski made as a mathematician speaking about physics, is something philosophers are still working out. But the central implication is clear enough: space and time are aspects of a single four-dimensional structure, and events at different times are as real as events at different places. This is the block universe, and eternalism is its metaphysics.

Eternalism holds that past, present, and future events all equally exist. The battle of Hastings exists, though it does not exist now. The sun's eventual expansion into a red giant exists, though it does not exist yet. "Now" is an indexical like "here": it picks out the position of the user, but there is nothing metaphysically special about that position. The most real events are not the present ones; all events are equally real, and the apparent specialness of the present is an artifact of our temporal perspective, in the same way that the apparent specialness of "here" is an artifact of our spatial location.

Eternalism offers a metaphysics that sits naturally with our best physics. Special relativity, with its relativity of simultaneity and its unified four-dimensional spacetime structure, is most naturally interpreted as a block universe: the invariant spacetime interval, the light cone structure, the four-momentum — all of these are properties of events in the four-dimensional manifold, indifferent to which slice one calls "present." The block universe is how a physicist tends to think about spacetime when not on philosophical guard.

The challenge for eternalism is phenomenological and agential. It is the challenge of explaining why, if all times exist equally, the present feels so different from other times. Why does it feel as though things are happening now, and not merely located at the present temporal coordinate? William James expressed the worry: the block universe seems to make the future "already there" — crystallized, fixed, somehow unavoidably determined. This is not merely a feeling but a philosophical complaint. If my future actions are as real as my past ones, in what sense are they genuinely open?

The files in this section examine eternalism's arguments and difficulties, its connections with four-dimensionalism and special relativity, and the various eternalist responses to the challenge of explaining temporal experience. The central texts include D.H. Mellor's *Real Time* (1981) and *Real Time II* (1998), Ted Sider's *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001), and Huw Price's *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point* (1996).
