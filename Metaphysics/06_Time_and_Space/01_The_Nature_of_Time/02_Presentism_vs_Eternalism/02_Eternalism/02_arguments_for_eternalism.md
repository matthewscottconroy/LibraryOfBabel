# Arguments for Eternalism

The most direct argument for eternalism starts with a familiar observation about truth. "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" is true. What makes it true? The natural answer is that Caesar's crossing — the actual event — is what makes it true. Truthmakers must be real: a non-existent thing cannot make a proposition true. Caesar's crossing was in the past. Therefore, past events are real. That is the core of the eternalist's truthmaker argument, and it is considerably more intuitive than any of the presentist's alternatives (primitive tensed facts, ersatz representations, current traces). Eternalism has the simplest and most direct account of why truths about the past are true: they are made true by the past events, which exist at their temporal locations in the block.

The argument can be stated formally:

- P1. For every true proposition, there is something in virtue of which it is true.
- P2. "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" is true.
- P3. The best candidate for the truthmaker of this proposition is the actual event of Caesar crossing the Rubicon.
- P4. If this event serves as truthmaker, it must exist.
- P5. Caesar crossed the Rubicon in the past.
- Conclusion: Past events are real — i.e., eternalism is true with respect to the past.

This argument establishes the existence of the past, not the future. A growing block theorist can accept it: past events exist and serve as truthmakers, even if future events do not yet exist. The full eternalist argument must be supplemented with separate considerations to establish the reality of the future.

## The Argument from Special Relativity

We have already seen this argument in the context of presentism's problems, but its positive force for eternalism deserves emphasis. The key premises: special relativity entails that there is no frame-independent notion of simultaneity; the presentist's claim that only the present exists requires an objective, universal "now"; therefore, if presentism is false (and the growing block falls for the same reason), eternalism is true. This argument has been pressed by Putnam ("Time and Physical Geometry," 1967), Smart (*Philosophy and Scientific Realism*, 1963), Price (*Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point*, 1996), and Callender (*What Makes Time Special?*, 2017). The block universe is the interpretation of Minkowski spacetime that physicists and philosophers of physics most commonly adopt, and its motivation is not merely philosophical convenience but the structure of our best physical theory.

## The Argument from Causation

A subtler argument appeals to the demands of causal explanation. Causation runs between existing events: for E₁ to cause E₂, E₁ must exist (to produce the effect) and E₂ must exist (to be produced). Our best causal explanations are past-directed: the current state of the world is causally explained by its earlier states. The laws of physics describe how earlier states give rise to later states. If past states do not exist, these causal explanations appeal to non-existent facts — making them, at best, pseudo-explanations.

The eternalist provides a natural account: the causal relation holds between real events at different temporal locations in the block. Cause and effect both exist; they are related by the causal relation and separated by an appropriate spacetime interval. The presentist must explain how the present state of the world can be causally explained by states that no longer exist. One option: causation does not require the continued existence of the cause, only that the cause existed. But this makes "existed" a past-tensed predicate and raises the truthmaker question again.

## Formal Elegance and the Coincidence Problem

Ted Sider argues that eternalism combined with four-dimensionalism provides formal elegance that solves several metaphysical puzzles at once. Consider the statue-and-clay case: a statue and the lump of clay it is made of seem to be in the same place at the same time but are different objects. On four-dimensionalism, they share temporal stages during the period of coincidence but differ in their earlier and later stages. The lump existed before the statue was formed and will persist after it is destroyed; the statue did not exist before it was formed and ceases when the clay is reshaped. This solution requires that objects have temporal parts — which requires eternalism.

Notice also the problem of persistence: how does an object survive change? On four-dimensionalism, a persisting object has different temporal stages, each with its own intrinsic properties. There is no need to say the object "endures" through change — change is simply the distribution of properties across temporal stages. The demand for a single, enduring substance that genuinely gains and loses properties does not arise.

These advantages in formal elegance do not constitute direct evidence for eternalism's truth. But they reflect a coherence and unifying power that makes the view philosophically attractive — and they show that eternalism is not merely a physicist's convenience but a position with genuine metaphysical resources.
