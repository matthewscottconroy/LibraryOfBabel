# Problems for Eternalism

If all times are equally real in the block universe, why does the present feel so different from all the others? This is the question that never quite goes away, no matter how sophisticated the eternalist's account of temporal experience becomes. The block is static — there is no meta-time in which it could change — and yet we feel time passing. Either this feeling is a deep illusion, or it tracks something real that the block universe leaves out. Both answers have costs.

## The Problem of Temporal Passage

The challenge from temporal passage is not merely that eternalism "cannot explain" why time seems to flow. The deeper problem is whether the eternalist explanation is genuinely explanatory or merely dismissive.

Jenann Ismael has argued (*How Physics Makes Us Free*, 2016) that the eternalist explanation of temporal passage is actually quite rich: we are temporally extended beings embedded in the block, with differential cognitive access to earlier and later parts of the block. We form memories of earlier states and anticipations of later states; we receive causal inputs from earlier events and can send causal outputs to later events. This complex, asymmetric cognitive and causal structure generates the robust sense that time "flows" in a particular direction.

The A-theorist's response cuts deep: this explanation, however sophisticated, fails to capture what temporal experience is actually like. It explains why we *believe* time flows and why we have asymmetric access to past and future. But it does not explain why there is an objective present — a time that is not merely the time at which I happen to be located, but the time at which things are *actually happening now*. The difference between my cognitive situation and the reality of the present moment is precisely what the eternalist cannot explain.

This is sometimes called the "Now-Gap" — the gap between the perspective-dependent "now" of the B-theory and the objective present that the A-theory insists upon. Whether this gap represents a genuine explanatory failure of eternalism or a confusion about what we are owed is the crux of the debate.

## The Problem of Intrinsic Change

David Lewis identified "the problem of temporary intrinsics" in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986). The apple is green at t₁ and red at t₂. Intrinsic properties like "green" and "red" seem to be properties an object has purely in virtue of how it is, not in virtue of its relations to other things. But if the apple has both green-at-t₁ and red-at-t₂ permanently, these look like relational properties — properties the apple has in virtue of its relation to times — not intrinsic ones.

Lewis's preferred solution is temporal parts: the apple-at-t₁ is green intrinsically; the apple-at-t₂ is red intrinsically. There is no contradiction because the two stages are distinct objects. But the objection resurfaces: the apple (as a four-dimensional worm) has the property "has-a-green-stage" and "has-a-red-stage" permanently. This does not seem to be genuine change — it is a permanent distribution of properties across temporal locations.

The A-theorist insists: genuine change requires a single, wholly present entity that genuinely loses one property and gains another. The four-dimensionalist's "change" is just variation across stages, not genuine change of a substance. The eternalist's reply — that the demand for "genuine change of a substance" is a conceptual holdover from a pre-four-dimensionalist picture with no metaphysical basis — is coherent, but it changes the subject. Whether the subject has been changed in a way that preserves what matters is the question.

## The Problem of Agency

If all of my future actions are already part of the block universe — already there, already determinate — then in what sense am I genuinely deliberating when I consider what to do? William James articulated the worry vividly: on the block universe, the universe is "a parcel already made up," and our apparent deliberation is merely reading off what is already fixed.

The eternalist's standard response: the block universe does not entail determinism. Whether the future is determined depends on the laws of physics, not on the ontological status of future events. Quantum mechanics is indeterministic in many interpretations, so the future states of quantum systems are genuinely probabilistic. The block universe simply says that whichever outcome actually occurs is real; it does not say which outcome is determined in advance.

Moreover, genuine agency is compatible with the block: what makes my deliberation genuine is not that the future is ontologically absent, but that my deliberation plays a causal role in determining which future is actual. The fact that the outcome will eventually exist in the block does not undermine the deliberative process — it just means that when the future arrives, it will be real.

## The Epistemic Argument

The epistemic argument against the B-theory is this. Suppose I know all the B-series facts about the world — I know that it is 3:00 PM on a particular day, and I know my position in the B-series. Do I thereby know what time it is *now*?

The B-theorist says yes — knowing my temporal location in the B-series is all that "knowing the present time" amounts to. But there seems to be something distinctive about knowing that it is *now* 3:00 PM, as opposed to knowing timelessly that 3:00 PM has a particular position in the B-series. This "essential indexical" character of temporal self-location is associated with Kaplan's work on indexicals and with papers by Perry. It remains the most philosophically interesting challenge to the B-theory's claim to have fully reduced A-series facts.

The eternalist's response: "now" is fully captured by the indexical analysis. "Now" refers to the time of utterance, and knowing that it is now 3:00 PM just is knowing that 3:00 PM is the time of my current utterance. There is no additional "nowness" property needed.

Critics find this response inadequate because it equivocates between a "thin" sense of knowing (knowing a B-series fact plus knowing one's temporal location) and a "thick" sense (knowing that this, the vivid and present moment, is the moment in question). The eternalist collapses these; the A-theorist insists they are distinct. This is perhaps the most philosophically honest expression of what the debate is ultimately about.
