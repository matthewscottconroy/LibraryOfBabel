# The Growing Block Universe

There is something right about both presentism and eternalism, and something wrong about each. Presentism captures the intuition that the future is genuinely open — not yet written, not yet real. Eternalism captures the intuition that the past is permanently real — Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon really happened, and no future event can undo that. The growing block theory attempts to honor both intuitions simultaneously.

On the growing block view, developed by C.D. Broad in *Scientific Thought* (1923) and extended by Michael Tooley and others, the past and present are real — they exist in a permanent block — but the future does not yet exist. As time passes, new events occur and are added to the block, which grows at the leading edge of the present. The block is not static, as on eternalism, but it is not merely the present moment, as on presentism. It is an accumulating structure of everything that has ever happened.

This position has genuine metaphysical advantages. Past-tense truths are straightforwardly grounded in the permanent existence of past events — no elaborate truthmaker machinery required, no primitive tensed facts posited. The future's non-existence preserves the sense that future events are genuinely open and that our actions make a real difference. And temporal passage has a natural interpretation: the block growing is temporal passage, the genuine coming-to-be of new events.

But the growing block faces its own distinctive difficulty, one that is in some respects deeper than the objections against its rivals. Tim Button (*The Limits of Realism*, 2012) has pressed the epistemic problem forcefully: if we are located within the growing block, we seem to have no way of knowing that we are at the leading edge rather than somewhere in the middle. An entity located in the past portion of the block — fully real, with all its experiences and memories — would have experiences exactly like ours, including the sense that "now" is the present. There seems to be no cognitive signature that distinguishes being at the edge from being in the interior. This is a serious challenge that neither presentism nor eternalism faces in the same form.

The files in this section examine the growing block in detail, the question of the open future and its relationship to tense logic and quantum mechanics, and the epistemic problem and the various responses to it.
