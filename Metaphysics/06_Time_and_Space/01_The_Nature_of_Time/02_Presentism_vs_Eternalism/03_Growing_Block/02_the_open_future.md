# The Open Future

Here is a puzzle that begins with grammar. Consider the sentence "There will be a sea battle tomorrow." Is it true or false right now? If it is true, the battle seems inevitable — the future is determined. If it is false, the battle seems impossible. Neither seems right if we think the future is genuinely open and undetermined. Aristotle raised this puzzle in *De Interpretatione* chapter 9 and concluded that future contingents — statements about non-determined future events — lack a determinate truth value. The law of excluded middle, he suggested, fails for some future-tensed sentences.

One of the most important motivations for the growing block theory is precisely this intuition. If the future does not yet exist, future contingent statements are not yet made true or false. Truth (for future contingents) requires the relevant event to have occurred — to be part of the growing block. Until it has, the statement is neither true nor false. The growing block gives the open future a metaphysical grounding: the future is genuinely open because it is genuinely absent.

## Aristotle's Sea Battle

Aristotle reasons as follows: let P = "There will be a sea battle tomorrow." The law of excluded middle (LEM) says P ∨ ¬P. If LEM holds for future contingents, then either P is now true (the sea battle is fated to occur) or P is now false (it is fated not to occur). But if either alternative is necessary, there is no genuine contingency about the future.

Aristotle concludes that future contingents are neither true nor false — what is sometimes called "supervaluationism" about the future: a proposition is definitely true if true on all admissible completions of the future, definitely false if false on all completions, and indeterminate if true on some and false on others. The sea battle sentence is indeterminate because there are admissible futures in which a sea battle occurs and admissible futures in which it does not.

Growing block theorists endorse this semantic picture: since the future does not exist, future contingent statements are not yet made true or false.

## Tense Logic and the Open Future

A.N. Prior developed two contrasting tense-logical systems representing different positions on the openness of the future. Diodorean (linear) tense logic treats "It will be the case that P" as true iff P is true at some future time on the actual future timeline. This treats the future as determinate — there is a single actual future. Peircean (branching) tense logic treats "It will be the case that P" as true iff P is true at some future time on all possible future timelines consistent with the current state of the world. The future "branches" into multiple possible continuations, and what will definitely be the case is only what is true on all branches.

The Peircean system captures the open future more naturally and is the appropriate logic for a growing block where future events do not yet exist. It allows for genuine indeterminism about the future while maintaining bivalence for past and present-tense sentences.

## Quantum Mechanics and the Open Future

Quantum mechanics provides the most scientifically compelling support for the open future. In the standard (Copenhagen) interpretation, the evolution of a quantum system prior to measurement is described by a wavefunction — a superposition of possible states. When a measurement is made, the system "collapses" to one of the possible states, with a probability given by the Born rule. This collapse is genuinely indeterministic: the specific outcome is not determined in advance, even in principle.

For the growing block theorist, this quantum indeterminism supports the open future in a precise sense: the leading edge of the growing block is not fixed until quantum events "occur" (collapse). The block grows one event at a time, with each quantum measurement producing a new, definite event at the edge.

Interpretive matters complicate this picture, however. The many-worlds interpretation holds that there is no collapse: all outcomes occur, in different branches of a continuously branching universe. On this interpretation, there is no sense in which future events are "open" — all outcomes occur, and "indeterminism" is an artifact of our ignorance of which branch we are in. The growing block theorist who relies on quantum indeterminism for the open future must take a stand on the interpretation of quantum mechanics.

## The Open Future and Free Will

The most practically significant application of the open future is its connection to libertarian free will. The libertarian holds that genuine free agency requires that the agent could have done otherwise — that the future is genuinely open. If the future is fixed in the block universe, this openness is illusory, and with it libertarian free will.

The open future of the growing block provides the metaphysical space for libertarian free will: future events do not yet exist, so my future actions are not yet settled, so I am genuinely choosing between alternative futures. The openness of the future is metaphysically real, not merely epistemic.

We should note a limitation of this argument: even if the future is open, this does not by itself guarantee that my actions are free in the libertarian sense. An open future could be one in which future events are genuinely random rather than agent-determined. Libertarian free will requires not just indeterminism but a particular kind of agent-causation that goes beyond mere randomness. The open future is a necessary but not sufficient condition for libertarian free will.

## Semantic Commitments

The open future has implications for the semantics of future-tensed statements. If future events do not exist, the truth conditions of future-tensed statements are not determined by currently existing facts. The options are: bivalence fails (Aristotle); supervaluationism — future contingents are true if true on all admissible future continuations, false if false on all, indeterminate otherwise; probabilism — future contingents express probabilistic claims; or eternalism about truth — future contingents are evaluated relative to the completed future timeline.

Growing block theorists typically adopt the first or second option. The attraction of these options is that they align the semantics with the metaphysics: since future events are not real, future-tensed sentences lack the truthmakers that would make them true or false. When the relevant events occur and become part of the block, the sentences acquire determinate truth values.
