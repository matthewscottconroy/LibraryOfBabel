# Problems for the Growing Block Theory

The growing block has the comfortable appearance of a compromise position: it avoids presentism's truthmaker problem by making past events real, and it avoids eternalism's static block by making the future genuinely absent. But closer examination reveals that the growing block faces distinctive challenges that do not simply split the difference between its rivals. Some of these challenges are quite sharp.

## The Epistemic Problem

The most striking objection is due to Tim Button ("There's No Time Like the Present," 2006), and it is genuinely disturbing. On the growing block, entities in the past regions of the block exist and have complete psychological histories — memories, present experiences, the phenomenology of "now." An entity at the leading edge of the block also has a complete psychological history. The experiences of an entity in the middle of the block are phenomenologically indistinguishable from the experiences of an entity at the leading edge.

Therefore: no agent can know from their experiences alone whether they are at the leading edge (the true present) or embedded in the past. We might be in the past without knowing it.

The argument can be stated precisely:

- P1. On the growing block, entities in the past regions of the block exist and have complete psychological histories, including memories of the "past" and experiences of the "present."
- P2. An entity at the leading edge also has a complete psychological history.
- P3. The experiences of an entity in the middle of the block are phenomenologically indistinguishable from the experiences of an entity at the leading edge.
- P4. Therefore, no agent can know from their experiences alone whether they are at the leading edge or embedded in the past.
- Conclusion: The growing block entails radical uncertainty about whether we are at the present moment.

This is the "lost in the past" problem. It is serious because the growing block posits a difference (being at the edge vs. being in the middle) that makes no experiential difference. If the metaphysical difference between present and past makes no cognitive difference, it is unclear what work the distinction does.

Trenton Merricks argues that the leading edge is the only time at which there are facts about "which time is present" — past moments do not have a "present" quality; they are simply past. This is a primitive presentness that attaches only to the leading edge. But if the relevant phenomenology is exactly the same for past and present moments, and if the distinction between being present and being past is metaphysically primitive, the epistemic problem remains: we cannot know which side of this primitive distinction we are on.

## The Meta-Temporal Regress

The growing block says the block grows — new events are added over time. But what is this "growing"? It seems to require a meta-temporal process: the block has different sizes at different meta-times, growing as meta-time advances. But then there must be a growing block at the meta-level too, and the regress begins.

The growing block theorist can respond that the "growing" is not a genuine temporal process requiring meta-time but a fundamental feature of reality. The block grows in the sense that more events exist — progressively more comprehensive facts about the block are true — without this requiring a meta-time. This response makes the growing a primitive, brute feature of temporal reality, much like the primitivist A-theorist's response to the regress for temporal passage. It avoids the regress but at the cost of positing an unexplained "becoming" that is not further analyzable.

## The Relativity Problem

Like presentism, the growing block requires a global "present edge" — a hyper-surface that divides the past (which exists) from the future (which does not). But in Minkowski spacetime, there is no invariant notion of such a hyper-surface. Any choice of a global simultaneity surface to serve as the present edge is frame-dependent. Different inertial observers will disagree about which events are at the leading edge and which are in the block's interior. This seems to make the ontological distinction between "existing" (past/present) and "non-existing" (future) frame-relative.

Some growing block theorists accept that there is a privileged foliation of spacetime that defines the objective succession of "presents." This is the same neo-Newtonian move that presentists make: positing physical structure beyond what special relativity recognizes. Broad himself developed the growing block in a Newtonian context where absolute simultaneity is assumed; extending the view to a relativistic context remains a significant challenge.

## The Problem of the Present's Special Status

A subtler problem concerns what makes the leading edge "present" rather than merely "the temporally most recent." The growing block theorist says the leading edge is present; events at the edge are currently occurring; the edge is the "now." But what is this "presentness" of the leading edge? Is it a primitive property? If so, what work does it do beyond labeling the edge?

The moving spotlight theorist has a ready answer: the leading edge is present because it is illuminated by the spotlight of nowness — a primitive property that migrates through the block. But the growing block theorist typically does not invoke the spotlight; they want to say the edge is present because it is *the edge*. This makes presentness a structural feature (being the edge) rather than an intrinsic property.

This raises a sharp question: what is the metaphysical difference between the growing block and the eternalist block universe at an arbitrary temporal cross-section? The eternalist can describe any temporal slice as "the edge" relative to earlier slices. The growing block theorist must explain why there is a unique, objective edge — a truly present moment — rather than merely a matter of temporal ordering. Without such an explanation, the growing block risks collapsing into eternalism with a misleading label.

Despite these challenges, the growing block has attracted renewed philosophical attention precisely because it captures the asymmetry between the fixed past and the open future in a way that neither presentism nor eternalism can do straightforwardly. The challenges are real, but the theoretical motivation is genuine.
