# The Nature of Space

Newton and Leibniz disagreed about space with characteristic intensity. For Newton, space is an absolute container — a real, infinite, three-dimensional expanse that exists independently of the matter within it. Space would exist even if there were no matter; it is the background against which all motion occurs. Absolute space provides the standard for genuine rest and genuine motion: the water in Newton's famous rotating bucket climbs the sides of the bucket because it is genuinely rotating relative to absolute space, not just relative to the bucket.

For Leibniz, Newton's absolute space is a metaphysical absurdity. Space is nothing but the system of spatial relations among material things. There is no such thing as the location of a material body except relative to other material bodies; there is no such thing as an empty region of space. If God were to shift the entire universe two feet to the east while preserving all spatial relations among its parts, the result would be indistinguishable from what we started with — which means, Leibniz argued via his principle of sufficient reason, that the two scenarios are not really different scenarios at all. Space is purely relational.

The debate between absolute and relational theories of space — substantivalism versus relationism — has been transformed but not dissolved by modern physics. General relativity describes spacetime as a dynamical entity that can curve, ripple, and expand, interacting with the matter it contains. This makes spacetime look more like a substance — a real physical field — than a mere abstraction. But the debate about whether there is a genuine ontological commitment to spacetime as a substantival entity, or whether spacetime structure can be understood purely relationally, continues in sophisticated form.

This chapter develops the classical Newton-Leibniz debate, the bucket argument, the hole argument that threatened substantivalism in the context of general relativity, and the range of contemporary positions. The philosophy of space shows that even the most basic features of physical geometry resist straightforward ontological characterization.
