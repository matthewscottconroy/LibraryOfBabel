# Absolutism vs. Relationalism About Space

Does space exist independently of the objects that occupy it? Or is space nothing but the system of spatial relations among material objects — so that if there were no objects, there would be no space? These are the two poles of one of the oldest debates in the philosophy of nature: absolutism (Newton's position) and relationalism (Leibniz's position).

Newton argued for absolute space: a fixed, infinite, three-dimensional container that exists independently of any material objects that might be in it. Absolute space provides the standard of "true" rest and motion — a body is truly at rest if it is at rest relative to absolute space, regardless of its motion relative to other bodies. Newton's bucket experiment was designed to show that rotational motion is not merely relative: a bucket of water rotating with respect to absolute space develops a concave surface, even if it is not rotating relative to any other material object. The concavity is evidence of absolute rotation — rotation with respect to absolute space.

Leibniz rejected absolute space as metaphysically extravagant and theologically suspect. If space is an absolute container, then God could have created the universe ten feet to the left of where it is — a meaningless difference, Leibniz argued, since no observable distinction corresponds to it. Space is nothing but the order of co-existing things: to say two objects are one meter apart is to say that they stand in a certain spatial relation to each other, not that they each occupy positions in an independently existing spatial grid.

Ernst Mach extended Leibniz's critique: "absolute space" is a fiction; what Newton's bucket actually measures is rotation with respect to the mass distribution of the universe. Mach's principle — that inertial frames are determined by the distribution of matter — influenced Einstein's development of general relativity, which in some ways vindicates the relational approach, in other ways complicates it.

This section examines the Newton-Leibniz debate, Mach's critique, and the implications of general relativity for the absolutism-relationalism question.
