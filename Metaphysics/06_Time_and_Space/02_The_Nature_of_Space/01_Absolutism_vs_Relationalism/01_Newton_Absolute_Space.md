# Newton's Absolute Space

When Newton published the *Principia Mathematica* in 1687, he included a remarkable philosophical prologue — the Scholium on space, time, and motion — that set out his metaphysical commitments alongside his mechanics. Absolute space, he wrote, "in its own nature, without relation to anything external, remains always similar and immovable." It is not the relative arrangement of bodies; it is the permanent, immovable container in which all bodies are located and through which they move. This is not an ordinary physical claim but a metaphysical one: there is a real entity — absolute space — that exists independently of any body that occupies it and provides the ultimate reference frame for all motion.

The philosophical argument for absolute space proceeds through the rotating bucket thought experiment and its conclusion that absolute acceleration is physically real and detectable. The concavity of the water's surface depends on whether the water is rotating relative to absolute space, not merely on whether it is rotating relative to the bucket or the room. This gives absolute space a genuine physical role: it is what makes the difference between real rotation and the mere appearance of rotation.

Leibniz attacked this conclusion on metaphysical grounds that remain influential. If the universe were shifted five feet to the left in absolute space, the resulting world would be qualitatively indistinguishable from the original — all spatial relations among bodies would be preserved. But then there is no sufficient reason for God to have placed the universe where He did rather than five feet to the left. Absolute space allows for ontological distinctions with no qualitative differences, violating the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles. The exchange between Leibniz and Newton's representative Samuel Clarke, conducted through letters from 1715 to 1716, is the foundational document of the absolutism-relationalism debate.

The files in this section examine Newton's argument for absolute space in detail, the bucket argument and its legacy, and the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence. A particular concern is the question of what structural commitments absolute space actually requires: Michael Friedman and others have shown that Newton's own mechanics requires less than the full Newtonian absolute space — what is needed is the neo-Newtonian affine structure that defines absolute acceleration without defining absolute position or velocity. This reduction of the absolute to its physically necessary minimum is a methodological strategy that recurs throughout the philosophy of space and time.
