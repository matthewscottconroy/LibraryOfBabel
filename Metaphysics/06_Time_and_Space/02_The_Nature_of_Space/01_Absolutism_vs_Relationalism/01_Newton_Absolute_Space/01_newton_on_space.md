# Newton on Absolute Space and Time

We need to ask what the laws of physics are laws *about*. Newton's first law says that a body remains at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by an external force. Uniform motion — but with respect to what? A ship moving smoothly through calm water is in uniform motion relative to the water, but the water itself moves relative to the Earth, which moves relative to the Sun. We need a reference point that does not itself need to be referred back to something else. Newton's answer was absolute space.

Absolute space, for Newton, is "without relation to anything external" — immovable, infinite, homogeneous. An object's absolute position is its location in this framework; its absolute velocity is its rate of change of absolute position. These are objective features of the object, independent of its spatial relations to other objects. Absolute time "of itself, and from its own nature, flows equally without relation to anything external." Time advances uniformly and independently of physical processes.

These characterizations are from the famous "Scholium" to the Definitions at the beginning of the *Principia Mathematica* (1687). Newton is careful to distinguish "absolute" space and time — the genuine metaphysical realities — from "relative" or "apparent" quantities: the relative motion of one object with respect to another is observable and practically important, but it is grounded in the absolute motions of both objects in absolute space. The same amount of objective time elapses between any two distant events regardless of who measures it.

## The Epistemological Problem

Newton acknowledged a crucial epistemological difficulty: absolute space is not directly observable. We cannot stand "in" absolute space and determine our absolute position by looking around. What we can observe are positions and motions relative to other objects. This creates an epistemic gap between the metaphysics (absolute space and motion exist) and what we can observe (only relative positions and motions).

The globes thought experiment addresses this gap. Imagine two spheres connected by a cord, floating in an otherwise empty universe. If they are rotating around their common center of mass, the cord will be under tension; if they are not rotating, it will not be. Newton argues that the difference in tension reveals a difference in absolute rotation — even though there are no other objects in the universe relative to which rotation could be measured. The tension in the cord is a real effect. It must be caused by something. And that something is absolute rotation in absolute space.

This thought experiment aims to show that there are objective facts about absolute motion even in a universe with no observable reference points. The argument is a form of inference to the best explanation: absolute space best explains why the concavity and tension correlate with the water's and globes' intrinsic state of rotation rather than with their rotation relative to any particular object.

## Newton's Laws and Absolute Space

Newton's first law defines the class of "inertial frames" — frames in which the law of inertia holds. Any frame moving at constant velocity relative to absolute space is an inertial frame. Frames accelerating relative to absolute space are non-inertial; in them, fictitious forces appear — centrifugal force, Coriolis force, and so on.

A problem noticed by Leibniz and later exploited by Mach: the physics is invariant under the group of all uniform motions (boosts). That is, if the universe were moving uniformly at any velocity with respect to absolute space, the physics would be exactly the same. This means that absolute velocity — though metaphysically real for Newton — is in principle undetectable. Only relative velocities and absolute accelerations are observable. Absolute space thus plays a role in Newton's physics only through its connection to absolute acceleration, not through absolute position or velocity. This seems to leave a metaphysical excess: a commitment to something (absolute position, absolute velocity) that makes no observable difference.

## Historical Impact and Critique

Newton's account was immediately controversial. Leibniz attacked it in the correspondence with Clarke (1715–16) on metaphysical grounds — the Principle of Sufficient Reason and the Identity of Indiscernibles. Berkeley attacked it in *De Motu* (1721) on empiricist grounds: a concept that makes no observable difference is meaningless. Mach's critique in *The Science of Mechanics* (1883) was the most technically sophisticated: absolute space should be replaced by the "fixed stars" — the rest of the matter in the universe — as the reference for inertia.

Einstein's general relativity partially vindicated this Machian intuition, though it did not fully eliminate absolute geometric structure. Despite these critiques, Newton's framework was enormously successful for over two centuries. The concept of absolute space was eventually replaced in physics by the four-dimensional spacetime of special and general relativity, but the philosophical debate it generated — about the nature of space, the analysis of inertia, and the relationship between observable structure and physical reality — remains central to the philosophy of physics.
