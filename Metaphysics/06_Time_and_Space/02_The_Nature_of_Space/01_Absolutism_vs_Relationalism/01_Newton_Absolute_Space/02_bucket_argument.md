# The Bucket Argument

Fill a bucket with water and suspend it by a twisted rope. Release the rope: the bucket begins to spin, but the water surface is initially flat — the water is not yet rotating relative to the bucket. As the rotation is transmitted to the water, the surface becomes concave: the water "climbs" the walls. Stop the bucket but let the water continue to spin: the surface remains concave.

Newton's argument from this experiment is elegant. The concavity of the water surface is caused by the water's rotation. But notice what it is not caused by: it is not caused by rotation relative to the bucket. When the bucket first starts turning and the water has not yet caught up (Stage 2), there is maximum relative rotation between bucket and water — yet the surface is flat. When the bucket and water co-rotate together (Stage 3), there is zero relative rotation — yet the surface is concave. When the bucket is stopped and the water still spins (Stage 4), there is again relative rotation — and the surface remains concave. The concavity tracks something other than relative motion between bucket and water.

The four stages make this vivid. In Stages 1 and 2, the water has zero absolute rotation and a flat surface. In Stages 3 and 4, the water has non-zero absolute rotation and a concave surface. The surface shape correlates with absolute rotation, not with relative rotation. Newton concludes: the relevant rotation is absolute rotation — rotation with respect to absolute space.

The argument moves from an observable effect (the concave surface) to an unobservable cause (rotation in absolute space) by inference to the best explanation. Absolute space best explains why the concavity correlates with the water's intrinsic state of rotation rather than with its rotation relative to any particular object.

## The Argument Formalized

- P1. The concavity of the water's surface is caused by the water's rotation.
- P2. This rotation is not rotation relative to the bucket (since the surface is concave when bucket and water are co-rotating, and also when the water is spinning while the bucket is still — two configurations with different relative motion between water and bucket).
- P3. The rotation responsible for the concavity is also not rotation relative to the Earth's surface or any other nearby observable reference (since we can imagine performing the experiment in an otherwise empty universe with the same result).
- P4. Therefore, the relevant rotation is absolute rotation — rotation with respect to absolute space.
- Conclusion: Absolute space is the reference frame relative to which the physically relevant rotation is defined.

## Objections

Several important objections have been pressed. Leibniz objected that the bucket argument shows rotation produces observable effects, but it does not show these effects are caused by rotation relative to absolute space as opposed to rotation relative to some other standard. The argument underdetermines the choice between absolute space and a sophisticated relational account.

Mach's objection is more technically developed. The relevant standard of rotation is rotation relative to the "fixed stars" — the distribution of matter in the rest of the universe. The water becomes concave because it is rotating relative to the massive distribution of matter in the universe. Remove all the distant matter, Mach implied, and the concavity would disappear. This is an empirical prediction that differs from Newton's: for Mach, the bucket experiment in an otherwise empty universe would show no concavity because without any matter to rotate relative to, there would be no relevant rotation. Newton would say the surface becomes concave (if the water rotates absolutely); Mach would say it stays flat.

The modal objection adds a further worry: Newton's argument relies on what *would* happen in an otherwise empty universe. But the behavior of matter in a completely empty universe may differ radically from its behavior in the actual universe. We cannot simply extrapolate from the actual experiment to the hypothetical empty universe.

## The Bucket Argument and General Relativity

General relativity partially vindicates both Newton and Mach. In GR, the geometry of spacetime is determined by the distribution of matter (Einstein's field equations). A rotating body in GR "drags" the spacetime around it — the frame-dragging effect (Lense-Thirring effect), confirmed by the Gravity Probe B experiment in 2011. This is a form of Machian influence: the rotation of matter affects the local inertial frames of nearby objects.

However, GR does not fully satisfy Mach's principle. There are solutions to the Einstein field equations — including the Gödel universe and vacuum solutions with Minkowski spacetime — that have well-defined notions of rotation and inertia even without a distribution of matter. This suggests that GR is not fully Machian: the geometry of spacetime is not entirely determined by the distribution of matter, and there is irreducible geometric structure beyond what any relational account of matter can provide. The bucket argument thus remains relevant even in the context of GR: even in a dynamical spacetime, there are residual absolute features — the local inertial structure of the spacetime manifold — that resist a purely relational analysis.
