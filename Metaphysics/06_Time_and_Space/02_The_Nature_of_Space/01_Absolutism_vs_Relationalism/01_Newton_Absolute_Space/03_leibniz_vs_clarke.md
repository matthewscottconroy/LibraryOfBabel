# The Leibniz-Clarke Correspondence

Suppose God, in creating the universe, had to choose where to place it in absolute space. He could place it here, or five feet to the left, or rotated ninety degrees. Since absolute space is homogeneous — no one location differs from another in any intrinsic way — all these options are qualitatively identical. There is nothing to distinguish the actual universe from any of its absolute-space "shifts." But by the Principle of Sufficient Reason, everything that is the case has a sufficient reason for being as it is rather than otherwise. If there is no sufficient reason to place the universe at one location rather than another, God would have no reason to make any choice at all — and therefore could not have created the universe with a specific absolute position.

This is Leibniz's central argument against Newton's absolute space, advanced in the correspondence with Samuel Clarke (Newton's spokesman) in 1715–1716, the final year of Leibniz's life. The argument has two forms. The PSR form: absolute space allows for differences in absolute position with no sufficient reason, which is impossible. The PII form: the universe at location L and the "shifted" universe at location L+5 feet are qualitatively identical; by the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles, qualitatively identical situations are numerically identical; therefore, there is no fact about which location the universe occupies.

Clarke responded on Newton's behalf: God's will itself provides the sufficient reason for any choice. God can choose between symmetrically related alternatives without violating the PSR, because God's will is itself the sufficient reason. This connects to a theological dispute about divine freedom — Leibniz thinks an arbitrary choice of this kind would make God an irrational agent; Clarke thinks it reflects the genuine freedom of the will.

## The Leibnizian Arguments Reconstructed

The PSR form of the argument:

- P1. If absolute space exists, there are possible universes identical in all observable respects but differing in absolute position (the "shifted" universe).
- P2. By the Principle of Sufficient Reason, for any state of affairs that obtains, there is a sufficient reason why it obtains rather than some alternative.
- P3. If the actual universe and the "shifted" universe are observationally identical, there is no sufficient reason for God to have created one rather than the other.
- P4. Therefore, God would have no sufficient reason for creating the universe in its actual absolute position.
- P5. By the PSR, this is impossible.
- Conclusion: There are no genuinely distinct possible universes differing only in absolute position. Therefore absolute space does not exist.

The PII form of the argument:

- P1. The universe at location L and the "shifted" universe at location L+5 feet are qualitatively identical: they have the same arrangement of all matter and the same spatial relations among material things.
- P2. By the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles, qualitatively identical "situations" are numerically identical.
- P3. Therefore, the universe at L and the universe at L+5 feet are not two distinct possible situations but one.
- P4. Therefore, there is no fact about which location the universe occupies in absolute space.
- Conclusion: Absolute space does not exist.

## Clarke's Responses

Clarke accepts the PSR but denies that it excludes choices between qualitatively equivalent alternatives. God freely wills particular acts of creation, and the act of will is itself the sufficient reason. This connects to the doctrine of "liberum arbitrium indifferentiae" — the freedom of the will to choose between equivalent alternatives. Leibniz rejects this as tantamount to irrationality; Clarke accepts it as a feature of divine freedom.

On the PII: Clarke denies that the PII is unrestricted. Two particles might be qualitatively identical in all intrinsic properties but numerically distinct — differing only in their absolute spatial positions. The PII, on this reading, would collapse all such distinctions; but Clarke argues that this is a mistake, since numerical identity is not determined by qualitative identity. Absolute position is itself a kind of qualitative difference — a relational property of objects (their relation to absolute space) — and objects can differ in this relational property while being identical in all intrinsic properties.

This debate about the PII anticipates the modern debate about the identity of indiscernibles in quantum mechanics, where particles of the same type (bosons or fermions) are qualitatively identical but numerically distinct.

## The Shift Argument and Modern Physics

Leibniz's "shift argument" has a modern descendant in the "gauge argument" for relationalism. In modern physics, many theories have gauge symmetries: transformations that leave all observable quantities invariant. The existence of gauge symmetries suggests that the "extra" structure — absolute position in space, the absolute phase of a quantum field — is physically redundant. This is a sophisticated form of Leibniz's point: if no observable difference corresponds to a difference in absolute structure, then the absolute structure is scientifically otiose.

The hole argument in general relativity (developed by Earman and Norton) is perhaps the most technically precise version of the Leibnizian critique: it shows that manifold substantivalism in GR leads to radical indeterminism, analogous to the PSR violation Leibniz identified. The responses to the hole argument — sophisticated substantivalism, relationalism, structural realism — are the modern forms of the Clarke-Leibniz debate. The correspondence of 1715–1716, written in the context of Leibniz's rationalist theology and Clarke's Newtonian physics, thus launched a dispute whose technical descendants occupy philosophers of physics today.
