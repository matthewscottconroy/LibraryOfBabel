# Relationalism About Space

The relationalist about space holds that there is no such thing as absolute space — no immovable container in which bodies are located and relative to which their absolute positions are defined. Space is constituted by the relations among physical things: distances, directions, angles. When all the spatial relations among existing things are specified, there is no residual question about where "in space" the whole arrangement is located — because there is no space independent of the arrangement.

Leibniz's formulation remains the clearest: space is "the order of coexisting things." It is a relational structure, not a substance. The question "where in absolute space is the universe?" is like asking what is to the north of the North Pole — not a hard question but a malformed one.

The main challenge for relationalism is inertia. Newton's bucket thought experiment seems to show that absolute acceleration is physically real and distinguishable from merely relative acceleration. If the water is rotating relative to absolute space, its surface is concave. If it is merely rotating relative to the bucket — while both it and the bucket are non-rotating relative to absolute space — the surface is flat. The relationalist has no absolute space relative to which rotation is defined; but the concavity is real. What explains it?

Mach's response, developed in *The Science of Mechanics* (1883), was that inertial effects arise not from rotation relative to absolute space but from rotation relative to the total matter in the universe — the "fixed stars." Inertia is a relation between a body and all other bodies, mediated by some yet-to-be-specified interaction. This is still relational (inertia is grounded in matter-matter relations) but involves the global matter distribution rather than any local relative motion.

Contemporary relationalism has become more sophisticated, responding to the mathematical and physical developments that have followed Newton. The gauge-invariance argument shows that absolute spatial positions are unobservable and play no role in the observable content of Newtonian mechanics; this is a modern version of Leibniz's indistinguishability argument. The hole argument in GR shows that manifold substantivalism — the view that individual spacetime points are real with intrinsic identities — leads to radical indeterminism. Structural spacetime realism attempts to preserve the objective content of spacetime geometry without committing to either a substantial subject or a purely material relational base. The files in this section trace this development from Leibniz through Mach to contemporary structural approaches.
