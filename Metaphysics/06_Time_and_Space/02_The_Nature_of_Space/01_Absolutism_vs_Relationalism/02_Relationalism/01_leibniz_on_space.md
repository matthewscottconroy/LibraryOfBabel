# Leibniz on the Relational Nature of Space

If you removed every object from the universe — every particle, field, and piece of matter — would there be any space left? Newton says yes: absolute space would remain, the immovable framework that existed before objects were placed in it and would persist if they were removed. Leibniz says no: without objects to stand in spatial relations to one another, there is nothing for space to be. Space is not a container but an *order* — the order of co-existing things.

This relational view holds that spatial facts are facts about the spatial relations among simultaneously existing objects. A universe with no objects would have no space. "The distance between A and B" does not describe a region of some independent substance; it describes a relation between A and B, grounded in their properties and their relative positions. There is no space "in itself"; space consists in the spatial relations — distances, directions, angles — among actually existing objects.

Leibniz's relationalism is grounded in his broader metaphysics. For Leibniz, space and time are "well-founded phenomena" — real but grounded in the internal properties (perceptions and apperceptions) of the basic substances (monads). The spatial ordering of objects reflects the order of their confusedly perceived relations among monads. This idealist foundation differs from the realist relationalism of modern discussions, where spatial relations are taken as genuinely objective facts about the material world. But the negative thesis — space is not a substance — is shared, and the arguments Leibniz advanced for it remain relevant.

## The Principle of Sufficient Reason and Space

The most powerful argument Leibniz offers against absolute space is the PSR:

- P1. If absolute space exists, there are alternative possible states of affairs that differ only in the absolute position of all matter (the "shifted" universe, the "rotated" universe, etc.).
- P2. These alternative states are observationally and qualitatively indistinguishable.
- P3. By the PSR, for God to have chosen to create the universe at one absolute location rather than another, there would have to be a sufficient reason for the choice.
- P4. Since all alternative locations are qualitatively identical, no sufficient reason can distinguish them.
- P5. Therefore, God could not have created the universe at any particular absolute location.
- Conclusion: Absolute space does not exist.

This argument is extraordinarily influential. Its modern descendants include the "gauge argument" for relationalism — redundant gauge degrees of freedom indicate unreal structure — and the use of Leibniz equivalence in the philosophy of general relativity.

## The Identity of Indiscernibles

Leibniz's second argument invokes the PII: numerically distinct things must be qualitatively different in at least one respect. Consider the actual universe and a universe in which all matter is shifted five feet to the east. These two universes are qualitatively identical: every spatial relation among material objects is preserved. By the PII, they are not two distinct possible worlds but one. Therefore, there is no fact of the matter about the absolute location of the universe — there is only the relational structure of matter.

Clarke's response was to reject the unrestricted PII: two spheres of identical material constitution could differ in their absolute positions, even if qualitatively identical in all other respects. Absolute position itself constitutes a qualitative difference. For Leibniz, this is incoherent — there is no qualitative difference that is not grounded in intrinsic properties of substances; the idea of a "purely positional" qualitative difference makes no sense. For Newton (via Clarke), absolute position is a genuine relational property of objects (their relation to absolute space), and objects can differ in this relational property while being identical in all intrinsic properties.

## Leibniz on Time

Leibniz's relational account of time is structurally parallel to his account of space but grounds temporal relations in causation rather than co-existence. Time is the "order of successive things": the temporal relation of E₁ being earlier than E₂ consists in E₁ being causally prior to E₂. This causal-relational theory anticipates later causal theories of temporal order (Reichenbach's causal theory of time, van Fraassen's causal approach).

There is no absolute time flowing independently of events; time is constituted by the causal ordering of events. The question "how much time elapsed between two events?" is answered by the causal structure of the processes connecting them, not by an absolute clock. Leibniz's relational time is thus operationally closer to modern measurement-theoretic accounts of time than Newton's absolute time.

## Leibniz's Legacy

Leibniz's relationalism was largely eclipsed by Newton's physics for two centuries but was revived by Mach's empiricist critique of Newtonian absolute space and by Einstein's development of general relativity. The modern debate between substantivalism and relationalism in the philosophy of physics has roots in the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence that are explicitly acknowledged in the contemporary literature — see Earman, *World Enough and Space-Time* (1989) and Sklar, *Space, Time, and Spacetime* (1974). The PSR and PII arguments, while originating in a theological and rationalist context that few contemporary philosophers would fully endorse, have been reformulated in secular and technical terms and continue to generate productive philosophical debate.
