# Contemporary Relationalism About Spacetime

The primary challenge for any relational account of spacetime is the existence of absolute acceleration. Even granting that absolute position and absolute velocity are empirically inaccessible and metaphysically otiose, absolute acceleration is different: it has observable effects — centrifugal force, Coriolis force, tidal forces. If Newton's absolutism fails, the relationalist still needs to explain these effects without appealing to acceleration relative to absolute space.

Contemporary relationalism has developed sophisticated forms that go well beyond Leibniz's original claim that space is merely the order of co-existing things. Three strategies deserve attention.

Mach's strategy grounds the relevant "absolute" in the distribution of actual matter. As discussed in the previous section, this strategy is partially vindicated by GR through frame-dragging, but it fails for vacuum solutions. Julian Barbour has pressed the Machian program furthest, developing a relational interpretation of general relativity in *Absolute or Relative Motion?* (1989) and *The End of Time* (1999) in which there is no fundamental "time" in the description of the world — only a timeless "shape space" of relative configurations of matter. What we call "time" is a derived measure reflecting the sequence of configurations along a path through shape space. The block grows by adding new Nows; temporal order and passage are emergent from the atemporal relational structure. This is the ultimate Machian position: not only is there no absolute space, there is no fundamental time either.

The structural approach (Ladyman, French, Esfeld) takes a different route. What is real about spacetime is its structure — the invariant, relational properties encoded in the metric — without any underlying substance (spacetime points with haecceities) or purely relational base (facts only about material events). The metric field is the fundamental physical entity, not the manifold of points on which the metric is defined. The structure that structural realists take to be real is captured by the symmetries and invariants of the theory: the Lorentz group for special relativity, the diffeomorphism group for general relativity, the invariant interval, the causal structure, the geodesic structure, and the curvature.

## The Hole Argument and Relationalism

The hole argument (Earman and Norton, 1987) revitalized relationalism in the context of general relativity. Consider a spacetime model (M, g), where M is a smooth four-dimensional manifold and g is a metric field. A diffeomorphism φ: M → M maps each manifold point p to another point φ(p). The "dragged-along" metric g* takes the same value at φ(p) that g takes at p. The models (M, g) and (M, g*) are observationally equivalent: all relational/structural facts are the same. But if manifold points are real individuals with intrinsic identities, the two models represent different physical situations — the metric field is at different absolute locations in the manifold.

Now introduce a "hole" — a region containing no matter. Inside the hole, the Einstein field equations impose no constraint on the metric. A diffeomorphism that acts as the identity outside the hole but rearranges the metric inside generates a different solution that the field equations, given only the data outside the hole, cannot select. This is radical indeterminism — unacceptable for a deterministic theory. Earman and Norton argue that this shows manifold substantivalism is untenable.

The relationalist conclusion: the hole argument shows we should not be committed to individual manifold points as real entities. What is real is the relational/metrical structure — the facts about what metric values are at what structural positions — not the identity of which manifold points carry those values.

## Sophisticated Substantivalism and Structural Realism

The responses to the hole argument fall into two camps. Sophisticated substantivalism (Brighouse, Saunders, Pooley) accepts Leibniz equivalence — diffeomorphically related models represent the same physical situation — while maintaining that spacetime points are real. The key move: spacetime points have no intrinsic identities ("haecceities"). Their identities are constituted entirely by their structural/metrical properties. Points differ from each other only in their structural properties (different metric values, different curvature), not in any "bare" or "haecceitistic" way.

Structural spacetime realism (Ladyman, French, Esfeld) pushes further: what is real about spacetime is its structure — the symmetries, invariants, and relational properties encoded in the metric — without commitment to an underlying substance or to purely relational facts about events. The metric field itself is the fundamental physical entity. There are no spacetime points with intrinsic identities; there are only the structural relations encoded in the metric. This represents the most sophisticated contemporary form of the Leibnizian insight that space is fundamentally relational, while acknowledging the reality and objectivity of spacetime structure that physics has revealed.

Notice how far the contemporary debate has moved from Leibniz's original concern. The question is no longer simply whether space is a substance or an order of material things; it is whether the mathematical structure of spacetime — the manifold, the metric, the symmetry group — involves genuine individual entities at its base, or whether only the structural relations are real. The Leibniz-Newton dispute turns out to be the seed of a much more complex family of questions.
