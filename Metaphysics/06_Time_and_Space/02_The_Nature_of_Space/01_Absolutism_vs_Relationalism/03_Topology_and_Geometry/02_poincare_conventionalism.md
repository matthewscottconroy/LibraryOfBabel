# Poincaré's Conventionalism About Geometry

Suppose we measure the angles of a large triangle — using light rays as the sides, as in a geodesic measurement — and find they sum to less than 180°. The natural conclusion is that space has negative curvature, a hyperbolic geometry. Poincaré, in *Science and Hypothesis* (1902), challenges this inference.

We could, he argues, equally conclude that space is Euclidean but our measuring rods are contracted by a "universal force" — a force that distorts all physical objects equally, including our measuring instruments, in a way that mimics the effects of negative curvature. Both descriptions are empirically equivalent: they predict exactly the same measurement outcomes. The choice between them is conventional — a matter of which description we prefer, not a matter of fact that the evidence can decide.

This is Poincaré's conventionalism: the choice of a geometric framework for physics is conventional, not factual. We can always choose to describe the physical world using any geometry, provided we are willing to adjust our physical laws accordingly. Given any evidence that seems to indicate non-Euclidean space, the conventionalist can reintroduce Euclidean geometry by positing compensating deformations in all measuring instruments — the "universal force."

## The Conventionalist Thesis Stated Precisely

For any pair (G, P) — a geometry G plus a set of physical laws P — that correctly predicts all empirical observations, there exists an alternative pair (G*, P*) with G* ≠ G that also correctly predicts all the same observations. Geometry and physics are sufficiently entangled that any geometric claim can be maintained against empirical evidence by adjusting the accompanying physics.

The key device is the "universal force": a force that affects all physical objects (including measuring rods) equally and in proportion to their size, which thus cannot be directly detected by comparing objects of different sizes. If space appears non-Euclidean, the conventionalist attributes this to a universal force distorting all measuring rods rather than to genuine curvature.

Reichenbach objected: the universal force is not a real force. It does no explanatory work beyond the role of re-describing the non-Euclidean geometry. This is a purely linguistic maneuver, not a genuine physical alternative. Reichenbach argued we should choose the geometry that minimizes the positing of undetectable universal forces — which favors the non-Euclidean interpretation of our actual measurements.

## Einstein's Practical Refutation

Einstein's general relativity can be seen as a practical refutation of Poincaré's conventionalism. GR embeds the geometry of spacetime within a broader theoretical framework that connects geometry to matter-energy in a determinate way through the field equations G_μν = 8πG/c⁴ T_μν. This connection provides constraints on the geometry that are not present in Poincaré's scenario.

Three considerations favor the non-Euclidean interpretation over the Euclidean-with-compensating-forces alternative. Simplicity: the non-Euclidean geometry of GR is embedded in the simplest possible theoretical framework; the alternative (Euclidean geometry plus compensating forces) requires a much more complex set of laws to reproduce all the geometric effects. Predictive success: GR makes specific, quantitative predictions — the precession of Mercury's perihelion, gravitational lensing, gravitational waves — confirmed with extraordinary precision. The Euclidean-with-universal-forces alternative must produce exactly the same predictions by finely tuning the compensating forces, which is theoretically unattractive. Unified description: in GR, gravity *is* geometry; the same framework describes both the geometry of spacetime and gravitational phenomena. In the conventionalist alternative, there are two separate things — the Euclidean geometry and the compensating forces — where GR provides a unified account.

These considerations from simplicity, predictive power, and theoretical unification favor the non-Euclidean interpretation even if the two are strictly empirically equivalent.

## The Legacy of Conventionalism

Poincaré's conventionalism had enormous influence on the philosophy of science. The logical positivists (Carnap, Schlick, Reichenbach) took the conventionality of geometry to imply that geometric claims are analytic — true by stipulation rather than by fact. Reichenbach's *The Philosophy of Space and Time* (1928) developed a nuanced position: while the global geometry of space is conventional (different geometries can be adopted by adjusting force laws), some geometric claims are "coordinative definitions" — not empirical but specifying how physical standards (rigid rods, light rays) are to be used to measure the geometry.

Putnam and Grünbaum debated the conventionality of metrical geometry in the 1960s and 70s, with Grünbaum arguing that geometry is intrinsically conventional and Putnam arguing that it is not. This debate introduced the concept of "intrinsic metric" — whether the geometry of space has a determinate structure independent of how it is measured.

Contemporary philosophy of physics has largely moved away from conventionalism toward realism about spacetime structure. The success of general relativity in making precise, testable predictions about geometric phenomena makes it natural to take the curved spacetime geometry as a real, mind-independent feature of the world. But the philosophical insights of conventionalism — particularly the underdetermination of theory by evidence and the role of simplicity in theory choice — remain important methodological contributions.
