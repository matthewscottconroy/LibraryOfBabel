# Holes, Points, and Spacetime Topology

Does spacetime consist of points? The question sounds technical, but it connects directly to the debate about substantivalism and relationalism, and it raises fundamental questions about the identity of physical entities. Points are dimensionless mathematical idealizations; they have no size, no extent, no structure. If spacetime points are the basic constituents of spacetime — the "atoms" of the manifold — then spacetime is built up from these structureless individuals. This is the default view in the mathematics of general relativity. But it generates a serious philosophical problem.

The hole argument (Earman and Norton, 1987) targets precisely this view. Consider a spacetime model (M, g), where M is a smooth four-dimensional manifold and g is a metric field defined on M. A diffeomorphism φ: M → M is a smooth, invertible mapping of the manifold to itself. Define the "dragged-along" metric g* such that g*(φ(p)) = g(p): g* takes at φ(p) the same value that g takes at p. The models (M, g) and (M, g*) are observationally equivalent — all relational/structural facts are the same, all curvature is the same at the same structural locations.

Now introduce a "hole" — a region of M containing no matter. Inside the hole, the field equations impose no constraint (T_μν = 0). Apply a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity outside the hole but rearranges the metric values across manifold points inside. The original model (M, g) and the dragged model (M, g*) are both solutions to the field equations. They agree on all observable quantities. But if manifold points are real individuals with intrinsic identities — "haecceities" — then the two models represent different physical situations: the metric occupies different absolute locations in the manifold. Since the field equations, given only the data outside the hole, cannot determine which is actual, the theory is radically indeterministic. This is the hole argument.

## The Ontological Stakes

Three responses have been developed. **Manifold substantivalism** holds that spacetime consists of the manifold M plus the metric g defined on it, and that the points of M are real individual entities with intrinsic identities. On this view, the hole argument generates genuine indeterminism — the metric can be "dragged" across the manifold in the hole without detection. Most philosophers of physics find this unacceptable.

**Sophisticated substantivalism** (Brighouse, Saunders, Pooley) accepts that spacetime points are real but have no intrinsic identities. Their identities are constituted entirely by their structural/metrical properties — the properties they bear in virtue of being embedded in the larger spacetime structure. Points differ from each other only in their structural properties (different metric values, different curvature), not in any "bare" haecceitistic way. On this view, (M, g) and (M, g*) represent the same physical situation because the manifold points in both models have the same structural/metrical properties at the same structural positions. The hole argument is dissolved: there is no "dragging" of the metric across intrinsically characterized points, because the points have no identities beyond their structural roles.

**Relationalism** (Rovelli, Pooley) goes further: there are no spacetime points independently of the fields defined on them. "Spacetime" is constituted by the relational structure among events or field values. The metric is not a field "on" a manifold but rather the relational structure of the events themselves. The hole argument dissolves for a different reason: there are no individual spacetime points to drag the metric across.

## Spacetime Topology

The topology of spacetime is a global property — it concerns the overall "shape" of spacetime rather than its local metric structure. Locally, spacetime may look like flat Minkowski space, but globally it may have non-trivial topological features.

In a simply connected spacetime, every closed loop can be continuously contracted to a point. In a multiply connected (non-simply connected) spacetime, some loops cannot be contracted — like a path going through a "handle" or "tube." Wormhole spacetimes are solutions to Einstein's field equations that are multiply connected: a closed loop going through the wormhole cannot be contracted to a point. Some approaches to quantum gravity suggest that spacetime topology is not fixed but fluctuates at the Planck scale — "spacetime foam" (Wheeler's term) — with microscopic holes, handles, and other topological features constantly appearing and disappearing.

## Point-Free Topology and Whitehead

Alfred North Whitehead, in *Process and Reality* (1929) and *The Concept of Nature* (1920), challenged the primacy of points in spatial ontology. Rather than taking dimensionless points as the basic elements and building extended regions from them, Whitehead takes extended regions as primitive and derives points as limiting ideals.

On Whitehead's view, an ideal point is not a real entity but a mathematical limit approached by increasingly small regions. What is physically real is extended — events, processes, and regions, not geometric ideals. Points should be understood as useful mathematical fictions derived from the more fundamental structure of extended reality.

This is a form of mereological approach to spatial ontology: the basic spatial entities are wholes (regions), and their mereological relations (part-of, overlapping) constitute spatial structure. It represents a genuinely alternative metaphysical picture that challenges the standard view of points as the fundamental spatial entities and connects to contemporary formal ontology (Casati and Varzi, *Parts and Places*, 1999) and to computer science, where point-free topologies are used in formal reasoning about space and time.

The hole argument and the debate about points thus illustrate how questions that appear to be purely mathematical — what is the domain over which the metric field is defined? — connect to the deepest questions in the metaphysics of spacetime.
