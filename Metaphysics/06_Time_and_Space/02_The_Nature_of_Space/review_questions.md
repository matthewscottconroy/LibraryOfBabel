# Review Questions — The Nature of Space

1. Newton's bucket argument moves from the observable concavity of rotating water to the existence of absolute space via inference to the best explanation. Mach rejects the inference, arguing that the relevant standard of rotation is the fixed stars rather than an unobservable spatial container. What prediction does Mach's account make that Newton's does not, and why can that prediction not be tested directly? Does the untestability weaken Mach's position, or does Newton's commitment to an undetectable absolute position weaken his?

2. Newton acknowledges that absolute position and absolute velocity are empirically inaccessible — only relative positions and absolute accelerations are observable. This means that absolute position makes no observable difference. Leibniz treats this as decisive evidence against absolute space via the PSR and PII. Clarke replies that God's will provides the sufficient reason and that purely positional differences are genuine qualitative differences. Which response is more satisfying, and why does the dispute persist in modern form as the debate over gauge redundancy in physics?

3. Poincaré argues that the choice of geometry for physical space is conventional: any evidence for non-Euclidean space can always be redescribed as Euclidean space plus a universal distorting force. Reichenbach objects that the universal force is an idle posit that does no explanatory work beyond the non-Euclidean description. Einstein's general relativity is sometimes said to refute conventionalism in practice. Does GR actually settle the question of whether geometry is conventional, or does Poincaré's point survive even in the relativistic context?

4. Leibniz grounds spatial relations ultimately in the "confused perceptions" of monads, making his relationalism a consequence of a particular metaphysics of substance. Contemporary relationalists reject this idealist foundation but retain the negative thesis that space is not an independently existing substance. Assess whether the negative thesis can stand without the positive metaphysical picture Leibniz used to support it, or whether modern relationalism needs a different positive account of what spatial relations are grounded in.

5. The hole argument shows that manifold substantivalism — treating spacetime points as real individuals with intrinsic identities — generates radical indeterminism: the field equations cannot select between observationally equivalent models that differ only in which point carries which metric value. Sophisticated substantivalism responds by denying that points have intrinsic identities, while relationalism denies that there are manifold points at all. What is the residual difference between these two responses, and does either of them genuinely preserve what was philosophically attractive about substantivalism?

6. The detection of gravitational waves by LIGO in 2015 is offered as evidence for metric field substantivalism: a field that carries energy and momentum in a near-vacuum is a real physical entity, not merely a relational structure among material events. Assess this argument. Does the energy and momentum carried by gravitational waves require a substantivalist ontology of the metric field, or can a sophisticated relationist accommodate this while denying that the metric is a substance in any pre-relativistic sense?

7. Mach's Principle takes at least three distinct forms — weak, strong, and relational — and general relativity satisfies only the weak form. Vacuum solutions to Einstein's field equations, such as Minkowski spacetime and the Gödel universe, have well-defined inertial structure without any matter distribution. What does this show about the limits of the Leibnizian insight that space is constituted by relations among material things? And does it vindicate Newton, vindicate neither Newton nor Leibniz, or point toward a third position?

8. Whitehead proposed that spatially extended regions, not dimensionless points, are the fundamental spatial entities — points being mathematical limits derived from regions rather than primitives. Compare this with the question raised by the hole argument: if points have no intrinsic identities and are individuated only by their structural roles, are they any more "real" than Whitehead's limit-constructions? What is at stake in choosing between point-based and region-based spatial ontology for the broader debate about substantivalism and relationalism?
