# Spacetime

Einstein's special theory of relativity, published in 1905, transformed not just physics but the philosophy of space and time. The theory's central result — that the laws of physics are the same in all inertial reference frames, with the speed of light constant across all frames — implies that simultaneity is relative: two events that are simultaneous in one frame may be non-simultaneous in another. This is not a failure of our measuring instruments but a feature of the world: there is no frame-independent fact about which events are simultaneous.

The philosophical implication is dramatic. If there is no absolute simultaneity, there is no frame-independent present moment — no cosmic "now" that carves the world into past, present, and future for all observers. The three-dimensional world of common sense gives way to a four-dimensional spacetime manifold in which events are located by four coordinates, and what counts as "space" and what counts as "time" depends on the reference frame of the observer. Minkowski formulated this mathematically: the fundamental invariant is not spatial distance or temporal interval separately but the spacetime interval, which combines space and time in a way that is the same for all observers.

General relativity, Einstein's 1915 theory of gravitation, goes further: gravity is not a force propagating through flat spacetime but a curvature of spacetime itself, produced by mass and energy. The geometry of spacetime is dynamical, changing as matter moves and energy is exchanged. The universe has a history — a shape through time — that is described by the equations of general relativity.

Quantum gravity — the attempt to reconcile quantum mechanics and general relativity — suggests that our spacetime concepts may break down entirely at the Planck scale, where the smooth manifold of classical physics gives way to something more discrete or relational. This chapter develops the philosophical implications of special relativity, general relativity, and the quantum gravity frontier, asking what physics tells us about the deep structure of space and time.
