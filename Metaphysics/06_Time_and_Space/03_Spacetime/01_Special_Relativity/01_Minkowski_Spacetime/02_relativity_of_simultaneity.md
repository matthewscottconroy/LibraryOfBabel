# The Relativity of Simultaneity

Imagine a train moving along a track. An observer M stands at the midpoint of the train; an observer M' stands on the platform beside the track. Lightning strikes both the front (A) and rear (B) of the train. From M's perspective on the train, is this simultaneous? From M's perspective on the platform, it appears so — the light from both strikes reaches M' at the same time, and M' is equidistant from A and B in the platform frame.

But M, the observer on the train, is moving toward A and away from B. The light from A has a shorter distance to travel to reach M than the light from B. So the light from the front arrives first. The two strikes are not simultaneous in the train frame.

Here is the crucial point: both M and M' are right, according to their own frames. The events "lightning strikes A" and "lightning strikes B" are simultaneous in the platform frame but not in the train frame. This is not a trick of observation, not a limitation of our instruments, and not a mistake. It reflects a genuine feature of the structure of Minkowski spacetime: simultaneity is not an absolute relation but a frame-relative one.

## The Lorentz Transformation

The relativity of simultaneity is encoded in the Lorentz transformation. For two inertial frames S and S' (where S' moves at velocity v relative to S along the x-axis):

t' = γ(t - vx/c²)

x' = γ(x - vt)

where γ = 1/√(1 - v²/c²) is the Lorentz factor.

The crucial feature is the term -vx/c² in the transformation of time. Two events at the same time in S (t₁ = t₂) will have different times in S' if they are at different spatial positions (x₁ ≠ x₂):

t'₂ - t'₁ = γ·v(x₁ - x₂)/c²

This is non-zero unless v = 0 (the frames are the same) or x₁ = x₂ (the events are at the same spatial location). For spatially separated events, simultaneity is frame-relative.

## The Rietdijk-Putnam Argument

The argument from special relativity against presentism was formulated most sharply by Rietdijk (1966) and Putnam (1967). Consider Alice (A) and Bob (B) at the same spatial location at the same time. Alice is at rest; Bob is moving rapidly away. For Alice, events on her simultaneity surface are present — they co-exist with her. For Bob, who is moving, his simultaneity surface is tilted relative to Alice's (due to the Lorentz transformation). An event C, far away, that is on Bob's simultaneity surface but in Alice's future is real for Bob (it is present for him) but not yet real for Alice (it is future for her). If we accept that everything real for Bob is real, then C is real. By analogous reasoning applied to observers in all other frames, all events in the "elsewhere" of Alice's current moment are real.

The conclusion: events that Alice considers future are already real — the block universe obtains.

This argument has been extensively discussed. The crucial premise is the move from "C is present for some observer" to "C is real." Presentists deny that "present for some observer" is sufficient for existence. The argument assumes that if two things co-exist for any observer, then they both exist simpliciter — but the presentist can reject this principle, holding that existence is not frame-relative but is grounded in an absolute simultaneity relation that physics does not directly reveal.

## The Conventionality of Simultaneity

Even within a given inertial frame, the assignment of simultaneity to distant events involves a conventional element. To determine whether two spatially separated events are simultaneous, we need a method of synchronizing clocks at different locations. Einstein's synchronization convention uses light signals: send a light pulse from A to B; when it is reflected back to A, assign to the event of the reflection at B the time midway between the emission and reception at A.

Reichenbach noted that this convention assumes the speed of light is the same in all directions. One could use a different convention — ε-synchronization, where ε ≠ 1/2 represents a different assignment of the reflection time — and get a different simultaneity relation but the same observational predictions (since the observable is the round-trip time, not the one-way time). The conventional character of simultaneity suggests that questions about which events are "really simultaneous" may not have determinate answers beyond what is fixed by convention.

## Implications for the Philosophy of Time

The relativity of simultaneity bears directly on every main position in the philosophy of time. Presentism is directly threatened: if what exists is what is present, and presentness is simultaneity-with-now, then what exists is frame-relative. Eternalism is naturally accommodated: the block universe contains all events at all spacetime locations; there is no privileged present to be made relative. The growing block and moving spotlight are similarly challenged: their global "present edge" or "spotlight position" must be frame-independent, but the relativity of simultaneity undermines any frame-independent global simultaneity surface.

The relativity of simultaneity is thus the central physical challenge for any A-theoretic position that requires an objective, universal present. It does not decisively refute such positions — the responses developed by neo-Newtonian presentists and others show that — but it raises the cost of accepting them considerably, requiring a departure from the picture of spacetime that physics most naturally supports.
