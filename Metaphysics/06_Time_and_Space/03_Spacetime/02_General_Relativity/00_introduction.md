# General Relativity and the Geometry of Spacetime

If special relativity transformed our understanding of space and time, general relativity transformed our understanding of spacetime itself. Einstein's general theory identifies gravity not as a force acting across space but as the curvature of spacetime caused by the distribution of mass and energy. The geometry of spacetime is not fixed and flat, as in special relativity, but dynamic — it changes as matter moves and energy is redistributed. Massive objects warp spacetime around them; other objects move through this warped geometry along geodesics — the curved "straight lines" of curved spacetime — and this motion is what we experience as gravitational attraction.

The philosophical implications of general relativity for the metaphysics of space are profound. The old debate between Newton's absolute space and Leibniz's relational space is complicated, though not resolved, by general relativity. In general relativity, spacetime is a genuine physical entity — it has states, carries energy, and participates in causal interactions. This seems to favor absolutism: spacetime is a "thing" over and above the objects in it. But spacetime in general relativity is not the fixed, inert container of Newton; it is dynamically determined by the distribution of matter. This seems to favor relationalism: the geometry of spacetime is not independent of the matter it contains.

The "hole argument" — revived by Earman and Norton — generates a puzzle specific to general relativity. Because the equations of general relativity are diffeomorphism-invariant — the same physical situations can be described using different coordinate systems — there seem to be mathematical representations of distinct physical situations that differ only in which spacetime points are assigned which physical properties. This raises the question of whether spacetime points are genuine physical individuals (substantivalism) or mere mathematical artifacts of our description (relationalism). The debate has generated a rich literature on the ontological status of spacetime and the interpretation of gauge symmetries.

This section examines the metaphysics of general relativistic spacetime, the hole argument, and the implications for the absolutism-relationalism debate.
