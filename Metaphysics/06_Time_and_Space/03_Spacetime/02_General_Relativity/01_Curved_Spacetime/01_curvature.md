# Spacetime Curvature

Newton's mechanics treats gravity as a force: massive objects attract each other across otherwise flat space. Einstein's general relativity replaces this picture entirely. Gravity is not a force acting between massive objects through an empty arena; it is the curvature of spacetime itself. What we call "gravitational attraction" is the result of material objects following the straightest possible paths — geodesics — through a curved spacetime.

Consider a freely falling object. In Newton's picture, it is being pulled by the gravitational force of the Earth; it is accelerating. In Einstein's picture, it is in inertial motion, following its natural geodesic through the curved spacetime near the Earth. What is accelerating, on Einstein's account, is the person standing on the ground — the ground is pushing that person away from the geodesic they would naturally follow. The felt "force" of gravity is the experience of being prevented from following a geodesic.

This is the philosophical significance of spacetime curvature: gravity ceases to be a force in the Newtonian sense and becomes a consequence of geometry. A stone "falling" toward the Earth is following the straightest possible path through a spacetime curved by the Earth's mass.

## Geodesics and the Geometry of Gravity

In flat Euclidean space, the geodesic between two points is the straight line — the path of minimum distance. In Minkowski spacetime, the geodesic is the straight worldline — the path that maximizes the proper time between two events (the path followed by a free, force-free particle). In curved spacetime, geodesics are the "straightest possible paths," defined by the geodesic equation:

d²xμ/dτ² + Γμνσ (dxν/dτ)(dxσ/dτ) = 0

where Γμνσ are the Christoffel symbols, encoding the curvature of the spacetime at each point. A freely falling body follows this equation; it is not accelerated in the GR sense but simply follows the straightest possible path in a curved spacetime.

Curvature in Riemannian geometry is measured by the curvature tensor, which encodes how the geometry deviates from flatness at each point. In flat (Euclidean or Minkowski) geometry, the angles of a triangle sum to 180°. In positively curved geometry (like the surface of a sphere), the angles sum to more than 180°. In negatively curved geometry (hyperbolic), they sum to less than 180°.

## The Equivalence Principle

Einstein's development of general relativity was guided by the equivalence principle: the local effects of gravity are indistinguishable from the effects of acceleration. A person in a small enclosed room cannot tell by local experiments whether they are in a uniform gravitational field (on the Earth's surface) or accelerating at the same rate in empty space.

This has a striking implication: there is no absolute distinction between "gravitational" and "inertial" effects at the local level. What appears as a gravitational force in one frame (someone standing on the Earth's surface) appears as an inertial force (resistance to acceleration) in another frame (a freely falling elevator). The freely falling body experiences no local effects of gravity; its spacetime neighborhood is locally flat.

## The Einstein Field Equations

Einstein's field equations are the heart of general relativity:

G_μν = (8πG/c⁴) T_μν

The left side (G_μν, the Einstein tensor) encodes how spacetime is curved at each point. The right side (T_μν, the stress-energy tensor) encodes the distribution of mass, energy, momentum, and stress. The equations say: the curvature of spacetime at each point is proportional to the matter-energy density at that point. Where matter is present, spacetime is curved; where there is no matter, the equations admit a range of solutions including flat Minkowski spacetime and gravitational waves.

Several important exact solutions are known: the Schwarzschild solution (the metric outside a spherically symmetric mass, predicting the precession of Mercury's perihelion and gravitational lensing); the Kerr solution (outside a rotating mass, predicting frame-dragging, confirmed by Gravity Probe B in 2011); the FLRW metric (a homogeneous, isotropic universe, describing the expanding universe); and gravitational wave solutions (perturbations propagating at the speed of light, directly detected by LIGO in 2015).

## Philosophical Implications

In GR, spacetime structure is not independent of matter — as in Newton's absolute space — but is dynamically determined by it. Yet the metric field has its own degrees of freedom (gravitational waves, vacuum solutions) that are not purely derivative from material relations. This puts GR in an intermediate position: spacetime structure is dynamically coupled to matter but is not a purely relational order of matter. The nature of inertia is also revised: in GR, inertia is encoded in the geodesic structure of spacetime. A freely falling body is inertial; a body on the surface of the Earth is non-inertial. This is a radical revision of the Newtonian understanding, in which inertia is resistance to change from a state of uniform motion in absolute space.
