# The Hole Argument

Einstein discovered the hole argument himself in 1913, during the process of constructing general relativity. He initially believed it showed that generally covariant field equations were physically impossible — that any theory of gravity must break general covariance in order to have determinate, non-arbitrary predictions. He temporarily adopted the "Entwurf" theory as a compromise. Only in late 1915, after he recognized the flaw in his argument — which amounts to confusing active diffeomorphisms with passive coordinate changes — did he accept generally covariant equations: the final form of the Einstein field equations.

The philosophical analysis by Earman and Norton in their 1987 paper ("What Price Spacetime Substantivalism? The Hole Story," *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science*) revived the argument as a philosophical puzzle about the interpretation of GR rather than a physical objection to covariance. Their analysis showed that the argument poses a genuine metaphysical challenge for manifold substantivalism — the view that the points of the spacetime manifold are real individual entities with intrinsic identities.

## The Argument in Detail

We need to distinguish between two kinds of diffeomorphism. A passive diffeomorphism is a coordinate change — a different labeling of the same physical spacetime. This is merely a change of description. An active diffeomorphism is a genuine mapping of the manifold to itself, sending point p to φ(p). This is a change of physical situation *if* manifold points have intrinsic identities — it moves the metric field from one point to another.

The hole argument involves active diffeomorphisms. Let (M, g, T) be a model of GR, where M is the manifold, g is the metric, and T is the matter distribution. Let φ be a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside a "hole" region H but differs from the identity inside H. Define the "dragged" model (M, φ*g, φ*T), where φ*g and φ*T are the dragged-along metric and matter fields. By the diffeomorphism invariance of GR, (M, φ*g, φ*T) is also a solution to the field equations. Inside the hole (where T = 0), φ*T = 0 = T, so the matter distribution is the same in both models. But φ*g ≠ g inside H: the metric is dragged to different manifold points.

If manifold points have intrinsic identities, (M, g) and (M, φ*g) represent different physical situations — the metric occupies different absolute locations in the manifold. But the field equations, given only the data outside H, cannot determine which is actual. This is the indeterminism: even given the complete physical state of the universe outside the hole, the state inside is underdetermined.

## Responses: Leibniz Equivalence

The most straightforward response is to accept "Leibniz equivalence": diffeomorphically related models of GR represent the same physical situation. Just as Leibniz held that a universe shifted in absolute space represents the same physical situation as the original, (M, g) and (M, φ*g) represent the same physical situation. They are observationally and qualitatively identical, differing only in the absolute locations of metric values.

Leibniz equivalence is the standard response among physicists, who routinely treat diffeomorphically related models as representing the same spacetime. The philosophical challenge is to explain *why* diffeomorphically related models represent the same situation — which requires either denying that manifold points have intrinsic identities (sophisticated substantivalism or relationalism) or embracing a form of anti-essentialism about the properties of spacetime points.

## Sophisticated Substantivalism

Sophisticated substantivalism (Brighouse, Saunders, Pooley) accepts Leibniz equivalence while maintaining that spacetime points are real. Spacetime points have no intrinsic identities — their identities are constituted entirely by their structural/relational properties (their position in the metric structure, their causal relations to other points). On this view, (M, g) and (M, φ*g) represent the same physical situation because the manifold points in both models have the same structural/metrical properties at the same structural positions. The points in the "dragged" model are not "different points at different positions" but the same points described differently — with the metric redistributed in a way that preserves all structural properties.

This is a form of structuralism about spacetime points: points are not "bare particulars" with haecceities but are individuated by their structural properties alone. The identity of a spacetime point is not something over and above its structural role in the spacetime.

## Connections Across Metaphysics

The hole argument connects to broader metaphysical debates about individuality and identity. The distinction between manifold substantivalism (with haecceities) and sophisticated substantivalism (without haecceities) corresponds to the distinction between Strawsonian "particulars" (individuals with intrinsic identities) and structuralist "positions" (individuals constituted by their relational roles).

Notice also a connection to the presentism debate. The hole argument shows that the identity of spacetime points over time — in a region devoid of matter — is underdetermined by the physics. If points have no intrinsic identities (sophisticated substantivalism), there is no fact about which point in the current spacetime is identical to which point in the past. The persistence of spacetime points through time — and thus the persistence of past events in the growing block — requires the haecceities that sophisticated substantivalism denies. This suggests a subtle tension between the sophisticated substantivalist response to the hole argument and the growing block's account of temporal ontology: a tension that, to our knowledge, remains unexplored in the literature.
