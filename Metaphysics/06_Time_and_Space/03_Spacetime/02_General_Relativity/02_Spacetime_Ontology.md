# Spacetime Ontology

The Einstein field equations give a precise mathematical description of spacetime: the manifold of events, the metric that encodes the geometry, the curvature that couples to matter-energy. But what do these mathematical objects represent? Is there a spacetime substance out there in the world — a real physical entity as concrete as a rock or a gravitational wave — or is the formalism a convenient bookkeeping device for relational facts among material events? This is the question of spacetime ontology, and it is not settled by the physics alone.

At the minimum, we need to ask whether the points of the manifold are real individuals with intrinsic identities, or whether they are structural positions individuated entirely by their relational properties. The hole argument — which Einstein himself discovered in 1913, before he had the correct field equations, and which troubled him for two years — shows that if manifold points have intrinsic identities (haecceities), then GR is radically indeterministic: diffeomorphically related models represent different physical situations, but the field equations cannot distinguish between them. This result drove Einstein to accept what is now called "Leibniz equivalence": diffeomorphically related models represent the same physical situation. But accepting Leibniz equivalence requires a philosophical account of why — which demands either denying that manifold points have intrinsic identities or revising our account of what physical determinism requires.

The debate has settled into three main camps. Manifold substantivalism, with intrinsic point identities, faces the hole argument. Metric field substantivalism (Hoefer) holds that the metric field itself is the spacetime substance, with no underlying manifold of bare points. Sophisticated substantivalism (Brighouse, Saunders, Pooley) accepts real spacetime points but holds that their identities are constituted by their structural-metrical properties, dissolving the hole argument. Spacetime relationalism (Rovelli) denies that there are spacetime points independently of the physical fields defined on them.

Cutting across these positions is the question of what "dynamical" means for spacetime — whether the dynamically evolving metric of GR is better understood as a substance with its own degrees of freedom or as an expression of the relational structure of physical events. And beyond GR lies the question of quantum gravity: what spacetime ontology is compatible with a quantum theory of the metric? The files in this section address spacetime ontology directly, examine the dynamical character of the GR metric, and analyze background independence as a constraint on any adequate quantum gravity.
