# What Is Spacetime?

The question "what is spacetime?" has a deceptively clear mathematical answer: spacetime is a pseudo-Riemannian manifold (M, g), where M is a smooth four-dimensional manifold and g is a pseudo-Riemannian metric. This is a precise, well-defined mathematical object. But the philosophical question is different: what does this mathematical object *represent*? Is there a spacetime substance out there in the world — a real physical entity as concrete as a rock or a field — or is the formalism just a convenient bookkeeping device for relational facts among material events?

The question connects directly to the debates about substantivalism and relationalism we have already seen. The substantivalist holds that spacetime is a real physical entity: the manifold or metric field is as real as any other physical object, capable of interacting with matter and carrying its own degrees of freedom. The relationalist denies that there is anything over and above the physical fields and their relations. Structural spacetime realism offers a middle ground: what is real about spacetime is its mathematical structure — the symmetries, invariants, and relational properties encoded in the metric — without commitment to an underlying substance or to purely relational facts about events.

## Three Levels of the Question

We can usefully distinguish three levels at which the question "what is spacetime?" arises.

At the mathematical level, spacetime is described by a pseudo-Riemannian manifold (M, g). This is a precise and well-defined mathematical object. The question is what this mathematical object represents — whether it corresponds to a genuine physical entity or is a useful calculational tool.

At the representational level, we ask how different parts of the formalism correspond to physical facts. Do the points of M represent genuine physical entities? Does the metric g represent a field (a property of points) or is it itself the fundamental entity? Is the distinction between M and g genuinely meaningful, or is it a mathematical artifact?

At the ontological level, we ask what genuinely exists. Is there a spacetime substance? Are there spacetime points? Are all physical facts about spacetime reducible to facts about material events and their relations, or is there irreducible spacetime structure? These three levels are related but distinct. One can have a precise mathematical description and a settled representational scheme while remaining uncertain about the deep ontological questions.

## Spacetime and Causation

An important connection between spacetime and causation concerns the role of the metric in defining causal structure. In Minkowski spacetime, the causal structure (which events can influence which) is defined by the light cone structure — itself determined by the metric. In curved spacetime, the causal structure is also determined by the metric.

This creates a bidirectional relationship. If causation is taken as primitive, one might try to derive spacetime structure from causal facts — building the spacetime from the causal ordering of events. This is the approach of causal set theory, a program in quantum gravity that takes the causal relations among discrete events as fundamental and tries to recover the smooth spacetime geometry as an emergent description. Conversely, if spacetime structure is taken as primitive, causation might be derived from it: causation is just the relation of being timelike or lightlike separated. The relationship between spacetime and causation is bidirectional, with each having been used as the foundation for the other in different metaphysical programs.

## The Question's Implications for Physics

The philosophical question of what spacetime *is* has direct bearing on the research programs of theoretical physics. If spacetime is a substantive entity (the metric field), then quantum gravity requires quantizing that entity — treating the metric as a quantum field subject to the principles of quantum mechanics. This is the approach of loop quantum gravity: it quantizes the geometry of spacetime directly.

If spacetime is relational — constituted by the structure of material events and fields — then quantum gravity might not require "quantizing spacetime" at all. Instead, spacetime structure would emerge from the quantum mechanics of the underlying material fields. If spacetime is structural — neither a substance nor a purely relational order but an abstract mathematical structure — then quantum gravity requires identifying the more fundamental mathematical structure from which spacetime emerges as an approximation. This connects to approaches based on algebraic quantum field theory, topological quantum field theory, or string theory.

The philosophical question "what is spacetime?" is not a merely verbal dispute about how to describe a given physical situation. It has genuine consequences for which approach to quantum gravity is most physically motivated, and thus for the future of fundamental physics.
