# Emergent Spacetime

Here is a striking result from Ted Jacobson (1995). Take any spacetime event. Near that event, there is a "local Rindler horizon" — a surface defined by uniformly accelerated observers, analogous to a black hole event horizon. By the Unruh effect, quantum field theory in curved spacetime tells us that these accelerated observers detect a thermal bath of radiation. Apply the first law of thermodynamics to this radiation — dE = T dS — using the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy formula for the horizon area. The result, after the mathematics is unpacked, is the Einstein field equations. Gravity falls out of thermodynamics.

What this means is hard to overstate. If the equations governing the curvature of spacetime — the equations that tell matter how to move and matter how to curve space — can be derived from thermodynamic principles alone, then those equations may not be the fundamental equations of a fundamental force. They may be equations of state, analogous to PV = nRT for an ideal gas. Just as the ideal gas law is not a fundamental equation of motion for gas molecules but an emergent thermodynamic law arising from molecular statistics, the Einstein field equations might be an emergent thermodynamic law arising from the statistical mechanics of some more fundamental degrees of freedom we have not yet identified. Jacobson's result is one of several converging lines of argument suggesting that spacetime is not fundamental — that it emerges from a deeper, non-spatiotemporal structure.

## AdS/CFT and the Emergent Bulk

The most precisely developed case for emergent spacetime comes from the Anti-de Sitter/Conformal Field Theory correspondence, proposed by Juan Maldacena in 1997. The correspondence asserts a duality between two theories that look nothing like each other: a theory of quantum gravity in a (d+1)-dimensional anti-de Sitter spacetime (a spacetime with negative cosmological constant), and a conformal quantum field theory — an ordinary, non-gravitational quantum field theory — on the d-dimensional "boundary" of that spacetime. The two theories are not merely related by approximation or limiting procedure; they are physically equivalent. Any computation in the bulk gravitational theory can be performed in the boundary field theory, and vice versa.

The philosophical implication is direct. The (d+1)-dimensional bulk spacetime, with all its gravitational dynamics and geometric structure, is entirely encoded in the d-dimensional boundary quantum field theory, which contains no gravity and one fewer dimension. The extra dimension of the bulk corresponds, in the boundary description, to the energy scale of the renormalization group flow — a non-geometric quantity. The geometry of bulk spacetime emerges from the quantum state of the boundary. It is not a fundamental structure in its own right but is reconstructed from, or constituted by, the entanglement structure of the boundary degrees of freedom.

Recent work on "holographic entanglement entropy" (Ryu-Takayanagi, 2006; van Raamsdonk, 2010) makes this more vivid. The area of a minimal surface in the bulk spacetime encodes the entanglement entropy of a region of the boundary theory. Conversely, the connectivity and geometry of the bulk can be generated by or destroyed by the entanglement structure of the boundary. Mark Van Raamsdonk argued that in AdS/CFT, geometric connectivity — the fact that two bulk regions are connected — corresponds to entanglement between the corresponding boundary regions. If one imagines reducing the entanglement between two boundary regions to zero, the corresponding bulk regions become disconnected: the spacetime tears. Spacetime geometry, on this picture, is built from quantum entanglement.

We can put this more sharply: what the AdS/CFT correspondence suggests is that the question "what is spacetime?" has a surprising answer at the quantum level — spacetime is a way of representing the entanglement structure of a quantum system without gravity. Distance and geometry encode quantum information. This is not yet fully understood, and AdS/CFT applies strictly to spacetimes with negative cosmological constant (unlike our universe), but as an existence proof of emergent spacetime, it is powerful.

## Two Concepts of Emergence

We need to be careful about what "emergence" claims here. Philosophers distinguish at least two types.

Weak emergence: a phenomenon is weakly emergent from a base if it is in principle derivable from the base theory, even if the derivation is practically intractable. Temperature is weakly emergent from molecular kinetics: we can in principle derive it from the statistics of molecular motion, even if we cannot do so for every gas in practice.

Strong emergence: a phenomenon is strongly emergent from a base if it is not in principle derivable from the base theory — if the emergent description involves genuinely novel concepts that cannot be reduced to those of the base description. Consciousness is often taken as the candidate for strong emergence: even in principle, it is unclear how to derive phenomenal experience from physical facts about neural processes.

For spacetime, the question is which type of emergence is at stake. In AdS/CFT, the answer seems to be weak emergence: the bulk geometry is in principle encoded in the boundary quantum state, and there are systematic procedures — "bulk reconstruction" — for recovering the bulk geometry from boundary data. The geometry is derivable; it is just highly nontrivial to do so. On this view, spacetime is a representation of quantum-informational structure that is genuine but not fundamental.

Whether the emergence could be strong is less clear. If spacetime concepts — distance, duration, causal order — cannot even be approximately defined for generic quantum gravity states, then there is a sense in which they are not derivable in any straightforward way from the fundamental description. The spacetime vocabulary may only apply in restricted regimes (near-classical states with small quantum fluctuations), and outside those regimes, the vocabulary simply fails rather than reducing to something else.

## What This Means for the Metaphysics of Space and Time

The suggestion that spacetime is emergent — that it is not a fundamental feature of reality but an approximate description valid at scales much larger than the Planck scale — puts pressure on the debates we have been conducting throughout this module. The A-theory/B-theory dispute, the question of presentism versus eternalism, the debate between substantivalism and relationalism: all of these presuppose that space and time are at least approximately the right categories in which to describe fundamental reality. If they are emergent from a non-spatiotemporal quantum structure, these debates concern an effective rather than fundamental description.

That does not make them unimportant. The effective description is the one in which we live, and questions about the structure of that description are genuine questions about what is real at the scale of human-accessible physics. The question of whether presentism or eternalism is true is still a question about the structure of classical spacetime, which is a real structure even if it is emergent. But it does shift the register of the question. "Is presentism true?" becomes "does the effective description of spacetime favor presentism, given that spacetime is itself an effective description?" — which is a different and more complex question than the straightforward ontological one.

Rovelli has pressed the most radical version of this point, arguing in *Quantum Gravity* (2004) and *Reality is Not What It Seems* (2016) that at the Planck scale, the fundamental description of reality involves no space and no time — only quantum events and their relational structure. Space and time emerge from this relational structure at coarser scales, as approximate macroscopic notions. If Rovelli is right, the Leibnizian relationalist insight — that space and time are constituted by relations among physical things rather than independently existing substances — holds at the fundamental level, but even the material things among which the relations hold are not what classical physics imagines. The relata are quantum events; the relations are quantum-mechanical; and the result, at large scales, is something that looks like classical spacetime. The question "what is spacetime?" has become, at its deepest level, a question about the quantum mechanics of relations — and that question is still wide open.
