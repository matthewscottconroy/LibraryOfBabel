# Time Travel and Paradoxes

H. G. Wells's Time Traveller sat in his machine and, setting the dial, disappeared — arriving in the far future with the same matter-of-factness that a commuter arrives at the next station. This image of time travel seems coherent on its face: we all travel forward through time at one second per second; the time machine simply allows travel at a different rate, or in a different direction. But when we press the idea philosophically, paradoxes arise that test our most basic assumptions about time, causation, and identity.

The grandfather paradox is the most famous: suppose you travel back in time and prevent your grandfather from having children. Then you were never born; if you were never born, you cannot travel back in time; if you cannot travel back in time, your grandfather survives; if your grandfather survives, you are born; if you are born, you travel back... The contradiction seems to show that backward time travel is impossible. But the argument is more subtle than it first appears. Lewis argued that backward time travel is possible but that the time traveler is, by necessity, unable to kill the grandfather — not because of any supernatural intervention but because the past is fixed: what happened happened, and the grandfather survived. The time traveler tries and fails, perhaps by accident, perhaps by coincidence, but fails nonetheless.

The direction of time raises a different puzzle. The microscopic laws of physics are time-reversal invariant — they work equally well forward and backward. Yet we experience time as strongly directed: causes precede effects; entropy increases; we remember the past and not the future. Explaining this asymmetry in terms of the symmetric underlying physics requires either positing a special initial condition of low entropy at the beginning of the universe, or acknowledging that our asymmetric concepts emerge from a symmetric physics only under the right boundary conditions.

This chapter examines these paradoxes and their philosophical import, using time travel as a thought experiment to reveal what we are committed to about time, causation, and physical law.
