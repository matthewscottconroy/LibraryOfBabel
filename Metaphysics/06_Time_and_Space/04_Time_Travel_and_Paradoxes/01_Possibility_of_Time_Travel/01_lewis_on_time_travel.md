# Lewis on Time Travel

"What does it mean to say that time travel is possible?" This is not a question about wormholes or exotic matter or the engineering details. It is a conceptual question: is the very idea of backward time travel coherent, or does it generate a logical contradiction at the outset? David Lewis's "The Paradoxes of Time Travel" (1976) provides the definitive philosophical answer, and the answer is: the idea is coherent, the apparent paradoxes are not genuine contradictions, and the conceptual foundations are not in conflict with the block universe or with four-dimensionalism. What looks like impossibility is usually the result of conflating two different things — personal time and external time — or of misunderstanding what it would mean to "change" the past.

Lewis is not doing physics in this paper. He is not proposing a mechanism for time travel or arguing that it is physically achievable. He is doing conceptual analysis: identifying the structure of the concept, dissolving pseudoproblems, and locating the genuinely difficult questions. The paper is a model of the genre.

## Personal Time and the Structure of the Problem

The conceptual foundation of Lewis's analysis is the distinction between two kinds of time. External time is the objective temporal order of events in the spacetime manifold — the B-series ordering, the "time" in which historians write history and physicists write equations. Personal time is the time of a particular worldline — the proper time measured along a traveler's trajectory, corresponding to the aging of their body, the sequence of their memories, and the flow of their experience.

In ordinary circumstances these coincide: I age at roughly the rate that external time advances. But special relativity already tells us they can diverge. The muon created in the upper atmosphere travels for 2.2 microseconds of personal time but survives long enough, in external time, to reach the Earth's surface. Lewis's definition of time travel simply generalizes this: time travel occurs when personal time and external time diverge in the direction sense, not just in rate. The traveler's personal time advances from t₁ to t₂ (the traveler ages, forms memories, completes the journey), while their external time location moves from a later event to an earlier one.

This definition is neutral about mechanism. Lewis does not care whether the time machine is a wormhole, a CTC in the Gödel metric, or a DeLorean with a flux capacitor. The conceptual question is about the structure of the worldline, not the engineering. And the question is: is a worldline of this structure — one whose external time location decreases while personal time advances — logically coherent?

## The Block Universe and the Shape of Worldlines

The answer is straightforwardly yes, on the four-dimensionalist picture Lewis endorses. In the block universe, individuals are four-dimensional entities: their worldlines are curves through the four-dimensional manifold, parameterized by proper time. A worldline that curves back through external time is an unusually shaped curve, but it is still a curve. There is no logical barrier to a worldline connecting a point in the twenty-third century (in external time) to a point in the twentieth century, with personal time advancing in the normal direction along the curve.

The key feature of this picture is that all events in the block are fixed. The time traveler's visit to the past was always part of the past — it is encoded in the complete history of the world at the appropriate external time location. The traveler does not arrive in a mutable past that they can reshape; they arrive in the actual past, which is what it is. This is not a limitation unique to time travelers. Nothing can make Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon not have happened. The past is simply what it is, including any visits from the future that were part of it.

Here is Lewis's central conceptual point, expressed more sharply: the supposed impossibility of backward time travel usually rests on the assumption that the time traveler can "change" the past — can make it be the case that things happened differently from how they happened. But this is not what backward time travel offers. Backward time travel offers the ability to be in the past, to act there, to causally influence events there. It does not offer the ability to make the past have been different, because the past includes those very influences. The traveler's actions in the past were always part of the causal history that produced the present; they have always been in the block.

## The Consistency Constraint and Two Senses of "Can"

The most philosophically important contribution of Lewis's paper is his dissolution of the grandfather paradox using a distinction between two senses of ability.

Consider Tim, the time traveler. Tim travels back to a time before his grandfather had any children. Tim has a gun, knows how to use it, and is in the right place and time. In what sense can Tim kill his grandfather?

In one sense — call it the local sense — Tim can. He has all the relevant physical capacities, skills, and opportunities. Nothing in his immediate circumstances prevents him. A person in Tim's local situation (gun in hand, grandfather in front of him, no external interference) can certainly shoot. In this sense, Tim can kill his grandfather.

In another sense — the global or overall sense — Tim cannot. For Tim to kill his grandfather would require the complete history of the world to include that Tim killed his grandfather. But the complete history of the world includes Tim existing and making the journey, which requires his grandfather having had children. These two facts are inconsistent: Tim's grandfather cannot both have been killed before having children and have had children. The complete history rules out Tim's killing his grandfather. In this sense, Tim cannot kill his grandfather.

Lewis's resolution is that both of these are true simultaneously, in their respective senses. There is no contradiction, because they are not claims about the same thing. Tim has the local ability but lacks the global ability — just as I have the physical ability to lift my arm right now but (in some worlds) do not lift it, without any contradiction. The local ability is a fact about Tim's physical capacities; the global inability is a fact about the complete causal structure of history. The two can coexist without paradox.

What this means for the grandfather paradox: Tim will fail to kill his grandfather, but not because of any supernatural barrier, any magical force that prevents him, any special limitation on time travelers. He will fail because of the ordinary kind of reasons for which people fail to do things they are physically capable of doing — he will miss, or lose his nerve, or be distracted, or the gun will jam. Whatever the specific reason, the complete history of the world is self-consistent, and Tim's failure to kill his grandfather is just another event in that history.

## Four-Dimensionalism as the Background Commitment

It is worth making explicit the metaphysical commitment that underwrites Lewis's entire analysis. Lewis is a four-dimensionalist: persons and objects are four-dimensional spacetime worms, extended through time as well as through space. Personal time is the parameter along the worldline; it is not intrinsically different in kind from spatial extension along the worm. There is no metaphysical barrier to a worldline curving in any direction through the four-dimensional manifold, including back through external time.

On a three-dimensionalist picture — where objects are wholly present at each moment of their existence, not extended through time as worms — time travel raises different and more difficult questions. What is it for a wholly-present object to be at two different times simultaneously? How do we individuate the "same" person before and after the journey if personal identity requires a three-dimensional wholly-present individual rather than a four-dimensional worm? Lewis's framework sidesteps these questions by treating the time traveler as a four-dimensional worm with an unusual shape. The worm's earlier temporal stages are in the twenty-third century; its later personal-time stages are in the twentieth century; there is nothing puzzling about this once we accept four-dimensionalism.

Lewis is explicit about this connection: his analysis of time travel is not independent of his metaphysics but presupposes it. The four-dimensionalist framework is what makes time travel conceptually tractable. This does not mean that the framework is correct — it means that the feasibility of time travel, as a conceptual matter, is tied to the broader question of the metaphysics of persistence. Those who reject four-dimensionalism face a harder task in making sense of backward time travel; those who accept it find Lewis's analysis compelling.
