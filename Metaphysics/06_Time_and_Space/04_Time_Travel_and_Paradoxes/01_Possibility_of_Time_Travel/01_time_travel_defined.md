# Time Travel: Definitions and Varieties

Forward time travel is not science fiction. It is confirmed physics. An astronaut who travels at 0.99c for ten years of personal time returns to find that about seventy years have elapsed on Earth. The muons created in the upper atmosphere by cosmic ray collisions decay after 2.2 microseconds in their rest frame, but reach the Earth's surface because their lifetimes are dilated by their high velocity. GPS satellites must correct for relativistic time dilation — both special-relativistic slowing due to orbital speed and general-relativistic speeding due to altitude — or the system would be useless within hours. In all of these cases, personal time and external time diverge. That divergence is time travel in one direction.

The philosophically interesting question is whether the same divergence is possible in the other direction — whether a worldline can loop back through external time, depositing a traveler in their own past. This is what philosophers and physicists mean by backward time travel, and it is not obviously impossible. General relativity permits spacetime geometries containing closed timelike curves (CTCs) — worldlines that return to their starting event. Whether such spacetimes are physically realizable, and whether backward time travel raises genuine logical difficulties, are the central questions of this section.

## Personal Time, External Time, and the Varieties of Temporal Displacement

David Lewis's canonical framework ("The Paradoxes of Time Travel," 1976) begins with a conceptual distinction that clarifies the problem. Personal time is the time measured along a person's worldline — the duration of their experience, the aging of their body, the sequence of their memories and anticipations, the proper time ∫ ds/c along their trajectory. External time is the objective temporal structure of the spacetime manifold — the B-series ordering of all events. In ordinary circumstances, these advance in lockstep: one hour of personal time corresponds to one hour of external time, and the traveler moves in the same direction through both. Time travel — of any kind — is simply a mismatch between the two.

Forward time travel via special relativistic dilation is the tamest variety: personal time advances more slowly than external time, depositing the traveler in the external future. This is not merely possible but actual, confirmed by experiment, and indeed exploited in GPS engineering.

Backward time travel via CTCs is more radical. Here a worldline curves back through external time so that the end of the journey (in personal time) is earlier than the beginning in external time. A traveler on a CTC experiences normal passage of personal time — they age, form memories, undergo all the usual processes — but eventually arrive at a spacetime event that precedes their departure. General relativity provides several CTC-containing solutions. Kurt Gödel's 1949 rotating universe — a solution to Einstein's equations with rotating dust and nonzero cosmological constant — contains CTCs through every event; in the Gödel universe, any event can be connected to any other by a CTC. The Kerr metric for a rotating black hole contains CTCs inside the inner horizon. Morris and Thorne (1988) showed that traversable wormholes could in principle serve as time machines: if one mouth is accelerated to a high velocity relative to the other, special relativistic time dilation causes the two mouths to be connected across a temporal gap as well as a spatial one.

The tachyon scenario is worth mentioning separately. If faster-than-light particles existed, they would travel backward in time in some reference frames — the Lorentz transformation maps spacelike-separated events such that different frames disagree about their temporal order. Tachyons would allow information to be sent to the past, even without any person physically making the journey. But no tachyons have been observed, and their existence would violate the causal structure of Minkowski spacetime in ways that generate severe paradoxes.

## The Logic of Backward Time Travel

The logical constraint on any time travel scenario — what Lewis calls the consistency constraint — is simply that the complete history of the world must be self-consistent. A scenario is logically possible if and only if it generates no contradiction. This sounds obvious, but it has important consequences.

The natural worry about backward time travel is that a traveler in their own past can cause changes that prevent their journey from occurring — the grandfather paradox. Lewis's response is that change of this kind is not what time travel offers. What the time traveler arrives at is the past as it actually was. Their visit was always part of the causal history; it cannot be otherwise, for the same reason that nothing can make Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon not have happened. The past is fixed, and the time traveler is in it — they cannot make it be different from what it was.

This dissolves one apparent paradox. But it also raises a genuine question. The past is fixed; the time traveler arrives in it and acts. What constrains what they can do? Lewis's answer — to which we return in the next two files — involves a subtle distinction between two senses of ability. The traveler has, in a local sense, all the normal capacities a person has. But the complete history of the world already determines what the traveler does; their actions in the past are already encoded in the history that produced them. There is no contradiction between these two facts. The apparent paradox of backward time travel dissolves on careful analysis of what "possibility" and "change" mean in the context of a fixed block spacetime.

## Time Travel and Temporal Ontology

Notice that the coherence of backward time travel is more natural on some temporal ontologies than others. On the block universe picture — eternalism — the situation is clearest: all events exist in the four-dimensional manifold, and a worldline is simply a curve through that manifold. A curve that loops back through external time is an unusual shape, but there is nothing logically problematic about it. The time traveler's worldline is just a differently shaped curve in the same block.

For the presentist, backward time travel is conceptually problematic in a different way. If only the present moment exists — if past events have literally ceased to be — then there is no past for the time traveler to arrive in. The destination of the journey does not exist. Presentists have responded that what matters is that the past existed, and that is sufficient for it to be a possible destination; but whether this response is coherent depends on the details of the presentist theory of truthmakers and existence, and it is not obvious that it succeeds.

The growing block view occupies an intermediate position: the past exists (it is part of the growing block), so past events are genuine possible destinations for backward time travel. The future does not exist, so forward time travel to a not-yet-existing future is problematic on exactly the same grounds that backward time travel is problematic for the presentist. Each view has a direction of travel it can accommodate more easily than the other.

These are not merely abstract observations. They show that apparently science-fictional scenarios carry genuine weight in the debate about temporal ontology: what you think about the metaphysical status of past and future events constrains what kinds of temporal displacement you can coherently countenance.
