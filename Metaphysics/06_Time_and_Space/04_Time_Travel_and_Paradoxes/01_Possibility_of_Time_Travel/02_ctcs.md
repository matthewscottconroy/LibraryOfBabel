# Closed Timelike Curves

In 1949, Kurt Gödel published a solution to Einstein's field equations describing a universe filled with rotating dust — a cosmology that is homogeneous and isotropic but in which the matter everywhere rotates relative to the local inertial frames. The solution was exact and mathematically impeccable. Its philosophical implication was devastating, or so Gödel thought: in the Gödel universe, every event is connected to every other event by a closed timelike curve. Any observer can, in principle, travel along a future-directed timelike path and return to any event in their own past.

Gödel's use of this result was not to advertise the possibility of time travel. It was to argue for the unreality of objective time. If the laws of physics admit a cosmological solution in which there is no consistent global time function — no function that assigns a real number to each event and increases along all future-directed causal curves — then a uniform, objective passage of time cannot be a necessary feature of a physical universe. The flowing, directed time we experience cannot be read off from the geometry of spacetime; it must be something we bring to the physics, not something the physics provides. This is Gödel's philosophical argument from the Gödel metric.

## The Mathematics of Closed Timelike Curves

Let us be precise about what a closed timelike curve is. In general relativity, a spacetime is a pair (M, g) where M is a smooth four-dimensional manifold and g is a pseudo-Riemannian metric with signature (-,+,+,+). A worldline is a smooth curve γ: [a,b] → M whose tangent vector is timelike everywhere: g(γ'(s), γ'(s)) < 0 at each point. The worldline represents the trajectory of a material body. A closed timelike curve is a timelike worldline γ such that γ(a) = γ(b) — the curve returns to its starting event.

The causal significance of this is immediate. The causal future of any event p is J⁺(p): the set of all events reachable from p by a future-directed causal curve. In a spacetime with CTCs, p ∈ J⁺(p): every event is in its own causal future. Events can causally influence events in their own past. The normal asymmetric structure of cause and effect — cause precedes effect, information flows forward — breaks down.

The Gödel metric is the most philosophically interesting CTC spacetime, but not the only one. The Kerr metric, describing the geometry around a rotating black hole, contains CTCs inside the inner horizon. These are hidden inside the black hole and inaccessible from outside, but their mathematical existence in a physically realistic solution to the Einstein equations confirms that CTCs are not merely exotic curiosities. The Morris-Thorne traversable wormhole provides another route: by accelerating one wormhole mouth to a high velocity, one induces a relativistic time dilation between the two ends, so that the wormhole connects two different times as well as two different spatial locations. Entering one mouth exits at an earlier time from the other. This is a time machine in the most literal sense, constructed from ordinary GR without requiring any new physics beyond the existence of traversable wormholes — which would require exotic matter with negative energy density, a matter that remains unresolved.

A spacetime is called "chronological" if it contains no CTCs, "causal" if it contains no closed causal curves (null or timelike). GR is entirely neutral on whether the actual spacetime is chronological: the field equations do not forbid CTC solutions. The question of whether the actual universe is chronological is an empirical question that physics has not settled.

## Hawking's Chronology Protection Conjecture

Given that GR mathematically permits CTCs, is there some physical mechanism that prevents them from forming? Stephen Hawking proposed the Chronology Protection Conjecture (1992): the laws of physics conspire to prevent the formation of CTCs on macroscopic scales. When a spacetime is on the verge of developing CTCs — approaching the formation of a "Cauchy horizon," the boundary between the region without CTCs and the region with them — quantum field theory predicts that the vacuum energy diverges. The quantum fields become infinitely energetic at the nascent Cauchy horizon, generating a "quantum pressure" that prevents the horizon from forming and closes off the pathway to time travel.

This conjecture has been supported by calculations in specific models and partially undermined by others. It remains unproven in general, and some calculations in quantum gravity scenarios suggest it might fail. Philosophically, the Chronology Protection Conjecture is significant in a specific way: it makes the possibility of time travel hostage to the correct theory of quantum gravity. If the conjecture holds, it is a consequence of quantum effects near Cauchy horizons; those effects are governed by quantum field theory in curved spacetime, which is itself an approximation to the unknown theory of quantum gravity. Whether CTCs can form in the actual universe may depend on physics we do not yet have.

## Novikov's Self-Consistency Principle

Even if CTCs can form, a separate question arises: what causal structures are permissible in a spacetime that contains them? Igor Novikov (1983) proposed the self-consistency principle: the only physically realizable histories in a spacetime with CTCs are those that are globally self-consistent. Any history that generates a logical contradiction — including the grandfather paradox — is simply not physically realizable. The physics automatically selects out only the consistent solutions.

The principle can be illustrated with a concrete example. Imagine a billiard ball and a wormhole time machine. The ball rolls along a table toward the wormhole. If it enters, it emerges in the past on a trajectory to hit its past self and deflect it from entering the wormhole — which would prevent the hit, which would prevent the deflection, which is a paradox. Does the ball enter the wormhole or not?

Novikov's principle says: only self-consistent solutions are physical. And there are self-consistent solutions. One: the ball enters the wormhole, emerges in the past, delivers a glancing blow to its past self — just enough to deflect it slightly but not enough to prevent it from entering the wormhole. This history is self-consistent. The ball's future self hits its past self; the hit is exactly the kind of hit that preserves the causal loop. Such solutions always exist, at least for continuous causal maps, by the mathematics of fixed-point theorems.

This is the key point about Brouwer's fixed-point theorem in this context. Let f be the map from the "input" state of the CTC region to the "output" state — the causal map that takes the state before the loop to the state after the loop. Novikov's consistency principle requires f(x) = x: the state must be a fixed point of its own causal evolution. Brouwer's theorem guarantees that any continuous map from a compact, convex space to itself has at least one fixed point. For any time travel scenario with a continuous causal map, a self-consistent solution exists. The consistency principle does not eliminate time travel; it restricts it to fixed points of the causal evolution.

The philosophical interpretation of Novikov's principle is contested. Is it a logical necessity — inconsistent histories are simply impossible, like round squares? A metaphysical necessity — the past has a fixed character that cannot be otherwise? Or a nomological constraint — the laws of physics select consistent histories in the way they select energy-conserving histories? The choice among these readings shapes how we think about the freedom of agents in CTC spacetimes, as we consider next.

## CTCs and the Structure of Information

A further consequence of CTC spacetimes deserves mention. The physicist David Deutsch (1991) analyzed the computation a quantum system could perform if it interacted with a CTC. His consistency condition for quantum systems in CTC spacetimes mirrors Novikov's: the quantum state on the CTC must be a fixed point of the evolution, in the appropriate quantum-mechanical sense. The implication is remarkable: if CTCs existed, quantum computers interacting with them could in principle solve problems that are intractable for ordinary quantum computers — NP-complete problems in polynomial time, for instance.

This is philosophically significant not primarily as a practical observation about computation but as an illustration of how deeply CTCs would alter the structure of causation and information in physics. The normal asymmetry of cause and effect — information is created, flows forward in time, and cannot be sent to the past — underlies the entire architecture of our physics: thermodynamics, statistical mechanics, quantum measurement theory, the analysis of agency and deliberation. CTC spacetimes threaten all of it. Whether they are physically realizable is thus not merely a technical question about gravitational physics but a question about the deep causal architecture of the world.
