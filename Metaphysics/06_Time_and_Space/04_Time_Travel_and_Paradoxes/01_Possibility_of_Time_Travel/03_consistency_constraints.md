# Consistency Constraints on Time Travel

Suppose Tim travels to the past with the explicit intention of killing his grandfather before his father is born. Tim is not an incompetent; he has a loaded gun, a clear shot, and every relevant physical capacity. The question is not whether Tim has the ability to fire a gun, but whether he can fire it at his grandfather, with lethal effect, and thereby prevent his own existence. If he succeeds, he never exists, never makes the journey, and so his grandfather is never in danger. If he fails, we have an explanation of why he fails — but can that explanation be satisfying?

This is the grandfather paradox, and it has a structure that is worth separating into two distinct questions. The first is logical: is a history in which Tim both exists and kills his grandfather possible — is it a coherent possible world? The second is explanatory: even if no such world is possible, what is it that prevents Tim from succeeding? Why does the gun jam, or Tim's nerve fail, or the shot miss, on this particular occasion? These two questions require different kinds of answer.

## The Logical Constraint

Lewis's analysis makes quick work of the first question. A history in which Tim both exists and kills his grandfather before his father is born is simply a logical contradiction. Tim exists only if his father exists; his father exists only if his grandfather survived to have children; therefore Tim exists only if his grandfather survived. A history in which Tim both exists and kills his grandfather (before his father is born) is one in which Tim both exists and does not exist. That is not a possible world; it is a description that fails to describe anything.

The consistency constraint is therefore a consequence of the principle of non-contradiction, applied to temporal causal loops. No additional metaphysical principle is required; no special law of physics is invoked. Inconsistent causal loops are impossible for the same reason that p ∧ ¬p is impossible — they describe a situation in which the same proposition is both true and false.

We can distinguish three ways of reading this. On the logical reading, inconsistent histories are logically impossible: they cannot be coherently described. On a metaphysical reading, they are metaphysically impossible: the fixed character of the past makes alteration impossible, not merely logically but as a matter of what objects and events can do. On the nomological reading, they are physically impossible: the laws of nature (perhaps supplemented by the Novikov principle or some quantum gravity constraint) select only consistent solutions. Lewis's reading is the first. Novikov's principle, as a physical postulate, suggests the third. The distinctions matter for questions about the nature of Tim's freedom, to which we return.

## Local and Global Possibility

The second question — what explains Tim's failure — introduces a distinction that is crucial and often blurred. An action or event can be locally possible (possible given the immediate physical circumstances) while being globally impossible (impossible given the complete history of the world).

Tim has a gun, loaded, aimed, hammer cocked. Nothing in his immediate situation prevents him from pulling the trigger. He has the local physical capacity. In this sense — the sense relevant to whether Tim is acting freely, whether he is doing something he is capable of doing — Tim can try to kill his grandfather.

But the complete history of the world is such that Tim exists. Any complete history that includes Tim's existence includes his grandfather's survival. There is no complete history that includes both Tim's presence in the past and his grandfather's death before Tim's father is born. In this sense — the sense relevant to what the world permits as a total causal structure — Tim cannot kill his grandfather.

Both of these are true simultaneously, and there is no contradiction between them, because they are not about the same thing. The local possibility concerns Tim's physical capacities and immediate circumstances. The global impossibility concerns the structure of the complete causal history. Conflating these is the error that makes the grandfather paradox seem like a genuine contradiction. It is not a contradiction; it is two different truths stated without their proper qualifiers.

The practical upshot: Tim will fail to kill his grandfather, and the failure will have an ordinary explanation. Something will go wrong — the gun jams, the bullet misses, Tim has a sudden change of heart, his grandfather is shielded by a passing crowd, a convenient coincidence prevents the shot. The failure will look exactly like any of the ordinary ways that people fail to do things they are physically capable of doing. There is no supernatural barrier, no special time-travel enforcement mechanism. There is only the requirement that the complete history of the world be self-consistent — and that requirement is satisfied, in any given case, by whatever mundane contingency ensures the failure.

## The Question of Freedom

Does the consistency constraint undermine Tim's freedom? This is where the account becomes philosophically most interesting. Richard Hanley (*The Metaphysics of Star Trek*, 1997) argues that the constraint constitutes a form of temporal fatalism: Tim is constrained to fail, and this seems to mean that his actions are predetermined by the structure of the causal loop in a way that undermines genuine agency.

Lewis's response is to invoke the distinction between local and global senses of ability once more. Freedom — on any plausible account — does not require the global ability to produce any outcome whatsoever. I am free when I act from my own deliberation, intentions, and will; the fact that the outcome was not open in some global sense does not impugn the freedom of the deliberation. Tim deliberates, forms an intention, acts on it. The fact that the world conspires to frustrate his intention does not make him unfree — it makes him unlucky. People's intentions are frustrated all the time without their freedom being thereby undermined.

Harry Frankfurt's account of free action is useful here. On Frankfurt's analysis, free action requires acting on desires that one endorses on reflection — second-order desires to have the first-order desires that move one to act. Whether the outcome of one's action is globally possible is irrelevant to this. Tim acts freely in attempting to kill his grandfather if he acts from his own endorsed will; the consistency constraint bears on the outcome, not on the character of the deliberation.

The harder question is whether the consistency constraint implies that Tim's whole pattern of behavior is determined in a way that makes freedom illusory in a stronger sense. In a CTC spacetime, the causal loop is closed: Tim's presence in the past is caused by his future decision to travel back; his future decision is (in part) caused by events in the past that Tim influences. The causal structure is circular, not linear. Does this circularity constitute determination in a freedom-undermining sense?

The answer depends on whether determinism per se is freedom-undermining — which is, of course, the central question in the free will debate. If one is a compatibilist, the circular determination of CTC spacetimes is no more threatening to freedom than ordinary causal determination. If one is a libertarian incompatibilist, CTC spacetimes raise the same challenge as ordinary determinism, and the circularity of the determination adds nothing essential. The time travel case does not resolve the free will debate; it inherits all its difficulties.

## Bootstrap Loops and the Explanatory Constraint

The consistency constraint permits a class of cases that are puzzling for a different reason than the grandfather paradox. In a bootstrap causal loop, an object or piece of information exists in a closed causal loop with no external origin. A manuscript is brought from the future to the past, published in the past, and eventually becomes the very manuscript that is brought from the future. No one wrote the first draft; the information has no point of original creation. The loop is self-consistent — the manuscript at time T is the same manuscript as at time T + loop-length — but there seems to be something explanatorily deficient about it.

The Principle of Sufficient Reason, in a form applicable here, says that every entity has an explanation of its existence. The information in the bootstrap manuscript has no such explanation: it was never created, only circulated. This is sometimes called the problem of uncaused information, and it applies to any bootstrap loop, not just to manuscripts.

Two responses present themselves. One: the PSR, applied to circular causal structures, must be weakened. The bootstrap manuscript is explained by the causal loop — its existence at each time is causally explained by its existence at the previous time, and the loop as a whole is consistent. The PSR should not demand a "first cause" external to any closed causal structure; it should only demand a sufficient causal condition for each event, which the loop provides. On this view, bootstrap loops are explanatorily complete, even if they violate a prior demand for linear explanatory chains.

Two: bootstrap loops reveal a genuine explanatory gap that no consistent causal structure can fill. This is a version of the objection that circular explanations are not genuine explanations — that explaining A by B and B by A explains neither. If this is right, bootstrap loops are possible only in a technical, logical sense; they are deeply explanatorily deficient in a way that makes them metaphysically suspect even if not strictly contradictory.

The dispute between these responses is unresolved, and it connects to deeper questions about the structure of explanation, the role of the PSR in metaphysics, and whether causal grounding must be non-circular. The time travel case makes vivid a question about causation that arises in other contexts too — in debates about the coherence of circular grounding relations, in discussions of divine self-causation, and in the logic of self-referential systems generally.
