# Temporal Paradoxes

The philosophical puzzles generated by time travel go beyond the grandfather paradox. A family of "bootstrap" or "ontological" paradoxes arises when something that exists in the world owes its existence entirely to time travel, without any origin outside the time-travel loop. A traveler receives a mathematical proof from a future version of herself; she studies it, grows old, and eventually travels back to give it to her younger self — who had never independently discovered it. The proof has no point of origin: it was not discovered, it was passed from future to past in an endless loop. Does this bootstrap require a causal originator? Or can causal loops be self-explaining?

The metaphysics of causal loops challenges both the Humean and the non-Humean account of causation. On the Humean account, causation requires temporal precedence — cause before effect. In a temporal loop, the cause and effect are part of a circular structure with no temporal precedence, at least not an overall precedence. The non-Humean account of causation in terms of powers and mechanisms faces similar difficulties: what grounds the power to pass on the proof, if there is no initial instance of the proof being created?

The "bilking" paradox is another challenge. Suppose a sensor detects a particle arriving from the future and triggers a machine that prevents that same particle from being sent to the past. If the particle arrives, it is prevented; but if it is prevented, it never arrives to trigger the prevention; so it is sent, and arrives, and is prevented, and is never sent — a genuine contradiction. Unlike the grandfather paradox (which can be dissolved by the "fixed past" response), the bilking paradox generates a direct logical inconsistency.

This section examines these temporal paradoxes with care, assessing which are genuine contradictions, which are merely apparent contradictions dissoluble by the "fixed past" response, and which raise deeper questions about the metaphysics of causation and temporal structure.
