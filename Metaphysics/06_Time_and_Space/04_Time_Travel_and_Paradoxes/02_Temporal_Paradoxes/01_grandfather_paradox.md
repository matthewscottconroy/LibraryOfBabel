# The Grandfather Paradox

Tim travels to the past. He is armed, he is motivated, and he knows the address. He has identified the moment at which his grandfather is most vulnerable, before Tim's father has been conceived. Tim aims, Tim fires. If the bullet finds its mark — if Tim kills his grandfather before Tim's father is born — then Tim's father is never born, Tim is never born, Tim never travels back, and the grandfather is never in danger. But if the grandfather is never in danger, he survives, Tim's father is born, Tim is born, and Tim does make the journey. This is the grandfather paradox: an apparent logical loop in which the event that triggers the causal chain leading to Tim's existence also prevents that causal chain from occurring.

The question is not whether this scenario is disturbing, strange, or counterintuitive. The question is whether it is logically contradictory. A genuine logical contradiction would mean that backward time travel is self-refuting — impossible as a matter of logic, not merely difficult as a matter of physics. Lewis's analysis shows that the paradox does not reach that conclusion, and that what looks like a contradiction is the result of conflating two different senses of "can" — one local, one global.

## The Structure of the Paradox and Lewis's Dissolution

Let GS mean "grandfather survived until after Tim's father was conceived," TE mean "Tim exists," and TK mean "Tim kills his grandfather." The apparent paradox can be set out explicitly:

(1) If TK, then ¬GS.
(2) If ¬GS, then ¬TE.
(3) If ¬TE, then ¬TK.
(4) Therefore: if TK, then ¬TK.
(5) If ¬TK, then GS.
(6) If GS, then TE.
(7) Tim exists, so TE is true.

What follows? One might think: if (7) gives us TE, then TK is possible (Tim has the ability), but (4) says TK is impossible (it implies its own negation). Contradiction.

Lewis's response is that this mixes up two different things. There is a local sense of possibility — having the relevant physical capacities, skills, and opportunities in one's immediate circumstances — and a global sense — being part of any complete, self-consistent history of the world. Tim can kill his grandfather in the local sense: he has a gun, a line of sight, and the skill to use it. Nothing in his immediate physical situation prevents him from pulling the trigger. Tim cannot kill his grandfather in the global sense: no complete history of the world that includes Tim also includes the death of his grandfather before Tim's father is born. These two claims are both true, and they do not contradict each other, because they are about different things.

We can put this more sharply. The word "can" is context-sensitive. "Can Tim kill a person?" — yes, in general, he is physically capable. "Can Tim kill his grandfather, given that Tim exists in this history?" — no, because the history that contains Tim is one in which his grandfather survived. The second question has a different context, and the answer in that context is no. The paradox arises from ignoring the context shift.

## Why Tim Fails

This leaves a practical question that feels pressing: if Tim has the local ability, if he aims and fires, what goes wrong? What prevents the bullet from reaching its target? Lewis's answer is: whatever the actual story says went wrong. The gun jammed. Tim missed. His grandfather stepped aside. A passing vehicle blocked the shot. There is no single systematic mechanism — the consistency constraint does not operate by inserting a magical barrier. It operates by requiring that the complete history of the world be self-consistent, and that requirement is compatible with any contingent combination of events that happen to result in the grandfather's survival.

This might seem unsatisfying, even ad hoc. But Lewis's point is that this is no more ad hoc than the observation that logical impossibilities are always "prevented." It is impossible for me to simultaneously lift and not lift my arm; of course something prevents me from doing both simultaneously. The "something" is not mysterious — it is just the structure of the situation. The consistency constraint is a constraint on what histories are possible, and within that constraint, the details are filled in by the ordinary contingencies of the world.

Several other responses to the paradox exist alongside Lewis's. The many-worlds response holds that Tim, in traveling back, enters a different branch of the quantum multiverse. He can kill a grandfather in that branch — but that grandfather is not his own grandfather, who exists in Tim's original branch. The paradox dissolves because the object of the killing is not the right object. This response is physically motivated by many-worlds quantum mechanics but changes the concept of time travel: the traveler visits a parallel past, not their own. The impossibility response uses the paradox as a reductio: since backward time travel leads to contradiction, it is impossible. This is physically well-motivated by Hawking's Chronology Protection Conjecture, but it only works if the paradox is a genuine contradiction — and Lewis has shown that it is not.

## The Grandfather Paradox and Free Will

There is a genuine worry about freedom lurking in the vicinity of the consistency constraint, distinct from the question of whether the paradox is a logical contradiction. The worry is this: if Tim attempts to kill his grandfather but is always prevented by some contingent event, then the world seems to be arranging itself to thwart Tim's intentions in a way that resembles fatalism. Not logical fatalism — Tim's death is not guaranteed by the laws of logic — but a kind of cosmic fatalism in which the structure of the causal history conspires to prevent certain actions.

Lewis argues that this does not undermine Tim's freedom in any meaningful sense. Freedom requires the ability to act from one's own deliberation and will — not the guarantee that any particular action will succeed. Tim deliberates, forms intentions, acts on them. He is free in doing so. The consistency constraint bears on the outcome, not on the deliberation, and freedom is located in the deliberation.

Harry Frankfurt's account is useful here: free action requires acting on second-order endorsed desires, not guaranteed success. Peter van Inwagen and others have argued that the consistency constraint in time travel constitutes a form of soft determinism: Tim's actions are free in the compatibilist sense but the world conspires to prevent inconsistency. This is coherent as a form of freedom; it is not libertarian freedom with categorical ability to do otherwise. Whether this matters depends on whether one is a compatibilist or a libertarian about free will. The time travel case does not resolve that debate; it inherits all of its structure.

What the grandfather paradox does settle, for Lewis, is that backward time travel does not generate a logical contradiction. It generates a consistency constraint — a requirement that causal histories be self-consistent — but that constraint is not a logical impossibility. It is a structural feature of what histories can exist. Time travel to the past is possible, in Lewis's sense; the time traveler simply cannot change what the past was.
