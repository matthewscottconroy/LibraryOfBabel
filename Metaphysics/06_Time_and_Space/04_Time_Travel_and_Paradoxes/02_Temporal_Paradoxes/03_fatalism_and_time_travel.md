# Fatalism and Time Travel

There is an old and disquieting argument from logic alone to the conclusion that nothing we do makes any difference — that everything that happens was always going to happen, not because of causal determination but because of the structure of truth itself. Richard Taylor's formulation (1962) begins from bivalence: every proposition is either true or false. If "Tim will shoot his grandfather tomorrow" is true, then Tim's shooting his grandfather is already settled by the truth of that proposition — nothing Tim does now can make a true proposition false. If it is false, Tim's not shooting is equally settled. Either way, Tim's action tomorrow is now necessary or impossible; there is no genuine openness. The future is as fixed as the past, and for the same reason: what is true is true.

The time travel case sharpens this argument in a specific way. Tim has traveled to his grandfather's past. Whatever Tim does there — whether he shoots or refrains, speaks or stays silent, acts or watches — is already part of the actual history of the world. The past is fixed. Tim's actions in the past are fixed. Does this mean Tim is fatally constrained — that he has no genuine freedom to act in the past, but is merely going through the motions of an already-determined script?

Lewis's answer, derived from his analysis of the grandfather paradox, is that this confuses two different senses of constraint. The conclusion that Tim cannot change the past is not a conclusion about Tim's freedom in any action-relevant sense. It is a conclusion about the impossibility of logical contradiction. What is impossible is not Tim's acting freely in the past; it is Tim's making the past be different from what it already is. And that is not a constraint on Tim's freedom in any sense that matters for the free will debate — it is just the logic of the block universe.

## Taylor's Argument and Its Limits

Taylor's fatalist argument rests on a conflation that is worth isolating. The argument moves from "it is true that p" to "it is necessary that p" — from truth to necessity. But truth and necessity are different modalities. That I will eat breakfast tomorrow may be true without being necessary: it is true in the actual world but false in many nearby possible worlds. The inference from truth to necessity fails.

What Taylor might have intended is that the truth of a future-tensed proposition now ensures that proposition's outcome — that there is already a fact of the matter, so nothing I do can alter it. But "altering" a fact that is already settled is not what freedom requires. Freedom requires acting from one's own reasons and deliberation, not the ability to make true propositions false.

The Aristotelian response — that future contingent propositions lack truth-values prior to the relevant time — is one way out of Taylor's argument. If "Tim will shoot his grandfather" has no truth-value now, then there is nothing now for Tim's actions to violate. This response is available to A-theorists, who can hold that the future is genuinely open and that future-tensed propositions become true or false as events occur. It is unavailable to B-theorists, who hold that all events in the four-dimensional block are equally real and all propositions about events at any time are timelessly true or false. The time travel case illustrates the connection between the metaphysics of time and the freedom debate at exactly this point.

## Time Travel Fatalism Is Not Genuine Fatalism

The consistency constraint in time travel scenarios is importantly different from genuine fatalism, and the difference matters philosophically. Genuine fatalism holds that all of an agent's actions are predetermined — that in every respect, whatever the agent does was always going to happen, and the agent has no genuine ability to do otherwise. The consistency constraint holds only that contradiction-generating actions are impossible. Tim cannot kill his grandfather (given that Tim exists), but this does not constrain Tim's choice of breakfast, his conversations with people in the past, his decision to walk north rather than south, or any of the enormous range of actions that are compatible with his grandfather's survival.

We can formalize this. Let H be the fixed history of the world, and let T be the set of Tim's possible actions during his time in the past. The consistency constraint excludes from T any action A such that "Tim performs A" is inconsistent with H. Given how specific H is — the complete actual history of the world — only a narrow subset of Tim's logically possible actions conflict with it. The vast majority of what Tim might do is fully compatible with H, and those actions remain genuinely available to him in every relevant sense.

This is the point Richard Hanley presses (*The Metaphysics of Star Trek*, 1997): time travel fatalism, understood precisely, is not a global constraint on action but a local prohibition on contradiction-generating actions. It is no more a threat to freedom than the observation that I cannot simultaneously be in London and New York — some logically possible states of affairs are simply not available, and this does not make me unfree.

## The Fixity of the Past and the Free Will Debate

The time travel case does connect to the free will debate, but not in the way that fatalism suggests. Consider Peter van Inwagen's consequence argument. The past is fixed — not up to any agent now what happened before they were born. The laws of nature are fixed — not up to any agent what the laws are. If determinism is true, the present and future are entailed by the past and the laws; therefore the present and future are not up to any agent either. This is van Inwagen's incompatibilist argument (*An Essay on Free Will*, 1983).

The time travel case illuminates the structure of this argument by showing that "the past is fixed" is not as simple as it appears. Tim is a time traveler; his actions in the past are events in the past, but they are also events that are — in personal time — present and future relative to Tim's deliberative standpoint. Tim can deliberate about what to do in the external past just as we deliberate about what to do in the external future. The temporal concepts in van Inwagen's argument are complicated by the divergence of personal and external time that defines time travel.

A compatibilist response to van Inwagen holds that freedom is compatible with the causal determination of actions by prior events and laws — that what freedom requires is acting from one's own desires and reasons without external compulsion, not the categorical ability to have done otherwise. The time traveler case supports compatibilism in a specific way: Tim's actions in the past are causally connected to his deliberation, his character, his reasons — they are genuinely his actions, not something imposed on him from outside. The fact that those actions were always going to be part of the causal history does not make them unfree, any more than the causal determination of ordinary actions makes them unfree.

## The Theological Parallel

The connection between time travel fatalism and theological fatalism is illuminating. If God timelessly knows all events — including Tim's actions in the past — then those actions are settled from God's perspective in a way that seems analogous to the fixity of the past from the time traveler's perspective. The Molinist response — that God's foreknowledge is based on middle knowledge, knowledge of what free creatures would do in any counterfactual situation — parallels Lewis's response to the time travel case: the fixity of what is known does not eliminate the freedom of what is done, because freedom does not require the ability to violate logical or metaphysical necessities.

The open theist response — denying that God has foreknowledge of genuinely future free actions — parallels the A-theorist response to fatalism: future-tensed propositions lack truth-values until the relevant events occur, so there is nothing for God to know before the fact. On this view, both theological and time travel fatalism are defused by denying that there is a fixed, determinate future to be known. The debates are not identical, but they share a structure: in each case, the question is whether the "fixity" of something (the known, the past, the true) eliminates the genuine openness of deliberation and choice.

What the time travel scenarios add to the philosophical discussion of fatalism is not new arguments but new vividness. They make concrete, in a way that abstract modal arguments do not, the question of what it would be like to act in a context where the outcome is already settled. The answer — that Tim can still deliberate, choose, act freely in the local sense, and be responsible for what he does — is the compatibilist's answer to the fatalist argument in general. The time travel case does not establish that answer, but it provides a useful test of it.
