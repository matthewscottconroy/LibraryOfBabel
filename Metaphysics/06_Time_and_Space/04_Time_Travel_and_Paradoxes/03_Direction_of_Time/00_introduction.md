# The Direction of Time

Time seems to have a direction: the past is fixed and the future is open; we remember the past but anticipate the future; causes precede their effects; entropy increases. These asymmetries constitute the "arrow of time" — the sense in which time has a preferred direction, from past to future, that distinguishes it from space (which has no analogous preferred direction).

The puzzle is that the fundamental laws of physics are, for the most part, time-symmetric: they make the same predictions whether time runs forward or backward. If we filmed a simple elastic collision between two billiard balls and played the film backward, the reversed sequence would be equally consistent with the laws of physics. Yet macroscopic processes are not time-reversible: an egg falling and shattering will not spontaneously reassemble and leap back to the table. The irreversibility of macroscopic processes is not written into the laws; it must be explained by some additional principle.

The thermodynamic explanation of the arrow of time appeals to entropy. The Second Law of Thermodynamics states that the entropy of an isolated system tends to increase over time. But the Second Law is not a fundamental law — it is a statistical generalization. Why should entropy have been low in the past? The answer requires positing a Past Hypothesis: the early universe was in an extremely low-entropy state. Given this initial condition, the probabilistic tendency of entropy to increase explains the thermodynamic arrow of time.

Whether the thermodynamic arrow explains the other arrows — the causal arrow, the epistemic arrow (we know the past but not the future), the psychological arrow (we anticipate the future but not the past) — is a further question. Some philosophers hold that all the arrows are manifestations of the same underlying entropic asymmetry; others hold that some arrows are more fundamental than others.

This section examines the metaphysics of temporal direction, the relationship between the different arrows of time, and the prospects for a unified explanation.
