# The Psychological Arrow of Time

Consider the following asymmetry, so pervasive that it is almost invisible. You remember the past. You anticipate the future. You deliberate about what to do next, not about what you did last year. If you know that you underwent an unpleasant medical procedure last Tuesday and that you will undergo the same procedure next Tuesday, you dread the latter but not the former. You are more concerned about future pain than past pain of equal severity — what Derek Parfit called the bias toward the future. You feel that the present is somehow special, that now is different from then, that you are moving through time rather than merely existing at a time.

These asymmetries constitute what philosophers call the psychological arrow of time. They are, in some sense, what temporal experience is made of. The question is whether they are fundamental features of minded existence, requiring their own phenomenological account, or whether they are consequences of the thermodynamic and causal arrows — features of physical structure that our psychology reflects. The answer matters for the broader debate about temporal ontology: if the psychological arrow is fundamental, it provides evidence for the A-theory's claim that the present is genuinely special; if it is derivative, it supports the B-theorist's case that temporal becoming is an appearance rather than a feature of underlying reality.

## Memory, Records, and the Thermodynamic Ground

The natural first response is to ground the psychological arrow in the thermodynamic arrow. Memory is a form of physical record. A neural trace of past event E is a low-entropy physical structure in the brain that stands in a reliable causal relation to E: E produced the trace through a process running in the direction of entropy increase. The trace is reliable precisely because of this causal history — the correlation between trace and traced event was not coincidental but produced. A "memory" of a future event would be a trace formed before the event occurred; the trace could not have been produced by the event (which has not happened), so any correlation would be coincidental. The thermodynamic asymmetry of record formation thus grounds the memory asymmetry.

David Albert presses this point with characteristic sharpness in *Time and Chance* (2000). A physical record of any kind — a footprint, a fossil, a photograph, a neural trace — is a branch system that departed from thermodynamic equilibrium in the direction of entropy increase, forming a correlation with a past event. Records are traces of the past, not the future, because physical processes produce records in the direction they run: from earlier low-entropy states to later high-entropy states, and not in reverse. The asymmetry of our knowledge of past versus future thus mirrors the thermodynamic asymmetry, and both trace to the Past Hypothesis: the low-entropy initial condition of the universe.

Lewis's counterfactual analysis connects the same asymmetry to overdetermination. If event E occurred in the past, the present state of the world contains many traces of E — multiple independent records, memories, physical effects. In any counterfactual scenario in which E had not occurred, the present would have to be radically different in many ways. By contrast, if E is a future event, the present contains few traces; small differences in the present would suffice for E not to occur. Past events are "overdetermined" by the present in a way that future events are not. This is why memories are reliable and pre-cognitions are not.

## Husserl and the Phenomenological Counter-Account

But there is a tradition of analysis that resists deriving the psychological arrow from the thermodynamic one — that takes the temporal structure of experience as prior to any physical account. Edmund Husserl, in *On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* (1928), analyzed temporal experience in terms of three interlocking structures: retention (the just-past, held in consciousness as just-past, giving experience its flowing quality), primal impression (the punctual awareness of the current moment), and protention (the immediate anticipation of the next moment, the just-about-to-be that is already in consciousness before it becomes impression).

On Husserl's account, this retentional-impressional-protentional structure is not derived from anything physical. It is constitutive of consciousness as such — the framework within which any experience, including experience of physical processes, becomes intelligible. The psychological arrow is not a consequence of the thermodynamic arrow; it is a transcendental precondition of experience, including the experience of thermodynamic processes. Any physical account of temporal direction must already presuppose the phenomenological structure of time-consciousness in order to be intelligible to a subject.

The contrast with the Albert-Lewis approach is stark. For Albert and Lewis, the phenomenological asymmetry is a consequence of physical structure. For Husserl and his descendants, it is prior to any physical analysis. This is not merely a dispute about derivation order; it is a dispute about which concepts are fundamental. The physicalist approach begins with the objective physical world and asks how temporal experience arises from it. The phenomenological approach begins with experience and asks how the physical world is constituted within it.

## Deliberation and the Openness of the Future

A distinct dimension of the psychological arrow is deliberative: we deliberate about what to do in the future but not about what we did in the past. This seems to require that the future be open in a way the past is not. Bernard Williams and others have argued that deliberation is intelligible only if the deliberator genuinely believes that the outcome is not yet fixed — that their choice will make a difference to what happens next. If the future is already determined — already in the block — what is the point of deliberating?

Two answers present themselves. The epistemic answer: we deliberate about the future because we do not know what will happen, while we do know what happened in the past. Even if the future is determined, it is open epistemically, and deliberation is the rational process by which we navigate that epistemic openness. The causal answer: the past is causally closed, while our present actions causally influence the future. Deliberation is useful not because the future is causally undetermined but because our actions are part of the causal history that determines it. The second answer connects deliberation to the causal arrow: we deliberate about the future because our actions can cause future outcomes, and the causal arrow runs forward.

The affective asymmetry Parfit identified — caring more about future pain than past pain of equal severity — is connected to these considerations. Parfit argued in *Reasons and Persons* (1984) that this bias is not rationally required: from an impartial perspective, past and future harms are equally bad. The asymmetry is psychological, not rational. It tracks a practical asymmetry — anticipation is useful because we can prepare for and prevent future events, while we cannot prepare for past events — and evolution has shaped our affective responses accordingly. But Parfit's point remains: the practical asymmetry does not make the bias rationally obligatory. There is a normative question about whether we should give the weight to our future selves that we do, and the bias toward the future does not answer it.

## The Explanatory Question

The central metaquestion is whether the psychological arrow reduces to the thermodynamic one or has independent status. Three positions are available. The reductionist holds that memory, deliberation, and the felt flow of time are all explained by thermodynamic asymmetries in physical systems: the psychological arrow is the thermodynamic arrow, as it presents itself to cognitively complex thermodynamic systems. The independentist holds that the phenomenological structure of time-consciousness is a sui generis feature of minded beings that does not follow from physics; Husserl and the phenomenological tradition press this view. The constitutivist holds that the psychological arrow is partly constitutive of what we mean by "past" and "future": the analysis of temporal direction must start from the phenomenological and deliberative asymmetry, not from entropy, because our concepts of past and future are concepts we have as temporally-embedded experiencers.

Jenann Ismael's account in *How Physics Makes Us Free* (2016) attempts a reconciliation: the psychological arrow — including the sense of temporal passage — is real as a feature of the perspective of temporally embedded agents. It is not an objective feature of the four-dimensional block universe; each event in the block is equally real, equally present in the block's ontology. But it is a genuine feature of what it is like to be a creature that learns from the past and acts toward the future. The "flow" of time is not an illusion, on Ismael's account, but a perspectival reality — something that is genuinely there from within the perspective of a temporally embedded agent, even if it is not there from the "view from nowhen."

Whether this reconciliation is successful depends on whether one finds perspectival realities genuinely satisfying. The B-theorist who is drawn to Ismael's view can say: yes, the flow of time is real as a perspectival feature; no, it is not a feature of fundamental physical reality. The A-theorist will reply that this is just to say the flow of time is an illusion, described diplomatically. The debate here, as throughout the philosophy of time, connects ultimately to the question of what kind of thing deserves to count as real — and whether experience, viewed from within, has as much claim to reality as physics, viewed from without.
