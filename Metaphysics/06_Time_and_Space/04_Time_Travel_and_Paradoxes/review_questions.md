# Review Questions — Time Travel and Paradoxes

1. Lewis's analysis of the grandfather paradox distinguishes a local sense of "can" (Tim has the relevant physical capacities in his immediate circumstances) from a global sense (the complete causal history permits the outcome). The paradox dissolves because Tim can fire the gun locally but cannot kill his grandfather globally. Is this dissolution genuinely satisfying, or does it leave a residual explanatory puzzle: if Tim's gun always jams at the crucial moment whenever he is in the past, what explains this systematic coincidence without appealing to a mysterious "temporal enforcement mechanism"?

2. Lewis's entire analysis of time travel presupposes four-dimensionalism: the time traveler is a four-dimensional worm with an unusually shaped worldline, and personal time is the parameter along that worldline. The chapter notes that three-dimensionalism — the view that objects are wholly present at each moment — faces additional difficulties in making sense of backward time travel. Does Lewis's analysis therefore provide independent evidence for four-dimensionalism, or does it only show that time travel is coherent given four-dimensionalism, leaving open the question of which metaphysics is correct?

3. Backward time travel is most naturally accommodated by the block universe: the time traveler's worldline is simply an unusually shaped curve in a fixed four-dimensional manifold. Presentism has trouble because the past doesn't exist, while the growing block can accommodate travel to the past (which is real) but struggles with travel to the future (which isn't yet real). Does this asymmetry constitute a genuine objection to presentism and growing block, or can each view offer a principled response that preserves their core commitments?

4. The bootstrap paradox is consistent — no event implies its own non-occurrence — but the information in the causal loop has no point of original creation. Lewis accepts this as a brute fact analogous to the unexplained initial state of the universe. Critics argue that the plays, unlike the Big Bang's initial state, are the kind of thing whose instances always have explanations of a specific form (original authorship), and that the bootstrap loop fails to supply that form. Evaluate this disagreement: is "the kind of thing that requires a particular type of explanation" a coherent constraint on metaphysical possibility, or does it conflate metaphysical and epistemic norms?

5. Novikov's self-consistency principle holds that only globally self-consistent histories are physically realizable in a spacetime containing closed timelike curves, and uses Brouwer's fixed-point theorem to show that at least one self-consistent solution always exists. The chapter distinguishes three readings of this principle: logical necessity, metaphysical necessity, and nomological constraint. What follows for the freedom of agents in CTC spacetimes on each reading? Which reading is most defensible given the chapter's discussion of the grandfather paradox and fatalism?

6. Taylor's fatalist argument moves from bivalence — every proposition is now either true or false — to the conclusion that all actions are necessitated. The chapter contrasts this with the A-theorist's Aristotelian response: future contingents lack truth-values prior to the relevant time, so the future is genuinely open. Assess how this dispute about future contingents connects to the metaphysical debate between presentism/growing block (which can deny bivalence for future contingents) and eternalism (which seems committed to bivalence across all times). Does the time travel case expose a commitment that the B-theorist would prefer to avoid?

7. The thermodynamic arrow is grounded in the Past Hypothesis — the extremely low-entropy initial state of the universe — and is used to explain the causal arrow (causes before effects), the epistemic arrow (we know the past but not the future), and the psychological arrow (memory, anticipation, deliberation). Huw Price argues that the sense that the low-entropy initial state needs special explanation is itself a product of our temporal situatedness, not an objective puzzle. Evaluate Price's "view from nowhen" as a response. If the thermodynamic asymmetry is all there is to the arrow of time, what follows for the A-theorist's claim that temporal passage is a genuine feature of reality?

8. The psychological arrow — memory, anticipation, deliberation, Parfit's bias toward the future — appears to be grounded in the thermodynamic arrow through the mechanism of record formation. Husserl's phenomenological account resists this reduction, treating the retentional-impressional-protentional structure of time-consciousness as a transcendental precondition of experience rather than a consequence of physics. Which view better accounts for the explanatory target? And does Ismael's "perspectival realism" — the psychological arrow is real from within the perspective of an embedded agent, but not from the view from nowhen — offer a genuine middle ground, or does it simply rename the dispute between the A-theory and the B-theory?
