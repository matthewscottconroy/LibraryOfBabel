# The Soul as Form of the Body

Imagine an eye made of stone. It would have the shape of an eye, the color of an eye, but one thing would be missing: sight. And an eye that cannot see is an eye in name only. Aristotle uses this image to introduce one of the most influential ideas in the history of the mind-body problem. What makes the eye an eye — what constitutes its nature as a perceptual organ — is its capacity for vision. The soul of the eye, Aristotle says, is sight. Extend this: what makes the body a living body — as opposed to a corpse, which has all the same physical matter — is its capacity for vital functioning. The soul is that capacity. It is not a separate substance imprisoned in the body. It is the form (*morphe*) of the body: the organizing principle that makes a living body alive.

This is Aristotle's fundamental departure from Platonic dualism. In *De Anima*, he defines the soul as "the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially." The hylomorphic framework is central here: every material thing is a compound of matter (*hyle*) and form (*morphe*). For a bronze statue, the bronze is the matter and the shape is the form. For a living organism, the body — flesh, blood, bones — is the matter and the soul is the form. Just as the shape of a wax seal cannot be separated from the wax without ceasing to be that shape, the soul cannot be separated from the body without ceasing to be that soul.

## The Hylomorphic Framework in Detail

Understanding the soul-as-form doctrine requires Aristotle's wider metaphysics of potentiality (*dynamis*) and actuality (*energeia*). Matter is potential: the bronze can become a statue or a weapon. Form is actual: the statue's shape is what actualizes the potential of the bronze. Aristotle distinguishes two levels of actuality. The *first actuality* of a body is its capacity or disposition for vital activity — it has the organization needed for life even when not actively exercising that organization (as a sleeping human has the first actuality of being a rational animal even when not thinking). The *second actuality* is the exercise of those capacities — actually seeing, actually thinking.

The soul is the first actuality. This is why Aristotle says that if an axe were a natural body, its soul would be the capacity for chopping. Having a soul is being organized in a way that makes vital activities possible; exercising vital capacities (waking perception, active thinking) is the second actuality. This layered analysis allows Aristotle to explain sleep and unconsciousness: a sleeping human has a soul — first actuality — even while not actively exercising perceptual or rational capacities.

Aristotle's implicit argument against Platonic soul-body dualism can be reconstructed:

- P1: The soul is what makes a body alive.
- P2: What makes a body alive is its organization or form — the principle of vital functioning.
- P3: A form cannot exist independently of the matter it informs (except perhaps abstractly in thought).
- C: The soul cannot exist independently of the body it informs; soul-body separation is metaphysically impossible in the ordinary case.

This argument is powerful, but Aristotle himself introduces a notable exception: the *active intellect* (*nous poietikos*) may be separable — a point that generated centuries of commentary in the Arabic and medieval traditions.

## Functionalism's Aristotelian Roots

The soul-as-form doctrine anticipates contemporary functionalism in a direct way. The key idea shared by both is that mental states are defined by their functional role — what they do — rather than by the physical substrate that realizes them. For Aristotle, the soul of an eye is its function (seeing), and whatever has that function in the right organizational context has that soul regardless of what it is made of. For Hilary Putnam and contemporary functionalists, mental states are defined by their causal-functional roles in processing inputs and producing outputs, and can in principle be multiply realized in different physical substrates.

The parallel is not perfect: Aristotle's hylomorphism is biological and essentialist in ways that contemporary functionalism is not. Aristotle does not think just any physical system that plays the right causal role has a soul; the material must be the right kind of organic matter. This biological constraint is closer to what contemporary philosophers call "biological naturalism" (Searle) than to machine functionalism. But the structural insight — that what a thing is, is partly a matter of what it does — runs from Aristotle directly to the functionalist tradition.

## Objections to Soul-as-Form

Several objections deserve consideration. First, the unity of living organization may be less clear than Aristotle assumes: contemporary biology describes organisms in terms of molecular processes, evolutionary functions, and systemic organization, but there is no consensus on what "the form" of a living body is. Second, the active intellect — the capacity for abstract thought — presents a persistent difficulty. Aristotle seems to want it to be both the form of a particular human body and also in some sense separable from it, which is difficult to reconcile. Third, the view may face versions of the interaction problem: if the soul is the form of the body, and forms are in a sense abstract, how does the soul causally influence the body's activity?

Neo-Aristotelian hylomorphism in contemporary philosophy attempts to address these difficulties by developing a more rigorous account of what it means for form to be causally efficacious — the topic taken up in the discussion of contemporary hylomorphism.
