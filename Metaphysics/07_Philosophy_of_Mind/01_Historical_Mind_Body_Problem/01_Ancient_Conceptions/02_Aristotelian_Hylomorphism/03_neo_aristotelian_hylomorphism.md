# Neo-Aristotelian Hylomorphism

Philosophy of mind in the twentieth century offered two broad options: either mental states are identical to physical states (identity theory), or they float free of the physical in ways that make their causal efficacy mysterious (property dualism or substance dualism). Neither option felt quite right. Identity theory seemed to lose the mental — to explain it away rather than explain it. Property dualism preserved the mental but at the cost of leaving its relationship to the physical opaque. Contemporary neo-Aristotelian hylomorphism enters this impasse by reviving the Aristotelian framework of matter and form, arguing that the mind is the formal structure of the body — not an additional substance and not identical to any physical type, but the organizational principle that makes the body the kind of thing it is.

The view has attracted serious philosophical attention from figures including William Jaworski, whose *Philosophy of Mind: A Comprehensive Introduction* (2011) develops the position in detail, as well as Katharine Hawley, David Oderberg, and others. The core claim is that mental activity consists in the exercise of a structured whole's capacities for perception, thought, desire, and action — capacities that emerge from the organization of biological matter without being identical to any configuration of that matter described in purely physical terms. The mental is real and causally efficacious, but it is a form of the physical, not a separate substance or a property reducible to microphysics.

## The Central Hylomorphic Thesis

Contemporary hylomorphists make a distinction between a thing's *matter* — the stuff it is made of, described in purely physical terms — and its *structure* or *form* — the way that stuff is organized to constitute a unified whole with determinate capacities. The thesis is that mental states and events are constituted by the *formal properties* of biological organisms: by the way biological systems are structured for perception, thought, and action, rather than by the underlying physical substrate described in microphysical terms.

We can put this more sharply by considering a contrast. The fact that a brain is organized as a visual cortex, with particular connectivity patterns and functional specializations, explains visual perception in a way that no purely microphysical description of neural firing does. Form — organizational structure — is the explanatorily relevant level. Yet this organizational structure is physically instantiated. There is no non-physical substance. This generates a form of non-reductive naturalism: mental organization is real, causally efficacious, and physically grounded without being reducible to physics.

The hylomorphist distinguishes their view from two neighbors. Against property dualism: the hylomorphist does not posit any "phenomenal properties" that are genuinely distinct from physical properties. Mental organization is a kind of physical organization — organization of matter, not something added to matter from outside. Against type-identity theory: the hylomorphist denies that mental types are identical to physical types, because the same mental capacity can be realized in different physical substrates (multiple realizability), and because mental descriptions have autonomous explanatory roles that physical descriptions do not replace.

## The Causal Efficacy of Form and Kim's Challenge

The relationship to Jaegwon Kim's challenge is particularly important. Kim argued that non-reductive physicalism faces a dilemma: either mental properties reduce to physical properties (reductionism is correct) or they are causally inert (epiphenomenalism follows). The hylomorphist responds by questioning Kim's premise that causation operates exclusively at the level of microphysical events. If structures and formal organizations are genuine causal relata — if "the pattern of neural connectivity explains the perceptual response" is a genuine causal claim — then the dilemma is broken without embracing either reductionism or epiphenomenalism.

A central challenge, of course, is to explain how form — the organizational principle of a system — can be causally efficacious. If form is not a separate substance and not identical to any physical property, what is it, and how does it make things happen? Jaworski's response appeals to the distinction between *having* a capacity and *exercising* it. A system's capacity for vision is a real property — grounded in but not identical to any particular neural firing pattern — and the exercise of that capacity (actually seeing something) is a genuine event that plays a causal role in subsequent behavior. The capacity is formal, not material; but its exercise involves material processes. Form and matter are not two separate contributors to causation but two aspects of one causally efficacious system. Critics who find this insufficiently rigorous worry that "form" is doing metaphorical work that a more precise physical-functional description could replace without remainder. The neo-hylomorphist's reply is that no purely physical description of a visual system can explain why the system *represents* the world as it does, why its states have intentional content — and form is what is needed to supply that explanation.

## Hylomorphism, the Hard Problem, and Biology

One underexplored question is whether neo-Aristotelian hylomorphism provides resources for addressing the hard problem of consciousness. Some hylomorphists suggest that because the soul — formal organization — is a genuine feature of the world that cannot be eliminated in favor of microphysical description, the hylomorphic framework preserves the reality of phenomenal experience without positing anything ontologically spooky. Consciousness is real because formal organization is real; the felt quality of experience is what the exercise of the relevant formal capacities is like from the inside.

Critics counter that this merely relocates the hard problem: even granting that formal organization is real, we still face the question of why any formal organization — however complex — should give rise to phenomenal experience at all. The explanatory gap between third-person functional description and first-person phenomenal character does not obviously close just because we redescribe functional organization in hylomorphic rather than purely physicalist terms.

Neo-Aristotelian hylomorphism connects naturally to discussions of biological organization and emergence. Living organisms exhibit functional integration — the parts of an organism contribute to the functioning of the whole in ways that cannot be understood purely in terms of the parts' intrinsic properties — and this integration is precisely what hylomorphists mean by "form." Denis Walsh, Stuart Kauffman, and others in the philosophy of biology have argued for related positions, suggesting that organisms have genuine teleological natures that are not reducible to the aggregated behavior of their physical components. These convergent arguments give hylomorphism broader support than it might otherwise appear to have from philosophy of mind alone.
