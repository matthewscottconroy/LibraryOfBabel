# Plotinus and the Nous

There is a puzzle about knowledge that Plotinus thought no one before him had adequately resolved: if knowledge is a relation between a knowing subject and a known object, then subject and object are distinct, and the question immediately arises of how the subject can be certain its representations accurately capture something independent of it. Plotinus's radical solution is that at the highest level of cognition, the distinction between knower and known collapses. The intellect thinking mathematical truths *is* those mathematical truths. Subject and object are identical. This identification of knower and known at the level of pure intellection — not a psychological claim about our ordinary mental activity but a metaphysical claim about the nature of Nous — is the philosophical center of Plotinian Neoplatonism.

Plotinus (c. 204–270 CE) is the principal figure of Neoplatonism, and his system organizes reality into three fundamental levels or *hypostases*. From the One — the ultimate, ineffable first principle, beyond being and thought — emanates Nous (Intellect). From Nous emanates Soul, which in turn generates the material world. The human soul participates in all three levels, though it is primarily located at the level of Soul.

**The One** (*to hen*) is the ultimate first principle, which cannot be described in positive terms; any predication limits it. It is the source of all existence through a process of emanation that requires no effort or deliberation — just as the sun radiates light without being diminished.

**Nous (Intellect)** is the first emanation from the One. Nous contains the Platonic Forms as its objects and thinks them eternally. But in Nous, the distinction between thinker and thought is overcome: Nous is the Forms thinking themselves. This self-referential structure is what makes Nous the locus of genuine knowledge.

**Soul** (*psyche*) is the third hypostasis. Soul is responsible for the administration of the material world, containing the ordering principles (*logoi*) through which matter is structured. The human soul is a particular portion of the World Soul, capable of rational thought when it "looks up" toward Nous and capable of body-governance when it "looks down" toward matter.

## Self-Knowledge and the Identity of Knower and Known

Plotinus develops the account of intellectual self-knowledge in *Enneads* V.3. In ordinary cognition, there is a distinction between the knowing subject and the known object. This distinction generates the skeptical problem: how can a subject be certain that its representations accurately capture an independently existing object? Plotinus's answer is that at the highest cognitive level — pure intellection — this distinction collapses. The identity thesis has important implications. Intellectual self-knowledge is infallible: the intellect cannot fail to know itself, because knowing itself *is* being itself. And it provides a model for the mystical union with the One: in the highest mystical experience (*henosis*), the distinction between the individual soul and the One is temporarily overcome, and the soul participates directly in the One's self-subsistent unity.

Plotinus offers a complex account of the soul's relationship to embodiment. In one strand of his thought, closer to Plato, embodiment is a falling-away from the soul's true nature — a descent into matter that the soul must overcome through philosophical practice. In another strand, the soul's governance of the body is a natural expression of the Soul-hypostasis's generative activity, not a defect but a function. The goal of the philosophical life is *epistrophe* — return — in which the soul moves back up the metaphysical hierarchy through progressively higher forms of cognition: from sensation, through discursive reason (*dianoia*), to pure intellection (*noesis*), and finally to the mystical union with the One. Plotinus describes this experience in remarkable terms: the soul "goes out of itself" and loses awareness of distinction, achieving a kind of unmediated contact with the ultimate source of being.

## Reflexive Consciousness and Influence

One of Plotinus's most important and historically influential contributions is his account of consciousness as essentially self-reflexive. Genuine consciousness, for Plotinus, involves not merely being in a state but *knowing* one is in that state — a form of self-awareness that accompanies all genuine thought. This reflexivity is what distinguishes genuine mental activity from mere physical process. We can put this connection forward: Kant's transcendental unity of apperception — the "I think" that must be able to accompany all my representations — has a clear Plotinian ancestry. The higher-order thought theories of consciousness developed by Rosenthal and Lycan in contemporary philosophy of mind are, at a structural level, responses to the same insight: that genuine consciousness involves a kind of self-awareness or meta-cognition that distinguishes it from merely mechanical processing.

Plotinus explicitly rejects the Aristotelian view that the soul is the form of the body. The soul is genuinely separate and superior. The body does not enhance but impedes the soul's proper activity. The influence of Plotinus on subsequent philosophy — through Augustine, medieval Islamic philosophy (al-Farabi, Avicenna), and the Renaissance Neoplatonists — was enormous, shaping Christian theology's account of the soul and its relationship to God. Augustine's account of the mind's inner life as a trace of the Trinitarian structure of God, and his famous argument for the certainty of the cogito, draw directly on Plotinian themes. In contemporary philosophy, neo-Platonic ideas surface in debates about the unity of consciousness, the nature of self-knowledge, and the metaphysics of consciousness — often unacknowledged but present.
