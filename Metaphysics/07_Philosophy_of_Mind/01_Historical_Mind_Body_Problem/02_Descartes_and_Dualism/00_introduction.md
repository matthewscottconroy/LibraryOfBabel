# Descartes and Substance Dualism

Descartes's Meditations on First Philosophy is the founding document of the modern mind-body problem. Descartes's method of doubt led him, through the cogito, to the certainty of his own existence as a thinking thing; and his analysis of the relationship between thinking and extension generated the canonical formulation of substance dualism: mind (res cogitans) and body (res extensa) are radically distinct substances with no properties in common. The mind is essentially thinking — its nature is wholly constituted by thought; the body is essentially extended — its nature is wholly constituted by spatial properties. These two substances are completely different in kind, with no overlap.

The interaction problem immediately arises. If mind and body have no properties in common — no shared nature that would provide a medium for causal interaction — how do they causally affect each other? When I decide to raise my arm, a mental event causes a physical event; when a pin pricks my finger, a physical event causes a mental event (pain). Both forms of causation seem clearly real, yet Cartesian dualism cannot explain them. Descartes's own answer — that the pineal gland mediates the interaction — is not a philosophical answer; it merely relocates the problem without solving it.

Descartes's successors developed three main responses to the interaction problem. Occasionalism (Malebranche) holds that there is no genuine mind-body interaction; whenever a mental event occurs that would naturally be followed by a physical event, God intervenes to produce the physical event directly. Pre-established harmony (Leibniz) holds that God created mind and body with coordinated internal states so that they appear to interact without actually doing so. Epiphenomenalism (Huxley) holds that the mind is a byproduct of physical processes — physical events cause mental events, but mental events have no causal efficacy.

None of these solutions is satisfactory; each merely relocates or eliminates the problem rather than solving it. This section examines Descartes's dualism, the interaction problem, and the responses it generated.
