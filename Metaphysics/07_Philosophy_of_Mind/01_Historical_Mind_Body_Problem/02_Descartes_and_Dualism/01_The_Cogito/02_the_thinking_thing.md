# The Thinking Thing

Once Descartes has established that he exists — that there is at least this thinking — the next question is: what exactly is this "I" that exists? He goes through an inventory. Before the doubt, he thought he was a human being with a face, hands, and a body. The demon has put all of that in question. What remains? Thinking. Doubting, understanding, conceiving, affirming, denying, willing, refusing, imagining, feeling — all of these are activities that are still present to him with certainty, even if everything they are directed toward is uncertain. These activities, and nothing else, constitute what he is. He is a *res cogitans*, a thinking thing.

This is more than a merely autobiographical claim. Descartes is making a point about the essence of mind as such: the mind's defining property is thought, not extension. Physical objects are defined by spatial extension — they take up space, have shape, size, and position. The mind has none of these properties. You cannot ask how large a thought is, or where pain is located in three-dimensional space in the same sense you can ask where a pebble is. The mind, as a thinking thing, is defined by its capacity for thought alone.

## The Wax Argument

The Second Meditation's famous wax argument serves to establish the mind's epistemic priority over the senses — and to illuminate by contrast what the intellect is. Descartes takes a piece of wax fresh from a beehive: it has a definite smell, shape, color, size, and hardness. He melts it. Its smell, shape, color, size, and hardness all change — yet it is still the same wax. What persists through all the changes? Not any particular sensory quality, but something the intellect grasps: the wax is extended, flexible, and mutable. The conclusion Descartes draws is that the intellect, not the senses, is the primary cognitive faculty, and what the intellect grasps most clearly is extension, flexibility, and mutability. Moreover, in grasping the nature of the wax through the intellect, I am simultaneously confirming the reliability and priority of intellectual cognition. Perception itself, properly understood, is not a passive sensory reception but an active intellectual judgment.

The wax argument also reveals, obliquely, why Descartes thinks the mind is better known than the body. While the wax requires intellectual effort to be known (we must strip away its sensory qualities to grasp its essential nature), the mind is immediately present to itself. The act of grasping the wax *is itself* an act of mind, and that act is self-illuminating in a way that the wax is not.

## Mind as Transparent to Itself — and the Challenges

A central Cartesian thesis about the thinking thing is that the mind is transparent to itself: every mental state is a conscious state, and every conscious state is known to the mind that has it. There can be no unconscious thoughts. Thinking just is conscious activity. This transparency thesis is partly supported by the cogito: what makes the cogito certain is that the thinking is immediately and indubitably present to the thinker.

But the transparency thesis did not survive its critics. Leibniz introduced the notion of "petites perceptions" — very small, unconscious perceptions that constitute the background of mental life. The mind processes far more than reaches conscious awareness. Freud made unconscious mental states central to his theoretical framework: the most psychologically significant mental states — repressed desires, traumatic memories — are precisely those hidden from conscious access. Contemporary cognitive science has extensively documented processes — visual processing, motor control, language production — that are "mental" in the sense of being information-processing but inaccessible to consciousness. Fodor's theory of mental modules proposes that much cognitive processing is inaccessible to central cognition.

Descartes's catalog of the activities of the *res cogitans* — doubting, understanding, conceiving, affirming, denying, willing, refusing, imagining, and feeling — groups together states that contemporary philosophers often distinguish sharply: propositional attitudes (believing, doubting, willing), phenomenal states (feeling, sensing), and imagistic states (imagining). The grouping reflects his view that all these states share the essential property of being "modes of thought" — present to the mind and knowable with certainty. This creates problems when we ask whether phenomenal states — the qualitative character of sensation, the redness of a red experience, the painfulness of pain — are really just a species of cognitive state, or whether they have a distinctive character that resists purely cognitive characterization. This is precisely the issue Ned Block distinguishes as the difference between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness.

The characterization of the mind as a "thinking thing" also raises questions about unity and continuity. Is there a single thinking thing that persists through all my mental states? Hume challenged this: the self might not be a persisting substance but a bundle of momentary impressions. Buddhist philosophy independently arrived at a similar "no-self" (*anatta*) view. The legacy of the *res cogitans* is genuinely ambivalent: it emphasizes the irreducibility of the first-person perspective and the distinctive character of mentality, while distorting the picture of mental life by equating it entirely with conscious, transparent states.
