# Res Cogitans and Res Extensa

Descartes's metaphysics arrives at a startling simplicity. Strip away everything that can be doubted, and you are left with two radically different kinds of thing: the thinking thing and the extended thing. Everything created belongs to one category or the other. Mind is defined by thought — doubting, perceiving, willing, imagining — and has no extension, no spatial location, no size or shape. Matter is defined by extension — occupying space, having shape, size, and position — and by itself does not think. These are not two species of a common genus; they are ontologically fundamental in their own right. *Res cogitans* and *res extensa*: the thinking substance and the extended substance.

The cleanness of the distinction has genuine explanatory appeal. It explains why physics can proceed mechanistically and mathematically without any reference to mental properties — physical reality is purely quantitative. Galileo, Newton, and their successors pursued the program of explaining natural phenomena in purely quantitative terms, and the success of this program is one of the strongest historical arguments for some form of physicalism. It also explains the special features of mental life: privacy, immediacy, and the apparent transparency of thought to the thinker.

Descartes holds that each substance has a single essential attribute. For *res cogitans*, the essential attribute is thought (*cogitatio*); for *res extensa*, it is extension (*extensio*). Everything else about a substance is a "mode" of that attribute. For matter, all properties are modes of extension: shape, size, position, motion, and rest are all ways of being extended. For mind, all properties are modes of thought: doubting, imagining, willing, sensing, and feeling are all ways of thinking. This essentialist doctrine has important consequences. No mental property is a mode of extension, and no physical property is a mode of thought. Mental and physical properties belong to different categories with no overlap. This categorical separation is what makes substance dualism genuinely dualistic rather than merely a claim that we describe the same things in two different vocabularies.

## The Human Compound and Its Instability

Human beings, however, are a genuine composite of mind and body — not merely a mind operating a body, but a genuine "substantial union." The clearest evidence of this union, for Descartes, is sensation. Hunger, thirst, pain, and pleasure are not merely intellectual judgments about the state of the body; they are felt states that arise from the union itself. A disembodied angel could know that a body needed food, but it could not be *hungry*.

This account of the substantial union is philosophically unstable. Descartes insists that mind and body are really distinct substances and yet that human beings are genuinely unified. But if two substances can be genuinely unified into a third (the composite human being), it is unclear why the composite is not a substance in its own right, or why mind and body are still "really distinct" in the face of this unity. Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia pressed Descartes on this in their famous correspondence: how can we understand the causal interaction between substances that are so radically different in nature? Her question was not answered, and it set the agenda for the philosophy of mind for the next four centuries.

## The Problem That Launched a Tradition

The problem that immediately arises from the two-substance picture is interaction. If mind and body are so different in nature — the one unextended and thinking, the other extended and unthinking — how do they interact causally? When I decide to raise my arm, something in my mental life causes my arm to move. When I stub my toe, something physical causes pain. Both transactions seem to require causal influence across the substance boundary. But in Descartes's physics, causal influence requires contact, and contact requires extension. An unextended mind cannot make contact with anything.

This interaction problem was recognized immediately and has never received a fully satisfying Cartesian answer. It drove the subsequent history of dualism — occasionalism, parallelism, and eventually various forms of materialism — as philosophers tried to resolve the tension Descartes created. The landscape of contemporary philosophy of mind — physicalism, functionalism, property dualism, panpsychism — is in large measure an attempt to escape or dissolve the impasse generated by the Cartesian two-substance framework.
