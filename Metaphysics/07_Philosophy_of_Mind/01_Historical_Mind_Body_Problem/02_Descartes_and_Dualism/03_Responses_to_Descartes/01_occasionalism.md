# Occasionalism

If genuine causal power requires a necessary connection to its effect, and if no finite substance possesses such necessary connection, then no finite substance ever genuinely causes anything. This is Malebranche's diagnosis, and it generates his solution to the interaction problem. We had been looking for a mechanism by which an immaterial mind moves the body. But we had also been looking for a mechanism by which one billiard ball moves another, and we cannot find that either — not in any ontologically deep sense. The interaction problem is just the most vivid case of a general puzzle about causation among finite things. The answer, for Malebranche, is that God is the only genuine cause. Mental and physical events are "occasional causes" — occasions on which God acts, not genuine causes of their apparent effects.

Nicolas Malebranche (1638–1715) developed occasionalism as the principal philosophical response to the Cartesian interaction problem, with precursors in the Arab philosopher Al-Ghazali. Malebranche accepted Cartesian substance dualism but denied genuine causal interaction between mind and body. Instead, God acts as the universal causal intermediary: on every occasion when a mental event occurs, God produces the corresponding physical event, and vice versa. The view has the advantage of eliminating the mind-body interaction problem entirely — there is no causal traffic between substances because no finite substance ever genuinely causes anything. The cost is theological and explanatory: God becomes an ad hoc causal intermediary at every moment of apparent causation.

Malebranche's argument is philosophically sophisticated. He holds that the very concept of genuine causation — a power that necessarily produces its effect — applies only to a being with infinite power and knowledge. God alone has this power; created things are radically powerless. This is not merely an argument from ignorance (we cannot see how things causally interact) but a metaphysical claim: finite things simply do not possess the kind of being that genuine causal power requires. Notice that this position has a striking similarity to Hume's later analysis of causation. Hume argued that we never observe necessary connection between cause and effect — only constant conjunction, followed by psychological habit. Malebranche drew a theological conclusion from this observation (the necessary connection exists in God); Hume drew a secular, skeptical one (necessary connection is a fiction imposed by habit). The philosophical observation driving both positions is the same.

## Vision in God and the Epistemological Extension

Malebranche also developed a distinctive epistemology based on occasionalism: we see all things in God (*vision in God*). Our ideas are not representations produced in our minds but archetypes in God's intellect to which God allows us access. Knowledge is a kind of participation in divine Intellect. This epistemological occasionalism parallels the causal version: just as God is the only genuine cause, God is the only genuine source of representative content. Our "ideas" are not independent mental entities that somehow represent the world; they are God's ideas to which we have access. The reliability of our knowledge is thus guaranteed by God's nature — an epistemological parallel to Descartes's claim that clear and distinct perceptions track reality because God is no deceiver.

## Criticisms and Descendants

Leibniz's critique was that occasionalism makes God perform continuous miracles — intervening in the natural order at every moment to maintain the mind-body correlation. This seemed theologically undignified: a perfect creator would design a system that works by itself, not one requiring perpetual intervention. His pre-established harmony was proposed as a more elegant alternative.

Berkeley's idealism can be seen as a radicalization of occasionalism. If God is the universal cause and our ideas are God's ideas, why not eliminate material substance altogether and hold that reality consists only of minds and ideas? Occasionalism's denial of genuine physical causation seems to leave physical substance doing no work; Berkeley drew the logical conclusion and dispensed with it.

The occasionalist tradition in Islamic philosophy, particularly associated with the Ash'arite school and figures like Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), developed independently of and prior to Malebranche. Al-Ghazali argued in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* that the philosophers' commitment to necessary causal connections between natural events is an illicit rationalist constraint on divine omnipotence. Natural regularities are simply God's habitual activity, not necessary causal connections. This debate between the Ash'arite theologians and the Islamic Aristotelians — represented by Averroes, who defended genuine natural causation — parallels the Leibniz-Malebranche debate and anticipates the Hume problem about the nature of causal necessity. From a contemporary standpoint, occasionalism faces the challenge of all appeal to divine action: it is not a naturalistic explanation, and explanatory naturalism is a near-universal commitment in contemporary philosophy of mind.
