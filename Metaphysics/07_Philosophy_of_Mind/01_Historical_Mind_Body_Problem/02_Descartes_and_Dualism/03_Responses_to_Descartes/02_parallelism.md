# Leibnizian Pre-Established Harmony and Parallelism

Leibniz's objection to occasionalism was theological before it was philosophical: a God who must continually intervene to maintain the mind-body correspondence is less perfect than one who designed the system with perfect initial coordination. The watch-maker who has to keep adjusting the hands every hour has made a defective watch. Leibniz proposed instead that God, at creation, established a perfect synchrony between mind and body — like two clocks wound at the same time and set to keep identical time — without any genuine causal interaction between them ever being required. This is pre-established harmony, and it is the other major seventeenth-century response to the Cartesian interaction problem.

Leibniz's metaphysics makes this solution natural. Monads — the ultimate units of reality — are "windowless": no genuine causal influence passes between them. Each monad unfolds according to its own internal principle, expressing the entire universe from its own perspective. The soul is a monad; the body is a collection of subordinate monads. When I decide to raise my arm, the mental monad unfolds in a way that includes the representation of an arm being raised; simultaneously, the bodily monads unfold in a way that produces the physical movement. Neither causes the other. God established this coordination from the beginning. The apparent interaction is the manifestation of their pre-established coordination, not genuine causal exchange.

This picture has the virtue of elegance and internal coherence. The theological cost is front-loaded rather than ongoing, which seemed to Leibniz theologically more dignified. But it faces a fundamental objection: it seems to make all apparent causation illusory. If nothing genuinely causes anything — all monads being windowless — then the causal structure of science describes correlations, not genuine causal relations. Leibniz anticipated this and argued that "causal" descriptions of the world are legitimate at the level of phenomena, even if they do not correspond to the ultimate metaphysical reality of windowless monads.

## Spinoza's Parallel Attribute Theory

Though not strictly Leibnizian, Spinoza's dual-attribute monism is often grouped with parallelism. Spinoza holds that there is only one substance — God or Nature — with infinitely many attributes, two of which are knowable to us: thought and extension. Mental events and physical events are not causally related to each other; they are the same events described under two different attributes. The mind-series and the body-series are two descriptions of the same causal chain in Nature. The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things.

This is a genuinely different position from Leibniz's. Spinoza does not posit two series of events running in parallel; he posits one series of events describable under two attributes. The apparent mind-body interaction is not even an interaction — it is a single process described twice. This double-aspect view of the one substance anticipates contemporary identity theories and dual-aspect monism. The philosophical cost: it is unclear how the two attributes can be truly distinct, as Spinoza insists, if they always go together. If every mode under the attribute of thought has a corresponding mode under the attribute of extension, what content does the distinctness claim have?

## Epiphenomenalism as a Descendant

The philosophical trajectory from parallelism leads to epiphenomenalism — the view that mental events are causally inert by-products of physical events. The epiphenomenalist accepts the physical causal series as real and causal but denies that mental events cause anything physical. Mental events are "epiphenomena" — effects of physical processes that produce no physical effects themselves. T.H. Huxley, who introduced the term, used the image of a steam whistle on a locomotive: the whistle is caused by the engine but does not cause the engine to run. Mental events are the psychological whistle — real effects of neural processes, but causally inert with respect to subsequent behavior.

In its strict Leibnizian form, parallelism has no modern defenders. But the epiphenomenalist position it anticipates continues to attract philosophers who are physicalists about causation but reluctant to reduce mental properties to physical ones. Chalmers's position in *The Conscious Mind* (1996) is close to epiphenomenalism: phenomenal consciousness is a non-physical property of physical systems, and it does not causally contribute to behavior, which is fully determined by the underlying physical processes.

The challenge for any form of parallelism or epiphenomenalism is self-refutation: if my mental states — including my belief that epiphenomenalism is true — do not cause my behavior, including my assertions about epiphenomenalism, then there seems to be no reason to take those assertions as evidence for the position. This self-refutation objection, pressed by Kim and others, is a serious challenge to epiphenomenalist positions in contemporary philosophy of mind.
