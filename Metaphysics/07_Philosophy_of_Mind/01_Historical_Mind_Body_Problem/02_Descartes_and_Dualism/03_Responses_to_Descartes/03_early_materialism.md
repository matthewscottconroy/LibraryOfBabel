# Early Materialism: Hobbes, Spinoza, and La Mettrie

Not all early modern philosophers accepted the Cartesian framework. Several developed materialist alternatives holding the mind to be either identical with or entirely dependent on the material body. Their arguments are recognizable in everything that followed.

Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) maintained that only bodies exist and that thought is nothing but computation — the manipulation of representations by the brain. In *Leviathan* (1651): "REASON, in this sense, is nothing but Reckoning (that is, Adding and Subtracting) of the Consequences of general names agreed upon." Reasoning is a formal, mechanical operation on symbols — exactly what computational functionalism would say three centuries later. Sensation is a form of mechanical motion in the nervous system; imagination is decayed sensation. Hobbes was thus both a materialist and a proto-computationalist, anticipating the computational theory of mind by three centuries. His materialism was partly politically motivated: he wanted to exclude immaterial souls from playing any role in political legitimacy, clearing space for his social contract theory.

La Mettrie (1709–1751), a physician, extended Descartes's mechanistic account of animals to humans in *L'Homme Machine* (Man a Machine). Descartes had held that animals are automata — mechanical systems whose behavior requires no soul; La Mettrie simply removed the exception for humans. We too are machines, and our apparent rationality, morality, and consciousness are products of the machine's complexity. His argument rests heavily on medical observation: bodily conditions — fever, malnutrition, drug use, and aging — profoundly affect mental functioning. If mind were genuinely separate from body, bodily changes should not alter mental life. Since they do, mind depends entirely on body. This is exactly the argument Lucretius made for Epicurean atomism.

Spinoza (1632–1677) took a different and more subtle approach. Rather than asserting materialism or dualism, he held that there is only one substance — God or Nature — of which mind and body are two attributes. His argument that there is only one substance proceeds from the claim that a substance is defined by its attribute, and that two substances with different attributes cannot interact and hence cannot be part of the same causal order. Since there is only one causal order, there can be only one substance, with infinitely many attributes. Two of these attributes are accessible to finite minds: thought and extension. Every mode of substance is describable under both attributes simultaneously. A human being is a mode of substance describable under thought (as a mind with ideas) and under extension (as a body with a certain physical constitution). These two descriptions do not describe two different things but the same thing under two aspects. Spinoza's "dual-aspect monism" anticipates contemporary versions of the two-aspect theory and type-B physicalism.

## The Difficulty These Materialisms Share

The difficulty for Hobbes's materialism is the same one that faces contemporary eliminative materialism: folk psychological categories (beliefs, desires, intentions) must either be identified with physical brain-states or eliminated in favor of a physicalist account that dispenses with them. Hobbes's inclination is eliminativist — folk psychology is to be replaced by a materialist science of behavior — but he provides no worked-out account of what this replacement would look like. La Mettrie's dependence argument is powerful as an observation but underdetermines the conclusion: mental states could be causally dependent on physical states without being identical to them. The dependence argument establishes at most that the mind is not independent; it does not show that it is nothing but matter.

Spinoza's philosophical cost is different. It is unclear how the two attributes can be truly distinct, as Spinoza insists, if they always go together. If every mode under the attribute of thought has a corresponding mode under the attribute of extension, what content does the distinctness claim have? And if thought and extension are genuinely distinct, their identity seems mysterious rather than illuminating.

These early materialisms lacked the neuroscientific resources to develop in detail, but they established the intellectual credibility of the position and set the terms for the modern debate. The mind-brain identity theory of the 1950s and 1960s — Place, Smart, Armstrong — is the rigorous successor to the materialist tradition inaugurated by Hobbes and La Mettrie, equipped with the resources of scientific neurophysiology and analytic philosophy of language.
