# Enlightenment to 19th Century: Mind in an Age of Science

The Enlightenment transformed the context of the mind-body problem by grounding the authority of physical science and generating confidence that the mechanistic picture of nature that Newton's physics had established could be extended to cover all of nature, including the human mind. If the movements of the planets can be fully explained by gravitational mechanics, why not the movements of the animal body? And if the body is a mechanism, why not the mind — or at least those aspects of the mind that seem to depend on the body?

La Mettrie's L'Homme Machine (1747) stated the reductionist conclusion bluntly: man is a machine; the soul is merely a name for the organizing principle of the body; and the distinction Descartes had drawn between thinking substance and extended substance is a philosophical fiction. Consciousness, thought, and sensation are functions of the brain and nervous system, just as digestion is a function of the stomach. This was a provocation as much as a theory — La Mettrie was expressing a materialist conviction that the science of his time could not yet vindicate in detail.

Kant's response to the Enlightenment mechanists was more subtle. Kant distinguished the empirical psychology of the self — the study of the self as a natural object, subject to the same deterministic laws as the rest of nature — from the transcendental analysis of the self as the condition of experience. The self that gives unity to experience is not itself a natural object; it is the formal condition of any empirical knowledge. This distinction between the empirical and transcendental self permitted Kant to be simultaneously a determinist about nature and a defender of the rational autonomy of persons.

The nineteenth century saw the rise of experimental psychology — the attempt to study the mind scientifically using introspection, reaction times, and psychophysical methods — and the beginning of neuroscience. These developments sharpened the mind-body problem by making it concrete: specific mental functions could be correlated with specific neural processes, raising the question of what the correlation amounts to.

This section traces the development of the mind-body problem from the Enlightenment through the nineteenth century, identifying the major intellectual currents that shaped the contemporary debate.
