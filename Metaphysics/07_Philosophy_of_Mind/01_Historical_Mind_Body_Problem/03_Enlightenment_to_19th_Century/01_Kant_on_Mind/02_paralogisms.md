# Kant's Paralogisms of Pure Reason

Rational psychology — the metaphysical discipline that claimed to derive substantive knowledge of the soul from pure reason alone — held that the soul is a substance, that it is simple and therefore indestructible, that it is numerically identical over time, and that it is related to possible objects of experience as their condition. All four claims seemed to follow from the nature of the thinking subject. Kant's strategy in the "Paralogisms of Pure Reason" section of the *Critique of Pure Reason* is to show that each inference rests on the same fallacy: taking a formal, logical feature of the "I think" for a substantive metaphysical fact about a particular thing.

Each paralogism has the same structure. It takes a property that the "I" has in its role as the formal unity of apperception and incorrectly concludes that the I has this property as a substantial being. The fallacy consists in sliding between two senses of "I": the I as a formal logical unity (the transcendental "I think") and the I as a determinate entity susceptible to metaphysical description. This is exactly the error Kant attributed to the Cartesian cogito: the inference from "there is thinking" to "there is a substantial thinker" is not analytically valid; it requires the additional premise that all thinking must be the activity of a substance — and this is precisely what Kant denies.

## The First Paralogism: Substance

The rational psychologist argues:
- P1: That which thinks is a substance (since thinking requires a subject).
- P2: I think.
- C: I am a substance.

Kant's critique turns on an ambiguity in "subject." As a logical subject, the "I" is the subject of all my judgments — the thing to which all properties are attributed. But being a logical subject does not entail being a substance in the metaphysical sense — a thing that persists independently and possesses properties as modes. The logical role of subject is purely formal and reveals nothing about the ontological category of the thinker.

## The Second Paralogism: Simplicity

The rational psychologist argues:
- P1: Composite things can be resolved into their parts; simple things cannot be destroyed by resolution.
- P2: The soul is simple (the unity of consciousness is undivided).
- C: The soul is indestructible.

Kant's critique: the unity of consciousness is a formal unity of representations, not a metaphysical simplicity of the thinking subject. A physically composite brain could generate a formally unified stream of consciousness; the formal unity of the "I think" is compatible with the underlying thinker being complex. This critique is prescient: contemporary neuroscience has shown that the unified character of conscious experience is produced by massively parallel, distributed, composite neural processes, exactly as Kant predicted.

## The Third Paralogism: Personal Identity

The rational psychologist argues that the persistence of the "I" through time establishes the numerical identity of the soul across different times. Kant replies that the persistence of the "I think" as a formal unity is compatible with the replacement of the underlying substance: just as a series of rubber balls passing through a tube might transmit motion without any single ball persisting throughout, a series of underlying states might transmit the formal unity of consciousness without any single substance persisting. The numerical identity of the "I" in logical function does not establish the numerical identity of a substance.

This anticipates Derek Parfit's analysis of personal identity in *Reasons and Persons* (1984). Parfit argued that personal identity is not what matters in survival; what matters is psychological continuity, which does not require the persistence of a single substance. The Kantian critique of the personal identity paralogism can be read as clearing the ground for this Parfitian analysis.

## The Paralogisms and Contemporary Self-Knowledge

The paralogisms are historically significant because they undercut rationalist psychology from within: even granting the transcendental framework, Kant shows that substantive claims about the soul's nature cannot be derived from pure reason. They anticipate contemporary debates about whether introspection can deliver substantive metaphysical knowledge. Sydney Shoemaker's work on self-knowledge and immunity to error through misidentification engages directly with themes Kant raised. Shoemaker argues that first-person judgments about one's own psychological states are "immune to error through misidentification" — I cannot be wrong about who is in pain when I judge that I am in pain — but this immunity is a structural feature of how self-referring thoughts work, not a sign of privileged access to an immaterial substance. The special character of self-knowledge is formal, not substantive. The parallel with Kant is exact.
