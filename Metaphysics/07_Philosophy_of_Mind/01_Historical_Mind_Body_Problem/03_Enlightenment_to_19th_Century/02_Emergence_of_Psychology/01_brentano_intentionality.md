# Brentano and Intentionality

Consider the difference between a rock and a thought. The rock can be described exhaustively in terms of mass, volume, and molecular structure. A thought about a unicorn cannot. The thought is *about* something — the unicorn — even though no unicorn exists anywhere. Rocks are not about anything. This simple observation is, for Franz Brentano (1838–1917), the fundamental divide between the mental and the physical. Intentionality — the directedness of mental acts toward objects — is the mark of the mental. Every mental act (perceiving, judging, loving, hating, desiring) is directed toward an object. No physical thing has this property. Brentano introduced this thesis in *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874), and it has structured the philosophy of mind ever since.

The object of a mental act is what Brentano called "intentionally inexistent" — it is "immanent" in or "contained in" the act, not necessarily an external existent. When I think of a unicorn, the unicorn exists as an intentional object of my thought, even though no actual unicorn exists in the world. The phrase "intentional inexistence" generated interpretive controversy: Brentano appears to say that the object of a mental act exists "in" the act — but does this mean that mental objects are always mental? If so, it would imply idealism. Later interpreters, especially Husserl, distinguished between the act, the intentional content (the way the object is represented), and the possible real object (what the content is about). This act-content-object distinction became central in phenomenology.

Brentano classified all mental phenomena into three fundamental classes distinguished by their intentional mode. *Presentations* (*Vorstellungen*) are the most basic: having an object before the mind in the most minimal sense, without taking any stance toward it. All other mental acts are based on presentations. *Judgments* are acts in which one affirms or denies the existence of a presented object — a binary character that distinguishes them from mere presentations. *Phenomena of love and hate* — emotions, desires, will — are acts in which one takes a positive or negative attitude toward a presented object. This taxonomy grounds the distinction between cognitive, doxastic, and conative mental states that plays a central role in contemporary philosophy of mind and action theory.

## Inner Perception and the Epistemology of Psychology

Brentano distinguished between *inner perception* (*innere Wahrnehmung*) and *inner observation* (*innere Beobachtung*). Inner perception is the immediate awareness of one's own current mental acts — the infallible, non-introspective consciousness that accompanies every mental act. When I see a tree, I am immediately aware (without a separate act of attention) that I am seeing. This immediate awareness is incorrigible. Inner observation, by contrast, is the reflective, attentive focus on one's mental acts that introspective psychology requires. Brentano argued that inner observation is impossible in strict terms: to attend to one's mental act is to change it, so the act one observes is never the act one attends to. This creates a methodological problem for introspective psychology that behaviorism would later seize on.

## The Naturalization of Intentionality

The problem Brentano identified — how physical systems could have intentional states — is a central issue in contemporary philosophy of mind. The problem of non-existent objects — how one can think about things that do not exist — has generated a remarkable literature. Frege's sense-reference distinction, Meinong's theory of non-existent objects, Russell's theory of descriptions, and contemporary possible-worlds semantics all attempt to address the puzzle that "intentional inexistence" raised.

The contemporary debate about naturalizing intentionality attempts to explain, in purely physical and functional terms, the aboutness that Brentano identified as irreducibly mental. Dretske's informational theory holds that a mental state has a certain content if it is causally produced by — and naturally carries information about — a specific type of state of affairs. Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory holds that a representation R means P if and only if R-tokens that are not caused by P-instances depend asymmetrically on R-tokens that are caused by P-instances. Millikan's teleosemantic theory grounds content in evolutionary function. Each of these is an attempt to explain in naturalistic terms the property Brentano thought was categorically distinct from anything physical. Whether any of them succeeds is still very much in dispute.
