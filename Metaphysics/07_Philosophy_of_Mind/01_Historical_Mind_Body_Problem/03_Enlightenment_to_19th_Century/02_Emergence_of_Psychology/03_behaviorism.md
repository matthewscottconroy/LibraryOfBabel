# Behaviorism

The introspective psychologists of the late nineteenth century had made a bet: consciousness can be studied scientifically by training subjects to report accurately on their own mental states. The bet failed. Different laboratories using the same introspective method reached contradictory conclusions about the fundamental elements of conscious experience, and there was no principled way to adjudicate between them. If psychology is to be a science, it must study something publicly observable. Behavior is publicly observable; consciousness is not. This is the argument that gave behaviorism its momentum.

Methodological behaviorism in psychology — associated with J.B. Watson and B.F. Skinner — held that psychology should restrict itself to the study of observable behavior, stimulus-response relationships, and should not posit unobservable internal mental states. Watson was direct: psychology has no more need of "consciousness" than physics has need of the phlogiston. Logical behaviorism in philosophy — associated primarily with Gilbert Ryle, with roots in Carnap and the Vienna Circle — went further by claiming that mental vocabulary does not refer to inner, private mental events but is rather a way of describing behavioral dispositions. To say that Jones believes it is raining is to say that Jones is disposed to carry an umbrella, to stay inside, to agree with "is it raining?" and so on. Mental states are logical constructs out of behavioral dispositions.

The logical positivist motivation matters here. The Vienna Circle held that the meaning of a sentence is its method of verification; a sentence that cannot in principle be verified by empirical observation is meaningless. Applied to psychology: "Jones is in pain" means something about Jones's behavioral responses — wincing, crying out, seeking relief. Inner phenomenal pain, as something over and above behavioral disposition, is on this view literally meaningless. Carnap's "Psychology in Physical Language" (1932) attempted to show that all psychological statements could be translated into statements about physical states and behavioral dispositions. The translations turned out to be impossible to provide — this was a technical failure — but the program motivated a generation of philosophers to take the behaviorist approach seriously.

## Ryle and the Category Mistake

Ryle's *The Concept of Mind* (1949) is the philosophical classic of logical behaviorism. Ryle argued that Descartes committed a "category mistake" — treating the mind as a "ghost in the machine" when mental predicates in fact describe patterns of behavior and capacity. His category-mistake argument is memorable. Suppose a visitor to Oxford is shown the colleges, libraries, sports grounds, and offices. After viewing all of these, the visitor asks: "But where is the University?" They have assumed that "the University" is an additional thing alongside the buildings — a separate entity. But "the University" refers to the organization of all those things, not a further item in the list. Similarly, "the mind" is not an additional inner thing alongside behavior — it is the disposition or organization of behavior.

Ryle used the concept of a "disposition" to handle mental ascriptions. To say someone is intelligent is not to say they have a hidden internal state of intelligence; it is to say they are disposed to perform intelligently across a wide range of tasks. Dispositions are real — they make predictions possible — but they are not inner occurrent states.

## Objections and the Cognitive Revolution

Behaviorism's failures were decisive, and three objections are worth marking clearly. First: the same behavioral disposition can be produced by radically different inner states. Two people might behave identically while having very different mental lives — one genuinely in pain, one pretending. Behaviorism cannot distinguish these cases. Second: the behavioral dispositions associated with a given belief depend on the believer's other beliefs and desires. Jones's belief that it is raining produces umbrella-carrying only if Jones wants to stay dry, believes the umbrella will work, and so on. There is no finite behavioral specification of any single belief independent of all others. This holism makes the reduction impossible. Third: phenomenal states — pain, color experience, emotional feeling — seem to have an intrinsic character entirely absent from behavioral descriptions. A spectrum-inverted person who experiences red as we experience green but has identical behavioral dispositions is a conceptual possibility. If so, behavioral dispositions do not exhaust mental content.

Noam Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* delivered the coup de grâce. Chomsky argued that language acquisition cannot be explained in terms of stimulus-response conditioning: children acquire grammatical rules they have never been taught and generate sentences they have never heard. This requires positing an internal language acquisition device — an abstract grammatical competence entirely beyond the reach of behaviorist description.

Despite its failure as a reductive theory, behaviorism left lasting marks. The insight that mental attributions are connected to patterns of behavior persists in functionalism. The functionalist holds that mental states are defined by their functional (causal) roles, which include connections to behavioral outputs — but adds that inner causal structure matters too. Functionalism is behaviorism corrected for the objection that inner causes are real and explanatorily relevant.
