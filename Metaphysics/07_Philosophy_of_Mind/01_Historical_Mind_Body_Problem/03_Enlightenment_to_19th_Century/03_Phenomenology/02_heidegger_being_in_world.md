# Heidegger and Being-in-the-World

When you write with a pen, you are not normally aware of the pen. You are aware through the pen of the paper, the words, the thought you are trying to express. The pen withdraws from attention into the activity. When the pen runs dry, the withdrawal ends: now the pen is an object, something that has failed you, something you must attend to as a thing. Heidegger calls these two modes "ready-to-hand" (*zuhanden*) and "present-at-hand" (*vorhanden*). The crucial point is the order of priority. The Cartesian tradition started with explicit, thematic mental representations and asked how they relate to the world. Heidegger inverts this: absorbed, skillful engagement with the world is more fundamental. Explicit representation is derivative, arising when our absorbed coping is disrupted.

This inversion is the organizing idea of *Being and Time* (1927). Martin Heidegger (1889–1976) argued that Husserl's phenomenology remained trapped within the Cartesian framework it sought to overcome, by starting with the isolated subject's experience and asking how it relates to the world. Heidegger proposed to replace this starting point with a more fundamental structure: *Being-in-the-world*. Being-in-the-world is not the Cartesian picture of a mind inside a body inside a world. It is a unitary structure: Dasein — human existence — just is always already in a world, engaged with things and people in a practical, concerned way. The primary mode of engagement is not theoretical observation but practical coping. And practical coping is, ordinarily, non-thematic: we use tools without thinking about them, navigate environments without representing them, pursue projects without explicitly deliberating about each step.

## Dasein and Existence

Heidegger's fundamental ontological distinction is between things that merely exist — objects that are simply present — and Dasein, whose being is at issue for it. Dasein exists in the mode of caring about its own existence. The concept of *thrownness* (*Geworfenheit*) captures the fact that Dasein always finds itself already in a situation it did not choose: a particular historical, cultural, linguistic, and bodily situation that pre-structures what is possible for it. One is thrown into a world with particular moods (*Stimmungen*), which are not subjective emotional states but fundamental ways in which the world opens up for one. Anxiety (*Angst*), for Heidegger, is the mood that discloses Dasein's fundamental groundlessness — the absence of any fixed, pre-given essence. The concept of *projection* (*Entwurf*) captures the forward-directed structure of existence: Dasein is always ahead of itself, projecting toward possible ways of being. Existence is not a static state but an ongoing process of self-interpretation and self-constitution through choices and projects.

Heidegger's analysis of death connects all of this. Dasein's being is essentially temporal, stretched out between birth and death. It is only by anticipating its own death — its "ownmost, non-relational, certain, and indefinite" possibility — that Dasein can take ownership of its existence and achieve authentic selfhood. Authentic existence means owning one's finitude rather than fleeing into the "they" (*das Man*), the anonymous public world in which no one is individually responsible for anything.

## Implications for Cognitive Science and AI

Contemporary cognitive science has drawn heavily on Heidegger. Hubert Dreyfus used Heideggerian arguments to criticize classical AI — which modeled cognition as the manipulation of explicit symbolic representations — and to argue for embodied, situated cognition as the proper model of intelligence. In *What Computers Can't Do* (1972) and subsequent works, Dreyfus argued that the GOFAI (Good Old-Fashioned AI) program misses what is fundamental about human intelligence: its situatedness in a concrete, practical world. Human expertise is not the application of explicit rules but the exercise of skills that are embodied and context-sensitive — emerging from years of engaged practice, not from the formal encoding of knowledge. Skillful coping is primary; explicit rule-following is secondary and derivative.

This critique anticipated many of the difficulties of early AI systems and informed the development of embodied robotics and situated AI. Andy Clark, Alva Noë, and others have developed related themes under the heading of "embodied cognition." Whether contemporary deep learning systems escape Dreyfus's critique is a live question: these systems exhibit impressive behavioral flexibility without encoding explicit rules. But whether they engage with the world in the sense Heidegger had in mind — with care, projects, and finitude — remains deeply contested, and the question connects directly to debates about strong versus weak AI.
