# Important Terms and Ideas: Enlightenment to 19th-Century Philosophy of Mind

**Dual-Aspect Theory**
Spinoza's view that the one infinite substance has both thought and extension as attributes. Mind and body are not distinct substances but the same reality described under two aspects. Mental and physical events are parallel expressions of one underlying reality—neither reduces to the other.

**Pre-established Harmony**
Leibniz's solution to the interaction problem: God pre-programmed all monads at creation so that their internal developments unfold in perfect coordination. Mind and body do not interact causally; they run in parallel like two synchronized clocks set by the same clockmaker.

**Materialism / Mechanism**
The Enlightenment thesis (developed by La Mettrie, d'Holbach, and others) that human beings are entirely material systems governed by mechanical laws. Mental properties are products of the organization and motion of matter; no immaterial soul is required to explain thought, feeling, or behavior.

**The Paralogisms**
Kant's critique of rational psychology: the metaphysical arguments that the soul is a substance, is simple, is numerically identical over time, and stands in a specific relation to objects. Kant argues each argument commits a logical error, confusing features of the logical subject of thought with features of a metaphysical substance.

**Transcendental Unity of Apperception**
Kant's term for the "I think" that must be able to accompany all my representations—the formal unity of consciousness. This is not a substantial self but a logical condition for the possibility of experience; knowledge of it does not support claims about the soul's nature or immortality.

**Idealism**
The view that reality is fundamentally mental or mind-dependent. German Idealists (Fichte, Schelling, Hegel) developed increasingly systematic idealist accounts of mind and nature in response to Kant, arguing that the distinction between mind and world is itself a product of self-positing reason.
