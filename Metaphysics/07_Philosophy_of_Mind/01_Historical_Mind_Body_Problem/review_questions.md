# Review Questions — The Historical Mind-Body Problem

1. Plato's Principle of Non-Opposition is supposed to establish that the soul has distinct parts by showing that a single thing cannot simultaneously desire and refrain from desiring. Why is this argument not decisive, and what does Aristotle's alternative account of psychological conflict look like?

2. Plato treats the soul-body relation as essentially adversarial — the body distracts, deceives, and corrupts the soul's pursuit of knowledge. Aristotle treats the soul as the form of the body, making separation unintelligible. What is at stake between these two pictures, and which faces the more serious internal difficulties?

3. Plato's arguments for immortality — the Affinity Argument, the Argument from Recollection, and the Form of Life Argument — are offered as mutually reinforcing. Yet Cebes objects that even granting pre-existence, post-mortem survival does not follow. Which of Plato's arguments, if any, escapes this objection, and what does the failure of the others reveal about the structure of his case?

4. Aristotle's levels of soul — nutritive, sensitive, rational — form a nested hierarchy in which higher capacities depend on lower ones. Does this hierarchical structure support or undermine the claim that the active intellect is separable from the body? What hangs on how we answer this question for Aristotle's account of personal survival?

5. Descartes's cogito is meant to establish not just that thinking is occurring but that a thinking *substance* exists. Lichtenberg objected that "there is thinking" (*es denkt*) is all the data warrant. What would need to be true for Lichtenberg's objection to succeed, and what does Kant's reading of the cogito add to this critique?

6. Princess Elisabeth's challenge to Descartes about mind-body causal interaction is sometimes dismissed as a demand for a mechanism where none is possible. But the interaction problem is structural, not merely mechanical. Explain the structural form of the problem, and assess whether occasionalism and pre-established harmony genuinely dissolve it or merely relocate it.

7. Brentano identified intentionality — the directedness of mental acts toward objects — as the mark of the mental, distinguishing it from anything physical. Merleau-Ponty later argued that the body itself has a form of intentional directedness ("motor intentionality") that is not mediated by mental representations. Are these two uses of "intentionality" compatible, and what would it mean for Brentano's thesis if Merleau-Ponty is right?

8. Kant's transcendental unity of apperception is presented as a formal condition on experience rather than a discovery of a substantial self. How does this position navigate between Descartes (the self is a substance discovered by introspection) and Hume (there is no self, only a bundle of perceptions)? Does Kant succeed in avoiding both extremes, or does he merely defer the question of what the "I" amounts to?
