# Physicalism and Materialism

The most natural response to Descartes's substance dualism — two fundamentally different kinds of thing, mental and physical, interacting somehow — is to deny one of the two substances. Idealism denies matter and reduces everything to mind; physicalism denies mind (as a separate substance) and holds that everything is ultimately physical. Physicalism is the dominant position in contemporary philosophy of mind, but it comes in importantly different forms with importantly different commitments.

The most straightforward version is type identity theory, associated with Smart and Place in the 1950s: mental state types are identical to brain state types. Pain just is C-fiber firing — not caused by it, not correlated with it, but strictly and literally identical to it. This provides a clean materialist ontology: there are no additional mental substances or properties over and above physical ones. The serious difficulties arise from multiple realizability: the same mental state (pain) can be realized in different physical substrates — human neurons, octopus neurons, silicon circuits, if functionalists are right. If pain can be realized in multiple physical systems, pain cannot be identical to any particular type of physical state.

Non-reductive physicalism attempts to preserve physicalism without type identity. Mental properties are real but not identical to physical properties. They are nonetheless determined by physical properties — there is no mental difference without a physical difference (supervenience) — but they are not reducible to them. Non-reductive physicalism faces the exclusion problem: if physical events have sufficient physical causes, and mental properties are distinct from physical properties, there seems to be no room for mental properties to do any causal work. A mental property that makes no causal difference seems to be an epiphenomenon — causally inert, along for the ride.

This chapter develops both forms of physicalism, the arguments for them, and the serious objections they face. Understanding these positions is necessary preparation for the subsequent chapters on dualism, functionalism, and the hard problem of consciousness.
