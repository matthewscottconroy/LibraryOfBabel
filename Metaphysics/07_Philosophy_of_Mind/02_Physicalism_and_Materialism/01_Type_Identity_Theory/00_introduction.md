# Type Identity Theory

The identity theory of mind — associated with Place, Smart, and Feigl in the late 1950s — offered what seemed like the most straightforward resolution of the mind-body problem: mental states just are brain states. Not correlated with, not caused by, not parallel to — identical to. Pain is identical to C-fiber firing (or whatever the correct neural correlate turns out to be). Belief that it will rain is identical to some pattern of neural activity. The mind is the brain, and the apparent gap between mental and physical description is no more mysterious than the gap between "water" and "H₂O" — two descriptions of the same thing.

Type identity theory holds that mental types — types of mental state — are identical to physical types — types of neural or physical state. Every mental state type corresponds to a physical state type, and the two descriptions pick out the very same type of state. This is the strongest form of mind-brain identity: it allows genuinely reductive explanation of mental phenomena in physical terms, grounds mental causation in physical causation (since mental states just are physical states), and promises to eliminate the explanatory gap between psychology and neuroscience.

The decisive objection to type identity theory is the multiple realizability argument, pressed by Hilary Putnam. If pain is a mental state type that can be realized in humans, octopuses, and (potentially) silicon robots with very different physical constitutions, then pain cannot be identical to any particular physical state type, since the physical state types that realize pain in these different creatures are completely different. The type identity theory would imply that octopus pain and human pain are different kinds of pain, if they are realized by different physical types — which seems wrong. And it would deny that any non-biological creature could feel pain — which seems question-begging.

Multiple realizability suggests that mental properties are not physical properties at the level of types, even if mental tokens (particular mental state instances) are identical to physical tokens. This opened the way for non-reductive physicalism. This section examines type identity theory, the multiple realizability objection, and the responses available to the identity theorist.
