# Place and Smart: Origins of the Identity Theory

Two options had dominated the philosophy of mind: either mental states are immaterial (dualism) or they are just behavioral dispositions (behaviorism). Both options had been found wanting. Dualism could not explain causal interaction; behaviorism could not account for the intrinsic felt character of mental states — pain really hurts, and no behavioral disposition captures what hurting is. In the late 1950s, U.T. Place and J.J.C. Smart proposed a third option: mental states are identical to brain states. Not merely correlated with them, not merely caused by them, but identical — in the same way that water is identical to H₂O and lightning is identical to electrical discharge.

U.T. Place's 1956 paper "Is Consciousness a Brain Process?" argued that consciousness is a brain process in precisely this sense: an empirical identity, not a logical necessity. "Consciousness is a brain process" is synthetic, contingent, and could turn out to be false — but it is the best scientific hypothesis available. Place carefully identified what he called the "phenomenological fallacy" — a systematic confusion that seemed to make the identity theory impossible. When I describe my visual experience of a red tomato, I seem to describe the experience as having a red, round, sensory quality. It can seem to follow that there is something red and round in my head. And no brain process is red and round. Therefore, the experience cannot be identical to a brain process. Place's response: when I describe my experience, I am describing what it is an experience *of* — I am reporting the content, not the intrinsic properties of the experience itself. A photograph of a red tomato is not itself red. Similarly, an experience that represents redness is not itself red. The phenomenological fallacy is the mistake of reading a description of the content of an experience as a description of the intrinsic properties of the experience.

J.J.C. Smart extended and refined Place's proposal in "Sensations and Brain Processes" (1959). Smart's contribution was the topic-neutral analysis of sensation reports. A report like "I see a reddish-orange after-image" should not be analyzed as "There is in me a reddish-orange entity" — which generates a regress — but rather as: "There is something going on in me that is like what goes on when I am in the presence of reddish-orange things in good light with normal vision." This analysis is topic-neutral: it does not specify whether the inner state is mental (in the Cartesian sense) or physical. It merely says that something is going on of a certain relational kind. The identity theorist can then say: whatever that inner state is, it turns out (empirically) to be a brain state.

## Methodological Revolution and Historical Significance

Place and Smart wrote in the aftermath of logical behaviorism's dominance. Their proposal offered a third way: there are genuine inner mental states — not behavioral dispositions — and these inner states are physical brain states. Mental states cause behavior, and they do so because they are identical to the neural states that cause behavior in virtue of their physical-chemical properties. This was methodologically revolutionary: it made the mind-body problem an empirical problem, to be confirmed or disconfirmed as neuroscience advances. This moved the debate from conceptual analysis to scientific research — a methodological shift that shaped subsequent philosophy of mind profoundly.

Herbert Feigl, in "The 'Mental' and the 'Physical'" (1958), independently developed similar ideas with special attention to phenomenal properties — what he called "raw feels." Feigl argued that raw feels are identical to brain states, and that the apparent mystery of consciousness dissolves once we accept this identity. The question of why raw feels should accompany particular neural processes — what Feigl called the "nomological dangler" problem — is dissolved if the raw feels just are those neural processes, described under a different concept. Feigl's contribution anticipates later discussion of the "type-B physicalist" position: the view that there is a conceptual gap between phenomenal and physical concepts, but no metaphysical gap, because phenomenal properties are identical to physical properties even though this cannot be known a priori. Together, Place, Smart, and Feigl made the identity theory a serious scientific hypothesis and the target against which functionalism, non-reductive physicalism, and property dualism were subsequently developed as alternatives.
