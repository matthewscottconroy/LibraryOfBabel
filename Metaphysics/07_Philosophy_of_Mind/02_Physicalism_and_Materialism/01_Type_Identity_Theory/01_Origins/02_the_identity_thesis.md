# The Identity Thesis

When scientists discovered that water is H₂O, they did not merely discover that every sample of water is accompanied by some H₂O. They discovered that water and H₂O are the same thing — numerically identical, not merely correlated. The identity is a posteriori (we had to discover it) and contingent in some sense (we could have been wrong), but it is strict identity nonetheless: there is nothing more to water than being H₂O. The mind-brain identity theory proposes the same kind of identification for mental and neural states. Pain is not merely correlated with C-fiber firing; pain is C-fiber firing, just as water is H₂O. The concepts are different — which is why the identity is informative rather than trivial — but what the concepts pick out is numerically the same thing.

Several clarifications matter immediately. The identity thesis is a *type* identity claim: it identifies types of mental state (pain, belief that it is raining, desire for water) with types of neural state (C-fiber firing, a particular pattern of prefrontal cortex activation, and so on). This is to be distinguished from *token* identity: the claim that individual instances (tokens) of mental events are identical to individual instances of physical events, but without asserting that the types correspond. Token identity is much weaker and is accepted by most physicalists; type identity makes the stronger claim that the type pain is the type C-fiber firing. Putnam's multiple realizability objection specifically targets this stronger claim.

The identity is a posteriori and contingent. "Pain = C-fiber firing" is analogous to "water = H₂O" — a scientific discovery about what something is, not a conceptual truth. Just as we discovered by empirical investigation that water is H₂O, we will discover by neuroscientific investigation which neural process pain is identical with. The identity theory is thus a scientific hypothesis, not a piece of conceptual analysis.

## The Model of Scientific Reduction

The identity thesis is modeled on successful scientific reductions: temperature was identified with mean molecular kinetic energy; lightning with electrical discharge; water with H₂O. In each case, a commonsense concept was identified with a scientific concept through empirical investigation. The identity allowed scientific explanation to proceed without remainder: once we know that temperature is mean molecular kinetic energy, we understand why objects with high temperature burn us, why heat spreads from hot to cold, and so on. The identity theorist proposes to do the same for mental states. Once we identify pain with C-fiber firing, we will understand why pain has its behavioral effects — causing avoidance behavior, attention allocation — in terms of the neural properties of C-fiber firing. The phenomenal character of pain — the "what it is like" — will also turn out to be a property of C-fiber firing, once we understand it properly.

The implications are significant. If mental types are brain types, then there is nothing "over and above" the physical in mental life. Consciousness, thought, and desire are all physical — described in mentalistic vocabulary that we have not yet learned to translate fully into neural vocabulary. Eliminative materialists will later argue that this translation will show folk psychology to be a bad theory that should be abandoned; the identity theorist holds that the folk categories will survive as approximately correct specifications of neural kinds.

The type identity theory also has implications for the completeness of physical explanation. If every mental type is a neural type, then every mental event is fully explained by its neural properties. There is no need for any additional mental-level explanation beyond what the neural explanation provides. This is a form of strict reductionism: the mental reduces to the physical, and psychological explanation reduces to neuroscientific explanation. It is precisely this reductionism that multiple realizability challenges: if pain can be realized by radically different neural structures in different species — or, more radically, by silicon systems — then what neural type is the type pain identical to? This is the question that functionalism was designed to answer.
