# The Revival of Type Identity Theory

Type identity theory was declared dead by multiple realizability. But reports of its death were premature. The theory has experienced something of a revival in recent decades, partly in response to problems encountered by functionalism and non-reductive physicalism, and partly due to new arguments in its favor.

The most significant impetus for revival came from an unexpected source: Jaegwon Kim's work on mental causation. Kim argued that non-reductive physicalism faces an exclusion problem — if mental properties are genuinely distinct from physical properties, then either mental events are causally inert (epiphenomenalism) or they overdetermine physical effects alongside their physical realizers (systematic overdetermination). The most natural escape from this dilemma is reduction: mental properties are physical properties, and mental causation is just a specification of physical causation. This is type identity. The irony is that multiple realizability was supposed to deliver us from identity theory, but Kim's exclusion argument suggests that the destination — non-reductive physicalism — is unstable. The fork in the road leads either back to identity or forward to epiphenomenalism.

Ned Block and Robert Stalnaker's paper "Conceptual Analysis, Dualism, and the Explanatory Gap" pressed a related point. The apparent explanatory gap between physical and phenomenal descriptions does not establish any metaphysical gap. The appearance of a gap is a cognitive or epistemic phenomenon — it reflects our concepts and our limited ability to see how physical processes give rise to consciousness — not a reflection of the underlying metaphysical reality. Just as water = H₂O is a genuine identity even though one can conceive of water without conceiving of H₂O, pain = C-fiber firing might be a genuine identity even if one can conceive of pain without conceiving of C-fiber firing.

## New Wave Reductionism and Contemporary Defenders

Brian McLaughlin and others developed "new wave reductionism" — a sophisticated form of type identity theory that takes seriously the lessons of multiple realizability while preserving the reductionist ambition. New wave reductionism holds that psychological generalizations are reducible to neurobiological ones, but the reduction is not a straightforward translation; it involves showing how the higher-level generalizations are approximations or abstractions of lower-level regularities. The key insight is that scientific reduction is not elimination. When chemistry reduces to physics, chemical kinds are not shown to be illusory — they are shown to be physical kinds described at a higher level of abstraction. Similarly, when (eventually) psychology reduces to neuroscience, mental kinds will be shown to be neural kinds described at a higher level of abstraction. The reduction vindicates mental kinds rather than eliminating them.

David Papineau, in *Philosophical Naturalism* (1993) and *Thinking about Consciousness* (2002), develops a broadly physicalist and broadly identity-theoretic position. Papineau's central argument is that the causal completeness of physics — the principle that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause — implies that mental events must be identical to physical events if they are to be causally efficacious. Papineau also argues that the philosophical intuitions supporting property dualism — the intuition that phenomenal properties are something more than physical properties, even if correlated with them — are explicable in terms of a cognitive illusion. We have two conceptual modes of access to our mental states: a phenomenal mode (directly experiencing them) and a physical-functional mode (thinking about them as neural or functional states). Using both modes of access creates the illusion of two properties, when there is really only one property accessed in two ways.

Contemporary defenders of type identity theory are more sophisticated than the original Place-Smart proposals, taking seriously the lessons of multiple realizability, the explanatory gap, and the phenomenal character of experience — while insisting that these considerations do not ultimately establish any non-physical properties. Whether they are right remains genuinely open. The revival of type identity demonstrates, at minimum, that the multiple realizability argument, while powerful, was not the final word.
