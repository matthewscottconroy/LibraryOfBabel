# Folk Psychology

When I explain that Jones went to the store because he believed it was open and desired to buy milk, I am deploying a framework so familiar it feels like merely describing the facts. This is folk psychology — the everyday practice of explaining and predicting behavior by attributing mental states: beliefs, desires, intentions, emotions, memories. It is "folk" because it is the commonsense psychology practiced by everyone, not the theoretical psychology of trained researchers. And the status of this framework — whether it constitutes a genuine theory, and whether that theory is approximately true or radically false — is one of the central questions in the philosophy of mind.

Two questions dominate. First: is folk psychology a theory, or something else? Treating folk psychology as a theory (the "Theory Theory") invites the question of whether the theory is true or false. The "Theory Theory" (Gopnik, Wellman, Stich) holds that folk psychological competence involves possessing a tacit theory, with general laws like "If an agent wants P and believes that doing A will bring about P, then, ceteris paribus, the agent will do A." Children acquire these generalizations through development — theory of mind develops around age 4, marked by the ability to pass false belief tasks. An alternative, the "Simulation Theory" (Gordon, Heal, Goldman), holds that folk psychological prediction works by mentally simulating the target person's situation: imagining being in their position and asking what you would do, rather than applying explicit generalizations. On this view, folk psychology does not require theoretical knowledge at all — only the ability to use one's own cognitive and motivational systems as a model of others'. The eliminativist needs to establish that folk psychology has theoretical commitments if the historical analogy (to phlogiston, caloric) is to work. Theoretical interpretations make folk psychology vulnerable to empirical refutation; practical skill interpretations do not.

## Folk Psychological Generalizations and Their Status

The central folk psychological generalization is the belief-desire schema: if agent A wants state S and believes that action X will bring about S, and has no stronger conflicting desire or belief, then A will do X. This generalization is striking in two respects. It is extraordinarily broad, applying across all humans and apparently to many non-human animals. And it has indefinitely many exceptions: agents can fail to act as predicted due to weakness of will, cognitive impairment, self-deception, compulsion, and countless other factors. The ceteris paribus clause is so demanding that the generalization may have no clear instances.

Eliminativists (Stich, P. Churchland) argue that these ceteris paribus generalizations are scientific liabilities: theories with unlimited escape clauses are unfalsifiable, and unfalsifiable theories are not scientifically respectable. Defenders of folk psychology (Fodor) argue that all special-science generalizations are ceteris paribus — biology, economics, and geology all have laws qualified by exceptions. The appropriate response is to seek conditions under which the generalizations hold, not to eliminate them.

## Dennett's Intentional Stance

Daniel Dennett's intentional stance holds that folk psychology is not a theory about inner mental states but a predictive strategy. When we adopt the intentional stance toward a system — treating it as if it has beliefs and desires — we can often accurately predict its behavior. The intentional stance is instrumentally valuable without requiring that beliefs and desires be real entities in the way rocks and cells are real.

Dennett occupies an intermediate position. Against the eliminativist: beliefs and desires are "real patterns" in the behavior of complex systems — not fictional, but genuinely predictively relevant. Against the Fodor-style realist: beliefs and desires are not discrete inner states with determinate physical realizers; they are patterns that emerge at the level of the whole system's behavior, and their attribution is a matter of strategic interpretation rather than factual description. The folk psychology debate connects to broader questions in philosophy of science about the status of theoretical posits, the relationship between eliminability and reduction, and Sellars's distinction between the "manifest image" — everyday categories — and the "scientific image" — the categories of fundamental science.
