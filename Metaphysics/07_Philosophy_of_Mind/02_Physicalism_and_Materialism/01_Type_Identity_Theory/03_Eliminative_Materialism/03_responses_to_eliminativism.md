# Responses to Eliminative Materialism

Eliminative materialism has attracted several lines of criticism, ranging from self-refutation arguments to arguments that folk psychological categories are more robust than eliminativists assume.

The self-refutation objection is the most direct. Eliminativism, if true, cannot be believed, endorsed, or argued for — since believing, endorsing, and arguing presuppose folk psychological categories: beliefs, commitments, reasons. If there are no such things as beliefs, then the eliminativist cannot have the belief that eliminativism is true. This is not a formal contradiction, but it suggests that eliminativism undermines the very cognitive practices — rational argument — that its proponents engage in. Paul Churchland's response is that this objection assumes any adequate replacement for folk psychology must preserve folk categories. Future neuroscientific vocabulary may fulfill the roles currently played by "belief" and "argument" without using those concepts. But this is a promissory note; the actual cash value remains unclear.

The success objection is more powerful. Folk psychology is extraordinarily successful as a predictive and explanatory framework. We constantly and accurately predict each other's behavior by attributing beliefs and desires. If folk psychology were radically false, this success would be mysterious. Jerry Fodor has been the most persistent and systematic defender of folk psychology's scientific respectability. In *Psychosemantics* (1987) and *The Elm and the Expert* (1994), Fodor argues that folk psychological generalizations are indispensable for predicting and explaining human behavior, and that indispensability is evidence that they capture real causal patterns. Moreover, cognitive science — seeking to understand the neural implementation of cognition in terms of information-processing structures — presupposes that there are cognitive structures to implement. Eliminativism, by denying that there are beliefs and desires, would eliminate the very phenomena that cognitive science is trying to explain.

## Baker's Practical Realism and the Comparison with Other Eliminations

Lynne Rudder Baker defends folk psychology through "practical realism" — the view that folk psychological categories are indispensable for practical agency and social life. We cannot engage in moral reasoning, hold each other responsible, participate in social institutions, or make ordinary decisions without deploying folk psychological concepts. This practical indispensability reflects a genuine domain of causal explanation irreducible to any lower-level description. Eliminativism, taken seriously, would have absurd practical consequences: we could not hold each other morally responsible, promise, apologize, or engage in the forms of recognition that constitute moral community.

Notice, too, that the comparison with phlogiston and caloric may not be apt. Phlogiston was eliminated — but oxygen still exists; the explanation changed, not the reality being explained. Caloric was displaced by kinetic energy — the reality being explained (temperature, heat transfer) was vindicated, though the explanation changed. By analogy, even if folk psychology's explanations are imperfect, the reality it is trying to explain — the intentional behavior of agents — is real. Mature neuroscience might displace folk psychological explanations without eliminating the intentional, reason-responsive behavior that folk psychology tracks. This is the "hermeneutic" response: folk psychological categories are indispensable for describing the intentional dimension of human behavior, even if they do not describe discrete inner neural states.

Dennett's intentional stance occupies its own intermediate position. Against the eliminativist, beliefs and desires are "real patterns" in behavioral dispositions — not fictional entities. Against the Fodor-style realist, they are not discrete inner states with determinate physical realizers but patterns at a different level of description. The question of whether this intermediate position is stable — or whether it collapses into either eliminativism or realism — is still very much in debate.
