# Important Terms and Ideas: Type Identity Theory

**Type Identity Theory**
The view that mental state types (pain, belief, desire) are identical to neural state types—that what it is to be in pain is to be in a certain type of brain state. This is a strong reduction of psychology to neuroscience, claiming that the types themselves are identical, not merely correlated.

**Token Identity**
The weaker claim that each token mental event is identical to some token physical event, without requiring that the same mental type always corresponds to the same physical type. Token identity is compatible with multiple realizability, which type identity is not.

**Multiple Realizability**
Putnam's objection to type identity theory: the same mental state type (e.g., pain) can be realized by different physical substrates in different organisms—or even in silicon. If pain is multiply realizable, it cannot be identical to any single physical type. This objection drove philosophers toward functionalism.

**Neural Reduction**
The project of reducing psychological explanations to neurophysiological ones: showing that mental states, properly understood, are nothing but neural states described at a higher level of abstraction. Smart and Place argued that this reduction is empirically achievable and philosophically desirable.

**Theoretical Identity**
Smart's analogy: the identity of mental states with brain processes is like the identity of water with H₂O or lightning with electrical discharge—an empirical discovery that identifies two descriptions of the same thing. No a priori argument establishes such identities; they are discovered by science.

**The Leibniz's Law Objection**
An objection to identity theory: if pain and C-fiber firing are identical, they must have all the same properties. But pain has properties (a specific phenomenal feel) that C-fiber firing seems not to have, and C-fiber firing has properties (location, electrochemical structure) that pain seems not to have. Identity theorists respond by distinguishing the properties of the state from properties of our knowledge of it.
