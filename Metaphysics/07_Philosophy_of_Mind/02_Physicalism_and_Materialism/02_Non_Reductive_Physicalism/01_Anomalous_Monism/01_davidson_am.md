# Davidson's Anomalous Monism

Here is a puzzle. When a person acts for a reason — takes an umbrella because she believes it will rain and wants to stay dry — that mental event (the belief-desire combination) causes a physical event (reaching for the umbrella). Mental causation of physical events seems undeniable. But there are no strict psychophysical laws — no law that says "whenever someone has the belief that rain is coming and the desire to stay dry, they reach for an umbrella." Mental predicates, unlike physical predicates, cannot be connected by strict, exceptionless laws to physical predicates. So how can mental events cause physical events if causation requires lawful connection?

Donald Davidson's anomalous monism, developed in "Mental Events" (1970), navigates this puzzle by concluding that mental events *are* physical events — they are identical at the token level — even though mental properties are not reducible to physical properties. The position is non-reductive physicalism: monism because mental events are physical events, anomalism because mental properties resist lawlike reduction.

Davidson deploys three principles. The **Principle of Causal Interaction** holds that at least some mental events causally interact with physical events — an obvious datum of experience. The **Principle of the Nomological Character of Causality** holds that singular causal claims are made true by the existence of strict causal laws under which the events fall; if c caused e, there is a law connecting events of c's type to events of e's type under some description. The **Anomalism of the Mental** holds that there are no strict psychophysical laws; mental predicates cannot be connected to physical predicates by strict, exceptionless laws, because mental state attribution is holistic and normatively governed.

From these three principles, Davidson derives token identity:

1. Mental event M caused physical event P. (Causal Interaction)
2. Since M caused P, there is a strict law L such that M and P fall under L under some descriptions. (Nomological Character of Causality)
3. The mental description of M cannot figure in a strict law. (Anomalism)
4. Therefore, the description that figures in law L must be a physical description.
5. If M falls under a physical description, then M is a physical event — token-identical to a physical event.

## The Principle of Charity and Mental Holism

Davidson's rationalization of mental anomalism is based on the constitutive role of rationality in the mental. Mental state attribution is governed by the principle of charity: we attribute beliefs and desires so as to maximize the overall rationality and coherence of the attributed system. This normative constraint is not reducible to any causal-physical law. The physical is governed by causal laws; the mental is governed by normative standards. Moreover, mental state attributions are holistic: there is no way to attribute a single belief in isolation. To attribute the belief that it is raining requires attributing a host of related beliefs and desires. The web of mental state attributions must be globally coherent. Because mental descriptions are thus holistically and normatively constrained, no strict law can connect individual mental descriptions to individual physical descriptions.

This integration of reason-explanation and causal explanation is central to Davidson's overall philosophy of mind. Davidson argued (in "Actions, Reasons, and Causes," 1963) that reasons are causes — that the reason-explanation of action is a species of causal explanation. The belief and desire that rationalize an action also cause it. The logic of justification and the logic of causation converge. Anomalous monism occupies a distinctive space in the landscape: not dualism (mental events are physical events); not type identity theory (mental types are not physical types); not eliminativism (mental descriptions are genuine, if irreducible). And it preserves mental causation while denying that mental properties as such do the causal work — a tension that generated the epiphenomenalism objection discussed in the next file.
