# The Three Principles of Anomalous Monism

Davidson's argument in "Mental Events" deploys three principles he takes to be individually compelling but jointly surprising. Understanding each principle and the tensions between them is essential for evaluating anomalous monism.

The **Principle of Causal Interaction** holds that at least some mental events causally interact with physical events. When someone acts, a mental event (the belief-desire combination) causes a physical event (the action). Conversely, physical events cause mental events: a blow causes pain. Davidson regards this principle as so obvious that any view denying it is worse off than one that accepts the other two principles. It is also supported by neurological evidence: specific brain lesions reliably produce specific cognitive deficits, and stimulating specific brain regions produces specific mental effects.

The **Principle of the Nomological Character of Causality** holds that singular causal claims — "event c caused event e" — are made true by the existence of strict causal laws under which the events fall. Causation, for Davidson, is a relation between particular events that is underwritten by law: if c caused e, there is a law connecting events of c's type (under some description) to events of e's type (under some description). This is a Humean commitment: causation requires lawful regularity.

This principle is more controversial than the first. Causation-without-laws accounts — singularism, primitivism about causation — hold that causal relations between events can obtain independently of any law-like regularities. David Armstrong holds that causation is a direct relation between particulars, not mediated by laws. If this view is correct, Davidson's argument for token identity does not go through: mental events can cause physical events without being subsumed under physical laws, and hence without being identical to physical events.

The **Anomalism of the Mental** is Davidson's central and most controversial claim: there are no strict psychophysical laws. Mental predicates cannot be connected to physical predicates by strict, exceptionless laws. Davidson's reason: mental state attribution is holistic (you cannot attribute a single belief without attributing a whole system of beliefs) and normative (governed by rationality). No purely physical description can capture this holistic, normative character. The physical domain is governed by causal laws; the mental domain has its own constitutive principles irreducible to physical science.

## The Derivation and Its Implications

From these three principles, the derivation of token identity proceeds:

1. Mental event M caused physical event P. (From Causal Interaction)
2. Since M caused P, there is a strict law L such that M and P fall under L under some descriptions D(M) and D(P). (From Nomological Character of Causality)
3. The mental description of M cannot figure in a strict law. (From Anomalism of the Mental)
4. Therefore, D(M) — the description that figures in law L — must be a physical description.
5. If M falls under a physical description, then M is a physical event.
6. Therefore M is token-identical to a physical event.

The tension between the first two principles requires each mental event to be a physical event. The third principle prevents this token identity from grounding type identities. The result is a form of non-reductive physicalism: mental events are physical, but mental properties are not. Mental descriptions are genuine, even if irreducible. This position occupies a distinctive space: not dualism, not type identity theory, not eliminativism. And it preserves mental causation — mental events cause physical events — while denying that mental properties *as such* do the causal work. Whether that last claim is coherent is exactly the epiphenomenalism objection that Kim pressed.
