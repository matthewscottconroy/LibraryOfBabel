# Problems for Anomalous Monism

Two major lines of criticism have been pressed against Davidson's anomalous monism. The first — the epiphenomenalism worry — targets the causal efficacy of mental properties. The second — the Swampman objection — targets the adequacy of the causal history account of mental content.

The epiphenomenalism worry is the more fundamental. If mental events cause physical events via their physical descriptions — because causation requires nomological connection, and mental descriptions cannot figure in strict laws — then what causal work do mental properties themselves do? It seems as if the mental event *qua* mental, as having the specific content it has, as being the particular belief or desire it is, plays no causal role. The physical description carries all the causal weight. This is a form of epiphenomenalism about mental properties: they are causally inert by-products of physical processes.

Ernest LePore and Barry Loewer pressed this objection. If what does the causal work is always the physical property of the event (the one that figures in strict laws), then the mental properties are causally redundant. Davidson has not succeeded in showing that mental causation is real — he has shown only that mental events (under physical descriptions) cause things, not that they cause things *in virtue of* their mental properties.

Davidson's response: causal relations hold between events, not between properties. It is the event — not a description — that does the causing. The mental event *qua* event is the cause; the fact that it can also be described physically is what figures in the law, but this does not make the mental description causally irrelevant. Without the event being the mental event it is, the same effect would not have occurred.

## Kim's Exclusion Argument Applied to Anomalous Monism

Kim pressed the objection in its most rigorous form. The problem can be stated precisely:

- P1: For a property to be causally efficacious, it must be mentioned in the causal laws that underwrite the causal relation.
- P2: Mental properties are anomalous — they cannot figure in strict causal laws.
- C: Mental properties are causally inert — they are epiphenomenal.

Davidson can accept that mental events cause things (token identity gives him this) while still facing the charge that mental properties *qua* mental are causally irrelevant. The event that is a belief has causal power in virtue of its physical properties — those that figure in strict laws — not in virtue of its intentional content. But the intentional content seems to be precisely what matters for the rationality of action: I act for a reason, and the reason is the content of my belief and desire, not the physical description of the underlying neural event. Kim argues this shows anomalous monism to be unstable: either mental properties are causally efficacious (which requires type reduction) or they are epiphenomenal. The attempt to have both token physicalism and mental anomalism collapses into epiphenomenalism.

A related concern is whether Davidson conflates the epistemology of mental state attribution with the metaphysics of mental states. The normative constraint — that we attribute beliefs by holding people to rational standards — may not show that beliefs themselves are governed by rational norms rather than physical laws. It may show only that our *practice* of attribution is irreducibly normative, while the attributed states are themselves physical through and through.

## The Swampman Problem

The Swampman thought experiment — raised by Davidson himself in "Knowing One's Own Mind" — cuts across both points. Swampman is a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of Davidson created by a freak lightning strike. Does Swampman have thoughts? Davidson says no: mental properties depend on causal history, not just physical structure. Swampman's states have no appropriate causal connection to the world; they are not about anything because they were not caused by anything in the right way. But if mental content depends on causal history (a physical matter), then mental properties depend on physical facts in a way that complicates the claim of anomalism. The mental may be anomalous in the sense of not following physical laws, but it is still strongly determined by physical and causal facts in ways that undercut the robustness of the anomalous/physical distinction. Whether Davidson can hold together the Swampman case and mental anomalism is a question that remains philosophically live.
