# Supervenience Defined

Suppose we have two creatures who are physically identical — molecule for molecule, atom for atom — but one is in pain and the other is not. Is this possible? Most physicalists would say no: if two creatures share all their physical properties, they must share all their mental properties. There cannot be a mental difference without some physical difference. This intuition is what the concept of supervenience is designed to capture. Mental properties supervene on physical properties: necessarily, any two beings alike in all physical respects are alike in all mental respects.

Supervenience entered philosophy of mind primarily through Davidson's "Mental Events" (1970), where he used it to express the dependence of the mental on the physical without committing to type identity. His formulation: "mental characteristics are in some sense dependent on or supervenient on physical characteristics." The concept was then developed and made precise by Jaegwon Kim, who showed both its utility and its limits. Formally: a set A of properties supervenes on a set B of properties if and only if, necessarily, for any individuals x and y, if x and y are indiscernible with respect to all B-properties, then x and y are indiscernible with respect to all A-properties. The "necessarily" operator determines the strength: if interpreted as "in all metaphysically possible worlds," we get metaphysical supervenience; if "given the actual laws of nature," we get nomological supervenience.

Supervenience was introduced as a way of capturing the dependence of the mental on the physical without committing to type identity or full reduction. It expresses a constraint weaker than identity (which says mental types = physical types) and stronger than mere correlation (which might be accidental). It captures the "no mental change without physical change" intuition while leaving open the question of whether the mental is reducible to the physical.

## The Role of Supervenience in Physicalism — and Its Limits

For physicalism, the fundamental claim is that every mental fact is determined by physical facts. Supervenience gives a formal way of expressing this determination: physical facts are the base; mental facts supervene on them. But — and this is Kim's most important contribution to the debate — supervenience alone does not establish physicalism. Cartesian dualism is consistent with supervenience: God might have arranged things (in the Malebranchean manner) so that the same physical states are always accompanied by the same mental states, as a matter of divine will or pre-established harmony. This arrangement would satisfy the supervenience relation, but it would not make the mental physical. Supervenience is a necessary condition for physicalism but not sufficient.

Physicalism requires not just that physical facts determine mental facts (which supervenience captures) but also that the mental facts are constituted by, or grounded in, or in some stronger sense dependent on physical facts. The constitution or grounding relation is stronger than mere modal co-variation. This has led philosophers — Jonathan Schaffer, Kit Fine — to develop "grounding" as a more fundamental relation of metaphysical dependence. Mental facts are grounded in physical facts if the physical facts are the fundamental reason why the mental facts obtain — not merely correlated with them, not merely necessitating them, but constituting the deeper metaphysical basis for them. Physicalism, on this view, is more accurately stated as "mental facts are grounded in physical facts" than as "mental facts supervene on physical facts." The stronger formulation is both more accurate and more demanding.
