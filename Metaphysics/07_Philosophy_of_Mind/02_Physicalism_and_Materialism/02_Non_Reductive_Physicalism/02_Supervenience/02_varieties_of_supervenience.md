# Varieties of Supervenience

Not all supervenience claims are equal. The concept admits of many formal variants, and careful analysis of the variants is essential for evaluating what supervenience relations can and cannot establish.

*Local* supervenience says that the mental properties of an individual supervene on the physical properties of that individual. *Global* supervenience says that mental properties of a world (or region) supervene on the physical properties of that whole world (or region). Local supervenience is stronger: if two individuals are physically identical, they are mentally identical. Global supervenience merely requires that if two worlds are physically identical overall, they are mentally identical overall — compatible with local mental differences between physically identical individuals, as long as the global pattern is preserved.

*Weak* supervenience holds that within any given possible world, physically identical individuals are mentally identical. *Strong* supervenience holds that across all possible worlds, physically identical individuals are mentally identical. Kim introduced a further variant — global supervenience across worlds — which is required for physicalism to be a substantive metaphysical thesis.

*Nomological* supervenience holds that physical duplicates are mental duplicates given the actual laws of nature. *Metaphysical* supervenience holds that physical duplicates are mental duplicates in all metaphysically possible worlds. Physicalism is typically thought to require metaphysical supervenience: even if the laws were different, the same physical substrate could not give rise to different mental states. This distinction matters enormously for the hard problem.

## Supervenience, Zombies, and the Hard Problem

The varieties of supervenience distinction is central to Chalmers's presentation of the hard problem. Chalmers distinguishes between phenomenal consciousness supervening nomologically on the physical — which he accepts — and phenomenal consciousness supervening metaphysically on the physical — which he denies. If phenomenal consciousness only nomologically supervenes on the physical, then there are possible worlds with the same physical laws where physically identical beings lack consciousness. In that case, consciousness is not constituted by physical processes; it is only causally correlated with them.

This gives a new formulation of the zombie argument: a zombie world (physically identical to ours but lacking consciousness) is metaphysically possible even if nomologically impossible. If such a world is possible, phenomenal consciousness fails to metaphysically supervene on the physical, and physicalism — which requires metaphysical supervenience — is false.

The global-local distinction bears on this too. Philosophers have debated whether physicalism requires local or global supervenience. If only global supervenience is required, then a world in which some individuals are zombies might be globally supervenient on the physical — because the zombie world might be globally physically identical to our world while differing in the local mental properties of individuals. This means the zombie argument, to get off the ground against physicalism, must establish the failure of *local* metaphysical supervenience.

## Grounding and the Limits of Supervenience

The proliferation of supervenience concepts led philosophers to ask whether supervenience is the right concept for capturing physical determination of the mental, or whether something stronger is needed. Jonathan Schaffer and Kit Fine developed "grounding" as a more fundamental relation: mental facts are grounded in physical facts if the physical facts are the ultimate ontological basis for the mental facts — if the mental facts obtain *in virtue of* the physical facts. Grounding is asymmetric (physical grounds mental, not the other way) and explanatory (grounding facts explain why the grounded facts obtain). It is also hyperintensional: two facts can necessitate each other without grounding each other. This means supervenience — which is a modal relation — does not capture grounding.

Whether non-reductive physicalism is sustainable in the grounding framework depends on whether grounding without reduction is metaphysically coherent — whether there is a genuine relation between mental and physical facts that is stronger than mere supervenience but weaker than identity or reduction. This is an active area of metaphysical research with significant implications for the mind-body problem.
