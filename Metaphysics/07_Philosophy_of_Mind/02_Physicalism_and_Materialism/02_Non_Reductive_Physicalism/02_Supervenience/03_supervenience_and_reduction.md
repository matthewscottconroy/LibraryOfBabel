# Supervenience and Reduction

The central question in non-reductive physicalism is whether supervenience relations entail reducibility. Mental properties supervene on physical properties — every mental property is necessarily co-instantiated with some physical property. But does this necessitation amount to reduction? Non-reductive physicalists say no: supervenience without reduction is possible. Kim argues that this position is ultimately unstable.

The case for supervenience without reduction: the mental property is not identical to any physical property, and there is no "bridge law" specifying which physical property corresponds to which mental property. The relationship is one of dependence, not identity. This is analogous (non-reductive physicalists argue) to other dependence relations in nature: the shape of a statue supervenes on its molecular constitution, but "having a cuboid shape" is not identical to any specific molecular description.

Kim's challenge, developed in *Supervenience and Mind* (1993) and *Mind in a Physical World* (1998), is that the supervenience relation, if taken seriously, undermines the supposed distinctness of mental properties. If every mental property necessarily co-instantiates with some physical property, then for each mental type M, there is a physical type P that is both necessary and sufficient for M in the actual world and in nearby worlds. But that is just what reduction requires. Non-reductive physicalism tries to have it both ways: the physical necessitates the mental (supervenience), but the mental is distinct from the physical (non-reduction). Kim argues these commitments are inconsistent. Non-reductive physicalists have responded by arguing that reduction requires more than supervenience — it requires that the higher-level kinds correspond to natural kinds at the lower level, or that the lower-level description can replace the higher-level description for all explanatory purposes. Neither is entailed by supervenience.

## Kim's Functionalization Strategy and the Limits of Reduction

Kim's most constructive contribution is his "functionalization" strategy. He argues that the only mental properties that can retain causal efficacy in a physical world are those that can be "functionalized" — characterized in terms of their causal-functional roles and then identified with whatever physical property plays that role. If pain is just "the state that is typically caused by tissue damage and causes avoidance behavior," then we can identify pain with the neural state that plays this role — in humans, C-fiber firing; in octopi, a different state. This functionalization strategy achieves local reductions without universal type identity: multiple realizability becomes a feature of the functionalization, not a problem for it.

But Kim argues that this functionalization strategy works only for "functional" mental properties — those that can be characterized entirely in terms of causal relations. For phenomenal properties — qualia, the felt character of experience — the strategy fails. The redness of a red experience, the painfulness of pain — these are not exhausted by their causal roles. For phenomenal properties, neither functionalization nor supervenience-without-reduction is adequate. The choices are elimination or property dualism.

## The Explanatory Gap and Non-Reductionism

A central motivation for non-reductive physicalism is the explanatory gap — the fact that no physical description seems to explain why a given physical process should give rise to a specific phenomenal experience. Joseph Levine introduced this concept in "Materialism and Qualia: The Explanatory Gap" (1983). Even if pain is identical to C-fiber firing, there seems to be an explanatory gap: we cannot see why C-fiber firing should hurt. Non-reductive physicalists use this gap to motivate their position: the failure of physical-to-phenomenal explanation shows that phenomenal properties are not reducible to physical properties, even if they are supervenient on them. The gap is not a sign of non-identity (pace Chalmers) but a sign of irreducibility — a conceptual gap that does not reflect a metaphysical gap.

The debate between type-A physicalists (who deny any genuine explanatory gap once phenomenal concepts are properly analyzed) and type-B physicalists (who acknowledge the gap but deny it has metaphysical implications) turns on whether the explanatory gap is epistemological or metaphysical. Non-reductive physicalism typically takes the type-B position: the gap is real but not metaphysically significant. Whether this position is coherent is one of the central unresolved questions in contemporary philosophy of mind.
