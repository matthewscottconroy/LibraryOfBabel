# What Is Physicalism?

Physicalism — the view that everything that exists is physical, or that mental facts are fully determined by physical facts — seems obvious to many philosophers today. Yet giving a precise statement of the view has proved surprisingly difficult. The challenge is to specify what "physical" means in a way that makes physicalism substantive (not trivially true) while remaining clear (not so vague as to be uninformative).

The naive formulation — "everything is physical" or "the world is entirely physical" — faces the completeness problem: what is physical? If we say "whatever physics studies," then as physics expands (potentially to include consciousness), the claim becomes trivially true. If we fix physics at its current state, we make physicalism contingently true or false based on what current physics happens to include — which seems wrong. **Hempel's dilemma** identifies this precisely: if physicalism is defined in terms of current physics, it is likely false (current physics does not obviously accommodate consciousness). If defined in terms of ideal or completed physics, it is unknowable (we don't know what completed physics will include). Either way, the view has trouble.

Several responses have been developed. Papineau defines the physical by reference to what is "non-mental" rather than by reference to physics: something is physical if its description does not essentially involve mental concepts. This gives physicalism determinate content without requiring specification of completed physics. Stoljar's "via negativa" strategy is similar: physical properties are those whose instantiation does not depend on the instantiation of mental properties.

## The Formulations of Physicalism

Contemporary responses typically define physicalism as one of several increasingly demanding theses. *Supervenience physicalism* holds that there is no possible world with the same physical facts as the actual world but different mental facts — physical facts determine all facts. But as Kim showed, this does not capture anything distinctively physicalist without a stronger interpretation. *Grounding physicalism* holds that all facts are ultimately grounded in microphysical facts — mental facts obtain because and in virtue of physical facts, not the reverse. This is stronger than supervenience: it includes an explanatory dimension and an asymmetry claim. *Constitution physicalism* holds that all objects are ultimately constituted by the fundamental physical entities. *Token physicalism* holds simply that every particular mental event is identical to some particular physical event — the minimal commitment shared by most physicalists.

Most contemporary physicalists are *a posteriori* physicalists: they hold that the mental-physical identity is discovered by science, not settled by conceptual analysis. This is motivated by the parallel with other scientific identities — water = H₂O, temperature = mean molecular kinetic energy. These identities could not be known a priori; they required empirical investigation. Similarly, the identity of mental properties with physical properties (if it holds) is a posteriori, to be discovered by neuroscience. The failure of a priori reduction does not show that identity fails — only that it is not knowable a priori.

Type-B physicalism (Block, Stalnaker) is a version of a posteriori physicalism that takes the explanatory gap seriously: there is a genuine conceptual gap between phenomenal and physical concepts, but this gap does not mark a metaphysical gap. The two sets of concepts pick out the same property by different routes, and the identity is a posteriori. If physicalism is defined too weakly, as mere supervenience, it loses its distinctive content. If defined too strongly, as a priori reducibility of all mental concepts to physical concepts, it is almost certainly false. Finding a formulation that is substantive, accurate, and defensible remains one of the central tasks in contemporary philosophy of mind.
