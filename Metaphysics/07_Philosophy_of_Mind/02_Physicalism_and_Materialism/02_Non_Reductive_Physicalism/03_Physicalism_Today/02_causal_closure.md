# The Causal Closure of the Physical

Every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. This is the causal closure principle — also called causal completeness — and it has become the key premise in arguments for physicalism about the mind. Its force derives from a simple dilemma: if physical events have sufficient physical causes, and if mental events also cause physical events (as the principle of causal interaction asserts), then either mental events are physical (otherwise physical effects are overdetermined by two distinct causes) or mental events cause nothing (epiphenomenalism). Non-reductive physicalism, which wants to hold both that mental properties are distinct from physical properties and that they are causally efficacious, appears unstable.

Jaegwon Kim made causal closure the centerpiece of the exclusion problem, and the argument can be formalized:

- P1: Mental events cause physical events (Causal Interaction — obviously true in ordinary experience).
- P2: Every physical event has a sufficient physical cause (Causal Closure of the physical).
- P3: Mental events are not identical to physical events (assumed by Non-Reductive Physicalism and Dualism).
- P4: If a physical event has both a sufficient physical cause and a mental cause, and these are distinct, then the physical event is causally overdetermined.
- P5: Systematic, pervasive causal overdetermination of physical events by mental events is effectively impossible.
- C: Either P3 is false (mental events are physical events) or P1 is false (mental events do not cause physical events).

The argument forces a choice: physicalism (deny P3) or epiphenomenalism (deny P1). Kim argues that epiphenomenalism is unacceptable; therefore, physicalism follows.

## The Status of the Closure Principle

The causal closure principle is not an a priori truth — it is an empirical and theoretical commitment. Its motivation comes from the success of physics in explaining physical events without appealing to non-physical causes. Since the development of conservation laws and quantum mechanics, physicists have found no evidence that physical systems are influenced by non-physical factors.

Nevertheless, the principle faces challenges. Quantum indeterminacy raises the possibility that some physical events are genuinely underdetermined: there is no sufficient physical cause that determines exactly when a radioactive atom decays or how a quantum system collapses. If physical events can be underdetermined in this way, there may be room for non-physical causes to fill the gap — some physicists, following Penrose and Stapp, have proposed that mental intentions could influence quantum processes in the brain. Most philosophers find this unpromising: quantum indeterminacy is random, not rational, and it is unclear how mental causation could exploit random quantum events to produce rational action. A related challenge is that the closure principle may be a methodological assumption of physics rather than a metaphysical truth about nature. Dualists like Richard Swinburne argue that the history of science does not establish closure; it merely shows that physics has been successful in explaining physical events in physical terms.

## Overdetermination and the Constitution Response

The argument from causal closure relies on the claim that systematic overdetermination of physical events by mental causes would be unacceptable. Some philosophers (Yablo, Bennett) have argued that mental and physical causation do not compete in the way Kim assumes. If the mental is constituted by the physical, then mental causation is not in addition to physical causation — it just is physical causation at a higher level of description. The physical basis of the mental state causes the physical effect, and the mental state (being nothing over and above the physical basis) also causes the effect — not because there are two separate causal contributions, but because the same causal contribution is described at two levels.

This response requires the claim that mental properties and physical properties are related by constitution rather than identity — a claim that is itself contested. If constitution is a genuine relation that is not the same as identity, it is unclear how it avoids the overdetermination problem rather than merely relocating it. Nancey Murphy, Timothy O'Connor, and others have developed non-reductive or emergentist alternatives that challenge the closure principle directly, arguing for "downward causation" — the claim that higher-level organizational properties (including mental properties) can causally influence lower-level physical processes. Whether downward causation is coherent without violating closure, or whether it requires rejecting closure, is the central unresolved question in this debate.
