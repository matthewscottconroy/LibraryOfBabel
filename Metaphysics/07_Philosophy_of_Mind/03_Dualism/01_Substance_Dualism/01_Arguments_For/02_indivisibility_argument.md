# The Indivisibility Argument for Substance Dualism

A body can be divided. You can cut it in half, remove parts, and it remains a body — just smaller. But what would it mean to divide a mind? Descartes held that the mind is simple, indivisible: you cannot remove half of your understanding or partition your consciousness into spatial halves. This difference in essential properties — divisibility versus indivisibility — was for Descartes evidence that mind and body are distinct substances. The argument appears in the Sixth Meditation and it remains alive in contemporary discussions of the unity of consciousness.

Formalized as a Leibniz's Law argument:

- P1: The body has the property of being spatially divisible (it can be physically divided into parts).
- P2: The mind does not have the property of being spatially divisible.
- P3: By Leibniz's Law, if A and B differ in any property, A ≠ B.
- C: Mind ≠ Body.

Similarly: the body is spatially extended; the mind is not spatially extended; therefore, by Leibniz's Law, mind ≠ body. Both arguments assume that spatial divisibility and extension are genuine properties, and that the mind genuinely lacks them. Both assumptions have been contested.

## Leibniz's Mill Argument

Leibniz developed a related argument in the *Monadology* (§17). Suppose perception and thought are mechanical processes. Imagine the brain magnified to the size of a mill. If we walk inside and examine all the parts in motion — the neurons firing, the neurotransmitters diffusing, the electrical impulses propagating — we would find only parts pressing against parts. We would never find a perception, a thought, a feeling. No combination of mechanical interactions seems to produce something that has the character of conscious experience. This suggests that thought and perception are not mechanical processes and cannot be produced by any material organization.

This argument anticipates contemporary concerns about the hard problem: even a complete physical description of the brain seems to leave out something about the nature of conscious experience. The gap between physical description and phenomenal reality seems unbridgeable by any mechanical story. Notice that Leibniz's argument is about what can be *found* in the mill — it is phenomenological, not just metaphysical. The point is that the walker in the mill would have no way of locating thought in the mechanical operations, no matter how thoroughly they inspected.

## The Unity of Consciousness and Split-Brain Evidence

The unity of consciousness remains a live philosophical topic in connection with binding, global workspace theory, and the neural correlates of consciousness. At any moment, my experiences are unified: the redness I see, the music I hear, the pressure I feel, the thought I am thinking — all integrated into a single, unified field of awareness. This unity is not merely the co-occurrence of different experiences; it involves their integration into a single perspective. The binding problem in neuroscience asks how the brain achieves this unity, since different features of a visual scene are processed in anatomically distinct cortical regions.

Modern neuroscience complicates the indivisibility argument, however. Split-brain patients — whose corpus callosum has been severed — appear to have two independent centers of consciousness, suggesting that what we call the unified mind may be more divisible than Descartes assumed. Sperry's work showed that the two hemispheres can have conflicting information and different responses; Gazzaniga and colleagues documented cases in which the left hemisphere confabulates explanations for actions initiated by the right. Whether split-brain patients have one mind or two is itself philosophically contested. The dualist can argue that severing the corpus callosum affects the functional organization of the brain in ways that affect mental unity without literally dividing an immaterial mind. But this requires an account of how immaterial minds are related to physical organizations that the original indivisibility argument does not provide.
