# The Modal Argument for Substance Dualism

If pain is identical to C-fiber firing, then this identity — like all genuine identities — must be necessary. There is no possible world in which water is not H₂O once we know the real identity; similarly, if pain = C-fiber firing, then in no possible world could there be pain without C-fiber firing. But we can apparently conceive of pain without C-fiber firing — silicon beings in pain, creatures with radically different neurochemistry in pain. And the conceivability here has a special character: pain is picked out by its felt character, not by any causal-functional description, so the conceivability is not explained away by ignorance of necessary connections. This is Saul Kripke's argument in *Naming and Necessity* (1980), the most rigorous contemporary argument for a dualist conclusion. Though Kripke does not endorse substance dualism, his argument establishes that phenomenal mental states cannot be identical to physical brain states — which supports property dualism if not substance dualism.

The argument turns on Kripke's account of rigid designation. A rigid designator is a term that picks out the same object in all possible worlds. Proper names and natural kind terms ("water," "gold," "pain") are rigid designators. If two rigid designators pick out the same object in the actual world, then by the necessity of identity (if a = b, then necessarily a = b), they pick out the same object in all possible worlds — the identity is necessary, not contingent.

## Explaining Apparent Contingency

For identities like "water = H₂O," the apparent contingency (we can seemingly imagine water without H₂O) is explained by distinguishing primary and secondary intensions. The primary intension of "water" is "the watery stuff" — the transparent, tasteless, drinkable liquid prevalent in this environment. In another possible world, the watery stuff might be XYZ rather than H₂O; in that world, "water" in its primary intension picks out XYZ, not H₂O. The secondary intension of "water" — the actual natural kind, H₂O — is rigid. The apparent contingency of "water = H₂O" reflects variation in primary intension across worlds; the actual identity is necessary.

Kripke argues that this explanation is unavailable for the pain-C-fiber identity. The primary intension of "pain" is not "the state that typically causes such-and-such behavioral responses" — that might be C-fiber firing in the actual world and something else in another. The primary intension of "pain" is its felt character. In any world, "pain" picks out states with that felt quality. Therefore, there is no variation in primary intension across worlds that explains the apparent contingency of "pain = C-fiber firing." The appearance of contingency is genuine, and genuine contingency is incompatible with identity.

## Responses

*Type-A physicalism* (Lewis, Tye, Dretske): "Pain" does not rigidly designate a felt quality but is a functional/topic-neutral term. Phenomenal concepts pick out their referents by description — by what typically causes them or what roles they play — not by intrinsic phenomenal character. If "pain" is a description, the primary/secondary intension distinction applies, and the apparent contingency is explained in the same way as "water = H₂O."

*Type-B physicalism* (Block, Stalnaker): Pain is a rigid designator of its phenomenal character, and there is a genuine a priori gap between phenomenal and physical concepts. But this gap is epistemic rather than metaphysical: we access one and the same property through two different modes of presentation. The appearance of contingency reflects the distinctness of our modes of access, not any genuine modal separation.

*Chalmers's response*: Kripke's argument is essentially correct, and its conclusion is that phenomenal properties are not identical to physical properties. The correct response is to accept property dualism (or naturalistic dualism) rather than to explain away the conceivability of C-fiber firing without pain.

Even if Kripke's argument does not establish full substance dualism, it strongly supports property dualism. If phenomenal properties cannot be identified with physical properties — because the identification would have to be necessary but is manifestly contingent — then there are genuine non-physical properties, instantiated by persons, that are not captured by any physical description. The modal argument provides the clearest philosophical case for what Chalmers calls "strong" property dualism: phenomenal properties are genuinely, irreducibly distinct from physical properties, even if necessarily correlated with them.
