# Contemporary Defenders of Substance Dualism

The standard narrative is that substance dualism died with Descartes — or at most with Princess Elisabeth's unanswered letters. The narrative is false. A small but philosophically serious group of contemporary philosophers have defended versions of substance dualism, and their defenses are sophisticated enough that dismissing them requires more than gesturing at the interaction problem. What is common to these defenses is a willingness to turn the methodological pressure around: yes, substance dualism faces difficulties, but physicalism faces the hard problem, and a false theory is not preferable to a true but ontologically complex one.

Richard Swinburne's *The Evolution of the Soul* (1986) rests on two pillars. The first is the essential unity and indivisibility of the conscious self: whatever I am, I am not divisible in the way physical objects are, and the question of where I am if my brain is divided is a genuine metaphysical question with no physical answer. The second is the apparent contingency of the soul-body connection: it seems coherent to suppose that I might have existed in a different body, which shows I am not identical to my body. Swinburne's response to the interaction problem is that causal connections between mind and brain are natural necessitations — regularities established by the laws of nature rather than mechanical transactions. The soul is causally connected to the brain not through spatial contact but through nomological law.

John Foster's *The Immaterial Self* (1991) develops an analytic defense of Cartesian dualism that takes the interaction problem seriously rather than dismissing it. Foster's account invokes natural necessitation as the causal glue between mind and brain, arguing that the causal relation between immaterial substances and physical events need not be mechanical to be genuine. E.J. Lowe, working from his four-category ontology, distinguished substances, kinds, attributes, and modes, and argued that the self is a substance of a distinctive kind — mentally propertied, embodied, developmentally dependent on the physical, but not reducible to it. In *Personal Agency* (2008), Lowe argues that the self's interaction with the brain is explained through natural relations between kinds: the kind "human self" is naturally connected to the kind "human brain" in ways that generate regular causal co-variation without requiring any mysterious energy transmission across an ontological divide.

## O'Connor's Emergent Individuals and the Hard Problem Argument

Timothy O'Connor's emergent individualism represents a naturalistic form of substance dualism that attempts to overcome the Cartesian disconnection from the physical world. O'Connor argues that physical complexity can give rise to genuinely new substances — not merely new properties of existing entities but new entities with their own irreducible causal powers. The conscious self is such an emergent individual: arising from the neural complexity of the brain, causally dependent on it, but a genuine substance in its own right. This is "strong emergence" — the appearance of new substances, not merely new functional descriptions — and O'Connor distinguishes it sharply from the weak emergence most physicalists accept.

The objection nearly writes itself: if the emergent individual must causally interact with the physical substrate from which it emerged, the interaction problem re-emerges. O'Connor's emergent self must influence the brain, and specifying how faces the same obstacles as Cartesian interactionism. What strong emergence adds to the picture is a story about origin — physical complexity generates the new substance — but a story about origin is not yet a story about ongoing causal interaction. Notice also that O'Connor's view requires strong emergence of substances to be coherent as a metaphysical category, and most philosophers resist this: emergence of properties (even irreducible properties) is one thing; emergence of new substances with new causal powers is another, and the mechanism is correspondingly more mysterious.

The most important contemporary argument for substance dualism is not any of these specific theories but a general dialectical point. The parsimony argument for physicalism assumes that physicalism can account for the facts of consciousness. If it cannot — if no physical description can account for the felt character of experience, as the hard problem alleges — then physicalism is not merely simpler but possibly false. And a false theory is not preferable to a true but ontologically complex one. This reversal shifts the dialectic: the question is not whether substance dualism faces difficulties (it does) but whether those difficulties are worse than the difficulties facing physicalism. Contemporary substance dualists argue that given the hard problem, the explanatory gap, and the persistent failure of functionalist accounts to capture qualia, dualism's difficulties are not obviously more severe. Whether this argument succeeds depends entirely on how seriously we take the hard problem — which is precisely the question on which the entire contemporary debate about consciousness turns.
