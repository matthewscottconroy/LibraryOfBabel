# Important Terms and Ideas: Contemporary Substance Dualism

**Substance Dualism**
The view that mind and matter are two fundamentally different kinds of substance: mental substances (souls, minds) are not made of matter and have no spatial properties; material substances occupy space and lack intrinsic mentality. Human persons consist of both kinds, somehow united.

**Interactionism**
The form of substance dualism that holds that mind and body causally interact: mental events (decisions, desires) cause physical events (bodily movements), and physical events (sensory stimulation) cause mental events (perceptions, pains). The central challenge is explaining how non-physical causation of physical events is possible.

**Epiphenomenalism**
A non-interactive form of dualism: mental states are caused by physical states but have no causal power over the physical. The mind is a byproduct of brain processes, like a shadow—real but causally inert. Epiphenomenalism avoids the interaction problem but raises questions about the utility of consciousness.

**Parallelism**
The view that mental and physical processes run in parallel with perfect correspondence but without causal interaction. Leibniz's pre-established harmony is the classic form; contemporary parallelism is usually dismissed as an ad hoc dodge of the interaction problem.

**Simple Substance**
Swinburne's characterization of the soul: a simple, unanalyzable individual that cannot be identified with any complex physical arrangement. Its simplicity is meant to support personal identity over time: unlike the body, a simple substance cannot gradually change into something else.

**Irreducibility of Experience**
A key motivation for contemporary substance dualism: phenomenal consciousness—the subjective, qualitative "what it's like" of experience—seems to be irreducible to any functional or physical description. If physicalism cannot account for qualia, some form of dualism may be required.
