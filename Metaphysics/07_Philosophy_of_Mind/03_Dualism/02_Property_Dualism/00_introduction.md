# Property Dualism

Property dualism holds that there is only one kind of substance in the world — physical substance — but that this substance has two fundamentally different kinds of properties: physical properties (mass, charge, spatial extension) and mental or phenomenal properties (the qualitative character of experience, subjectivity, consciousness). The physical and mental properties of the brain are distinct and irreducible, even though they inhere in the same underlying substance.

Property dualism emerged as a response to the difficulties of substance dualism while preserving what seemed compelling in the dualist intuition: that subjective experience has a character that cannot be fully captured in purely physical terms. By accepting that there is only one kind of substance, property dualism avoids the interaction problem — the mental properties of the brain are causally connected to other physical properties in the ordinary way, since they belong to a physical system. But by insisting that mental properties are distinct from and irreducible to physical properties, property dualism preserves the intuition that there is something more to consciousness than mere physical organization.

Chalmers's "naturalistic dualism" is perhaps the most carefully developed contemporary property dualism. Chalmers accepts that phenomenal consciousness — the qualitative character of experience — is not reducible to physical organization, and that any adequate theory of the mind must go beyond the purely physical. His positive proposal invokes "psychophysical laws" — laws connecting physical structures to phenomenal properties — that supplement the laws of physics without violating them. Phenomenal properties supervene on physical properties via these laws, but they are not identical to physical properties.

The main objection to property dualism is epiphenomenalism: if mental properties are genuinely distinct from physical properties, and if the physical world is causally closed, then mental properties have no causal efficacy — they are mere byproducts of physical processes with no power to make anything happen. A world physically identical to ours but with different (or absent) phenomenal properties would behave in exactly the same way. This is deeply counterintuitive.

This section examines property dualism in its various forms, the motivations for it, and the epiphenomenalism problem.
