# Property Dualism

Two pressures pull in opposite directions. Against substance dualism, the interaction problem and the evidence from neuroscience seem decisive: mental causation is mediated by brain processes, and positing a separate immaterial substance creates problems with no satisfying solutions. Against reductive physicalism, there seems to be a genuine explanatory gap between physical descriptions of the brain and phenomenal descriptions of experience — no amount of information about neural firing patterns seems to logically entail what the experience feels like. Property dualism is the attempt to honor both pressures at once: keep the physical ontology — one kind of substance — while insisting on the irreducibility of mental properties.

The position is not the same as saying mental properties are mysterious or unexplained. It is saying that the world contains more than one fundamental kind of property. Mental properties supervene on physical properties — there can be no change in phenomenal character without some change in physical constitution — but supervenience is not identity, and "supervenes on" is not "is reducible to." The experience of seeing red may correlate reliably with a particular pattern of neural activity, but the correlation does not explain away the redness of the experience; it only shows where in the physical world to look for the correlate.

Several distinct positions fall under the property dualist label. Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal properties are causally inert by-products of physical processes — real but doing no causal work. Interactionist property dualism holds that phenomenal properties have genuine causal powers, but must then explain how non-physical properties influence physical processes without violating causal closure. Double-aspect theory, associated with Spinoza and more recently with Thomas Nagel, holds that the same underlying event has both physical and phenomenal aspects, neither reducible to the other. Chalmers's naturalistic dualism — perhaps the most carefully elaborated contemporary version — holds that phenomenal properties are fundamental features of the world, connected to physical properties by psychophysical laws that are additional to the laws of physics.

## The Stability Question: Kim's Challenge

Property dualism's appeal is its ability to take the hard problem seriously without the ontological extravagance of positing immaterial substances. But it occupies an unstable position, as Jaegwon Kim has argued. The exclusion argument presses from the physical direction: if mental properties are genuinely distinct from physical properties, and physical events have sufficient physical causes, then mental properties are excluded from any causal role. Property dualism threatens to collapse into epiphenomenalism — the view that phenomenal properties are real but do nothing causally.

The dualist has two responses, and neither is fully satisfying. The first is to accept epiphenomenalism: yes, phenomenal properties are causally inert, but they are still real and genuinely distinct from physical properties. The cost is that all apparent mental causation — my reaching for ice because my hand hurts — is done entirely by the neural events, and the phenomenal hurt is an epiphenomenal passenger. The second is to defend interactionism: phenomenal properties genuinely influence physical processes, and causal closure must be revised or qualified to make room for this. The cost is that this sounds like a retreat toward substance dualism's interaction problem, now transposed from substances to properties.

Chalmers's naturalistic dualism attempts a third path: phenomenal properties are connected to physical processes by fundamental psychophysical laws, and questions about causal relevance may have a different character for fundamental properties than for higher-level ones. The analogy is to other fundamental physical properties — mass, charge, spin — which are not themselves explained in terms of more basic properties but simply figure in the fundamental laws. By analogy, phenomenal consciousness should be accepted as fundamental because it is needed to explain phenomenal phenomena. Critics respond that the analogy fails: mass and charge figure in precise mathematical laws and have determinate causal powers; phenomenal consciousness, as the property dualist understands it, resists mathematical characterization and may lack any causal role at all. Whether property dualism can answer Kim's challenge while preserving genuine causal relevance for the phenomenal is the central open question the position faces.
