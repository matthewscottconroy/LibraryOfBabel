# Property Dualism Defined

The interaction problem showed that positing an immaterial substance creates more difficulties than it resolves. But abandoning substance dualism does not force us to say that mental properties are simply identical to physical ones. Property dualism occupies the position between these two: there is one kind of substance — the physical — but two irreducibly distinct kinds of properties. Mental properties, specifically phenomenal properties, are not identical to or reducible to physical properties. They supervene on physical properties — no mental change without some physical change — but supervenience is not reduction, and the felt character of experience is not captured by any physical-functional description.

Notice what this position gains and what it costs. It gains freedom from the interaction problem, because mental events are physical events even if their mental properties are not physical. It costs a clean story about mental causation, because if physical events have sufficient physical causes, it is not obvious what work a non-physical phenomenal property is doing causally. The central challenge for property dualism is precisely this tension: phenomenal properties seem real and causally relevant, but their causal relevance is difficult to secure without either violating physical causal closure or conceding that they do nothing.

The motivation for accepting the cost comes from the hard problem of consciousness, as Chalmers formulated it. The "easy problems" — explaining attention, learning, memory, perceptual discrimination, behavioral control — can be addressed by functional and computational theories. Hard as these problems are in practice, they are easy in principle: solving them means finding the functional mechanisms that perform the relevant operations. But the hard problem is different: it is explaining why there is something it is like to be in these functional states at all — why neural processing is accompanied by phenomenal experience rather than occurring "in the dark." Even a complete neuroscientific account of pain, specifying every neuron that fires and every behavioral disposition activated, leaves open the question of why this neural activity should hurt. The explanatory gap between physical-functional description and phenomenal character cannot be closed within a purely physical framework, which suggests that phenomenal properties are genuinely distinct from physical ones.

## Varieties and the Stability Question

Property dualism comes in several forms, differentiated primarily by what they say about phenomenal causation. Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal properties are real but causally inert — by-products of physical processes that produce no physical effects. This avoids the causal closure problem but at the cost of making our apparent rational agency an illusion: if my experience of pain plays no causal role in my reaching for ice, the reaching is entirely caused by neural events, and the hurt is along for the ride. Interactionist property dualism holds that phenomenal properties are causally efficacious, but then faces the same challenge as substance dualism's interaction problem — if physical events have sufficient physical causes, how do non-physical phenomenal properties make any causal difference? Double-aspect theory, associated with Spinoza and more recently with Thomas Nagel, holds that the same event has both physical and phenomenal aspects, neither reducible to the other. Chalmers's naturalistic dualism is perhaps the most carefully articulated contemporary version: phenomenal properties are fundamental features of the world, like mass or charge, governed by psychophysical laws that are additional to the laws of physics and not derivable from them.

Ned Block's distinction between access consciousness (A-consciousness: information being available for use in reasoning, reporting, and behavioral control) and phenomenal consciousness (P-consciousness: the felt, experiential character of mental states) maps directly onto the property dualist's central distinction. Physical-functional theories can account for A-consciousness; what they leave unexplained is P-consciousness. The zombie scenario shows — for the property dualist — that A-consciousness does not entail P-consciousness: a system could be functionally identical to a conscious being while having no phenomenal properties at all.

The stability question for property dualism was pressed forcefully by Jaegwon Kim. If mental properties are genuinely distinct from physical properties, and every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, then phenomenal properties seem excluded from any causal role. Property dualism collapses into epiphenomenalism unless mental properties can be reduced to physical ones — in which case it collapses into reductive physicalism. Whether property dualism can find stable ground between these two outcomes is the central question the position must answer.
