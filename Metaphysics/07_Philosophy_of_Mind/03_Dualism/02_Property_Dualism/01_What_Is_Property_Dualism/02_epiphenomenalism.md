# Epiphenomenalism

T.H. Huxley's metaphor is worth sitting with: the steam whistle on a locomotive. It is caused by the engine, it is real, it makes genuine sound — but it does nothing to move the train. Consciousness, on Huxley's "On the Hypothesis that Animals Are Automata" (1874), stands in exactly this relation to the brain. Physical processes produce mental events as side effects, perfectly correlated with them, always accompanying them — but the mental events cause nothing. The physical causes the mental; the mental never causes the physical.

The appeal of this position is that it resolves two otherwise incompatible commitments at once. Phenomenal consciousness is real — there is something it is like to see red, feel pain, hear music. Physical processes have sufficient physical causes — causal closure is not violated. Epiphenomenalism satisfies both by making the conscious side of things inert: consciousness exists but does nothing, which means it never competes with or adds to the physical causal story. The interaction problem simply does not arise, since there is no interaction from mental to physical.

Frank Jackson's 1982 paper "Epiphenomenal Qualia" is the most important contemporary defense of the position, though Jackson himself later abandoned it. Jackson's argument centers on the knowledge argument — Mary's Room — as establishing that qualia are not physical properties. If qualia are real but not physical, and if physical events have sufficient physical causes, then qualia must be causally inert. Jackson argued that this conclusion, while counterintuitive, is forced: we must pay the price of causal inertness for phenomenal properties specifically, while functional-intentional properties remain causally efficacious. He subsequently recanted, coming to believe that the knowledge argument does not establish the non-physicality of qualia.

Chalmers occupies a more uneasy position. His naturalistic dualism may entail a form of epiphenomenalism: if phenomenal properties are genuinely distinct from physical properties, and physical events have sufficient physical causes, then phenomenal properties cannot causally contribute to physical events. Chalmers's response is that phenomenal properties play a constitutive role in the identity of psychological states even if causally inert, and that our verbal reports about our experiences can be explained by the systematic psychophysical correlations between physical states and their phenomenal accompaniments. Critics find this response inadequate: if phenomenal properties cause nothing, then my reports about my experiences are caused entirely by physical processes, with no guarantee that those processes track the phenomenal character of the experiences. The reliability of introspection is undermined — and introspection is the primary evidence for phenomenal properties in the first place.

## The Self-Refutation and Evolutionary Problems

The self-refutation objection strikes at the root. Consider the reasoning that leads to epiphenomenalism. Step 1: we observe that we have phenomenal experiences — the redness of red, the painfulness of pain. Step 2: we reason that these experiences are not physical. Step 3: we conclude they are causally inert. But step 1 involves introspective reports. My introspective judgment "I am now experiencing redness" is a cognitive event produced by physical brain processes. If epiphenomenalism is true, this judgment is entirely caused by the physical processes — not by the phenomenal experience of redness. The phenomenal experience plays no causal role in producing the judgment. This means the evidence for phenomenal consciousness — our introspective reports — is not caused by the experiences themselves. The reports might be accurate, or they might be exactly the reports a zombie system would produce: systems that behave as if conscious, without being so, making all the same introspective claims. If the evidence for epiphenomenalism could equally be produced by zombie systems, it does not establish that there is anything to be epiphenomenal about. The position undermines its own evidential basis.

The evolutionary objection presses from a different direction. The human brain consumes approximately 20% of the body's energy budget while comprising only 2% of its mass. The selective pressure to maintain this costly organ must be very high, which implies that brains confer substantial adaptive advantages. If consciousness is epiphenomenal — if it contributes nothing to behavior — then there is no selective pressure for the physical processes that produce consciousness specifically, as opposed to physically identical processes that produce behavior without consciousness. Consciousness becomes an inexplicable free rider: pervasive, metabolically costly to produce, yet conferring nothing. This is possible in principle, but it is evolutionarily surprising enough to count against the position. A crucial distinction remains, however: epiphenomenalism is not eliminativism. The epiphenomenalist preserves the reality of consciousness at the cost of its causal efficacy. The eliminativist — Dennett, in his argument in *Consciousness Explained* (1991) that the traditional conception of qualia is incoherent — denies that consciousness as traditionally understood exists at all. These are sharply different positions with sharply different costs.
