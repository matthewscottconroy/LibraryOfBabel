# Emergence and Property Dualism

When water molecules aggregate, something new appears: wetness, viscosity, surface tension. None of these properties belongs to a single H₂O molecule. They are, in some sense, emergent. But this kind of emergence raises no philosophical difficulties for the physicalist: given a complete description of the molecules and their interactions, the macroscopic properties are in principle derivable. What is surprising is not that they arise but that they are difficult to predict from the bottom up. This is weak emergence — epistemological, not ontological. The emergent property is nothing over and above the lower-level properties; it is just the lower-level properties described at a higher grain.

The question that property dualism presses is whether all emergence is weak. If phenomenal consciousness is only weakly emergent, then a complete physics would in principle entail the existence and character of consciousness — which is the physicalist position. The derivation may be practically impossible, but in principle the phenomenal follows from the physical. If consciousness is strongly emergent, then no laws of physics, however complete, could entail the presence or character of phenomenal experience. Strong emergence introduces genuinely new properties into the world that cannot be derived from any lower-level description.

C.D. Broad first rigorously distinguished emergent from resultant properties in *The Mind and Its Place in Nature* (1925). He argued that some properties of complex wholes are genuinely emergent — not even ideally derivable from properties of their parts. Broad's emergent properties require "trans-ordinal" laws connecting lower-level properties to higher-level emergent ones — laws of emergence rather than laws of composition, specifying how new properties arise when lower-level properties are organized in certain ways. Chalmers's psychophysical laws are trans-ordinal laws in exactly this sense: they specify which physical states are accompanied by which phenomenal states, without being derivable from physical laws. The existence of these laws would constitute strong emergence of consciousness, and their existence is what Chalmers means by "naturalistic dualism."

Philosophical accounts of the higher-lower relationship fall on a spectrum. Strict reductionism holds that mental properties are identical to or definable in physical terms; there is no genuine emergence. Weak physicalist emergence holds that mental properties are not identical to physical ones and resist easy prediction, but are in principle derivable from a complete physical description. Non-reductive physicalism holds that mental properties supervene on and are causally grounded in physical properties but are not reducible to them — stronger than weak emergence, but not strongly emergent in the full ontological sense. Strong emergence holds that phenomenal properties are genuinely novel and require additional psychophysical laws. Substance dualism holds that mental properties are properties of a genuinely non-physical substance entirely.

## Strong Emergence and Its Problems

Strong emergence is philosophically attractive because it seems to capture two obvious facts at once: consciousness arises from physical complexity (supporting the physicalist's ontological commitment) while no physical description seems to entail phenomenal character (explaining the explanatory gap). But the position faces serious pressures from both directions.

Critics argue that strong emergence is either incoherent or indistinguishable from substance dualism in a thin disguise. If a property is genuinely not derivable from physical properties, then there must be additional laws governing its emergence. These trans-ordinal laws are empirically testable — they specify regularities that can be observed — but unexplained: why do these particular laws hold? Physicalism provides a unified theoretical framework in which all regularities are ultimately derivable from fundamental physical laws. Strong emergence fragments this unity without explaining why it does so at precisely the point where phenomenal properties appear.

Kim's exclusion argument creates a further problem for emergentist accounts that include downward causation — the idea that higher-level emergent properties causally influence lower-level processes. If every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, there is no room for emergent properties to make additional causal contributions. The emergentist must either deny causal closure or accept that higher-level properties cause things "in virtue of" their physical realization — which threatens to dissolve the genuine novelty that strong emergence was supposed to provide. The debate between Chalmers's property dualism and type-B physicalism turns on whether the conceptual gap between physical and phenomenal descriptions reflects a metaphysical gap (strong emergence, genuine property dualism) or merely a feature of our concepts compatible with underlying physical-phenomenal identity. Both parties agree there is a conceptual gap; they disagree about what it implies.
