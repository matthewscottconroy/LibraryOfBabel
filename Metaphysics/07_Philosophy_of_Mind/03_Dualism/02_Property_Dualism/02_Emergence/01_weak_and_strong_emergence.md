# Weak and Strong Emergence

Mark Bedau's formal account of weak emergence runs as follows: a macro-level property M of system S is weakly emergent if it can be derived from S's micro-level properties, but only by simulation — by actually running S forward from its initial conditions, not by any shortcut derivation. Consider Conway's Game of Life: the appearance of gliders — moving patterns that propagate across the grid — is weakly emergent in exactly this sense. No shortcut formula predicts which initial configurations produce gliders; you have to simulate. But given the rules and initial conditions, the gliders are fully derivable in principle. The surprise is epistemic, not ontological. The gliders are nothing over and above the underlying cells updating according to their simple rules.

Weak emergence is compatible with strict physicalism. The emergent property is not metaphysically novel; it is just the lower-level properties described at a higher grain. The wetness of water weakly emerges from H₂O molecules in this sense. If consciousness is only weakly emergent, then a complete physics would specify initial conditions and laws sufficient to derive the occurrence and character of consciousness — not by any conceptual shortcut, but by simulating the neural processes in sufficient detail. This would explain why consciousness seems surprising and not easily derivable from physical descriptions while being compatible with physicalism. The derivation may be practically impossible, but its possibility in principle is what matters for the metaphysics.

Strong emergence is a fundamentally different category. A property is strongly emergent from lower-level properties when it is genuinely not derivable even in principle from a complete lower-level description. Strong emergence requires additional principles or laws — what C.D. Broad, writing in *The Mind and Its Place in Nature* (1925), called "trans-ordinal" laws — that specify how higher-level properties arise when lower-level properties are organized in certain ways, without being entailed by the lower-level laws themselves. These laws are not laws of composition or interaction; they are laws of emergence. Strong emergence introduces genuinely new properties into the world that are invisible at the lower level no matter how complete or detailed the lower-level description is.

## Implications for Philosophy of Mind

For the philosophy of mind, the weak/strong distinction is not terminological but load-bearing. If phenomenal consciousness is strongly emergent, then physics cannot in principle entail consciousness even in a completed, ideal theory. Phenomenal properties require additional psychophysical laws not contained in any physical theory. This is Chalmers's position: his psychophysical laws are trans-ordinal laws in Broad's sense, specifying which physical states are accompanied by which phenomenal states. The hard problem is, at bottom, the claim that consciousness exhibits strong emergence — that the conceptual gap between physical description and phenomenal description reflects a genuine metaphysical gap.

If consciousness is only weakly emergent, then the conceptual gap is an epistemic artifact of our position as finite reasoners who cannot perform the requisite derivation. The gap is real but misleading: it tracks our cognitive limitations, not any discontinuity in the fabric of nature. This is the position type-B physicalists defend. Both parties agree that there is an a priori gap between phenomenal and physical concepts — that no amount of physical description logically entails any phenomenal description. They disagree about whether this gap is merely conceptual (weak emergence, compatible with physicalism) or reflects genuine ontological novelty (strong emergence, property dualism).

Critics of strong emergence argue that any "additional law" governing emergent properties introduces something beyond the physical. The trans-ordinal laws are empirically testable — they specify regularities that can be observed — but unexplained by any physical theory. Their existence would be a brute contingent fact at the fundamental level. Physicalism's advantage is that it provides a unified theoretical framework in which all regularities are ultimately derivable from fundamental physical laws; strong emergence fractures this unity at precisely the point where phenomenal properties appear, without explaining why. Whether this counts against strong emergence or just reveals the genuine novelty of consciousness is the crux of the debate.
