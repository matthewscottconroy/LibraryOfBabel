# Objections to Emergentism about Consciousness

Emergentism about consciousness faces pressure from both directions — from physicalists who think it concedes too much and from dualists who think it concedes too little. This is what we should expect from a position that tries to occupy middle ground: each side has good reason to argue that the middle is unstable. Let us work through the main objections carefully, because together they reveal why the emergentist position, despite its appeal, is so difficult to sustain.

Kim's exclusion argument is the most systematic. If emergent mental property M causes physical event P, then we can ask: does M's causation of P violate the causal closure of the physical? If P already has a sufficient physical cause, then M either overdetermines P (both M and its physical base are sufficient causes of the same event) or M is causally inert. Systematic overdetermination of all physical events by emergent mental properties is implausible — it would mean that every bodily movement has two independent sufficient causes. The formal version makes the point clearly:

- P1: If emergent mental property M causes physical event P, there is some sufficient cause of P.
- P2: Physical events have sufficient physical causes (causal closure).
- P3: M is not a physical property — it is genuinely emergent, i.e., non-physical.
- P4: If P has both a sufficient physical cause and a non-physical cause M, then P is causally overdetermined.
- P5: Systematic causal overdetermination of all physical events by mental events is implausible.
- C: Either M does not cause physical events (epiphenomenalism) or M is a physical property (not genuinely emergent).

The emergentist who wants downward causation must reject a premise. The most natural moves are to challenge P2 (deny strict causal closure) or to challenge P4 (argue that mental and physical causes are not two independent sufficient causes but two descriptions of the same underlying causal process). Both moves are available, but both carry costs: challenging closure requires revising our best-confirmed physics; dissolving the independence of mental and physical causes risks eliminating the very novelty that strong emergence was supposed to provide.

## Unpredictability, the Explanation Challenge, and Mysterianism

Strong emergence requires that emergent properties not be in-principle predictable from physical properties. But if a demon with complete physical knowledge would be unable to predict consciousness, the position becomes difficult to distinguish from property dualism. The emergentist insists that consciousness is still a property of physical systems — just one whose appearance requires additional laws. But those additional laws are trans-ordinal laws external to physics, and their existence raises the explanation challenge: what is it about these specific physical configurations that gives rise to consciousness rather than some other property? Strong emergence answers: it just does. The laws specify which physical configurations produce which emergent properties, and these laws are primitive. This is analogous to the primitive character of fundamental physical laws — we cannot explain why gravity obeys an inverse-square law; it just does. But fundamental physical laws have precise mathematical structure, make testable predictions, and integrate into a unified theoretical framework. Trans-ordinal emergence laws governing consciousness would need similar precision, and no one has developed them.

The parsimony objection adds methodological pressure. Emergentism postulates additional principles governing the emergence of consciousness — bridging laws that are external to physics. Physicalism requires no such bridges. Property dualism posits a different kind of substance rather than a bridge. Emergentism seems to inherit the ontological complexity of both positions without the theoretical coherence of either. Colin McGinn's mysterialism attempts a different diagnosis: the gap between physical and phenomenal descriptions is permanent and principled, not because consciousness is strongly emergent, but because human cognitive faculties are constitutively limited in a way that prevents us from grasping the physical-phenomenal connection. The connection is real and physical — not a case of genuine strong emergence — but we cannot understand it. This is neither emergentism nor simple physicalism: it locates the explanatory gap in our cognitive architecture rather than in nature. Whether this is satisfying depends on how one feels about the prospect of a real physical connection that is in principle unintelligible to us.
