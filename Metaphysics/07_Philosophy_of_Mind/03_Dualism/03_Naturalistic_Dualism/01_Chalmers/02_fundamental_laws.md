# Fundamental Psychophysical Laws

The claim that consciousness is connected to physical processes by fundamental psychophysical laws sounds like a placeholder — a way of marking where the explanation should go without actually providing one. Chalmers is aware of this charge, and he attempts to give his psychophysical laws genuine content through several candidate principles.

The "principle of structural coherence" is his most developed proposal. The phenomenal structure of experience mirrors the functional organization of cognitive processes. If two functional states have the same structural relations to each other and to inputs and outputs, they will have the same structural phenomenal relations. The three-dimensional character of visual experience mirrors the three-dimensional structure of visual information processing. The temporal structure of auditory experience mirrors the temporal structure of auditory processing. The relational structure of emotional experience — some emotions feel more similar to others than to yet others — mirrors the relational structure of emotional processing. This structural mirroring is not coincidental; it is a law of nature connecting functional organization to phenomenal character. The content of experience is determined by the functional organization of the underlying cognitive system, even though the phenomenal character itself — the specific felt quality — is additional to any purely functional specification. The principle has testable implications: systems with identical structural information-processing organization should have identical phenomenal structure.

The fading qualia thought experiment supports the principle from a different angle. Suppose we replace neurons one by one with silicon chips that are functionally equivalent — same input-output behavior as the neurons they replace. If qualia were to fade as functional organization remained constant, the subject would have no way of knowing this: functional states unchanged, introspective dispositions unchanged, reports unchanged. But that would make introspection systematically unreliable in a way that seems incoherent. Therefore, qualia cannot fade independently of functional organization — which supports a psychophysical law connecting function to phenomenal character. A similar argument applies to "dancing qualia" — radical changes in phenomenal character while functional organization remains constant. Critics respond that these arguments establish a law about introspection, not about phenomenal experience itself: what cannot come apart is introspection and function, and phenomenal character might be a third thing that comes apart from both.

## Double-Aspect Information Theory and the Turn Toward Panpsychism

Chalmers's most speculative proposal draws on Claude Shannon's information theory and Gregory Bateson's notion of information as "a difference that makes a difference." Wherever there is information processing in the relevant physical sense — wherever a difference at one node makes a difference elsewhere — there is also an experiential dimension. Information is neither purely physical nor purely mental but a fundamental feature of reality with both a physical aspect (the causal relations constituting the information structure) and a phenomenal aspect (the qualitative character of experience). This double-aspect character connects Chalmers's framework to Russellian monism: the intrinsic character of fundamental physical entities might be phenomenal, with the structural-relational properties being all that physics captures.

The consequence is panpsychism or panprotopsychism: if all information has a phenomenal aspect, then even simple physical systems that process information — thermostats, logic gates — have some form of experience. Chalmers regards this as surprising but not obviously unacceptable. It is the price of taking phenomenal consciousness seriously as a fundamental feature of reality rather than a derivative one.

The deep problem for all of this remains: even after positing psychophysical laws, we have not explained why there are phenomenal properties at all. The hard problem is not solved by citing a correlation, however lawful. Why is there something it is like to be in a state that satisfies these structural-functional conditions? The psychophysical laws tell us which physical states are accompanied by which phenomenal properties, but they leave unexplained why the accompaniment exists at all. Chalmers acknowledges this honestly: naturalistic dualism is not a solution to the hard problem but a framework for living with it — taking it seriously as a genuine feature of reality that current physics cannot accommodate.
