# Prospects for Naturalistic Dualism

Naturalistic dualism sits in an awkward position. It is more defensible than substance dualism — no interaction problem in the Cartesian form, no separate immaterial substance requiring unexplained causal contact with the physical. It takes the hard problem seriously in a way that most physicalist positions do not. But it occupies a position that is difficult to stabilize, and the most interesting recent developments in Chalmers's own thinking are best understood as attempts to find more stable ground.

The most sophisticated physicalist response is the phenomenal concepts strategy, developed by Brian Loar, David Papineau, and others. The strategy holds that phenomenal concepts — the concepts by which we refer to our own phenomenal states in direct acquaintance — have a distinctive character that explains the apparent explanatory gap between physical and phenomenal descriptions, even if no metaphysical gap exists. When I form the concept "this pain," I do so by direct acquaintance with the pain itself, not by describing it through functional or physical properties. Phenomenal concepts are recognitional concepts based on acquaintance; they pick out their referents in a way that involves no description of physical or functional properties. This is why physical descriptions never seem to "add up to" phenomenal descriptions — they approach the same property from radically different conceptual routes. The zombie thought experiment appears to work because we conceive of the absence of what phenomenal concepts refer to (the felt quality, grasped by acquaintance) while keeping present what physical concepts refer to (the neural processes). But these are two modes of access to one and the same property.

Chalmers's response targets the gap this explanation leaves. Even granting the phenomenal concepts strategy, we should be able to explain why phenomenal concepts are satisfied — why the physical processes that satisfy physical descriptions also satisfy phenomenal descriptions. The strategy explains why there appears to be an explanatory gap (conceptual distinctness) but not why one property satisfies both physical and phenomenal descriptions. The hard problem re-emerges at this level: not "why does it seem like there is an explanatory gap?" but "why is there a property that is grasped both by functional description and by direct phenomenal acquaintance?" Whether the phenomenal concepts strategy can answer this second question is the crux of the debate.

## Russellian Monism and the Turn Toward Idealism

The combination problem threatens the double-aspect information theory from within. If consciousness or proto-consciousness is present at the level of elementary information processing, then human consciousness must somehow result from the combination of vast numbers of micro-level experiential properties. But how do micro-level phenomenal properties combine to produce the unified, structured phenomenal consciousness of a human being? This is the combination problem, originally raised for panpsychism, and Chalmers has addressed it explicitly in "The Combination Problem for Panpsychism" (2016). His tentative response has involved considering whether Russellian monism can avoid the problem by making the phenomenal the intrinsic nature of the physical rather than an additional property.

Russellian monism is perhaps the most popular alternative among sympathizers with Chalmers's framework. It holds that fundamental physical entities have intrinsic properties not captured by physics — which characterizes only structural-relational properties, not intrinsic natures. If those intrinsic properties are phenomenal or proto-phenomenal, then consciousness is the intrinsic nature of the physical, not an addition to it. This avoids the supplement-to-physics problem: the phenomenal and the physical are not two separate things requiring connecting laws but two aspects of one reality, with physics capturing the structural-relational aspect and phenomenology capturing the intrinsic aspect.

Chalmers's increasing openness to idealism reflects the natural terminus of this inquiry. If consciousness is fundamental and physics only captures the structural-relational, why not hold that reality is fundamentally experiential — that what we call the physical is a structural description of an order that is intrinsically experiential? Contemporary idealism, defended by Philip Goff and Bernardo Kastrup among others, attempts to answer Berkeley's central difficulty: how does one ground an intersubjective physical order in individual experiential facts? Chalmers describes Russellian monism as his preferred framework in recent work, while acknowledging the combination problem as its main unresolved challenge. Naturalistic dualism's ongoing value is not that it resolves the hard problem but that it makes precisely clear what any satisfying resolution would require.
