# Review Questions — Dualism

1. The conceivability argument holds that because we can coherently conceive of mind existing without body (or of zombies without phenomenal experience), mind and body must be distinct. Arnauld objected that we can conceive of water without conceiving of H₂O, yet they are identical. What makes the conceivability of zombies relevantly different from this case, according to Chalmers, and is the distinction persuasive?

2. Substance dualism faces the interaction problem: an immaterial mind has no spatial location, surface, or mass, so the Cartesian physics of contact causation cannot describe how it acts on the body. Epiphenomenalism avoids the interaction problem by denying mental-to-physical causation. Which bullet is more painful to bite, and is there a position that avoids both?

3. Eric Olson's pairing problem asks: if there are multiple immaterial minds, what pairs each mind with a particular body rather than some other? Spatial relations do the pairing for physical objects. What resources does the substance dualist have for answering this, and how serious is the challenge compared to the interaction problem?

4. Property dualism holds that phenomenal properties are non-physical while maintaining that mental events are physical. T.H. Huxley's epiphenomenalism is one form of property dualism — consciousness exists but causes nothing. The self-refutation objection presses that if phenomenal properties cause no behavior, introspective reports about those properties are not caused by the experiences themselves. How damaging is this to epiphenomenalism's evidential base?

5. Strong emergence — the thesis that some higher-level properties are not derivable even in principle from a complete lower-level description — is contrasted with weak emergence, where in-principle derivability exists but requires simulation. Why does this distinction matter for whether consciousness is a genuine problem for physicalism or merely an epistemic puzzle?

6. Chalmers's naturalistic dualism proposes fundamental psychophysical laws connecting physical states to phenomenal states, analogous to the fundamental laws of physics. He explicitly distinguishes this from substance dualism. Is this distinction stable? Does positing additional laws beyond physics constitute a form of dualism that inherits the interaction problem in new guise?

7. Property dualism and panpsychism (discussed later) are sometimes presented as the two serious alternatives to physicalism. Compare their explanatory strategies: property dualism says consciousness is a novel, non-physical property that emerges at a certain level of physical complexity; panpsychism says consciousness is present at the fundamental level. Which problem does each face more acutely — the hard problem or the interaction/causal closure problem?

8. The modal argument for substance dualism concludes from the conceivability of mind without body that mind and body are *possibly* distinct, and therefore actually distinct. What does it mean for the argument that "possible worlds" semantics is itself contested, and how do type-B physicalists (who deny the conceivability-to-possibility bridge) differ from type-A physicalists (who deny the conceivability of zombies altogether)?
