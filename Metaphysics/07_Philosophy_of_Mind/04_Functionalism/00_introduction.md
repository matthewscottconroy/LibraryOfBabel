# Functionalism

What makes a mental state the mental state it is? Pain, for example, is not defined by what it is made of — silicon, neurons, or something else — but by what it does: it is caused by tissue damage, it causes avoidance behavior and distress, it motivates seeking relief, it interacts with other mental states in characteristic ways. On the functionalist view, pain is the state that plays the pain-role — whatever physical or computational state that may be. This is functionalism: mental states are defined by their causal roles, their functional organization, not by their physical constitution.

Functionalism was developed in the 1960s and 1970s by Putnam, Lewis, and Armstrong, and it quickly became the dominant view in philosophy of mind. Its appeal is substantial: it explains multiple realizability (the same mental state can be realized in different physical systems, as long as those systems have the right functional organization), it connects philosophy of mind with cognitive science and artificial intelligence, and it avoids the interaction problem facing substance dualism.

The serious objections to functionalism were developed almost as quickly as the view itself. Ned Block's inverted qualia argument: if two people are functionally identical but one experiences red where the other experiences blue, functionalism predicts they are in the same mental state — but they are not. Block's absent qualia argument: a system with the right functional organization (an entire nation of people, each playing the role of one neuron) might have no experience whatsoever despite being functionally identical to a conscious being. China Brain cases dramatize the worry that functional organization is not sufficient for consciousness. Searle's Chinese Room argument targets computational functionalism specifically: understanding requires more than symbol manipulation, however systematically organized.

This chapter develops functionalism and its major critics, setting up the discussion of consciousness and the hard problem that follows.
