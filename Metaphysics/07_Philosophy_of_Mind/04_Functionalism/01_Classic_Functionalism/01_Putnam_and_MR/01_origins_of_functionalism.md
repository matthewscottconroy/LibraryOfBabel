# Origins of Functionalism

By the early 1960s, two positions in the philosophy of mind had established their inadequacy. Behaviorism had tried to analyze mental states as behavioral dispositions, but the analysis was circular and missed the causal story: when someone reaches for an umbrella because they believe it will rain, the belief is causally intermediate between the evidence and the behavior, not just a redescription of the behavior. The type identity theory corrected this by positing genuinely inner, causally efficacious brain states — but then ran into multiple realizability. If pain is C-fiber firing, only organisms with C-fibers can feel pain. Octopi, whose nervous systems differ radically from ours, and hypothetical Martians with silicon-based neurochemistry, seemed capable of pain on any reasonable view. The identity theory excluded them by fiat.

Functionalism emerged as a principled middle ground. Mental states are defined by their causal-functional roles — their relations to sensory inputs, behavioral outputs, and other mental states — without specifying what physical substrate must implement the role. Pain is whatever state plays the pain-role: caused by tissue damage, causing distress and avoidance behavior, interacting with beliefs and desires in characteristic ways. This allows for multiple realizability by definition: anything that plays the pain-role is in pain, regardless of its physical constitution. And it preserves inner states as genuinely inner — not reducible to behavioral dispositions — while giving them a physically neutral characterization.

The philosophical groundwork was laid by Alan Turing's work on computation, which showed that computational processes could be defined abstractly, independent of any particular physical implementation. Hilary Putnam's "Psychological Predicates" (1967) made the computer-mind analogy central: the mind is to the brain as a Turing machine is to its physical realization. A Turing machine can be realized in vacuum tubes, transistors, or biological neurons; what makes it the Turing machine it is is its abstract functional organization. Mental states similarly correspond to computational states in the machine table — the functional organization that determines how the system responds to inputs. Psychology is to neuroscience as program description is to hardware description.

## Lewis's Ramsey-Sentence Approach

A complementary tradition developed through David Lewis's use of the Ramsey-Lewis method. Lewis argued that mental state terms — pain, belief, desire — are implicitly defined by the commonsense psychological theory in which they figure. Take the theory T of pain: all the platitudes about when pain occurs, what typically causes it, what behavior it causes, and how it interacts with other mental states. Form the Ramsey sentence of T by replacing "pain" (and other mental state terms) with existentially quantified variables. The Ramsey sentence says: there exist states x, y, z such that they stand in the relations specified by T. Pain is then defined as whatever state occupies the pain-role in T — the state that is caused by tissue damage, causes avoidance behavior, and so on. This approach makes pain a functional kind while preserving its causal-role structure.

The success of classical cognitive science in the 1970s and 1980s seemed to vindicate the broader program. Cognitive processes could be studied as computational processes, and artificial systems performing cognitive tasks seemed to implement the relevant functional organization. Functionalism became the dominant view in philosophy of mind and the implicit assumption of cognitive science. But the success story is not unqualified. The Chinese Room (Searle) attacked machine functionalism directly: a Turing machine manipulates symbols without understanding their meaning, so if the mind is just a Turing machine, it doesn't understand anything either. The qualia objections — absent qualia, inverted qualia — attacked functionalism from the phenomenal direction: systems that implement the right functional organization might nonetheless lack any phenomenal experience, or have systematically different phenomenal experience, which shows that functional organization does not exhaust what it is to have a mind. These pressures have driven the development of neo-functionalist positions that attempt to repair the original theory rather than abandon it.
