# Putnam and the Origins of Functionalism

There is something poignant about the fact that the philosopher who gave functionalism its canonical formulation also became one of its most penetrating critics. Hilary Putnam developed functionalism in a series of papers in the 1960s — most centrally "Psychological Predicates" (1967) — as an alternative to both behaviorism and the type identity theory. The core idea is that mental states are defined by their functional roles: their causal relations to sensory inputs, behavioral outputs, and other mental states. What makes something a pain is not what it is made of but what it does. Pain is caused by tissue damage, causes distress and avoidance behavior, and interacts with beliefs and desires in characteristic ways. Any state that plays this role, in any physical substrate, is pain.

Putnam's approach drew directly on computer science. Just as the same program can run on different hardware — an algorithm does not specify the physical mechanism that implements it — the same mental state can be implemented in different physical substrates. A Turing machine can be realized in vacuum tubes, transistors, or biological neurons; what makes it the Turing machine it is is its abstract functional organization, not its physical substrate. Mental states are similarly identified with functional roles, not physical types. The mind is the program of the brain. Psychology stands to neuroscience as software description stands to hardware description: the same psychological description can be true of many different neural implementations.

This move had decisive advantages over the identity theory. Multiple realizability follows immediately: an octopus, a Martian, and a human can all be in pain if all three are in states that play the pain-functional-role, regardless of their different physical constitutions. The chauvinism of type identity — which denied mental states to any organism whose neurochemistry differs from ours — is avoided. And functionalism provided a framework for cognitive science: mental processes could be studied as computational processes, independently of their physical implementation. The formal machinery was clean: let T be the commonsense psychological theory of pain. Then "is in pain" is defined as "instantiates the state that plays the pain-role in T." This is a functional definition, specifying no physical property.

## The Software Analogy and Its Limits

The computer science analogy is philosophically illuminating, but Putnam himself eventually identified its limits. Programs have the semantic properties they have partly because they are interpreted — by users, or by other programs that assign meanings to their symbols. The functionalist must explain how the mind's "program" acquires its semantic properties — its intentional content — without appeal to an external interpreter. This is the problem of naturalizing intentionality, and functional role alone does not solve it. Two systems can have the same causal-functional organization while being about different things, as Twin Earth thought experiments demonstrate.

Putnam's recantation, developed in *Representation and Reality* (1988), turned on this point. Functionalism defines mental states by their causal-functional roles, but causal-functional roles do not fix semantic content. Moreover, the multiple realizability argument — which Putnam had used against type identity — applies to functionalism itself: if the same functional role can be realized by different physical types, there is a sense in which functional types are also subject to higher-order indeterminacy, threatening functionalism's own identification of mental types with functional types. Putnam's later work moved toward a form of direct realism and semantic externalism that is deeply at odds with the computational picture he had originally championed.

That the theory's primary architect came to regard it as fundamentally flawed is philosophically significant. It is not a refutation — many philosophers have defended and refined functionalism in ways that address Putnam's concerns — but it is a reminder that the elegance of the original formulation did not dissolve the underlying difficulties. What Putnam gave us was not a solution to the mind-body problem but the clearest formulation of a very promising approach to it: an approach that has generated some of the most productive debates in the philosophy of mind over the past fifty years.
