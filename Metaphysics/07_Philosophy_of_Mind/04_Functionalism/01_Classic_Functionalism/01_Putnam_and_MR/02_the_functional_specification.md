# The Functional Specification of Mental States

Functionalism says that mental states are defined by their causal-functional roles. But what exactly is a functional role, and how precisely must it be specified? Too coarse a specification leads to liberalism: thermostats count as minded, since they have states caused by temperature that cause heating responses. Too fine a specification leads to chauvinism: only beings with human-like functional organization qualify, which seems to rule out genuinely minded aliens whose psychology differs from ours in fine-grained details. Getting the specification right is not a technical afterthought; it is essential for functionalism to succeed as a theory of mind.

David Lewis's Ramsey-sentence approach provides the most systematic answer. Mental state terms — pain, belief, desire — are implicitly defined by folk psychological theory: the collection of platitudes about when pain occurs, what causes it, and what it causes in turn. "Pain is caused by tissue damage." "Pain causes avoidance behavior." "Pain interacts with desire to stay safe to motivate avoidance." The Ramsey sentence of this theory replaces mental state terms with existentially quantified variables: there exist states X₁, X₂, ... such that X₁ is caused by tissue damage, X₁ causes avoidance, X₂ is caused by perceived threats, and so on. "Pain" then rigidly designates the first component of the unique n-tuple that satisfies all the functional relations. If there is a unique such realization, "pain" has a determinate reference; if there is no realization, the terms fail to refer.

The folk psychological starting point generates several difficulties. Folk psychology is imprecise, potentially inconsistent, and possibly culture-relative — different communities may hold slightly different platitudes. More seriously, if eliminative materialists are right that folk psychology is radically false, then nothing realizes the folk theory and no state counts as pain. The Ramsey approach inherits whatever adequacy folk psychology has as a theory of mental states.

## Grain Problems and the Inferential Role Alternative

The grain of mental state individuation presents a persistent challenge. Should mental state types be individuated by broad functional role — anything that plays the pain-role is pain — or by narrow functional role specifying a particular pattern of functional relations? The broader the specification, the more room for multiple realizability; the narrower, the greater the risk of chauvinism. Ned Block distinguished functional role semantics (narrow, inferentialist role), Ramsey functionalism (defined by the full causal role in a psychological theory), and analytic functionalism (defined by conceptual analysis of folk psychology). Each has different implications for what systems count as genuinely mentally endowed.

The inferential role alternative focuses on the patterns of inference a mental state participates in rather than its causal connections to inputs and outputs. The content of a pain-belief is partly constituted by the inference from "I am in pain" to "I should avoid what caused this." But content externalism — Putnam's own Twin Earth argument and Tyler Burge's social externalism — creates problems for this approach. Inferential roles are determined by internal patterns of inference, but the content of mental states may be determined partly by external facts about the environment. Water-thoughts on Earth and water-thoughts on Twin Earth, where "water" is XYZ, have the same inferential roles but different contents. The inferential role approach generates a narrow content that does not match our ordinary content attributions.

The liberalism problem can be addressed by making functional specifications more complex and demanding — requiring not just causal connections to tissue damage and avoidance behavior, but integration with a complex motivational and cognitive system, appropriate update upon learning the damage is not serious, characteristic hedonic valence, and more. But this response faces a dilemma: either the required complexity is specified precisely — in which case the functionalist must show what exactly that complexity is — or it is vague, in which case the boundary between minded and non-minded systems is vague in a troubling way. This is not a decisive objection, but it places a burden of specification that functionalism has not yet fully discharged.
