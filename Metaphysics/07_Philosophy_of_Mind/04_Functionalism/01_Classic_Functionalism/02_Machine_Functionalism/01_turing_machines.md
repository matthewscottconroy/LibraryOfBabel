# Machine Functionalism and Turing Machines

Consider what Turing's abstract machine actually is. A Turing machine is a tuple (Q, Σ, δ, q₀, F): a finite set of internal states Q, an alphabet Σ, a transition function δ: Q × Σ → Q × Σ × {L, R} specifying the next state, the output, and the direction of tape movement given any input, an initial state q₀, and a set of accepting states F. The machine is defined entirely by this table. Two physically different devices that implement the same table are computationally equivalent — they are the same machine. What makes a Turing machine the machine it is has nothing to do with whether it is realized in vacuum tubes, transistors, or biological tissue. The abstract structure is everything.

Machine functionalism, as Putnam formulated it in "Psychological Predicates" (1967) and "Minds and Machines" (1967), proposes that mental states correspond to states in Q. Pain would correspond to some state q_pain such that the transition function maps (q_pain, typical tissue-damage inputs) to avoidance states and avoidance outputs. The mind is the machine table; whatever physical system implements the table has the relevant mental organization. The primary appeal is precision: unlike vague earlier functionalist talk of "functional roles," Turing machine states have a mathematically exact characterization. This provided the theoretical basis for classical cognitive science and the computational-representational theory of mind developed by Jerry Fodor, Zenon Pylyshyn, and others through the 1970s and 1980s.

The problems are equally precise. First, the finitude problem: Turing machines have finite state tables, but human minds seem to have a potentially infinite number of distinct psychological states — I can believe that 7 > 6, that 8 > 7, that 9 > 8, and so on indefinitely. Putnam himself later used this observation against machine functionalism. Second, the content problem: Turing machine states are defined entirely by their computational properties — their role in formal symbol manipulation — but they have no intrinsic semantic content. A machine table specifies transitions between symbolic states without any of those symbols meaning anything. This is the gap that Searle's Chinese Room exploits. Third, the grain problem: any physical brain runs as a computational system with far more fine-grained internal states than our folk psychological descriptions recognize. Which computational states correspond to which mental states, and on what principled basis?

## Machine Functionalism's Legacy

Despite these problems, machine functionalism's greatest contribution is methodological: it established the standard of rigor for functionalist theories. The demand that a functionalist account specify its functional roles precisely enough to have real theoretical content is one that subsequent proposals — causal role functionalism, teleofunctionalism, two-dimensional approaches — have all had to meet. The Turing machine model remains the clearest example of what a precise functional specification looks like, even if the specific identification of mental states with machine states faces the objections above.

Ned Block's later functional role semantics attempted to address the content problem by arguing that semantic content is determined holistically by functional role — the meaning of a representation is its entire pattern of inferential and causal connections to other representations and to the world. But the tension between computational and semantic characterizations of mental states remains live. Computation is syntax; meaning is semantic; and whether any syntactic process can constitute semantic content without external interpretation is precisely what the debate between computationalism and its critics has been about since the 1980s.
