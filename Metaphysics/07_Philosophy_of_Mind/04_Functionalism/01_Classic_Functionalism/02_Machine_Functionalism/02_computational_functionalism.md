# Computational Functionalism

The computational theory of mind, developed by Jerry Fodor and Zenon Pylyshyn through the 1970s and 1980s, takes the functionalist program one step further: not only are mental states defined by their causal roles, but cognitive processes are computations — sequences of operations on symbolic representations that implement algorithms. The mind is the program; the brain is the hardware. Mental representations have a syntactic structure, composed of simpler elements with their own syntactic properties, and cognitive operations are sensitive to that syntactic structure. This is how physical processes — which respond to syntax — can implement rational operations — which are defined semantically: the syntax tracks the semantics. The slogan is "syntactic vehicles for semantic content."

The architecture this requires is what Fodor called the Language of Thought (LOT): a system of mental representations with compositional structure. Complex thoughts are built from simpler ones by syntactic operations, and the semantic properties of complex thoughts are determined by the properties of their parts and the way they are combined. Three features of cognition are supposed to follow. Productivity: we can think indefinitely many distinct thoughts from finitely many primitive representations and a finite combinatorial rule set. Systematicity: if you can think that John loves Mary, you can think that Mary loves John — the same representational components arranged differently. Inferential coherence: transitions between thoughts are sensitive to their logical structure, reflected in their syntax. Fodor argued in *The Language of Thought* (1975) that LOT is not merely one theoretical option but an empirical necessity: no other cognitive architecture can explain all three features.

The grounding problem cuts against this picture. What gives the syntactic symbols their semantic content? Syntax alone is insufficient for semantics — the same physical symbol can represent anything, depending on interpretation. Two main candidates have been developed. Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory holds that the symbol "horse" means horse because tokens of "horse" that are caused by cows depend asymmetrically on tokens caused by horses, but not vice versa. The content is fixed by the nomic relation between symbols and worldly properties, with errors explained by the asymmetry. Ruth Millikan's teleosemantics holds that content is fixed by the evolutionary function of the representing system: "horse" refers to horses because the neural states serving as horse-representations were selected, in an evolutionary sense, for their role in tracking horses.

## Connectionism and the Hard Problem

Connectionist networks challenged the computational theory from an architectural direction, showing that sophisticated cognitive performance could be achieved by systems that do not manipulate discrete symbolic representations. In connectionist architectures, representations are distributed patterns of activation across networks of simple units; there are no discrete symbolic vehicles and no explicit combinatorial operations. Fodor and Pylyshyn's "Connectionism and Cognitive Architecture" (1988) argued that connectionist systems either implement classical computation — and thereby vindicate the LOT hypothesis — or fail to explain productivity and systematicity and are therefore inadequate models of cognition. The debate shaped cognitive science throughout the 1990s and has returned in a new form with the success of deep learning.

One critical limitation remains regardless of this architectural debate: computational functionalism provides a theory of cognitive architecture — how information is represented, stored, and manipulated — but does not address phenomenal consciousness. Syntactic operations on symbolic representations explain the flow of information through a cognitive system; they do not explain why any of this processing is accompanied by experience. The hard problem is not resolved by identifying mental processes with computational processes. This is Searle's core challenge: computation is syntax; consciousness involves something beyond syntax. A computational system running perfectly can be a zombie, implementing every cognitive function without any phenomenal experience. Whether this shows that computational functionalism is false about consciousness, or whether it shows that phenomenal consciousness is an additional phenomenon requiring a different theory, is the question on which the most fundamental disputes in the philosophy of mind turn.
