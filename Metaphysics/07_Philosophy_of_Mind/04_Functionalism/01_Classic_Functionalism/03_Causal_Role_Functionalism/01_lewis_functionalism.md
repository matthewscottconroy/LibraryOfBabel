# Lewis's Functionalism

David Lewis's functionalism, developed in "Psychophysical and Theoretical Identifications" (1972) and "Mad Pain and Martian Pain" (1980), is distinctive in combining a general functional definition of mental states with a species-specific identity theory. The combination is designed to honor both multiple realizability — the same mental state type can be realized by different physical states in different organisms — and the physicalist intuition that in any given organism, mental states just are physical states.

The central contribution is the Ramsey-Lewis method applied to folk psychology. Take the folk psychological theory T, the body of platitudes about when mental states occur, what causes them, and what they cause. Form the Ramsey sentence by replacing all mental state terms with existentially quantified variables: ∃x₁∃x₂...∃xₙ T(x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ). This says: there are states that jointly satisfy all the folk psychological claims. Mental state term mᵢ then refers to the unique xᵢ that occupies the relevant role in T in the actual world. If C-fiber firing in humans is what satisfies the pain-role, "pain" in humans refers to C-fiber firing — contingently, as an empirical matter. In Martians with pressurized fluid in tubes playing the pain-role, "pain" refers to that pressurized-fluid state. Lewis's two-clause definition captures this:

1. Functional clause: pain = the state that occupies the pain-role (purely functional, allows multiple realizability).
2. Identity clause: in humans, pain = C-fiber firing (species-specific, contingent on empirical facts about what plays the role in humans).

## Mad Pain, Martian Pain, and the Limits

"Mad Pain and Martian Pain" tests this framework with two contrasting cases. Mad pain: suppose someone whose C-fiber firing causes pleasure rather than playing the pain-role. Does their C-fiber firing constitute their pain? Lewis says no: a mental state is pain only if it occupies the pain-role. If C-fiber firing does not play the pain-role in this person, it is not their pain — something else is. Mad pain illustrates that functional role, not physical type, is definitive. Martian pain: suppose Martians have pressurized fluid in tubes occupying the same causal role as our pain. Lewis says yes, they feel genuine pain — the role is what matters, and functional identity constitutes being the same mental state type.

Several problems press against this framework. The stability problem: Lewis's approach requires that folk psychology be stable and univocal enough to serve as the basis for mental state definition. If different people hold subtly different folk psychological theories, there is no single theory to Ramsify, and mental state terms may lack determinate reference. The uniqueness problem, raised by Frank Jackson: the Ramsey-Lewis method requires that the folk psychological theory be uniquely satisfied. If multiple distinct sets of states could jointly satisfy the Ramsified theory, the Ramsey sentence does not uniquely determine the reference of mental state terms. Modified Ramsey approaches have been developed to add uniqueness constraints, but these require further commitments about the theory's form. The chauvinism worry at the species level: by making the identity clause species-specific, Lewis implies that human pain and Martian pain are different natural kinds even if functionally identical — which seems to fragment mental state types in a way that undermines the explanatory unity functionalism was designed to provide. Lewis's framework remains among the most sophisticated in the literature, notable precisely because it makes these tensions visible and forces them to be addressed explicitly.
