# Functional Definition and Its Limits

The project of functionally defining mental state types — giving necessary and sufficient conditions for being a pain, a belief, a desire in terms of causal-functional role — faces challenges that have driven much of the subsequent development in philosophy of mind. These are not technical difficulties that can be resolved by refining the definitions; they reflect structural features of the approach itself.

Consider the holism problem first. Folk psychological theory is holistic: the role of any single mental state term is specified in terms of other mental state terms. Defining "pain" requires mentioning beliefs, desires, attention, memory — which are themselves defined functionally, in terms of yet more mental states. The result is a web of mutual dependencies rather than a set of independent, explicit definitions. The holism is not necessarily a problem — theoretical term definition is always somewhat holistic — but it makes functional definitions sensitive to the entire structure of folk psychology. If that structure changes under scientific revision, the reference of mental state terms changes with it. Whether folk psychology is stable enough to ground determinate reference is an open empirical and philosophical question.

The Twin Earth problem cuts deeper. Suppose on Twin Earth there is a substance XYZ, functionally indistinguishable from water in ordinary circumstances but with a different molecular structure. A Twin Earther whose internal states play exactly the water-role has states that represent XYZ, not water — even though their internal functional organization is identical to an Earthling's. Putnam's conclusion: content is not in the head. It is partly determined by what is actually in the environment. If so, functional definition cannot determine content fully: two people with identical functional roles can have different content because they live in different environments. This is a fundamental challenge for internalist causal role functionalism. The frame problem adds a practical dimension: specifying all the causal connections of even a simple belief — the belief that it will rain interacts differently with every other belief and desire in the system — requires potentially infinite case specification, suggesting that functional definitions must be holistic in a very strong sense.

## The Central Limitation: Phenomenal Character

The most discussed challenge is the most fundamental. No functional definition captures the phenomenal character of mental states. The argument:

- P1: Any functional definition of pain specifies only causal-relational properties of the state.
- P2: The phenomenal character of pain — what hurting feels like — is not a causal-relational property but an intrinsic qualitative property.
- C: Therefore, no functional definition of pain captures its phenomenal character.

P2 is the contested premise. Functionalists who identify phenomenal character with representational content — Tye's PANIC theory (Poised, Abstract, Non-conceptual, Intentional Content) and Dretske's representationalism — deny P2. Higher-order functionalists — Rosenthal, Lycan — identify phenomenal character with a specific kind of higher-order functional organization. Critics of functionalism — Chalmers, Nagel, Jackson in his original position — accept P2 and take it to refute functionalism about phenomenal consciousness. Block's access/phenomenal distinction makes the stakes precise: functional definition may adequately capture access consciousness — information being available for reasoning, reporting, and behavioral control — while leaving phenomenal consciousness unexplained. Externalist functionalism adds environmental constraints, attempting to determine wide content through functional role plus external causal history. These revisions are not retreats from functionalism but developments within it. They acknowledge that the original internalist formulation was incomplete while preserving the core commitment: what makes something a mental state is its role in the causal economy of a system.
