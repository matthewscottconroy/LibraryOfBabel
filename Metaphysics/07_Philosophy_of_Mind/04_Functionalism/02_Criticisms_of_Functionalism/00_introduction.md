# Criticisms of Functionalism

Functionalism's elegant solution to the multiple realizability problem came with a cost: it seemed to leave out what matters most about the mind. A system can have all the right functional relations among its internal states — the right input-output profile, the right causal interactions — while lacking any phenomenal experience. This is the intuition behind Ned Block's "absent qualia" objection: a functional duplicate of a conscious being might have no phenomenal consciousness whatsoever, while being functionally identical. If this is possible, then phenomenal consciousness is not captured by functional organization.

Searle's Chinese Room argument is the most famous objection to functionalism. Imagine a person in a room who speaks no Chinese but follows a rule book for manipulating Chinese symbols: given input symbols (questions in Chinese), she produces output symbols (answers in Chinese) according to the rules. From outside the room, her outputs are indistinguishable from those of a fluent Chinese speaker; functionally, she passes the test. But she does not understand Chinese — she is just manipulating symbols according to syntactic rules, with no semantic content. Searle's conclusion: syntax (computational or functional organization) is not sufficient for semantics (understanding, meaning) — and by extension, for consciousness.

Block's "inverted qualia" objection poses a related challenge: it seems possible that two people have functionally identical mental states — the same functional role in their cognitive system — while experiencing phenomenally different things. When I see red and you see red, we both respond in the same way to red things, have the same beliefs about red things, and use "red" in the same way. But perhaps what I experience phenomenally when seeing red is what you would call green, and vice versa. Functional organization does not determine phenomenal character.

These objections have spawned both defenses of functionalism and development of alternative positions. This section examines each objection carefully, assessing the functionalist's resources for responding.
