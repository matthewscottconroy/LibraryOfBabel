# Inverted Qualia

Consider two people — call them Normal and Invert — with functionally identical visual systems. Normal calls traffic lights red and experiences, phenomenally, what we ordinarily call red experience. Invert also calls traffic lights red and behaves in exactly the same ways Normal does. But the phenomenal character of Invert's experience when looking at a red traffic light is, intuitively, what Normal experiences when looking at something green. Their functional profiles are identical — the same states are caused by the same inputs, produce the same outputs, and interact with the same other states in the same ways. But their phenomenal experiences are systematically inverted.

If this scenario is coherent, phenomenal character is not determined by functional organization. Invert and Normal share all functional properties but differ in phenomenal ones. Therefore, functional properties are insufficient for phenomenal consciousness, and functionalism about phenomenal consciousness is false.

The coherence question is the critical issue. Color experience has a rich structure: red is more similar to orange than to blue; complementary colors are maximally contrasting; yellow is warm, blue is cool; colors occupy determinate positions in a multidimensional phenomenal space. If this structure is itself a feature of phenomenal experience, then a simple inversion might not preserve all these structural relations. If red experience feels warm and green experience feels cool, then Invert's experience of red-wavelength light — which produces green-like experience — would feel cool. This might be subtly detectable in unusual circumstances.

## Shoemaker's Functionalist Response and Block's Rejoinder

Sydney Shoemaker argued that a consistently developed inverted spectrum — one that preserves all structural relations among color experiences — would require the inverted qualities to have systematically different functional roles. If the warmth of red, the coolness of blue, the similarity relations among neighboring colors are all functional features, then Invert's inverted colors would play different functional roles from Normal's. The inversion is functionalism-refuting only if we assume in advance that phenomenal character is non-functional — which begs the question.

Block's rejoinder is direct: Shoemaker's argument assumes that phenomenal structure is exhausted by functional structure. But there may be a "phenomenal structure" that is not captured by any functional description — the specific feel of red as distinct from its structural relations to other colors. If phenomenal structure exceeds functional structure, the consistent inversion might preserve all functional relations while inverting something over and above those relations. This is not an argument against Shoemaker so much as an identification of where the real disagreement lies: whether phenomenal character involves intrinsic properties beyond any functional characterization.

Notice that inverted qualia do not, by themselves, refute physicalism. Even if phenomenal character is not determined by functional organization, it might be determined by physical neural organization. Physical realization might be more specific than functional realization: different physical states can play the same functional role while having different phenomenal characters. Normal and Invert could have physically different visual systems that produce functionally identical but phenomenally different states. Chalmers responds that inverted qualia are conceivable even for physically identical systems in different possible worlds — phenomenal spectrum inversions. If this is coherent, then physical organization is also insufficient to determine phenomenal character, and some form of dualism or panpsychism is required. Whether phenomenal spectrum inversions are genuinely conceivable in this stronger sense, or whether conceivability here tracks metaphysical possibility, is precisely where the debate with type-B physicalists is joined.
