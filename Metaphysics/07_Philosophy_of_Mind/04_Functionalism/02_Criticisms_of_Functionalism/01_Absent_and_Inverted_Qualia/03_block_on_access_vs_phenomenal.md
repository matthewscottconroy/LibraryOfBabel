# Block on Access and Phenomenal Consciousness

The absent and inverted qualia objections press against functionalism, but they work only if there is a real distinction between what functionalism can explain and what it cannot. Ned Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, developed in "On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness" (1995), makes this distinction precise. It is one of the most influential conceptual contributions to philosophy of mind in the past thirty years, and understanding it is essential to understanding what the debate between functionalists and their critics is actually about.

A mental state is access-conscious (A-conscious) if its content is poised for direct use in reasoning, rational action control, and verbal report. Block's more precise definition: a representation is A-conscious if it is inferentially promiscuous — available for use in inference — directly available for rational control of action, and directly available for verbal report. Access consciousness is a functional notion, defined entirely by the state's causal-functional role in the cognitive economy. Any system with the appropriate causal organization can have A-conscious states. A state is A-conscious in virtue of what it does: it makes its content globally available.

A mental state is phenomenally conscious (P-conscious) if there is something it is like to be in that state — if it has qualitative, subjective character. P-consciousness is what Nagel identified in "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974): the intrinsic felt quality of experience. Block argues that P-consciousness is not a functional concept and cannot be fully characterized in functional terms. The hurtfulness of pain is not identical to any functional property, even if it is causally correlated with many.

## Independence and Theoretical Significance

Block argues that the two kinds of consciousness can come apart in both directions. A-consciousness without P-consciousness: the China Brain has the functional organization for access-conscious states — information globally available, poised for reasoning and report — but presumably has no phenomenal consciousness, no qualitative "what it is like." Blindsight patients respond to visual stimuli in their blind field without conscious visual experience; some information may be available for behavioral control — A-conscious — while lacking phenomenal character. P-consciousness without A-consciousness: Block suggests there may be phenomenally conscious states not poised for reasoning or verbal report — the feel of background music, the qualitative texture of peripheral visual experience outside the spotlight of attention.

The theoretical significance is sharp. Functionalism can account for A-consciousness — it is defined in functional terms. The absent qualia challenge is specifically a challenge for P-consciousness. If phenomenal consciousness requires more than access consciousness, functionalism is at best a theory of A-consciousness, not of consciousness in the philosophically deepest sense. Global Workspace Theory and Higher Order Theories explain why certain states are available for reasoning and report — they explain A-consciousness. They do not explain why those states feel like anything. The hard problem of consciousness is specifically a problem about P-consciousness, not A-consciousness.

Three main criticisms press against the distinction itself. First: the distinction is unclear. The characterization of P-consciousness as "something it is like" is itself philosophically loaded and, Dennett argues, any coherent description of P-consciousness collapses into a description of A-consciousness — phenomenal properties just are functional properties described from a different angle. Second: P-consciousness as Block characterizes it may be a theoretical posit with no real correlate. Illusionists like Keith Frankish argue that what we call "phenomenal character" is a cognitive illusion — a representation of having properties that are not actually present. Third: the putative cases of dissociation are controversial. Whether the China Brain genuinely has A-conscious states without P-consciousness, rather than simply having complex functional organization without either kind of consciousness, is not obvious. Despite these objections, the distinction has been enormously productive. It forces clarity about what exactly is being explained, and it makes visible the gap between the easy and hard problems of consciousness.
