# Liberalism and Chauvinism in Functionalism

Ned Block's "Troubles with Functionalism" (1978) identified a dilemma that has structured the debate about functionalism ever since. Any functionalist account must specify the level of abstraction at which functional organization is described. The more abstract the description, the more systems satisfy it, and the more liberal the theory. The more specific the description, the fewer systems satisfy it, and the more chauvinistic. Neither horn is acceptable.

The liberal horn: if functional organization is described abstractly enough, systems that clearly lack minds — thermostats, economies, the population of China coordinated by satellite to simulate a brain — may satisfy the functional description and be attributed mental states. The China Brain thought experiment makes this vivid: every person in China simultaneously simulates one neuron, with satellite networks simulating synaptic connections. The system has exactly the causal organization of a human brain. If functionalism is correct, the China Brain should be conscious. But it seems not to be: the population of China is not unified into a single experiential subject. There is nothing it is like to be the China Brain.

The chauvinism horn: if functional organization is described specifically — tied to human neurological organization — it excludes beings that clearly have minds but are constituted differently. A Martian processing information through liquid-crystal networks rather than neurons, but performing all the same cognitive tasks, would be denied mental states on a chauvinistic functional specification. This denies minds to beings that clearly have them, and it defeats the original purpose of functionalism, which was to overcome exactly this kind of biological chauvinism in type identity theory.

Block's diagnosis is that both horns are generated by the same feature of functionalism: trying to define mental states by functional organization alone, without any independent constraint on what counts as the right kind of functional organization. There is no non-question-begging way to specify the relevant level of functional description without already having an account of what minds are. The specification either admits too many systems or too few, because functional description per se does not track the property that distinguishes minded from non-minded systems.

## Responses and What Rides on Them

Several responses have been developed, none fully satisfying. Refined functionalism adds constraints on the type of functional organization — requiring it to be implemented in a causally connected, spatially unified system, in real time, with appropriate dynamics. This rules out the China Brain without ruling out alien minds. But the additional constraints are difficult to specify without circularity: "genuine" causal connection, "appropriate" dynamics, and "real" temporal scale all risk smuggling in substrate-specific requirements. Biological naturalism accepts the chauvinism: consciousness requires biological implementation, which rules out the China Brain and the Martian alike. Most philosophers find the denial of Martian minds deeply implausible.

Block's own response is to concede the domain: functionalism gives a correct account of access consciousness but not phenomenal consciousness. The liberalism/chauvinism dilemma applies specifically to phenomenal consciousness, and functionalism should be understood as a theory of the functional dimension of mind rather than of mind in its entirety. This response is honest about the limits of functionalism but at the cost of acknowledging that the most philosophically significant dimension of mind — phenomenal experience — falls outside the theory's reach.

The practical stakes are not trivial. The liberalism problem matters for moral philosophy: the right theory of mind bears on which systems merit moral consideration. If functionalism attributes consciousness to the China Brain, disrupting its coordination seems potentially morally problematic — which is absurd. If functionalism is used to deny minds to AI systems that genuinely have them, it underwrites unjustified moral disregard. Getting the level of description right is not just an academic exercise in specification; it has real consequences for how we treat the systems we build and interact with.
