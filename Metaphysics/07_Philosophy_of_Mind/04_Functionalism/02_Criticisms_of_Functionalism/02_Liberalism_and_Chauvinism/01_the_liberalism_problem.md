# The Liberalism Problem

The liberalism problem for functionalism is structural, not a matter of insufficient specification. Any formulation of functionalism that is broad enough to accommodate the multiple realizability that motivates the theory — that allows octopi, Martians, and silicon systems to have minds — will also be broad enough to admit systems that clearly lack minds. The question is not whether a better specification could solve this problem; it is whether the problem reflects something that functional description alone cannot capture.

The thermostat is the simplest case. It has an internal state (the temperature reading) caused by environmental inputs (ambient temperature) and producing outputs (activating heating or cooling) that affect the environment and the state. If functional role is specified loosely enough, the thermostat satisfies a simplified functional specification for "belief that the temperature is above threshold." But thermostats do not have beliefs. The problem recurs at higher levels of complexity: economies have states caused by inputs and producing outputs, with "preferences" inferable from behavior. Does a nation's economy have beliefs and desires? On a sufficiently abstract functionalist specification, it might qualify.

Block's China Brain thought experiment illustrates the problem at the scale of full cognitive organization. If every person in China simultaneously simulates one neuron, with satellite networks simulating synaptic connections, the system has exactly the causal organization of a human brain. If functionalism is correct, it should be conscious — it implements the same functional organization as a conscious brain. But the China Brain seems not to be conscious: the population of China is not unified into a single experiential subject. There is nothing it is like to be the China Brain. The people involved do not pool their experiences into a single meta-experience.

## Causal Connectivity and Why It Does Not Fully Solve the Problem

The most promising constraint is to require that the functional organization be implemented in a causally connected, spatially unified, real-time system. The China Brain fails this constraint because its components are too spatially dispersed and the satellite connections are too slow to count as genuine neural-analog causal connections. This response has initial plausibility — brains are spatially unified in a way that matters for their causal dynamics.

But the response faces several difficulties. First, what makes a causal connection "genuine"? Speed-of-light satellite connections seem causally real. The response must specify why the speed of causal transmission matters for mentality, and the natural answer — that it must match the timescales of neural processing — risks being a covert appeal to biological substrate. Second, if spatial unity and temporal dynamics are the relevant constraints, functionalism starts to resemble a position that privileges particular physical implementations over others, which is exactly the chauvinism the theory was designed to avoid. Third, and most importantly, the liberalism problem and multiple realizability pull in opposite directions: the key argument for functionalism over type identity requires that the same mental states be realizable by many different physical systems. But the more systems can realize a mental state, the more liberal the theory. The liberalism problem is, in effect, the question of whether multiple realizability has been pushed too far — and any response that limits the range of realizers must explain why those limits are principled rather than arbitrary.
