# The Chauvinism Problem

The chauvinism problem is the mirror image of the liberalism problem, and together they constitute Block's dilemma. If the functional specification is drawn too narrowly — if it requires that mental states be implemented in human-like neural architecture — it will deny minds to beings that genuinely have them: Martians with different cognitive architecture, octopi with radically different nervous systems, future AI systems implemented in silicon. This is embarrassing for functionalism in particular, because avoiding biological chauvinism was one of the theory's main selling points. Type identity theory was chauvinistic: only beings with C-fibers can be in pain. Functionalism was supposed to improve on this by identifying mental states with functional roles rather than specific physical realizers. If the functional specification must be drawn narrowly enough to avoid the liberalism problem, it reintroduces a different chauvinism — computational or organizational rather than biological.

Lewis's "Mad Pain and Martian Pain" illustrates the worry concretely. The Martian feels genuine pain — with qualitative character, influencing behavior in the way pain does — but its pain is realized by pressurized fluid systems in tubes rather than neurons. If functionalism requires that the pain-role be implemented in the same way as human pain, the Martian's pain fails to qualify. We should be willing to attribute pain to any system that genuinely suffers, regardless of physical substrate. Lewis's response was to distinguish the general functional role (what makes a state pain across all species) from the specific realizer (which physical state plays that role in a given species). But this depends on being able to specify the general functional role without building in neurological specificity — which is precisely what is in question.

## Searle's Embrace of Chauvinism

Searle's biological naturalism is explicitly chauvinistic: consciousness requires the specific causal powers of biological neurons, and silicon systems cannot be conscious regardless of their functional organization. Searle embraces this, arguing it is well-motivated rather than arbitrary. The reason brains produce consciousness is that they have specific biological properties that silicon lacks, and those properties are the relevant ones. The question is whether this claim has any content beyond an assertion of faith in biological substrate.

The objection is that biological naturalism is an empirical claim — the claim that biological neurons have unique consciousness-producing causal powers — but Searle provides no characterization of what those powers are that would make the claim testable. If a silicon system implementing the same functional organization as a brain has all the behavioral and reportorial signatures of consciousness, what would Searle count as evidence that it is not conscious? The invocation of "specific biological causal powers" risks being unfalsifiable: any behavioral evidence for silicon consciousness can be dismissed by appealing to the substrate. At that point, the position is not an empirical hypothesis but a conceptual commitment dressed in empirical language.

The liberalism and chauvinism problems jointly reveal that the crucial question functionalism must answer is: what is the principled basis for the relevant level of functional description? Any answer to this question either reintroduces substrate dependence or leaves the level of description arbitrary. This is the pressure that has driven the development of neo-functionalist positions — teleofunctionalism, two-dimensional approaches, realizer functionalism — each attempting to add the principled basis that simple causal role functionalism lacks.
