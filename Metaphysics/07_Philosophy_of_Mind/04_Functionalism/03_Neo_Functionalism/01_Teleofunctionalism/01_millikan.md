# Millikan on Proper Functions and Mental Content

Ruth Millikan's teleological theory of content, most fully developed in *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories* (1984), is the most systematic attempt to naturalize intentional content by grounding it in biological function. The central move is to shift from asking what causes a mental representation on any given occasion to asking what it was selected to track over evolutionary time. These are different questions, and their answers can come apart — which is precisely what makes the teleological account do genuine philosophical work.

Millikan distinguishes direct proper functions from derived proper functions. A direct proper function is the function a trait has in virtue of its evolutionary history: the heart's direct proper function is to pump blood, because hearts that pumped blood were selected for in ancestral organisms. The proper function is not what a token does on any given occasion but what it was selected for — what it must do to reproduce tokens of its own type. A derived proper function applies to tokens in virtue of the proper functions of the system that produces them: a particular pheromone signal has the derived proper function of attracting mates because the signaling system that produces it was selected for its mate-attracting effects. Mental representations have derived proper functions: they are produced by neural systems selected for their ability to track features of the environment. The concept DOG has the derived proper function of representing dogs because the neural systems that produce dog-representations were selected for their role in tracking and responding to dogs.

The key philosophical payoff is a naturalistic account of misrepresentation. Purely causal theories face the disjunction problem: if the concept 'horse' is caused by horses and also by mules-mistaken-for-horses, the content is (horse or mule-mistaken-for-horse), not horse. Millikan solves this by distinguishing what a representation has the proper function of tracking (horses) from what occasionally causes it (mules): the proper function picks out the content, not every actual causal trigger. A representation misfires — represents X as Y — when it is caused by something other than what it has the proper function of tracking. The frog's fly-detector represents flies; when it fires in response to a BB pellet, the frog misrepresents the BB as a fly.

## The Indeterminacy Problem and Biological Externalism

The indeterminacy problem presses Millikan's account at its most foundational point. The proper function of the frog's detector might be to track small dark moving things, or to track nutritive things, or to track flies specifically. Multiple descriptions of the selection process are equally supported by the evolutionary data. Millikan has developed criteria for selecting the most proximate proper function — the most specific description of the function subject to selection — but critics argue that the indeterminacy is not resolved. There are many competing descriptions of what was selected for, and no principled way to choose among them that does not beg the question in favor of one content attribution over another.

Millikan's theory is strongly externalist: content is determined by evolutionary history, not by the current causal situation or the individual's own history. This has a striking implication for thought experiments: a brain-in-a-vat whose neural states are caused by a computer rather than by the world they nominally represent does not have the same intentional content as an ordinary person, because the brain's neural states lack the appropriate evolutionary history. Their causal origin is wrong in the evolutionary sense. The proper function of the relevant systems was selected in environments where those states tracked real-world properties; the vat environment disrupts this history. Whether this consequence is acceptable or whether it reveals a problem with the evolutionary grounding is a matter of ongoing dispute. What is not in dispute is that Millikan's work provides the most rigorous attempt to answer the question of how representational content is possible in a purely natural world.
