# Teleofunctionalism

Pure causal role functionalism faces what is sometimes called the disjunction problem. If a frog's visual system has a state S that fires in response to flies, and also fires — by misfire — in response to small dark pellets, what does S represent? The purely causal account seems forced to say it represents (fly or small dark pellet). But the frog's detector is a fly-detector, not a (fly or small dark pellet)-detector. Something is going wrong in the purely causal analysis. Teleofunctionalism responds that what determines the content of a mental state is not merely what causes it on any given occasion but what it was selected, in the evolutionary sense, to track. S was selected because of its fly-tracking history — past instances of S contributed to fitness by reliably responding to flies. So S represents flies, and the pellet-caused firings are misrepresentations. Content is grounded in biological function, not in current causal relations alone.

This is the core idea of teleofunctionalism, developed most systematically by Ruth Millikan in *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories* (1984) and in Dretske's *Explaining Behavior* (1988). The supplement to standard causal role functionalism is a historical, evolutionary dimension: a mental state represents X because it was selected to track X, because past instances contributed to fitness by reliably responding to X. Natural selection has shaped cognitive systems to have states that function to detect, track, and respond to features of the environment. Intentional content is a kind of biological norm: a state should track X because tracking X is what it was selected to do, and this norm holds even when the state misfires.

The advantages over pure causal functionalism are real. Misrepresentation becomes explicable: a state misfires when it is caused by something other than what it has the proper function of tracking. Normativity is grounded: there is a fact about what a state should track, independent of what actually triggers it on any occasion. And the disjunction problem is addressed: evolutionary history selects for specific content, not disjunctions of every actual cause.

## Millikan's Proper Functions and the Indeterminacy Problem

Millikan distinguishes direct proper functions — the function a trait has in virtue of evolutionary history, as the heart's proper function is to pump blood — from derived proper functions, which tokens inherit from the proper functions of the systems that produce them. Mental representations have derived proper functions: they were produced by neural systems selected for their ability to track features of the environment. The concept DOG has the derived proper function of representing dogs because the neural systems that produce dog-representations were selected for their ability to track and respond to dogs.

The indeterminacy problem presses: which description of the evolutionary selection is correct? The frog's detector might have the proper function of tracking small dark moving things, or nutritive things, or flies specifically. Multiple descriptions are equally supported by the evolutionary data. Millikan has developed criteria for selecting the most proximate proper function, but critics argue that the indeterminacy is not resolved and that evolutionary history underdetermines content in ways that undermine the theory's precision.

Dretske's individual-history approach addresses a related problem. Many human concepts — PRIME NUMBER, QUANTUM FIELD, JUSTICE — have no plausible evolutionary history. They do not track environmental features relevant to fitness in ancestral environments. Evolutionary teleosemantics seems unable to account for their content. Dretske supplements evolutionary function with acquired function: a state represents X if it was recruited, through individual learning history, to serve as an indicator of X. This extends the teleological account to newly acquired concepts at the cost of expanding what counts as "biological function" to include learned functions — which may or may not be teleological in any strict sense.

John McDowell presses the deepest objection: intentional normativity cannot be reduced to biological function because conceptual norms require rational responsiveness to reasons, not just natural-historical selection. A creature whose states track flies by biological function is not thereby responding to reasons; it is behaving in a way that was selected for. Genuine intentionality, on McDowell's view, requires sensitivity to the logical relations among concepts — the kind of responsiveness that only creatures embedded in a shared practice of reason-giving can have. Whether teleofunctionalism can accommodate this, or whether it gives at best an account of proto-intentionality in creatures lacking genuine conceptual capacities, is among the deepest questions in the theory of intentional content.
