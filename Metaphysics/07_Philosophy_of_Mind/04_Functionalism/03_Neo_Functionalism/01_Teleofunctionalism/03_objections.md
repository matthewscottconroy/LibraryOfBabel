# Objections to Teleological Theories of Content

Teleological theories ground intentional content in biological function. The objections they face reveal a deep tension between two desiderata that are both necessary for a theory of content: naturalistic respectability — content must be explicable in terms of natural, non-intentional facts — and semantic richness — content must be fine-grained, determinate, and normative enough to explain inference, misrepresentation, and the full range of intentional phenomena. Teleofunctionalism represents the most developed attempt to satisfy both, and the debate about its objections is, in effect, a debate about whether a fully naturalistic theory of intentional content is possible at all.

The indeterminacy problem is the most pressing. Given an organism's evolutionary history, multiple descriptions of a state's proper function are equally supported by the data. The frog's visual detector was selected because it tracked small dark moving things, or because it tracked flies, or because it tracked food sources, or because it triggered the snap reflex in the presence of nutritive objects. Without a principled way to select one description as the correct proper function, the content of the state is indeterminate — which undermines the theory's claim to explain determinate representational content. Millikan has developed criteria for selecting the "most proximate proper function" — the most specific description that was subject to natural selection — and Karen Neander has developed related criteria focusing on the specific mechanisms and discriminative variations that were selected. Whether these criteria resolve the indeterminacy or merely relocate it is contested.

The new function problem cuts from a different direction. If a state that was selected for function F is repurposed in an organism's lifetime to perform a new function G, does it now have the content G? Purely evolutionary teleosemantics says no — the content is still F, determined by evolutionary history. But this seems wrong if the state now reliably and consistently tracks G and is used for G-relevant purposes in the organism's cognitive life. Dretske's individual-history approach addresses this by incorporating learning history: content is determined by what a state was recruited to indicate during development. This extends the teleological approach beyond evolutionary history but at the cost of complicating the clean evolutionary grounding.

## Abstract Concepts and the Normativity Objection

Many important human concepts have no plausible evolutionary history. PRIME NUMBER, SHAKESPEARE, QUANTUM FIELD, JUSTICE — none tracks an environmental feature relevant to human fitness in ancestral environments. Teleofunctionalists have proposed that abstract concepts are constructed from more primitive tracking capacities through abstraction, metaphor, and scaffolded cultural learning: JUSTICE from evolved capacities for social reciprocity detection, PRIME NUMBER from basic numerical tracking capacities extended through mathematical practice. Whether this fully accounts for the content of abstract concepts without circularity — without appealing to intentional processes to build new intentional content — remains an open question.

John McDowell's normativity objection is perhaps the deepest challenge. Content has normative implications: there is a fact about what a representation should represent, even when it misfires. McDowell argues that this normativity cannot be reduced to natural facts about evolutionary history because conceptual norms require rational agency — the capacity to respond to reasons as reasons — which is not captured by any biological selection story. A creature whose states track flies by evolutionary function is not thereby responding to reasons; it is behaving in a way that was selected for. The natural-biological and rational-normative domains are, on McDowell's view, categorically distinct, and teleosemantics conflates them.

Defenders of teleosemantics respond that biological normativity — the sense in which an organism's organ can function correctly or malfunction — is a genuine form of normativity, not merely a metaphor. The debate about whether biological norms ground rational norms, or whether these are categorically different, connects teleosemantics to broader disputes in metaethics and the philosophy of norms. Whether a fully naturalistic theory of intentional content is achievable depends on how one answers this question.
