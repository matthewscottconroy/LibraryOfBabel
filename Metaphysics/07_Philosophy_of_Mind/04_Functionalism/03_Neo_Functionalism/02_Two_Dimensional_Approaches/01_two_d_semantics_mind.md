# Two-Dimensional Semantics and the Mind

The paradigm case for two-dimensional analysis is "Water is H₂O." The statement appears contingent — we can imagine a world where the watery stuff is XYZ, not H₂O — but is, according to Kripke, metaphysically necessary. Two-dimensionalism explains this by distinguishing two ways the statement can be evaluated. Primary intension of "water": "the watery stuff in the actual world" — whatever plays the functional role associated with water (transparent, drinkable, falls as rain, etc.). Secondary intension of "water": H₂O — what the primary intension actually picks out, now rigidly designated across all possible worlds. When we imagine a world where the watery stuff is XYZ, we are evaluating the primary intension in a different context. The secondary intension of "water" (in our language, fixed by the actual world) still picks out H₂O in every possible world. The apparent contingency arises from the primary intension; the actual identity is necessary in the secondary sense.

Applying this to "pain is C-fiber firing":

- Primary intension of "pain": whatever plays the pain-functional-role in the actual world — the state caused by tissue damage, causing distress and avoidance behavior, interacting with other states in characteristic ways.
- Secondary intension of "pain": C-fiber firing (assuming that is what plays the pain-role in the actual world), rigidly designated.

On this analysis, "pain is C-fiber firing" is necessary in the secondary sense. The conceivability of zombies reflects a primary-intension possibility: a world where something different plays the pain-functional-role, or nothing does. This does not show that C-fiber firing could exist without pain — because in the secondary sense, "pain" just is C-fiber firing. The apparent contingency of "pain is C-fiber firing" is explained without accepting a genuine metaphysical contingency.

## The Phenomenal Concepts Problem

The materialist use of two-dimensional semantics requires that phenomenal concepts have functional primary intensions. If "pain" picks out its referent via the functional role description, the zombie conceivability is only primary — a conceivability that reflects the context-dependence of the primary intension, not any genuine metaphysical possibility. The hard problem becomes an artifact of our conceptual apparatus.

Chalmers denies that phenomenal concepts have functional primary intensions. Phenomenal concepts, he argues, are acquaintive rather than descriptive: when I think "this pain," I pick out the phenomenal property directly by acquaintance, not via any functional description. The primary and secondary intensions of phenomenal concepts coincide — both directly designate the phenomenal property. This means there is no functional primary intension that could explain away the conceivability of zombies: the conceivability is genuine and metaphysically robust. The two-dimensional framework, used this way, becomes evidence against physicalism rather than a tool for defending it.

Robert Stalnaker's diagonal proposition framework offers a more conservative alternative. For Stalnaker, the two-dimensional structure tracks features of communication and context rather than fundamental metaphysical structure. The explanatory gap reflects our epistemic situation — phenomenal concepts are contextually fixed in ways that make them appear to refer beyond the physical — but this is an artifact of how concepts work in context, not evidence of any property that is non-physical. Stalnaker's framework is less committal than Chalmers's; it promises to explain the appearance of the gap without either the materialist's a priori entailment program or the anti-materialist's strong emergence. Whether it succeeds depends on whether an epistemic diagnosis of the gap is genuinely satisfying or whether it leaves the most important question — why is there experience at all — untouched.
