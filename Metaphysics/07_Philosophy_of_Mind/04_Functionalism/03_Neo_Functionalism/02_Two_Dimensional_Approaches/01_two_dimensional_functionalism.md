# Two-Dimensional Approaches to Mental Content

The explanatory gap between physical and phenomenal descriptions seems to show that no physical description entails any phenomenal description. Two-dimensional semantics, developed by David Chalmers, Frank Jackson, and Robert Stalnaker, provides a framework for analyzing whether this appearance of a gap reflects a genuine metaphysical discontinuity or a feature of how our concepts work. The framework is a precision instrument — and it is used to cut in opposite directions by different philosophers.

Every concept or expression has two dimensions of semantic evaluation. The primary intension (A-intension) fixes what the term picks out as a function of the world considered as actual — it gives the descriptive meaning, the role the term plays in determining reference. The secondary intension (B-intension) fixes what the term picks out in worlds considered as counterfactual — once reference is fixed by the actual world, the term designates rigidly across possible worlds. For "water," the primary intension is roughly "the watery stuff" — whatever fills the functional role of water in the actual world. The secondary intension is H₂O — what the primary intension actually picks out, now designated rigidly. "Water is H₂O" appears contingent (we can imagine the watery stuff being XYZ) but is necessary in the secondary sense: in all possible worlds, "water" designates H₂O, the stuff the primary intension picked out in the actual world.

Applied to pain: the primary intension of "pain" picks out whatever plays the pain-functional-role in the actual world; the secondary intension rigidly designates whatever physical state that turns out to be. The conceivability of zombies — beings physically identical to us but without pain — is explained, on the materialist use, by the primary intension: we conceive of a world where something plays the pain-functional-role differently, not a world where C-fiber firing lacks its secondary intension. The apparent explanatory gap between physical and phenomenal descriptions reflects the divergence between functional (primary) and physical (secondary) intensions, not a genuine metaphysical gap.

## The Anti-Materialist Use and the Phenomenal Concepts Dispute

Frank Jackson's "conceptual analysis and physicalism" program uses two-dimensional semantics to defend physicalism: if phenomenal concepts have functional primary intensions, all phenomenal truths follow a priori from physical truths plus knowledge of which physical facts hold in the actual world. This is the a priori entailment strategy.

Chalmers uses the same framework to argue against materialism. His key move: phenomenal concepts do not have functional primary intensions. When I think "this very pain," I pick out the pain by direct acquaintance, not via any functional description. The primary and secondary intensions of phenomenal concepts coincide — they both directly designate the phenomenal property. This means there is no divergence between functional and physical intensions that could explain the explanatory gap; the gap reflects a genuine metaphysical difference. If this is right, zombies are both primarily and secondarily conceivable, the zombie argument goes through in full force, and physicalism is false. The two-dimensional framework becomes a tool for diagnosing physicalism's failure rather than its defense.

The debate about whether phenomenal concepts have functional primary intensions is thus the crux of whether two-dimensional semantics serves the materialist or anti-materialist purpose. Stalnaker's more conservative framework argues that the explanatory gap reflects an epistemic feature of our situation — our phenomenal concepts are contextually determined in ways that make them appear to refer beyond the physical — rather than a genuine metaphysical gap. All parties agree that the framework clarifies what is at stake; they disagree about which side the evidence supports.
