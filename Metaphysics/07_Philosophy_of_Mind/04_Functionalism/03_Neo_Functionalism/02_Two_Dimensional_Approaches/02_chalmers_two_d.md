# Chalmers and Two-Dimensional Consciousness

Physicalists have argued that the explanatory gap between physical and phenomenal descriptions is analogous to the apparent gap between "H₂O" and "water" — both are a posteriori identity statements that seem contingent but are metaphysically necessary. Two-dimensional semantics explains the water case: the primary intension of "water" is a functional description ("the watery stuff"), so the apparent contingency reflects variation in the primary intension across worlds, not genuine metaphysical contingency. "Water is H₂O" is necessary in the secondary sense even though it appears contingent when we consider the primary intension. Chalmers accepts this two-dimensional framework but argues that the phenomenal case is fundamentally different.

The difference: the primary intension of "water" is functional — it picks out its referent via a description, not directly. But the primary intension of "pain," Chalmers argues, is not functional. Pain is picked out by its felt character, by direct phenomenal acquaintance, not by any description of its causal role. There is no functional intermediary between the phenomenal concept and its referent. Therefore, the apparent contingency of "pain = C-fiber firing" is not explained by a divergence between primary and secondary intensions — it reflects genuine metaphysical contingency. The reference of "pain" is fixed directly by the phenomenal property, which could exist without C-fiber firing.

Chalmers's full argument:

- P1: Phenomenal concepts pick out their referents by direct acquaintance — the primary and secondary intensions of phenomenal concepts coincide.
- P2: If P1, then zombies are metaphysically possible, not merely primarily conceivable — there is no two-dimensional divergence that blocks the inference from conceivability to possibility.
- P3: If zombies are metaphysically possible, phenomenal properties are not identical to physical properties.
- C: Physicalism about phenomenal consciousness is false.

The key step is P1 — the claim that phenomenal concepts are pure acquaintance concepts. This distinguishes the phenomenal case from the water/H₂O case and is what allows the zombie argument to go through in full force.

## Physicalist Responses and What Rides on Them

The functional primary intension response holds that phenomenal concepts do have functional primary intensions — they refer to whatever plays the phenomenal role in the actual world. If so, zombies are not even primarily conceivable, because we cannot coherently imagine beings with the right functional organization that lack phenomenal consciousness. This is the type-A physicalist strategy, associated with Dennett and others who deny that zombies are genuinely coherently conceivable.

The type-B physicalist response — associated with Loar, Papineau, Levine — accepts that phenomenal concepts are recognitional (acquaintance-based) rather than functional but denies that recognitional reference establishes non-physical properties. Even if primary and secondary intensions coincide for phenomenal concepts, this does not establish that the referent is non-physical. The gap between phenomenal and physical concepts is a conceptual gap reflecting different modes of presentation, not a metaphysical gap reflecting different properties. Chalmers's response to type-B physicalism: this strategy is coherent but unmotivated — it has no independent support beyond the desire to defend physicalism. The type-B position accepts the full force of the conceivability argument — that zombies are coherently conceivable — and then asserts without additional argument that this conceivability fails to track any metaphysical gap. Whether this assertion is well-supported, or whether it simply restates the physicalist commitment without defending it, is the most important question in contemporary philosophy of consciousness.
