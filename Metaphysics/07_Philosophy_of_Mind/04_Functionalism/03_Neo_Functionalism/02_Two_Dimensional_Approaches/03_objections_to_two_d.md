# Objections to Two-Dimensional Approaches to Consciousness

Two-dimensional semantics has been applied by physicalists (Jackson, Stalnaker) to close the explanatory gap and by anti-physicalists (Chalmers) to demonstrate that the gap is real. Both uses have attracted serious objections. The deepest disagreement concerns whether phenomenal concepts are more like "water" — with a functional primary intension — or more like proper names — referring directly without a descriptive intermediary. Different assumptions here generate very different conclusions about what two-dimensionalism shows.

Against Chalmers's anti-materialist use: physicalists argue that phenomenal concepts do have functional primary intensions, so the zombie conceivability is only primary — a conceivability that reflects variation in the functional description, not genuine metaphysical possibility in the secondary sense. If "pain" picks out "whatever plays the pain-functional-role in the actual world," then a world where the pain-functional-role is absent is a world where "pain" has a different primary extension, and this does not show that C-fiber firing could fail to be pain in the secondary sense. This is the type-A response. Type-B physicalism — Loar, Papineau — accepts that phenomenal concepts are recognitional rather than functional but denies that this establishes metaphysical irreducibility. Recognitional concepts can refer to physical properties via a distinctive mode of presentation, and the conceptual gap between phenomenal and physical descriptions reflects this difference in modes of presentation, not any difference in the properties themselves.

Against Jackson's physicalist use: Jackson argues that all concepts have descriptive primary intensions, and that this secures the primary conceivability of zombies without establishing metaphysical possibility. Critics argue this requires descriptivism about all concepts, which faces Kripkean objections. Proper names and natural kind terms do not seem to have purely descriptive primary intensions — "Aristotle" is not equivalent to any description of Aristotle's properties, and substituting a misdescription for the name fails to preserve reference in every context. If descriptivism fails for ordinary names, it may fail for phenomenal terms too.

## Loar's Third Category and the Core Dispute

Brian Loar argues that phenomenal concepts resist two-dimensional analysis because they occupy a third category: neither descriptive (primary intension picks out via functional description) nor purely directly referential (secondary intension coincides with primary). Phenomenal concepts are recognitional concepts that refer to physical properties via a distinctive mode of presentation — direct acquaintance. The explanatory gap arises because recognitional and physical concepts present the same property under irreducibly different modes of presentation. Two-dimensionalism fails to capture this because it assumes all concepts fall into either the descriptive or the directly referential camp.

The deepest objection to the whole two-dimensional program applied to consciousness is that the framework clarifies the structure of the debate without resolving it. Both physicalists and anti-physicalists can coherently use the framework, depending on their assumptions about phenomenal concepts. The real philosophical action lies in those assumptions — whether phenomenal concepts are functional or acquaintive, whether conceptual gaps track metaphysical gaps, whether the conceivability-possibility inference holds for ideal reflection. The two-dimensional framework makes these assumptions precise and testable, which is a genuine contribution. But it does not provide independent grounds for choosing among the positions. The debate about consciousness must ultimately be resolved not by semantic analysis alone but by a combination of semantic analysis, careful phenomenology, and a theory of what phenomenal concepts refer to and why.
