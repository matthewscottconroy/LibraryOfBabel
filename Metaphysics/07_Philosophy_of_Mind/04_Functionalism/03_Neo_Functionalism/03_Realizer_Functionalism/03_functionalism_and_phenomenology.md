# Functionalism and Phenomenology

Consider what it is like to be in pain. There is the hurt — not the tissue damage, not the avoidance behavior, not the distress signal propagating through neural circuits — but the felt quality of hurting. A complete functional characterization of pain specifies all the causal relations: inputs (tissue damage signals), internal transitions (amplification, spread to emotional centers), outputs (withdrawal behavior, avoidance learning, verbal report). Even the most complete specification of these causal relations does not tell us why the state has a qualitative character at all. Why is there a hurt rather than a mere information-processing response to damage? This is Joseph Levine's explanatory gap: the gap is asymmetric. We can explain in functional terms why physical damage produces avoidance behavior, distress, reports of pain. We cannot explain in functional terms why it produces the experience of hurt. The phenomenal character remains unaccounted for by any functional description.

The most influential functionalist response to this challenge is representationalism (also called intentionalism): phenomenal character is identical to representational content. What it is like to see red just is representing red in the appropriate way. Phenomenal properties are a species of intentional properties. Tye's PANIC theory (*Ten Problems of Consciousness*, 1995) holds that phenomenal states are Poised Abstract Non-Conceptual Intentional Contents — representational states with specific functional characteristics that account for their phenomenal properties. The experience of red represents redness; the experience of pain represents bodily damage. This is a functionalist move because representational content is itself functionally characterized: the redness-representing state is the state that responds to red surfaces and causes red-discriminatory behavior. If this succeeds, the phenomenal is reduced to the representational, which is reduced to the functional.

Critics press the phenomenal residue objection: even after fixing all representational facts, something about the phenomenal character remains — the specific intrinsic quality of the experience, not just what it represents. Inverted qualia cases illustrate this: two experiences could represent the same thing while having different phenomenal characters. If so, phenomenal character is not identical to representational content. Block's access/phenomenal distinction supports the critique: representational content is a species of access consciousness, and what representationalism explains is access to content — not the phenomenal character over and above content.

## The Continental Phenomenological Challenge

The challenge from phenomenology takes a different form in the Continental tradition. For Husserl, Heidegger, and Merleau-Ponty, experience is not passive reception of inputs processed by computational mechanisms. It is characterized by intentionality — experience is always of or about something, directed toward objects in the world in ways that involve how objects are presented to consciousness, not merely the stimuli that cause states. It has temporal flow — retention of the just-past, anticipation of the about-to-come, and primal impression of the now — constitutive of experience itself, not merely a functional feature. And it is structured by the body as a lived body (Leib), not merely as an objective organism (Körper): the phenomenology of action, perception, and emotion is shaped by embodiment in ways that functional descriptions of input-output processing cannot capture.

Hubert Dreyfus, Shaun Gallagher, and Dan Zahavi have argued that these phenomenological structures impose genuine constraints on any theory of mind that computationalist and functionalist theories systematically ignore. Functionalism is not merely incomplete — it is based on a mistaken picture of what experience is. Francisco Varela's neurophenomenology attempts a constructive response: first-person phenomenological descriptions and third-person neuroscientific descriptions must be developed in tandem, each informing the other. Neither eliminates the other; both are genuine constraints on theory. This approach partially vindicates the functionalist intuition that functional and phenomenal descriptions are connected, while insisting that functional description is not sufficient for phenomenological description. The connection requires ongoing empirical and conceptual work, not reduction. Whether neurophenomenology succeeds in making this connection precise — rather than merely gesturing at it — remains an open and important question.
