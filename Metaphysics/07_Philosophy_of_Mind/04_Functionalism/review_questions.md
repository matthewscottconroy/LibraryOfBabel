# Review Questions — Functionalism

1. Functionalism was motivated partly as a response to behaviorism's failures (it missed inner causal states) and partly to type identity theory's failures (multiple realizability). Explain why functionalism is not simply a compromise between these positions but a genuinely distinct view with its own commitments and vulnerabilities.

2. Searle's Chinese Room argues that syntax is not sufficient for semantics: the room produces appropriate Chinese outputs without understanding anything. The systems reply holds that understanding is a property of the whole system, not a component. Searle internalizes the system — the person memorizes everything — and claims no understanding results. Does the internalization response actually work against the systems reply, or does it beg the question against functionalism?

3. The absent qualia objection (Block's China Brain) and the inverted qualia objection target functionalism from different angles. Do they have the same force, or is one more damaging? If qualia can be absent while functional organization is preserved, does this show that functionalism is false, or only that it is incomplete?

4. Block's distinction between access consciousness (functional availability) and phenomenal consciousness (what-it-is-like-ness) is crucial to evaluating the absent qualia objection. If this distinction is accepted, does it save functionalism from the objection or merely concede the objection's point while changing the subject?

5. The liberalism problem and the multiple realizability argument pull in opposite directions: multiple realizability requires that many physically different systems can have the same mental states, but liberalism results from allowing too many systems to qualify. Is there a principled way to constrain the set of realizers without making the constraint covertly biological or physical — which would undermine the original motivation for functionalism?

6. Teleofunctionalism grounds mental content in evolutionary selection history rather than in current causal role. This handles the disjunction problem (the frog represents flies, not small dark moving things, because that is what it was selected to track). But the Swampman objection presses: a spontaneously assembled human duplicate has no evolutionary history; does it therefore have no mental content? Which response to the Swampman objection is most defensible?

7. Functionalism is sometimes presented as the natural heir of Aristotle's hylomorphism: both define mental states by what they do rather than what they are made of. But neo-Aristotelian hylomorphism insists that the relevant matter must be the right *kind* of organic matter, not just anything that plays the functional role. What is at stake between functionalism and biological naturalism on this question, and does the answer turn on empirical or conceptual considerations?

8. Given the difficulties raised by absent qualia, inverted qualia, the liberalism problem, and the Chinese Room, is functionalism better understood as a complete theory of mind, a theory of cognitive/intentional states only, or a useful heuristic that tracks something important without providing the final word? What would a successor to functionalism need to provide that functionalism lacks?
