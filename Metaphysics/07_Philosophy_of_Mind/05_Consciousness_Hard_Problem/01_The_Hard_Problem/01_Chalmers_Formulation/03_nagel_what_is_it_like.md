# Nagel: What Is It Like to Be Something

Bats perceive via echolocation. We can learn everything about bat neuroscience: how auditory cortex processes sonar signals, how bats navigate, how they catch insects. We can build complete functional models of bat perceptual behavior. But Thomas Nagel argues, in "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974), that none of this tells us what it is like to be a bat — what echolocation feels like from the inside. The bat's phenomenal experience is inaccessible to us not merely because of practical limits but because of the objective/subjective gap itself.

The bat example is carefully chosen. We can partially imagine what it is like to have different visual experiences — inverted colors, enhanced acuity — because vision is a modality we share. Echolocation is so different from anything we experience that our imaginative resources give out completely. We cannot form a conception of what echolocation is like from the inside; we can only describe its functional role in the bat's behavior. Nagel is careful to note that this does not require that the bat's experience is inaccessible in principle to all minds — only that it is inaccessible to minds like ours. This, he argues, is philosophically significant.

The argument rests on a sharp distinction between objective and subjective facts. Objective facts are available to any observer from any point of view — they are the kind of facts captured in physics, chemistry, and biology, facts that are equally available whether you are a bat or a neuroscientist. Subjective facts are essentially tied to a particular point of view; they can only be fully grasped from within that perspective. The character of conscious experience is a subjective fact in this sense. No amount of third-person neuroscience gives us a first-person grasp of another's experience.

Nagel's argument does not commit him to substance dualism. He is making an epistemological and conceptual point: our current scientific methods, which work beautifully for objective facts, are structurally unsuited to capture subjective ones. Reductionist accounts typically proceed by showing that a phenomenon is nothing over and above some more basic physical process — and this works when the phenomenon in question is objective. But if consciousness is essentially subjective, reduction cannot proceed by the usual route. What is needed, Nagel suggests, is a new kind of objective phenomenology — a way of making subjective experience scientifically tractable without distorting it into something else. He does not claim to have developed such a phenomenology; he claims that its absence is why the mind-body problem remains genuinely open.

## Responses and Nagel's Later Work

The functionalist response holds that the subjective-objective gap is generated by the distinctive character of phenomenal concepts — recognitional concepts that pick out physical properties by acquaintance — not by any genuine metaphysical difference. Once we understand how phenomenal concepts work, the gap dissolves into a conceptual distinction, not a metaphysical one. The eliminativist response (Dennett) treats Nagel's argument as depending on taking phenomenal intuitions at face value; on a heterophenomenological stance, reports of "what it is like" are data about functional states, not evidence for non-physical properties. The mysterian response (McGinn) agrees with Nagel that the gap reflects something real but concludes it is permanent: our minds cannot grasp the connection between objective neural processes and subjective experience, any more than a color-blind person can grasp the color red. The panpsychist response accepts Nagel's insight that subjectivity cannot be derived from objective physical descriptions but draws a different conclusion: physics itself always involves a subjective dimension, and the objective description of physics is always a partial description of a reality that includes an experiential aspect.

Nagel developed the implications of the paper in *The View from Nowhere* (1986), arguing that the tension between subjective and objective points of view runs through all areas of philosophy — ethics, epistemology, and metaphysics — not just philosophy of mind. The hard problem of consciousness is, for Nagel, one instance of a general tension between how things appear from a particular perspective and how they are described from no perspective in particular. This broader context is important: the hard problem is not an isolated puzzle about neurons but a manifestation of one of the deepest tensions in how we understand ourselves as beings in the world.
