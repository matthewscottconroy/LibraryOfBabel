# The Explanatory Gap

Joseph Levine introduced the term "explanatory gap" in "Materialism and Qualia: The Explanatory Gap" (1983) to identify something precise about the failure of physical explanations to illuminate why physical processes give rise to particular qualitative experiences. Even if we accept that pain is identical to C-fiber firing — even if there is no metaphysical gap, no separate non-physical substance — there seems to be a gap in our understanding. We can explain why C-fiber firing causes avoidance behavior, distress, and verbal reports. We cannot explain why it feels like something painful rather than nothing at all.

Compare two cases of reduction. Temperature reduces to mean kinetic energy: once we know the identity, we can derive all the macroscopic thermal properties from the microscopic description. The explanation is illuminating — we understand why systems with greater mean kinetic energy have higher temperatures, because temperature just is mean kinetic energy. The reduction closes with understanding. Pain allegedly reduces to C-fiber firing: even if we accept the identity, we cannot similarly derive the qualitative character of pain from the description of C-fiber firing. Why should these electrochemical events be painfully felt? The question persists after the identity is stated. The water-temperature reduction leaves no residue of unexplained phenomenal properties; the consciousness reduction does. This asymmetry is what constitutes the explanatory gap.

Levine was careful in his original formulation to distinguish the explanatory gap from a metaphysical gap. The gap is epistemological rather than ontological — a gap in our ability to explain, not necessarily in nature itself. Even if pain is physically identical to C-fiber firing, we might lack the conceptual resources to see how the identity explains the qualitative character. The gap might be in us, not in the world. This epistemic interpretation was developed by Brian Loar's phenomenal concepts strategy: we access the same physical state through two distinct modes — neural concepts (relating to functional and causal role) and phenomenal concepts (involving direct acquaintance with the quality). The explanatory gap arises because these two modes of access are conceptually distinct, not because they track metaphysically distinct properties.

## Levine's Later Development and the Persistence of the Gap

In *Purple Haze: The Puzzle of Consciousness* (2001), Levine came to see the gap as reflecting both epistemic and metaphysical features. The gap is epistemological in that it reflects something about how we conceptualize consciousness — phenomenal concepts present their referents via a direct experiential mode, not via any descriptive or inferential route, which makes their connection to physical concepts opaque even when the referents are identical. But the gap may also reflect a metaphysical truth: even if pain is physically identical to C-fiber firing, something about this connection seems contingent in a way that would not exist in a true conceptual reduction. Levine independently coined the term "phenomenal concept" to capture this: phenomenal concepts are not just another mode of description but a fundamentally different kind of cognitive access.

Chalmers's stronger claim is that the explanatory gap tracks a genuine metaphysical gap: qualia are not physical properties, and closing the explanatory gap requires either showing that phenomenal concepts are not genuinely distinct from functional concepts — which Chalmers denies — or accepting property dualism.

Neuroscientific research on neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs) identifies the minimal neural events jointly sufficient for a specific conscious experience. But NCC research does not obviously close the explanatory gap. Finding that experience X is correlated with neural activity Y establishes a correlation, not an explanation. The question remains: why is Y correlated with X rather than Z, or with no experience at all? NCC research identifies which physical events accompany consciousness; the explanatory gap asks why any physical events accompany consciousness. The correlations are important evidence and constrain theories, but their interpretation requires a philosophical framework that NCC research alone cannot provide. Whether the explanatory gap is a temporary puzzle that better science will eventually dissolve, or a permanent feature of the relationship between objective and subjective descriptions of reality, is the deepest open question in philosophy of mind.
