# Phenomenal Concepts

When you attend to your experience of red and think "this very quality," you are deploying a phenomenal concept. Such concepts are recognitional: they directly pick out an experiential property without going via any description or functional profile. They are acquired through direct acquaintance with the experience itself, not through causal-functional characterization. This distinguishes them from physical concepts, which describe neural states in third-person terms. The phenomenal concept strategy, associated with Brian Loar, Sydney Shoemaker, David Papineau, and others, holds that the appearance of a gap between phenomenal and physical descriptions is an artifact of this conceptual distinctiveness, not evidence of anything non-physical.

The strategy explains several puzzles simultaneously. Why does the knowledge argument seem compelling? Because Mary's phenomenal concepts are new — they could not be had without the experience — even though the properties they pick out were always there in the physical world. The concepts are new; the properties are not. Why do zombies seem conceivable? Because our phenomenal and physical concepts are conceptually independent, so their combination can be imagined without contradiction. But conceivability does not entail metaphysical possibility when concepts are recognitional or indexical rather than descriptive.

The varieties of phenomenal concepts differ in how they characterize the mode of reference. Loar's recognitional concept theory holds that phenomenal concepts refer to types of state by direct pattern-matching: when I think "this pain," I recognize a type of state by its intrinsic character without any descriptive mediation. The concept is self-presenting in that having the experience constitutes deploying the concept. Loar argues that recognitional concepts can refer to physical properties without the reference being mediated by any functional or physical description. Papineau's use-mention theory holds that phenomenal concepts involve a use of the very experience they conceptualize: when I think about pain phenomenally, I use a mental token of pain to think about pain. The concept is partly constituted by an instance of what it refers to, which explains why phenomenal concepts can only be acquired through experience. Perry's indexical theory treats phenomenal concepts as analogous to "I" and "now" — indexicals that pick out their referents contextually — which explains both their first-person character and their resistance to third-person description.

## The Key Explanatory Claim and Its Limits

The strategy must explain two things: why phenomenal concepts seem to refer to something non-physical, and why the conceivability of zombies does not establish non-physical properties. On the first: phenomenal concepts are conceptually independent of all physical concepts. Being recognitional, use-based, or indexical means the concept does not trade on any physical or functional description. It therefore appears to pick out something that could float free of physical properties — but this is an appearance generated by the character of the concept, not by the character of the property. On the second: zombies seem conceivable because we can entertain phenomenal concepts without entertaining any corresponding physical concept. But conceptual independence does not establish metaphysical independence.

Three objections press against the strategy. The circularity objection: phenomenal concepts are said to refer to experience via experience — this explains the distinctiveness of the concept but not the distinctiveness of any property it tracks. The revelation objection: if phenomenal concepts are fully transparent to their referents, there is no hidden physical nature for the concept to be "really" tracking while seeming mysterious; the property the concept picks out is just what is presented, and what is presented appears not to be a physical property. The self-undermining objection: if phenomenal concepts are radically isolated from any physical description, introspective knowledge of phenomenal states does not constrain our physical theories; but if introspection gives us knowledge of our mental states, and those states are physical, introspection must somehow constrain physical theory. The phenomenal concept strategy appears to sever introspection from physical theory, which is a cost that defenders of the strategy must address. Whether any of these objections is decisive, or whether the strategy can be refined to meet them, is among the most actively debated questions in contemporary philosophy of mind.
