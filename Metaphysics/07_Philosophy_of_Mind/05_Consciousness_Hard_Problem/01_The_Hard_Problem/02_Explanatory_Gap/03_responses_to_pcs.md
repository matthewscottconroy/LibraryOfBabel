# Responses to the Phenomenal Concept Strategy

The phenomenal concept strategy (PCS) accepts the hard problem's diagnosis — phenomenal concepts are conceptually independent of physical concepts — while rejecting its conclusion — phenomenal properties are non-physical. It tries to explain why consciousness seems hard to explain without conceding that it is genuinely non-physical. Whether this is a stable position depends on whether the explanation can be completed without smuggling in features that effectively concede non-physicalism.

The most pointed objection is the "explanatory gap reloaded," developed by Katalin Balog and pressed by Levine. Suppose we grant everything the PCS says — that phenomenal concepts are recognitional, use-based, or indexical, and that this explains why they appear conceptually independent of physical concepts. We still need to explain why these concepts apply to physical properties at all. If phenomenal concepts are fully independent of physical concepts in their mode of presentation, then the fact that they refer to physical properties is itself a further mystery. Why should recognitional concepts acquired through direct experience pick out neural states? A dualist has a straightforward answer: because phenomenal concepts pick out genuine phenomenal properties, which are not physical. The physicalist must explain how concepts that entirely bypass physical description nonetheless refer to physical properties. This is the original explanatory gap, now applied to concept-property relations rather than property-property relations.

Papineau's response grounds phenomenal concept reference in causal-historical relations. Phenomenal concepts refer to physical states via causal chains: the neural state causes the experience, and the experience causes the deployment of the phenomenal concept. The reference is secured by a causal-historical relation even though the concept's content includes no physical description. This parallels the reference of proper names: "Aristotle" refers to Aristotle via a causal-historical chain, not via any description that uniquely identifies him. Once we understand this causal-historical grounding, the mystery of how phenomenal concepts refer to physical properties dissolves — they refer to them for the same reason any recognitional concept refers to its type.

## The Transparency Objection and the Strategic Position

Martine Nida-Rümelin's transparency objection cuts at a different joint. When I am directly acquainted with my phenomenal state, I know it through and through — acquaintance is supposed to be complete epistemic access. If the physical nature of the state were what I know by acquaintance, then the physical nature would be fully revealed — and acquaintance would give me physical knowledge. Since acquaintance manifestly does not give me physical knowledge (I do not learn about neural firing patterns by having pain), the property known by acquaintance is not a physical property. PCS defenders respond that acquaintance is not fully transparent in the relevant sense: acquaintance gives knowledge of the state under a phenomenal mode of presentation, not knowledge of the state's intrinsic physical nature. The physical nature is "hidden" even from acquaintance, which is why physical knowledge is not given by experience.

The strategic position of the PCS is philosophically interesting precisely because it occupies contested ground. It accepts that phenomenal concepts have a distinctive character that generates the appearance of an explanatory gap. It denies that this appearance tracks a metaphysical gap. Whether this denial is earned or merely asserted — whether the causal-historical account of reference and the non-transparency account of acquaintance genuinely dissolve the mystery or merely relocate it — is the question the debate turns on. If the PCS succeeds, it shows that physicalism can be maintained while being fully honest about what makes consciousness hard. If it fails, it shows that any attempt to explain the appearance of mystery will either trivialize consciousness by explaining it away or vindicate dualism by acknowledging that consciousness has properties that resist physical characterization.
