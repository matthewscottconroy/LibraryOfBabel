# The Zombie Argument

A philosophical zombie is physically and functionally identical to a conscious human in every detail — same atoms, same neural connections, same functional organization, same behavioral outputs — yet has no conscious experience whatsoever. There is nothing it is like to be a zombie. Its internal states process information and produce behavioral outputs just as ours do, but no subjective feel accompanies any of it. The zombie argument, most closely associated with Chalmers though related versions appear in Robert Kirk and Keith Campbell, proceeds from the conceivability of zombies to the conclusion that consciousness is not physical.

The argument:

1. P-zombies are conceivable — there is no logical or conceptual contradiction in the description.
2. If P-zombies are conceivable (in the relevant sense), they are metaphysically possible — there is a possible world in which beings physically identical to us exist but have no consciousness.
3. If P-zombies are metaphysically possible, then consciousness is not logically entailed by physical facts.
4. If consciousness is not logically entailed by physical facts, then consciousness is not identical to or constituted by physical facts.
5. Therefore, physicalism about consciousness is false.

The argument has been challenged at almost every step, and working through the challenges illuminates the structure of the entire debate.

Against premise 1: some philosophers deny that P-zombies are genuinely conceivable. Once we fully understand what physical and functional organization amounts to, we will see that phenomenal experience is entailed. Our apparent conceivability of zombies reflects cognitive limitations rather than any genuine modal fact. Dennett is particularly insistent: if you can coherently conceive of a zombie, you have not really understood consciousness; there must be something your zombie is missing that you have not noticed.

Against the conceivability-to-possibility inference (premise 2): others grant conceivability but deny that it establishes metaphysical possibility. Type-B physicalists hold that phenomenal concepts are recognitional concepts that are conceptually independent of physical concepts, which means we can conceive of phenomenal and physical facts coming apart. But conceptual independence does not entail metaphysical independence. The conceivability of zombies reflects the structure of our concepts, not the structure of reality.

Against premises 3-5: these follow analytically if premise 2 is granted. The work is done by the conceivability-to-possibility inference.

## The Argument's Contribution

The zombie argument's contribution to philosophy of mind is not that it settles the debate but that it makes explicit what is at stake. Before Chalmers, anti-physicalist arguments were typically based on intuitions about qualia, the knowledge argument, or the explanatory gap. The zombie argument synthesizes these concerns into a rigorous modal argument: not just "consciousness seems non-physical" but "consciousness is metaphysically possible without the physical, therefore non-physical." Physicalists must either deny the conceivability of zombies (type-A physicalism) or deny the conceivability-to-possibility inference (type-B physicalism). Each strategy has its own costs, and the zombie argument has forced physicalists to be more precise about what their view entails. The argument is the best-articulated case for anti-physicalism about consciousness currently available.
