# Conceivability to Possibility

The argumentative crux of the zombie argument is the move from conceivability to metaphysical possibility. The general principle is that ideal conceivability — conceivability that survives careful rational reflection — is evidence for metaphysical possibility. Descartes used this principle in the Meditations: he can clearly and distinctly conceive of mind without body, and what is clearly and distinctly conceivable is possible, so mind can exist without body. Chalmers deploys a sophisticated version of the same principle.

The principle requires refinement to be plausible. Mere imaginability is not enough: we can imagine a round square in the loose sense of picturing round and square things in rapid succession, but a round square is not genuinely possible. Chalmers distinguishes prima facie conceivability (immediate apparent conceivability that might be undermined by reflection) from ideal conceivability (what remains conceivable after ideal rational reflection). The relevant principle is: ideal conceivability entails metaphysical possibility.

The well-known challenge from mathematics: it seems conceivable that Goldbach's conjecture is false — we cannot derive a contradiction from its negation by simple reasoning. But if Goldbach's conjecture is necessarily true, its negation is not genuinely possible despite seeming conceivable. Chalmers responds that the conceivability of Goldbach's negation is not ideal conceivability. If we could reason perfectly in mathematics, we would resolve the question, and the conceivability would collapse. Zombie conceivability is different: there is no proof that zombies are impossible, no physical theory that establishes that phenomenal experience must accompany physical processes of a certain kind, and the conceivability persists after any amount of careful reflection. This is why zombie conceivability is robust in a way that the conceivability of mathematical falsehoods is not.

## Type-A and Type-B Responses

Type-A physicalists (Dennett, Frankish) deny that zombies are even primarily conceivable. Once we fully understand what physical and functional organization amounts to — once we grasp how information processing, self-modeling, and behavioral control work in their full complexity — we will see that phenomenal consciousness is entailed. The tendency to conceive of zombies reflects not a modal fact but a conceptual limitation: we do not yet understand what physical-functional organization fully involves, and the zombie thought experiment exploits this ignorance.

Type-B physicalists (Loar, Papineau, Jackson in later work) grant that zombies are conceivable but deny that conceivability establishes metaphysical possibility. The conceivability of zombies reflects the conceptual independence of phenomenal concepts from physical concepts: phenomenal concepts are recognitional or use-based rather than descriptive, so there is no a priori connection from physical descriptions to phenomenal descriptions. But conceptual independence does not entail metaphysical independence. The same physical property can be picked out by conceptually independent concepts without there being two metaphysically distinct properties.

The type-B strategy faces a further challenge: it must explain what grounds the claim that zombies are impossible despite being conceivable. If the only access we have to modal truths is through conceivability, then holding that zombies are conceivable but not possible requires some additional epistemic resource. Chalmers's two-dimensional framework provides the type-B physicalist with a partial answer: there is a primary-intension sense in which zombies are possible and a secondary-intension sense in which they are not. But this move, as Chalmers argues, is available only if phenomenal concepts have functional primary intensions — which is precisely what Chalmers denies. The debate about the conceivability-to-possibility inference is, at its deepest level, the same debate as the debate about whether phenomenal concepts are functional or acquaintive, whether there is an a priori or a posteriori connection between physical and phenomenal facts, and whether the explanatory gap tracks a metaphysical or merely a conceptual discontinuity.
