# Responses to the Zombie Argument

Physicalists have developed several strategies to resist the zombie argument, and they differ at the level of diagnosis: is the problem with the conceivability of zombies, with the conceivability-to-possibility inference, or with the conception of phenomenal properties that makes the argument seem compelling?

The inconceivability response, pressed most forcefully by Dennett, argues that zombies only seem conceivable because we have not thought carefully enough about what physical-functional identity really implies. If you imagine a being truly behaviorally and functionally identical to a conscious person — including all subtle discriminative capacities, emotional reactions, verbal reports, and self-monitoring — you will find you have imagined something with experience after all. The residual intuition that something is missing reflects a failure to fully inhabit the physical-functional description, not a genuine discovery about consciousness. What exactly is the zombie missing? Not discrimination, not self-monitoring, not verbal report, not emotional response. The only thing the zombie is missing is "phenomenal experience" — but if phenomenal experience is not a functional property, we must ask what we are even talking about. Dennett's heterophenomenology treats reports of "what it is like" as data about functional states, not evidence for non-physical properties. On this view, the "inner glow" of experience is a confused concept, and the zombie description turns out to describe a fully normal person once the confused concept is stripped away.

The anti-Dennett response is direct: the inner glow is not a confused concept — it is precisely what the hard problem is about. To say we can strip it away and still have a "fully normal person" is to beg the question against the reality of phenomenal experience.

The phenomenal concepts strategy holds that the conceivability of zombies reflects the sui generis character of phenomenal concepts — their independence from physical concepts — not the non-physical nature of phenomenal properties. Zombies seem conceivable because we can entertain phenomenal concepts without entertaining any corresponding physical concept. But conceptual independence does not entail metaphysical independence.

## Illusionism and Evaluating the Responses

Keith Frankish's illusionism accepts that zombies are conceivable but argues they are not missing anything real — because phenomenal properties as traditionally conceived do not exist. The zombie argument has the form: zombies are possible, therefore consciousness is non-physical. Illusionism's response: zombies are possible, because phenomenal properties as traditionally conceived — intrinsic, ineffable, non-relational qualitative properties — are illusions. Since phenomenal properties do not exist, there is nothing for zombies to lack. What does exist is "quasi-phenomenal" states: functional states that we systematically misrepresent as having phenomenal properties. We are all zombies, in the sense that we lack genuine phenomenal properties; what we have are functional states that generate the illusion of phenomenal character. The zombie argument shows not that consciousness is non-physical but that the naive folk concept of phenomenal consciousness is confused.

The self-referential quality of the debate is worth noting explicitly. Every response depends on views about the nature of consciousness that are themselves at issue. The phenomenal concepts strategy assumes phenomenal concepts are a special kind of recognitional concept — contested. Illusionism assumes phenomenal properties as traditionally conceived do not exist — but the zombie argument is designed to show that at least possible worlds exist where something matching that description is absent. A priori physicalism assumes all facts are derivable from physical facts — which is what the zombie argument challenges. What the debate reveals is that the zombie argument is not a self-contained proof but a probe: it forces each position to commit to specific theses about the nature of consciousness, concepts, and modality, and the value of the argument lies in the precision with which it maps the logical space of available positions.
