# Important Terms and Ideas: The Hard Problem of Consciousness

**The Hard Problem**
Chalmers's distinction: the "easy" problems concern explaining cognitive functions—how the brain integrates information, focuses attention, reports mental states. The hard problem concerns explaining why these processes are accompanied by subjective experience—why there is something it is like to undergo them.

**Explanatory Gap**
Levine's term for the apparent inability to explain, even given complete physical knowledge, why physical processes produce the phenomenal properties they do. Even if pain is C-fiber firing, we cannot understand from the physical description why C-fiber firing hurts. The gap suggests something is left out.

**Phenomenal Consciousness**
The subjective, qualitative aspect of experience—what it is like to see red, feel pain, or taste coffee. Phenomenal consciousness is what the hard problem concerns: why any physical process gives rise to this subjective dimension.

**Access Consciousness**
Block's distinction: a mental state is access conscious if it is available for use in reasoning, reporting, and guiding behavior. Access consciousness is what the easy problems explain. Phenomenal consciousness is distinct: a state can be phenomenally conscious without being fully access conscious (as in some cases of masked perception).

**The Conceivability Argument**
Chalmers's principal argument for the hard problem: philosophical zombies are conceivable (beings physically identical to us but lacking phenomenal consciousness); if they are conceivable, they are possible; if they are possible, then phenomenal consciousness is not physically necessitated—the hard problem is genuine.

**Illusionism**
Frankish's deflationary response: what Chalmers calls "phenomenal consciousness" is not a genuine property of mental states but an introspective illusion—we systematically misrepresent our own functional states as having qualitative properties they do not really have. The hard problem dissolves once the illusion is unmasked.
