# Qualia

There is something it is like to taste coffee: a specific, rich, somewhat bitter phenomenal quality that is entirely familiar to you if you have tasted it and entirely opaque to you if you have not. This felt quality — the "what it is like" of experience — is what philosophers call qualia. Qualia are the intrinsic, phenomenal properties of conscious experience: the redness of red as it appears to vision, the painfulness of pain as it feels from the inside, the distinctive character of each sensory quality.

Qualia have seemed to many philosophers to be the clearest evidence that consciousness cannot be fully explained in physical or functional terms. The explanatory gap argument runs: we can imagine a physical duplicate of a human being — same neurons, same functional organization, same behavior — who has no qualia whatsoever. This "philosophical zombie" performs all the cognitive functions that conscious beings perform, but there is nothing it is like to be it. If zombies are conceivable, they are possible (the conceivability-to-possibility inference); and if they are possible, then qualia are not identical to any physical or functional property.

Frank Jackson's "Mary's Room" thought experiment makes the case in a different way. Mary is a scientist who has complete physical knowledge of the human visual system — she knows every neuroscientific fact about color vision — but has been raised in a black-and-white room and has never seen colors. When she finally leaves the room and sees a red apple for the first time, does she learn something new? Intuitively, yes: she learns what it is like to see red. But if she knew all the physical facts, and there is still something she did not know, then the phenomenal character of color experience is not captured by any physical fact.

Physicalists have developed various responses: eliminativism (there are no qualia — the concept is confused), representationalism (qualia are representational properties), and higher-order theories. This section examines each response alongside the arguments for taking qualia seriously as a genuine metaphysical datum.
