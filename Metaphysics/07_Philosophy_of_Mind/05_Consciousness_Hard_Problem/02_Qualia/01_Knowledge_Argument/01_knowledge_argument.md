# The Knowledge Argument

Mary is a brilliant scientist who has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room. She has learned everything there is to know about the physical basis of color perception: which wavelengths produce which neural responses, which responses produce which behavioral dispositions, how the visual cortex processes color information, how color concepts are acquired and deployed. She has studied every patient, every psychophysics experiment, every neuroscientific study. She knows every physical fact about color vision.

One day, Mary is released and sees red for the first time. Frank Jackson, who introduced this thought experiment in "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982), argues that she learns something new: she learns what it is like to see red. If she already knew all the physical facts, and she still learns something new upon first seeing red, then what she learns is not a physical fact — it is a fact about the qualitative character of color experience that is not captured by any physical description.

The argument:

1. Mary knows all the physical facts about color vision before her release.
2. Mary learns something new when she first sees red.
3. Therefore, there are facts about color experience that are not physical facts.

The argument is an inference from epistemology to ontology: from the claim that Mary gains new knowledge to the conclusion that there are non-physical facts. The inference is standard — if there is something one does not know, there is a fact one does not know about — but it is more controversial in this context than in ordinary cases.

## The Original Context and Jackson's Epiphenomenalism

Jackson presented the knowledge argument alongside a companion case about Fred, a person with unusual color discrimination who can reliably distinguish two types of ripe tomatoes that look identical to everyone else — he sees them as two distinct colors, red1 and red2. Normal humans cannot know what it is like to see red1 and red2 because we lack the relevant experience. Mary is more powerful because the limitation is not in her perceptual system but in her environment: she has complete access to every physical and functional fact. The limitation is in what kind of information is accessible without experience, not in what is available in principle.

In his original presentation, Jackson accepted the conclusion and drew a further one: epiphenomenalism. If phenomenal properties are non-physical, they must be causally inert — given causal closure, only physical properties cause physical effects. Jackson later recognized that epiphenomenalism is deeply self-undermining in this context: if phenomenal properties are causally inert, then Mary's new belief "this is what red looks like" is not caused by her phenomenal experience but by the neural response. She is not really learning about phenomenal experience at all. The self-undermining character of epiphenomenalism was a significant factor in Jackson's conversion to physicalism.

## Responses and Jackson's Recantation

The most important physicalist response is the ability hypothesis (Lewis, Nemirow): Mary does not learn a new fact — she gains a new ability, the ability to recognize, remember, and imagine red experiences. Knowing-how is different from knowing-that; Mary gains a new competence without gaining new propositional knowledge. The response requires a principled distinction between ability knowledge and propositional knowledge, and critics press that it seems implausible that Mary does not learn anything propositional — surely she comes to know what red looks like, and that seems to be a fact.

The phenomenal concepts strategy (Loar, Papineau) holds that Mary gains the same propositional knowledge under a new mode of presentation: she learns which physical state is the one she previously knew about via physical description, now grasped via direct phenomenal acquaintance. The new mode of presentation — acquaintive rather than descriptive — makes it feel like new knowledge about a new fact, but the fact itself is not new. Jackson eventually adopted a version of this view and recanted his original anti-physicalist conclusion. Whether this recantation successfully defends physicalism or merely demonstrates that Jackson lost confidence in his original position is itself debated — many philosophers think the original argument retains its force even if Jackson himself abandoned it.
