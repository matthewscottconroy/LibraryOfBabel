# Mary's Room

Consider what it would mean to know everything. Mary is a brilliant scientist who has lived her entire life in a black-and-white room. She has never seen colors — never seen a red apple, a blue sky, a green field. Yet she has mastered the complete science of color vision: which wavelengths stimulate which cone types, how signals propagate through the visual cortex, what neural patterns correlate with reports of "red," every psychophysics experiment ever run, every neuroimaging study, every model of color processing. She knows, in Jackson's phrase, all the physical facts. The room stipulation is not mere whimsy — it is designed to ensure there is no physical or functional fact about color vision she has missed. If she has missed something, the argument is weakened, since she might simply learn that fact when she sees red for the first time.

Now the door opens. Mary steps out and sees a ripe tomato. Something happens. Jackson's intuition — shared, he thinks, by most readers — is that she exclaims "So that's what it's like!" She learns what red looks like. She learns something. But if she already knew all the physical facts, and she still learns something upon seeing red, then what she learns is not a physical fact. Phenomenal facts — facts about what it is like to have an experience — are not entailed by the physical facts. Qualia are non-physical.

The argument that follows has a clean structure: (1) Before leaving the room, Mary knows all physical facts about color vision. (2) After leaving the room, she learns something new. (3) Therefore, there are non-physical facts — facts about the qualitative character of experience that a complete physical description leaves out. The target is *physicalist completeness*: the thesis that all facts supervene on physical facts, that a complete physical description of the world entails a complete description of everything. If Mary's new knowledge is genuine, physicalism is false.

## The Thought Experiment Examined

Notice what the design of the case achieves. The black-and-white room ensures completeness: Mary is not just well-informed but exhaustively informed. The "sees red for the first time" moment is carefully placed after that completeness is established. And the new knowledge is specified to concern experience itself — not some third-person observable fact she happened to miss — but what red looks like from the inside.

This last point matters. Mary could in principle predict, before leaving the room, everything she will say and do when she first sees red. She knows the wavelength will trigger the relevant neural response; she knows that response correlates with verbal reports like "this is what I was missing." What she cannot predict, the argument claims, is what the experience is like — the qualitative feel of redness as it presents itself to her. That is the residue that physical description leaves behind.

The phenomenal concept strategy, in its application to Mary's case, denies that this residue is ontologically significant. Before leaving the room, Mary knew: "State S is the state correlated with 700nm wavelength, causing such-and-such verbal reports." After leaving, she knows: "State S is *this* — the state I am currently in, with this qualitative character." The second piece of knowledge deploys a phenomenal concept — use-based, acquaintance-based — that she could not deploy before, because phenomenal concepts require the experience. But the fact grasped is the same physical fact. No new ontology is required; what is new is the mode of presentation. Mary learns which physical state her previous knowledge was about, expressed under a phenomenal guise.

## The Ability Hypothesis and the Intuitive Residue

The ability hypothesis (Lewis, Nemirow) offers a different deflationary reading. What Mary gains when she sees red is not new propositional knowledge — not knowledge of a new fact — but new know-how: the ability to recognize, remember, and imagine red experiences. When we say she "learns what it's like to see red," we are reporting a gain in ability, and "knowing what it's like" is grammatically misleading. Consider: knowing what it is like to swim cannot be conveyed in propositions — you have to do it. Mary is in exactly that position before leaving the room. Upon leaving, she gains the relevant competence.

There is something right about this. But the objection presses: we can separate the abilities from the knowledge. Suppose Mary, upon leaving the room, suffers an immediate memory impairment that prevents her from imagining red or forming stable memories of red experiences, but leaves her recognition capacity intact. She still knows what red looks like — she knows it when she sees it — but she has lost the imagining and remembering abilities. The knowledge and the abilities come apart. Conversely, someone might acquire the recognition ability through some unusual non-experiential mechanism while lacking the knowledge. If the abilities are neither necessary nor sufficient for the knowledge, the hypothesis mislocalizes what is at stake.

A deeper problem: the abilities Lewis and Nemirow describe are specified in phenomenal terms — the ability to recognize *red experiences*, to imagine *what red looks like*. Their content is already phenomenal. The hypothesis seems to presuppose, rather than explain away, precisely the phenomenal content the knowledge argument is designed to establish.

Despite the sophistication of both responses, many philosophers find that something is left over. The phenomenal concepts strategy explains why Mary's transition involves a new concept, but it does not explain why the physical state has phenomenal character at all. That a neural state has qualitative redness seems to be a further fact — not captured by the story about how Mary gains a new mode of presentation. Jackson himself, even after his recantation, acknowledged that the original intuition is powerful and that any adequate physicalist response must explain why it is so compelling, not merely deflect it. The debate over Mary is, at its root, the hard problem in miniature: not just "what does Mary learn?" but "why is there anything it is like to be Mary in the first place?"
