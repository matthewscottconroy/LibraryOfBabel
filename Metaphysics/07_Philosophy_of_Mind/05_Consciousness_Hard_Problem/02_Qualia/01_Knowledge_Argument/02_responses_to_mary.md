# Responses to Mary

The knowledge argument has generated a literature of responses, and what is striking about them is that they cluster around a single question: does Mary gain new propositional knowledge — knowledge of a new fact — or does she gain something that merely resembles propositional knowledge while being, in the end, something else? The responses answer this question differently, and the differences expose the real fault lines in the debate.

The ability hypothesis (Lewis, Nemirow) says Mary gains not new knowledge-that but new knowledge-how: the ability to recognize, remember, and imagine red experiences. When we say she "learns what red is like," our grammar is misleading. "Knowing what it is like to swim" is not really knowing-that — you cannot convey it in propositions alone. Mary gains the relevant recognitional and imaginative competence, and that is all the "learning" amounts to. The "old knowledge in new modes" response holds that Mary gains new propositional knowledge, but of a physical fact she already knew under a new mode of presentation. She now grasps state S under a phenomenal concept rather than a physical-functional one. No new fact has been discovered; what is new is the concept deployed. The "physicalist-friendly" or disjunction response treats the cognitive novelty as akin to learning a name for a person you already know under a different description — new from the inside, not new in the world.

Each of these responses represents a distinct strategy, and each has been refined and criticized. The debate over Mary is not a contained puzzle but a pressure point: every physicalist response must eventually say where the phenomenal character of experience is located in the physical story, and that question does not dissolve just because we have given a name to our answer.

## The Ability Hypothesis in Detail

Lewis and Nemirow press the grammatical point: "knows what it is like" has the same surface form as "knows that the sky is blue" but a different logical structure. Consider the parallel with knowing how to ride a bicycle. No amount of propositional instruction — "apply pressure to the right pedal, lean into the turn" — fully constitutes that knowledge. You have to do it. If "knowing what it is like to see red" is similarly know-how, then Mary's transition involves gaining a competence, and no new facts need be posited to explain it.

The challenge for the hypothesis is to explain why we find it natural to say that Mary *learns* something, not merely *acquires* something. Learning paradigmatically involves coming to know a new fact. If Mary gains only abilities, the right description seems to be that she acquires new skills — not that she learns what red looks like. The hypothesis systematically redescribes what the intuition presents as factual acquisition and calls it something else.

More pressingly: the abilities described are already phenomenal in their content. The ability to recognize *red experiences*, to imagine *what red looks like* — these are specified in terms that presuppose the phenomenal character the hypothesis is supposed to explain. If we try to specify the abilities without presupposing phenomenal content, we get something like "the ability to respond differentially to 700nm stimuli" — which Mary had before leaving the room. The hypothesis either presupposes phenomenal content or collapses into something Mary already had.

## The "New Concept" Response

The phenomenal concepts strategy applied to Mary's case holds that she gains new propositional knowledge — genuine knowledge-that — but about an old fact grasped through a new concept. Before the release: Mary knows "state S is correlated with 700nm wavelength, producing such-and-such reports." After the release: Mary knows "state S is *this* — the state I am now in, with this qualitative character." The new concept is a phenomenal concept, acquired through direct acquaintance, and it picks out the same physical state her earlier knowledge described.

What is new is the concept, not the fact. And since phenomenal concepts are use-based — they require an instance of the very experience they are about — Mary could not deploy them before having the experience. This explains both why she gains knowledge upon release (she deploys a new concept) and why the knowledge seems genuinely new (phenomenal concepts are cognitively independent of physical-functional concepts). Papineau refines this: the phenomenal concept Mary gains is partly constituted by an instance of the experience itself, making it irreducibly experiential in character while its referent remains a physical state.

The objection is that this response cannot account for the intuitive sense in which Mary learns something *substantive* about experience. If she is merely deploying a new concept for an old fact, why does the experience of red seem to reveal something about what the state *is* — its inner character — rather than just how Mary is now related to it? The phenomenal concepts strategy implies that all the information content was always there; Mary was just not thinking about it in the right way. But it seems as if something genuinely new about the qualitative character of redness becomes accessible only through the experience itself, not just a new way of packaging what was already known.

## The Phenomenal Residue

Many philosophers find that every physicalist response leaves an explanation unaccounted for. Chalmers puts the point precisely: even granting that Mary's new knowledge is a new way of accessing an old physical fact, there is still the question of why the physical fact has phenomenal character at all. That certain neural states produce the experience of redness — that there is something it is like to be in state S — is itself a fact that Mary's complete physical education does not explain. The physicalist responses address the question "Does Mary learn a new propositional fact?" but leave open the question "Why does the neural state have phenomenal character?" These are distinct questions, and the knowledge argument trades on both.

Anti-physicalists draw the direct conclusion. If Mary learns something new — a genuine fact about what red looks like — and this fact was not entailed by her complete physical knowledge, then phenomenal facts are irreducible to physical facts. The inference is to property dualism: phenomenal properties are real, non-physical properties instantiated by physical systems. Chalmers's version emphasizes that what Mary gains is knowledge of a genuinely new *property* — the phenomenal property of redness — that cannot be described in any physical vocabulary however complete. This is not a limitation of Mary's education but a reflection of the non-physical nature of phenomenal properties.

The debate about Mary thus mirrors the broader debate between physicalism and property dualism in miniature: each response to the knowledge argument corresponds to a position in the larger metaphysical dispute, and the same considerations that make the responses plausible or implausible as answers to the Mary case show up again, in different dress, in the general dispute about whether physical facts suffice to determine phenomenal facts.
