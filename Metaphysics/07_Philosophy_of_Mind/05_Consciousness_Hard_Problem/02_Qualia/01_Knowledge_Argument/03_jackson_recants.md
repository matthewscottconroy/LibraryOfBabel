# Jackson Recants

One of the most striking episodes in late twentieth-century philosophy of mind is that Frank Jackson — the inventor of the knowledge argument — publicly reversed his position. The author of one of the most compelling arguments against physicalism came to endorse physicalism and to explain away the very intuitions that drove his original case. This reversal deserves careful attention, not because it settles anything, but because it illuminates exactly what the knowledge argument requires and where its pressure points are.

Jackson's original position in "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982) was that qualia are real but causally inert. The argument for this followed from two commitments: causal closure of the physical (every physical event has sufficient physical causes), and the reality of phenomenal properties as non-physical. If both are true, phenomenal properties either causally influence physical events — violating closure — or they do not, and we have epiphenomenalism. Jackson initially accepted the second horn. Phenomenal properties accompany neural processes without causally influencing them, like the steam whistle that accompanies but does not drive the locomotive.

What Jackson came to find unbearable about this position was its self-undermining character. If phenomenal properties are causally inert, then our *beliefs* about phenomenal properties are caused by our neural states, not by the phenomenal properties themselves. When I believe "I am in pain," this belief is caused by C-fiber firing, not by the phenomenal character of pain. The belief is not responsive to the phenomenal fact — it tracks the physical co-occurrence, not the phenomenal state. And this has a further consequence that strikes right at the heart of the knowledge argument: if Mary's new belief "this is what red looks like" is caused by her neural response to red light rather than by the phenomenal character of her experience, then she is not learning about phenomenal experience at all. She is gaining a belief caused by a new physical state. The knowledge argument was supposed to show that Mary gains knowledge of phenomenal facts; epiphenomenalism implies she gains no such thing. The argument that was designed to support dualism provides no reason to be a dualist, once we accept the causal closure that dualism requires us to accept.

## The Recantation and Its Critics

Jackson's revised view, articulated in a series of papers in the late 1990s and 2000s, holds that Mary does learn something new but not something that goes beyond the physical. She learns which physical state has the phenomenal character she previously knew about only in physical-functional terms. The new knowledge involves a new concept — a phenomenal concept — for an already-known physical fact. Phenomenal concepts are recognitional concepts that track physical properties by direct acquaintance. When Mary sees red for the first time, she acquires a recognitional concept of the physical state that constitutes red experience, allowing her to think about that state through direct acquaintance rather than through descriptive physical-functional terms. The cognitive novelty is real; the metaphysical novelty is not.

This revised view preserves causal closure without sacrifice: if phenomenal consciousness is physical, then beliefs about phenomenal properties are caused by the phenomenal properties themselves, which are physical, and the loop is closed. The knowledge argument was based not on a genuine insight into non-physical phenomenal facts but on a confusion about the distinctive conceptual character of phenomenal concepts — their independence from physical-functional concepts creates the illusion that their referents are non-physical.

Several reactions to this reversal are philosophically significant. Physicalists welcomed it as evidence that even the most carefully developed anti-physicalist arguments can be met by a sophisticated physicalist response. Critics argued that Jackson had not engaged with the core intuition: even if Mary's new knowledge is knowledge of a physical fact under a new concept, there remains the question of why the physical fact has phenomenal character at all. The phenomenal concepts strategy explains the epistemic situation — why Mary's knowledge seems new — but leaves the metaphysical situation untouched. Why does state S have phenomenal redness? That question does not disappear when we note that Mary has gained a new concept for it.

A more nuanced reaction holds that Jackson's revised view is more continuous with his original position than it appears. Both acknowledge that phenomenal concepts have a distinctive character; the revision concerns what they refer to — physical states rather than non-physical qualia — not how they function. And many philosophers believe the original knowledge argument retains its force even if Jackson himself abandoned it. The argument stands or falls on its own merits, not on its inventor's subsequent commitments. Jackson's recantation illustrates the dialectical pressure that epiphenomenalism exerts on dualist views, and the price any dualist must pay for non-physicalism: the more seriously you take causal closure, the harder it becomes to assign any explanatory role to phenomenal properties — including the role of causing beliefs about themselves.
