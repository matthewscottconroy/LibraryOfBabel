# Phenomenal Character and Qualia

When I am in pain, there is something it is like to be in pain. When I taste coffee, there is something it is like — a specific qualitative feel — that distinguishes that experience from tasting tea or seeing blue. This "what it's like" is what philosophers call phenomenal character, and the intrinsic qualitative properties that constitute it are called qualia. They are, in a phrase, the feels of experience: the redness of red, the bitterness of quinine, the ache of a headache.

Several features of phenomenal character make it philosophically distinctive. It seems to be intrinsic — built into the experience itself, not a matter of how the experience relates to other things. It seems to be directly present to the subject, without mediation by inference; I do not infer that I am in pain from behavioral evidence. It seems resistant to complete verbal description: we can convey which wavelength produces red experience, but not the redness itself. And it is, by definition, conscious — there is no such thing as phenomenal experience one is entirely unaware of. These features — intrinsicness, immediacy, ineffability, and the essential connection to consciousness — have made phenomenal character the central puzzle for any theory of mind.

They have also attracted systematic challenge. Dennett's "Quining Qualia" (1988) argues that the properties attributed to qualia are individually unstable and collectively incoherent. Consider intrinsicness: suppose you discover you have been drinking the same coffee for a year but now perceive it differently. Is the coffee quale the same or has it changed? If qualia are intrinsic properties of experiences, there should be a determinate answer. But our intuitions about the case vary depending on how it is described, which suggests qualia are not the stable intrinsic properties they were supposed to be. Against privacy: the absent qualia argument purports to show that functional duplicates might lack qualia — but this argument proceeds from third-person considerations that should, on the privacy view, be irrelevant to qualia. Against ineffability: if qualia are genuinely ineffable, then introspective reports are not really about the quale but about something functionally associated with it. That undermines the evidential basis for believing in qualia.

Dennett's conclusion is that qualia are confused posits. What we call "qualia" are functional states that play certain cognitive and communicative roles; the appearance of intrinsic, ineffable, private qualities is itself a functional feature — how the system represents its own states.

## The Standard Response and the Qualia Problem for Functionalism

Most philosophers find this conclusion too quick. The standard realist response is that Dennett's examples trade on edge cases and theoretical assumptions that do not disturb the core intuition: there is something it is like to be in pain, and that "what it's like" is not captured by any description of causal roles. To deny this requires a revisionary program so ambitious that it seems to simply change the subject. What needs explaining is not explained away by pointing out that some thought experiments involving qualia are theoretically loaded.

Two thought experiments crystallize what is at stake for theories of mind. Inverted qualia: if it is possible for two people to have the same functional organization but systematically different qualia — your red experience being my green experience — then qualia are not determined by functional organization. They are additional, non-functional properties. Absent qualia: if it is possible for a system to have exactly the right functional organization without any qualia, then functional organization is not sufficient for qualia. Together, these constitute the qualia problem for functionalism. Whatever the functional story, there appears to be a further fact about what experience is like, and this further fact resists functional explanation. Both arguments connect directly to the zombie argument: if zombies are possible, absent qualia are possible; if spectrum inversion is possible, inverted qualia are possible.

A recurring challenge for any non-functional account of qualia is mental causation. If qualia are intrinsic, non-relational properties of experiences, how do they cause anything? Pain hurts, and the hurting causes avoidance behavior — but the hurting is the quale, and if the quale is non-functional, it does not interact with the functional systems that produce avoidance. This is the epiphenomenalism worry: qualia may be causally inert byproducts of neural processes, accompanying the causal flow without contributing to it. Property dualists who accept the reality of non-physical qualia must either accept epiphenomenalism — with all its self-undermining consequences — or find a way to make qualia causally efficacious that is consistent with the causal closure of physics. Neither option is comfortable. That discomfort has pushed many philosophers back toward physicalism, and the debate about qualia is ultimately inseparable from the debate about whether the physical story is complete.
