# Phenomenal Properties

The smell of coffee, the feel of sandpaper, the ache of a headache: each has a distinctive character that makes it the kind of experience it is. Philosophers call these phenomenal properties — the properties that characterize what an experience is like from the inside. The term "qualia" is sometimes used interchangeably, though it carries specific theoretical associations that "phenomenal properties" is meant to be more neutral about.

What distinguishes phenomenal properties from functional ones is precisely their resistance to relational characterization. Functional properties are defined by causal roles — inputs, outputs, and relations to other states. Phenomenal properties seem to have something over and above this: an intrinsic "what it's like" that cannot be fully captured in dispositional or relational terms. Two states might play identical functional roles while differing in phenomenal character — which is exactly what inverted qualia thought experiments are designed to show. The apparent intrinsicness of phenomenal properties is one of the things that makes them philosophically troublesome.

A second distinctive feature is their direct presence to consciousness. Pain is not inferred; it is given in the experience itself. Phenomenal properties seem self-presenting in a way that physical or functional properties are not — they are immediately available to the subject in a manner that does not require any further cognitive work. This immediacy has led some philosophers to describe phenomenal consciousness as involving a kind of intrinsic presentedness: experience carries its own character without mediation.

The ontological status of phenomenal properties is contested along predictable lines. Property dualists hold that they are genuine non-physical properties instantiated by physical systems. Type identity theorists hold that they are identical to neural properties, and that the appearance of irreducibility reflects features of our concepts — specifically, that phenomenal concepts and neural concepts pick out the same property via different routes. Non-reductive physicalists hold that phenomenal properties supervene on physical properties without being identical to them, though this faces Kim's challenge: if phenomenal properties are not physical, how are they causally efficacious given the causal closure of physics? Illusionists such as Keith Frankish (2016) deny that phenomenal properties as traditionally conceived exist at all — the appearance of such properties is a cognitive illusion, and what we actually have are "quasi-phenomenal" functional states that we systematically misrepresent as having phenomenal character. What everyone agrees on is that phenomenal properties present a special explanatory challenge.

## Varieties and the Representationalist Response

Phenomenal properties fall into several broad categories with distinct philosophical profiles. Sensory qualia — the redness of red, the bitterness of quinine, the warmth of sun on skin — are the paradigm cases and have been studied most extensively in the philosophy of perception. Affective qualia — the hedonic valence of pleasure and pain, the unpleasantness of disgust, the warmth of affection — differ from sensory qualia in carrying a positive or negative evaluation built into the experience. The hedonic valence of pain is not merely a neutral qualitative feel but an intrinsically aversive one, and this evaluative dimension raises questions about the relationship between phenomenal character and motivation. Cognitive phenomenology — whether there is something it is like to entertain a thought — is contested: Strawson and Siewert argue for a distinctive phenomenal character attached to cognitive activity itself; others hold that phenomenal character is confined to sensory and affective experience.

Representationalists (Tye, Dretske, Lycan) offer the most developed physicalist account of phenomenal properties. On Tye's PANIC theory, phenomenal properties are representational properties: the redness of red experience is the experience's representing a certain surface reflectance property; the painfulness of pain is the experience's representing tissue damage. There are no irreducibly phenomenal properties over and above representational ones. Phenomenal properties are what representational properties are, when the representation is non-conceptual and poised for the right cognitive roles. This has the advantage of naturalizing phenomenal properties: if they are representational, and representational properties can be given a teleofunctionalist or causal analysis, they can be accommodated within a broadly physicalist framework.

The main objection to representationalism turns precisely on the inverted qualia case. Two experiences can represent the same properties — redness, warmth — while differing phenomenally. If phenomenal character were identical to representational content, inverted qualia would be representationally impossible: two people cannot represent the same property differently while representing it in the same way. The phenomenal residue — what remains when representational content is held fixed — is what representationalism cannot account for. Whether this residue reflects a genuine non-representational feature of experience or a confused aspect of our phenomenal concepts is exactly what is at issue between representationalists and their critics, and it is ultimately the same question that drives the hard problem.
