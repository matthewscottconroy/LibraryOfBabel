# Qualia and Physicalism

The central challenge qualia pose for physicalism is structural, and it is worth stating plainly before considering any particular response. Physical descriptions are, in principle, complete: every fact about matter, energy, and causal interaction is captured in a sufficiently detailed physics. Yet phenomenal facts — facts about what it is like to see red or feel pain — appear not to be entailed by any physical description. We can know everything there is to know about C-fiber firing and still, it seems, not know what pain feels like. We can know everything about 700nm wavelengths and the V4 cortex and still, it seems, not know what red looks like. How can physicalism claim to be complete in the face of this apparent gap?

The most direct response is type identity theory: qualia are identical to physical (neural) properties. Pain just is C-fiber firing; the redness of red experience just is a particular pattern of activity in color-responsive cortical neurons. The appearance of a gap between phenomenal and physical descriptions reflects our use of conceptually independent vocabularies for the same properties — the same situation as "water" and "H₂O," which co-refer while picking out a property under different modes of presentation. The challenge for identity theory is that the connection between phenomenal and physical properties seems to lack the explanatory intelligibility we find in paradigm scientific reductions. We can explain why water is wet, why ice floats, why it dissolves salts — given the molecular structure, these properties are intelligible. We cannot analogously explain why C-fiber firing hurts, why redness looks the way it does. The intelligibility gap persists even after the identity is asserted.

Functionalism substitutes functional properties for neural ones: qualia are defined by causal roles rather than intrinsic physical realizers, which has the advantage of multiple realizability. But functionalism faces the inverted qualia and absent qualia objections — functional organization seems neither necessary nor sufficient for phenomenal character. Non-reductive physicalism attempts to acknowledge the special character of qualia while maintaining that they are not ontologically distinct from physical properties: qualia supervene on physical properties without being identical to them. Kim's challenge hits this position hardest. If phenomenal properties are not physical, they cannot be causally efficacious given causal closure. If they are causally efficacious, they must be physical or must causally overdetermine physical effects in a way that conflicts with parsimony. Non-reductive physicalism is unstable: it collapses into either reductive physicalism or epiphenomenalism.

## The Completeness Argument and the Supervenience Question

Consider a streamlined version of Chalmers's argument. God creates a complete physical duplicate of our world — same atoms, same causal laws, same configurations. Is it a complete duplicate? If phenomenal facts are physical facts, yes — trivially. But Chalmers argues that God could, in addition to creating the physical world, choose to add or subtract consciousness from certain systems. If this is coherent, phenomenal facts are not automatically fixed by physical facts, and physicalism's claim to completeness fails. This is the zombie argument reframed: if a complete physical description does not determine phenomenal facts, physicalism is incomplete.

Defenders of non-reductive physicalism (Block, Fodor, Levine) respond by questioning Kim's assumption that causal closure entails that only physical properties are causally efficacious. Higher-level properties — being fragile, being the president — seem to be causally efficacious even though they are realized by physical properties. If higher-level causation is coherent in general, it may be coherent for phenomenal properties specifically. The debate then shifts to whether phenomenal properties are genuine higher-level properties in the relevant sense or something more exotic that makes the standard story inapplicable.

What physicalism requires — and this is the crux — is not merely that phenomenal properties correlate with physical ones but that they are grounded in, constituted by, or reducible to them. Supervenience alone does not establish this: even a substance dualist could accept psychophysical supervenience if phenomenal properties co-vary perfectly with physical properties without being identical to them. The debate about qualia and physicalism is at bottom a debate about what kind of grounding relation is available, and whether any such relation can be made coherent without presupposing the physical character of the properties being grounded. The hard problem is hard precisely because no functional or physical account seems to close the explanatory gap between objective description and subjective experience, and every attempt to close it by identifying phenomenal with physical properties leaves the intelligibility gap intact.
