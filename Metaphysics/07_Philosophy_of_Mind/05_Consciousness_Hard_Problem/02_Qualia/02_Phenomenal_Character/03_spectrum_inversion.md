# Spectrum Inversion

John Locke raised the possibility in the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689): "Whether the idea that a violet produced in one man's mind by his eyes, were the same that a marigold produced in another man's, and vice versa? This can never be known." Locke was not making a philosophical argument about functionalism — the concept did not exist — but he planted the seed of the modern inverted spectrum thought experiment: the systematic matching between phenomenal qualities and physical properties might differ across individuals in an entirely undetectable way.

The modern version, sharpened by Block and others in the 1970s, is a challenge to functionalism. Imagine Invert: functionally identical to a normal human, making exactly the same color discriminations, calling ripe tomatoes "red," stopping at red lights, agreeing on every color judgment. But Invert's color experiences are systematically inverted. What looks red to you looks green to her, what looks blue looks orange, and so on across the spectrum. Her behavior is indistinguishable from yours because the inversion is systematic — she learned to call the experience she has when looking at tomatoes "red," the same way you did, and her discrimination capacities are identical to yours. The phenomenal character of her experiences, however, is entirely different.

If this scenario is coherent — if there is a possible world in which Invert exists and is behaviorally indistinguishable from Normal — then phenomenal character is not determined by functional organization. The quale of red experience is something over and above the functional role that red-representing states play. Functionalism is false.

## Coherence and Shoemaker's Response

The coherence of the scenario is contested. Color experience has a rich qualitative structure: red is more similar to orange than to blue; red, orange, and yellow feel "warm" while blue, green, and violet feel "cool"; there are unique hues (phenomenologically pure: red, yellow, green, blue) and binary hues (phenomenologically mixed: orange, purple, cyan); red-green and blue-yellow are complementary opponent pairs. Any inversion that leaves Invert behaviorally indistinguishable from Normal must preserve all these structural relations. A simple red-green inversion might work because the two are complementary and play symmetric roles in the color system. A complete spectral inversion is more constrained and may be impossible without introducing behavioral differences.

Sydney Shoemaker developed the most sustained functionalist response in *Identity, Cause, and Mind* (1984) and subsequent papers. If color qualia have intrinsic structural properties — warmth, coolness, similarity relations — and if those structural properties are themselves functional properties (they play roles in guiding attention, emotion, and aesthetic response), then a genuine inversion would introduce functional differences. If red experience is "functionally warm" — if its phenomenal warmth influences behavior in ways that phenomenally cool experiences do not — then Invert, whose red is phenomenally green and therefore functionally cool, would behave differently from Normal in detectable ways. The inversion would be behaviorally visible.

Shoemaker's conclusion: if phenomenal structure is exhausted by functional structure, spectrum inversion is self-undermining. Being behaviorally indistinguishable requires playing the same functional roles; playing the same functional roles requires the same phenomenal structure; so Invert cannot have inverted qualia and be behaviorally indistinguishable.

## The Anti-Functionalist Reply and the Modal Question

Block and Chalmers reply that Shoemaker's argument assumes what needs to be proved. The claim that phenomenal structure is exhausted by functional structure is precisely what the inverted spectrum argument challenges. If qualia have intrinsic phenomenal properties over and above their functional roles — if the warmth of red experience is a phenomenal property, not a functional one — then two qualia can differ intrinsically while playing the same functional role. Invert's red experience is phenomenally green, and therefore phenomenally cool, but this phenomenal coolness does not translate into any functional difference because the functional role of "warmth" is played by whatever experience is normally caused by warm-wavelength light. In Invert's case, that is the phenomenally green experience. Functional role is determined by causal history, not by intrinsic phenomenal character, and the two can come apart.

An important distinction remains. The inverted spectrum scenario might be functionally possible — there is a possible world where functionally identical beings have inverted qualia — while being physically impossible. If physical organization determines phenomenal organization more specifically than functional organization does, then physical duplicates might always be phenomenal duplicates even if functional duplicates need not be. Chalmers accepts this distinction but argues that even physical possibility is not enough for physicalism: physicalism requires that phenomenal facts be logically entailed by physical facts, not merely that physical duplicates happen to be phenomenal duplicates in our world. If there is a metaphysically possible world where the same physical facts obtain but different phenomenal facts hold, physicalism fails. The inverted spectrum argument thus connects to the zombie argument at the deepest level — both are designed to show that phenomenal facts are not logically entailed by either functional or physical facts, and that phenomenal properties are therefore not functional or physical properties.
