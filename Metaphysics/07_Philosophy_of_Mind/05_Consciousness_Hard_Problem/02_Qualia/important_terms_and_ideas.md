# Important Terms and Ideas: Qualia

**Qualia**
The intrinsic, subjective, qualitative features of conscious experience: the redness of red, the painfulness of pain, the specific taste of coffee. Qualia are often characterized as ineffable (not fully expressible), intrinsic (not constituted by relational properties), private (accessible only to their subject), and directly apprehensible.

**Mary's Room (Knowledge Argument)**
Jackson's thought experiment: Mary is a brilliant scientist who knows all physical facts about color vision but has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room. When she leaves and sees red for the first time, does she learn something new? Intuition says yes—she learns what red looks like—suggesting qualia are not physical.

**Ability Hypothesis**
Lewis and Nemirow's response to the knowledge argument: Mary learns new abilities upon seeing red—to recognize, imagine, and remember red experiences—not a new piece of propositional knowledge. There is no irreducible phenomenal property to know; the apparent new knowledge is new practical know-how.

**Inverted Qualia**
The hypothesis that two people could have phenomenally inverted experiences—your "red" experience corresponds to my "green" experience—while being functionally identical. If coherent, this shows that qualia are not determined by functional organization and that there can be qualitative variation without behavioral variation.

**Absent Qualia**
The hypothesis that a system could be functionally identical to a conscious being—processing information, responding appropriately to inputs—while having no phenomenal experience whatsoever. If coherent, this shows that functional organization is not sufficient for consciousness.

**Qualia Eliminativism**
Dennett's view: the standard characterization of qualia (ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible) is incoherent. What we call qualia are actually complex functional/representational states that are neither ineffable nor intrinsic in the problematic sense. There are no qualia as traditionally conceived, only the reality that functionalism describes.
