# Theories of Consciousness

The hard problem has generated a proliferation of theories of consciousness, each attempting to explain how subjective experience arises from physical processes — or, if that is impossible, to explain why it seems to arise. These theories differ in their fundamental commitments, in the phenomena they take as central, and in the standards they invoke for what counts as an adequate explanation.

Integrated Information Theory (IIT), developed by Tononi, holds that consciousness is identical to integrated information — the measure Φ (phi), which quantifies how much information is generated by a system as a whole above and beyond the information generated by its parts. A system is conscious if and only if it has non-zero Φ; the richness of its conscious experience corresponds to its quantity of Φ. IIT has the advantage of making precise, testable predictions about which systems are conscious and to what degree; it has the disadvantage of implying that some simple computational systems (like logic gates arranged in certain ways) are conscious, while some complex systems (like feed-forward neural networks) are not.

Global Workspace Theory holds that consciousness arises from the global broadcasting of information in a neural workspace — a distributed system that makes information available across different functional systems of the brain. Consciousness is the result of information entering the workspace and being broadcast globally; what is not globally broadcast is processed unconsciously. This theory is well-integrated with cognitive neuroscience and has generated productive experimental research.

Predictive Processing theories (Friston, Clark) hold that the brain is fundamentally a prediction machine, and that consciousness arises from the brain's attempt to minimize prediction error. Phenomenal experience reflects the brain's model of the causes of its sensory signals; consciousness is a form of controlled hallucination that represents the brain's best guess about an external world.

This section surveys these and other leading theories of consciousness, assessing their empirical commitments and their philosophical adequacy.
