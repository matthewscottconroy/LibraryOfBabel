# Higher-Order Theories of Consciousness

Start with the distinction that motivates the whole approach. When I am conscious of pain, I am aware that I am in pain. When visual processing guides my hand as I reach for a cup, I need not be aware that this processing is occurring — and when I am not, the processing is unconscious. The awareness seems to make the difference. Higher-order theories (HOT) take this intuition seriously and turn it into a theory: a mental state M is conscious just in case the subject has a higher-order representation of being in M. Consciousness is not an intrinsic property of first-order states but a relational property — it involves being represented by a further mental state.

David Rosenthal is the principal defender of higher-order thought (HOT) theory. On his account, a first-order state (a visual experience, a pain) becomes conscious when accompanied by an appropriate higher-order thought: a non-inferential, assertoric, typically unconscious thought with the content "I am currently in state M." The HOT need not itself be conscious — if it had to be, we would need a higher-order thought about the HOT, and a regress would follow. What is required is simply that the HOT be present. The key prediction follows directly: any state accompanied by an appropriate HOT is conscious; any state not so accompanied is unconscious. This accounts for the distinction between conscious and unconscious processing and provides a functional criterion for consciousness.

Notice the explanatory advantages. HOT theory gives a principled account of why some visual processing occurs without being noticed — peripheral processing, preattentive feature detection — while other processing enters consciousness: the difference lies in whether a higher-order representation is formed. It explains introspective privilege: introspective reports express higher-order thoughts about first-order states. And it has clear naturalistic appeal: if higher-order representations are functional states, consciousness is a functional property, and a complete functional theory is possible.

## Rosenthal's Bold Claim and the Animal Implication

What makes Rosenthal's version distinctive is a further, bolder claim about phenomenal character. Not only is consciousness constituted by the presence of an appropriate HOT — the phenomenal character of a conscious experience is, in part, determined by the HOT's content. If my HOT represents my experience as being of red, then my experience is phenomenally as of red. Phenomenal character is not a brute intrinsic feature of first-order states but a relational property involving higher-order representation. This allows Rosenthal to handle misrepresentation cases: if my HOT represents my pain as located in my left hand when the first-order signal comes from my right hand, my experience is phenomenally as of pain in my left hand. The phenomenal content tracks the HOT's content.

A simplified argument form for the view:

- P1: Unconscious mental states are states the subject is not aware of.
- P2: Conscious mental states are states the subject is aware of.
- P3: Being aware of a mental state is being in a higher-order state that represents it.
- C: Conscious mental states are those accompanied by an appropriate higher-order representation.

The key premise is P3. Critics challenge it by arguing that there is a kind of first-person awareness — phenomenal consciousness — that is not constituted by any higher-order representation, or that is constituted by the first-order state's own self-presenting character rather than a separate higher-order state.

HOT theory has a striking implication for animal and infant consciousness. If consciousness requires higher-order thoughts with propositional content about mental states, only beings with sophisticated cognitive capacities can be conscious. Rosenthal accepts this: most animals are not conscious in the relevant sense. They have mental states, process information, and respond to stimuli, but without HOTs those states are not conscious. Many philosophers find this counterintuitive — mammals and birds display behavioral and physiological markers of pain and suffering, and denying them consciousness seems to conflict with our best neurobiology and our moral practices. Rosenthal's response is that what we owe to animals is not grounded in phenomenal consciousness but in their capacity for aversive functional states that provide reasons for moral concern whether or not consciousness is present.

## Variants and the Hard Problem

Rosenthal's HOT theory has competitors within the higher-order tradition. David Lycan's higher-order perception (HOP) theory requires higher-order inner scanning rather than higher-order thoughts — a form of internal monitoring analogous to perception. Uriah Kriegel's self-representationalism eliminates the separate higher-order state entirely: a conscious state represents both its object and itself. The state is both directed outward at the world and reflexively directed at itself as a self-presenting state. This avoids any regress, since no separate higher-order state is needed. The challenge for self-representationalism is explaining what it means for a state to represent itself, given that representation is typically a relation between distinct entities.

The most persistent challenge for all higher-order theories is that they appear to explain access consciousness — why certain states are available for reasoning, report, and behavioral control — without explaining phenomenal consciousness — why any state has qualitative character at all. A zombie HOT — a system with functional higher-order representations but no phenomenal experience — seems conceivable. If it is, HOT theory fails to explain what the hard problem is actually about. Rosenthal's response is direct: there is no coherent notion of phenomenal consciousness over and above access consciousness. "Phenomenal consciousness" as anti-physicalists conceive it is confused. What there is, is the access consciousness that HOT theory explains, and that is all consciousness is. This response essentially adopts a type-A physicalist position on the hard problem — the zombie intuition reflects a conceptual confusion, not a genuine modal insight. Whether this response is satisfying or merely reasserts what needs to be proved is a central dispute in the debate about higher-order theories.
