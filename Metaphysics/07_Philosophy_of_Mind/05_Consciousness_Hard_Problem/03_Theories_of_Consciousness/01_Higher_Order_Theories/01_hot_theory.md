# Higher-Order Thought Theory

The intuition driving higher-order thought theory is simple and compelling. Unconscious mental states — the visual processing that enables you to navigate a room without attention, the grammatical knowledge that shapes your speech — occur without you being aware of them. Conscious mental states are different: when you are conscious of pain, you are aware that you are in pain. What makes the difference? Higher-order thought (HOT) theory answers: a mental state is conscious just in case the subject has a suitable higher-order thought representing that first-order state.

David Rosenthal has developed this view across several decades, most fully in *Consciousness and Mind* (2005). On his account, a sensory state is phenomenally conscious if and only if there is a HOT — typically a background, non-conscious, non-inferential, assertoric thought — whose content is that one is currently in that sensory state. The HOT need not be in the focal spotlight of attention; it need not itself be conscious. It simply has to be present. Any state so accompanied is conscious; any state not so accompanied is unconscious. The theory combines a definitional claim with an explanatory one: phenomenal character is, in part, constituted by the HOT's representational content, not merely by the intrinsic properties of the first-order state.

That last point is the bold part. Consider the following argument:

- P1: Unconscious mental states are states the subject is not aware of.
- P2: Conscious mental states are states the subject is aware of.
- P3: Being aware of a mental state is being in a higher-order state that represents it.
- C: Conscious mental states are those accompanied by an appropriate higher-order representation.

Critics target P3: there might be a kind of first-person awareness — phenomenal consciousness — that is not constituted by any higher-order representation. The pain might present itself directly, as an intrinsic property of the experience, rather than being made conscious by a further mental state directed at it. HOT theory treats phenomenal character as relational; critics treat it as intrinsic.

## Misrepresentation, Animals, and Neuroscience

Rosenthal's claim that phenomenal character is partly determined by the HOT's content leads to interesting consequences. If my HOT misrepresents the location of my pain — representing it as in my left hand when the first-order signal comes from my right — my experience is phenomenally as of pain in my left hand. The phenomenal content tracks the higher-order representation, not the first-order state's actual properties. This is a strength of the theory in explaining phantom limb phenomena and certain pain disorders, but it strikes some as counterintuitive: shouldn't the phenomenal character of pain be determined by what is actually happening in the nervous system, not by what a higher-order state represents?

A distinctive and somewhat counterintuitive implication concerns animal and infant consciousness. If consciousness requires higher-order thoughts with propositional content about mental states, then beings lacking the cognitive resources for such thoughts — fish, birds, many mammals, human infants — are not conscious in the relevant sense. Rosenthal accepts this. He holds that most animals have mental states and process information, but without accompanying HOTs those states are not conscious. The grounds for moral concern about animals are then reframed: we need not appeal to their phenomenal consciousness but to their capacity for aversive functional states.

HOT theory has connections to cognitive neuroscience that are at least suggestive. Damage to prefrontal cortex, associated with higher-order representations and executive control, often impairs conscious access and reportability. Phenomena like blindsight — where patients respond to visual stimuli they report not seeing — suggest that first-order visual processing can occur without higher-order awareness. But these findings underdetermine the theory: Global Workspace Theory, which identifies consciousness with global information broadcast rather than higher-order representation, is equally consistent with many of the same data. Whether the neural mechanisms of consciousness specifically implement HOTs or global broadcast or some other architecture remains an open empirical question, and the philosophical debate turns on considerations that neuroscience alone cannot settle.

The deepest challenge is that HOT theory appears to explain access consciousness — why certain states are available for reasoning, report, and behavioral control — while leaving phenomenal consciousness unaddressed. A zombie HOT — a system with functional higher-order representations but no phenomenal experience — seems conceivable. If it is, the theory does not touch the hard problem. Rosenthal's response is to deny that phenomenal consciousness as anti-physicalists conceive it is a coherent notion: what we call phenomenal consciousness just is the access consciousness that HOT theory explains. That response, however, either begs the question against the hard problem intuition or collapses into a form of illusionism about phenomenal properties — which is a coherent position but one that needs to be distinguished from the claim that HOT theory solves the problem.
