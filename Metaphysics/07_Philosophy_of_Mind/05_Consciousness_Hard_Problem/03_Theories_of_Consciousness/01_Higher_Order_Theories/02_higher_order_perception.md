# Higher-Order Perception Theories

Rosenthal's HOT theory grounds consciousness in a higher-order *thought* — a propositional attitude with the content "I am currently in state M." But thoughts are lean, conceptual, and presumably rather thin compared to the phenomenal richness of conscious experience. This observation motivates higher-order perception (HOP) theories, which replace the cognitive higher-order state with a perceptual one. Where HOT theory requires a thought about the first-order state, HOP theory requires an inner perceptual representation of it. The claim is that consciousness involves inner sense — a quasi-sensory scanning of one's own mental states — rather than inner thought.

David Armstrong's version of the view emerges from his broadly functionalist philosophy of mind in *A Materialist Theory of the Mind* (1968). Armstrong identified consciousness with a second-order scan of first-order mental states: an internal perception by which the mind represents its own current states. Perceptual consciousness, for Armstrong, is primarily a capacity for discriminative responding. Full consciousness adds a second-order element — the capacity to respond to one's own mental states, to know what one is currently perceiving, thinking, or feeling. This second-order capacity is what distinguishes a conscious mind from a mere thermostat, and it is itself a perfectly naturalistic faculty that evolved to enable more sophisticated behavioral control. No mysterious additional ingredient is required.

William Lycan developed Armstrong's approach into a full-scale theory in *Consciousness* (1987) and *Consciousness and Experience* (1996). Lycan's inner sense theory posits a dedicated faculty of inner perception, analogous to outer perception in structure though directed inward. The inner sense scans first-order mental states and produces higher-order representations of them. Crucially, Lycan allows that inner sense representations are phenomenally rich: they are not mere propositional thoughts but quasi-perceptual representations that can represent the qualitative character of first-order states in fine-grained, non-conceptual ways. This gives HOP theory a potential advantage over HOT theory in accounting for the phenomenal richness of experience — experience often seems to outrun our ability to describe it, and quasi-perceptual representations might capture what conceptual reports cannot.

## Comparison with HOT Theory and the Regress Problem

The main difference between HOP and HOT theories concerns the character of the higher-order state. On HOT theories, the higher-order state is a thought with propositional content, conceptual and cognitive in character. On HOP theories, it is an inner perception — a quasi-sensory representation, potentially more fine-grained than any concept the subject can articulate. This difference matters for the explanation of phenomenal character: HOT theories struggle to explain why merely thinking about a first-order state makes it phenomenally conscious. HOP theories can appeal to the phenomenal richness of the inner sense representation itself — an inner perception of red experience might carry some of the qualitative character that a bare thought "I am having a red experience" does not.

But HOP theories face a regress concern. If inner sense representations are themselves phenomenally rich, they must be conscious. What makes them conscious? Another inner sense? This threatens an infinite regress unless inner sense representations are allowed to be directly conscious without higher-order representation — but then we need to explain what makes first-order direct consciousness possible, and HOP theory provides no answer for this case. Kriegel's self-representationalism, mentioned in the discussion of HOT theory, is in part a response to this regress: a conscious state represents both its object and itself, eliminating the need for a separate higher-order state and the regress it generates.

Both HOT and HOP theories are broadly consistent with evidence from cognitive neuroscience about the role of prefrontal cortex in conscious access and introspection. Lesions to prefrontal areas often impair metacognitive abilities while leaving first-order processing intact — as in some cases of blindsight and neglect — which fits the picture of consciousness requiring higher-order monitoring. But the theories face an empirical challenge from evidence suggesting that consciousness sometimes occurs without much prefrontal involvement: during dreaming, certain meditative states, and some cases of perceptual experience, phenomenal consciousness seems to occur without elaborate cognitive monitoring. Whether these cases constitute genuine counterexamples depends on fine-grained details of the theories and the interpretation of neuroimaging data — which is itself underdetermined between higher-order theories and competitors like Global Workspace Theory. What the comparison with HOP theory ultimately reveals is that the choice of higher-order *thought* versus higher-order *perception* is less decisive than the underlying question shared by all higher-order theories: why does any higher-order representation — conceptual or perceptual — suffice to generate phenomenal consciousness?
