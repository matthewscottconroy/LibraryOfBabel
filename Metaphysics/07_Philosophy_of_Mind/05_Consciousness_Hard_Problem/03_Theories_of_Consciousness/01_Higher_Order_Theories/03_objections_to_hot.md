# Objections to Higher-Order Theories

Higher-order theories face a cluster of objections that probe whether the higher-order structure is either sufficient or necessary for phenomenal consciousness. Working through them carefully reveals not just weaknesses in the theories but the structure of the problem they are trying to solve.

Begin with the misrepresentation problem. If a first-order state becomes conscious by being represented by a HOT, then a HOT that misrepresents a first-order state — or represents a state that does not exist — would generate a "consciousness" with incorrect or absent first-order content. Rosenthal accepts this consequence: one could be conscious of experiencing red even if no first-order color-representing state is active, because the HOT misrepresents what is occurring. Critics find this counterintuitive. If I am conscious of being in pain, there must actually be pain there. HOT theory allows for consciousness that floats free of the first-order states it is supposed to represent. Rosenthal's response is that misrepresentation cases are pathological and do not undermine the general theory — but if the HOT determines consciousness independently of the first-order state, consciousness is free-floating in a way that needs explanation beyond "these cases are unusual."

The animal and infant consciousness problem presses differently. Sophisticated HOTs plausibly require significant conceptual and cognitive machinery — the capacity to represent oneself as currently in a mental state, with propositional content. Infants and many animals lack the resources for such representations. Yet they appear to have phenomenally conscious experiences. Behavioral and neurological evidence suggests that vertebrates have rich phenomenal consciousness; the denial of consciousness to dogs and cats seems more theoretically motivated than empirically supported. Rosenthal accepts the implication and reframes the moral question: what we owe to animals is grounded in their capacity for aversive functional states, not in phenomenal consciousness. Many philosophers find this response both empirically questionable and morally troubling.

## The Core Insufficiency and the Phenomenal Character Problem

The most fundamental objection is insufficiency. Even granting that a first-order state is accompanied by an appropriate HOT, why does that make it phenomenally conscious? A system could have a functional first-order state and a functional HOT representing it — a "zombie HOT" — without there being anything it is like to be in that state. HOT theory identifies consciousness with a functional property and thereby seems to conflate access consciousness (the availability of information for reasoning and report) with phenomenal consciousness (the qualitative "what it's like"). Higher-order representation explains why the state is cognitively available and reportable. It does not explain why it feels like anything.

There is a related but distinct problem about phenomenal character specifically. The phenomenal character of pain — its specific hurting quality — seems to be a feature of the first-order pain state, not of the HOT that represents it. If the HOT represented a different state (a tactile sensation), the phenomenal character would be different, not because the HOT changed, but because the first-order state did. So phenomenal character is determined by the first-order state. But then HOTs explain which states are conscious without explaining what gives each conscious state its specific qualitative character — and first-order states seem to have that character independently of HOTs. Rosenthal's response is to distinguish: the HOT determines *that* there is a conscious state, while the first-order state determines *which* conscious state it is. Critics find this response incomplete, since it seems to require that first-order states have phenomenal character independently of HOTs — and then we need an account of what gives first-order states their phenomenal character, which HOT theory does not provide.

The bootstrapping problem adds a regress concern. If a state is conscious only when represented by a HOT, what makes the HOT itself conscious, when it is? A third-order thought? Rosenthal responds that HOTs are typically non-conscious — background thoughts that do not themselves require higher-order representations. But many find it unsatisfying to say that consciousness is constituted by a non-conscious state. Kriegel's self-representationalism is designed precisely to avoid this: a conscious state represents both its object and itself, collapsing the first-order/higher-order distinction. Critics question whether genuine self-representation is coherent, given that representation is typically a relation between distinct entities.

Despite these objections, HOT theory has been genuinely productive. It clarified the distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness in ways that have shaped the field. It generated testable predictions about the neural correlates of consciousness. And it motivated the development of alternative theories — self-representationalism, first-order representationalism, Global Workspace Theory, Integrated Information Theory — that attempt to preserve what is right about the higher-order insight while addressing what it leaves unexplained. The objections to HOT theory do not refute the broad higher-order approach; they specify what any adequate theory of consciousness needs to do that higher-order representation alone cannot accomplish.
