# Baars and Global Workspace Theory

Consider what it means for information to be conscious. You are reading this sentence; below the threshold of attention, your visual system is tracking the position of the page, your motor system is preparing minor postural adjustments, your auditory system is processing ambient sound. All of that processing occurs, and most of it influences your behavior, but you are not conscious of it. What distinguishes the processing you are conscious of from the processing you are not?

Bernard Baars answered this question in *A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness* (1988) with the central metaphor of a global workspace — a kind of cognitive bulletin board. Most processing is modular and unconscious: specialized processors handle perception, language, motor control, and memory in parallel, without direct communication. A state becomes conscious when information "enters" the workspace — when it is broadcast widely across the specialized modules, becoming available for integration, flexible control, and verbal report. Consciousness is not a property of particular brain regions or processing levels; it is a property of access. Information is conscious if and only if it is broadcast to the workspace, making it simultaneously available to all downstream processors.

This functional identification has considerable explanatory power. The limited capacity of attention follows from the workspace being a limited resource — only one stream of information can win the competition for global broadcast at any moment. The speed advantage of unconscious processing follows from modules not bearing the overhead of global broadcast. Inattentional blindness — failing to see an object in plain sight when attention is directed elsewhere — is explained by that object's failure to achieve workspace access. Priming and implicit memory effects reflect information that failed workspace access but was processed locally in specialized modules. The theory explains a range of conscious/unconscious distinctions in a unified framework, and it does so with the resources of cognitive architecture rather than any special phenomenal vocabulary.

## Empirical Traction and the Philosophical Limit

GWT gained significant empirical traction when Stanislas Dehaene and colleagues developed its neural implementation in Global Neuronal Workspace (GNW) theory. Long-range cortical neurons in prefrontal, parietal, and cingulate cortices form a distributed network capable of broadcasting local activity. When a stimulus achieves sufficient activation, these workspace neurons "ignite" — local sensory activity transitions to a sustained burst of long-range synchronized firing across frontal and parietal areas. The temporal signature is characteristic: conscious stimuli produce early (~100ms) local activity in sensory cortex — equivalent to unconscious processing — followed by a late (~300ms) sustained frontoparietal burst that is absent for masked or subliminal stimuli. The transition from unconscious to conscious processing has a non-linear, threshold-like, winner-take-all character, reflecting the competitive dynamics of the workspace.

This neural signature has been confirmed across multiple methodologies: fMRI imaging, EEG P3b components, masking paradigms, binocular rivalry, and disorders of consciousness research. Vegetative-state patients retain intact early sensory responses but show absent late frontoparietal activation — consistent with the claim that consciousness requires workspace ignition. The GNW theory thus bridges cognitive architecture and neuroscience in a way that few theories of consciousness have managed.

The philosophical limit, however, is precisely where GWT's explanatory ambitions come to rest. Chalmers accepts GWT as an account of *access consciousness* — why certain states are available for reasoning, report, and behavioral control — but argues it says nothing about *phenomenal consciousness*: why globally broadcast information feels like anything at all. One can conceive of a system with a fully functional global workspace — broadcasting information globally, integrating it across modules, reporting on it accurately — without there being any phenomenal experience. A "workspace zombie" is conceivable. If it is, global availability is logically compatible with the absence of experience, and GWT explains access without touching the hard problem.

Baars and Dehaene respond that the rich integration and self-referential character of globally broadcast information just *is* phenomenal experience — that experience arises when information becomes globally available, and the character of experience is determined by which information is broadcast and how. This response effectively identifies phenomenal consciousness with access consciousness, which is a substantive and contestable commitment. Block's access/phenomenal distinction is directly implicated: to identify the two is not an empirical discovery but a philosophical choice, and it commits GWT to the view that inverted and absent qualia are impossible — any functional duplicate of a conscious system is automatically conscious in the phenomenal sense. Whether these implications are acceptable depends on prior views about phenomenal consciousness that GWT itself cannot settle.
