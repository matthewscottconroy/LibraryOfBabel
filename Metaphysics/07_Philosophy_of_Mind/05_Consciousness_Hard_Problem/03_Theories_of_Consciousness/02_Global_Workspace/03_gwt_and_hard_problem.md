# Global Workspace Theory and the Hard Problem

GWT and GNW are primarily designed to explain the "easy problems" of consciousness: why conscious states are reportable, accessible to reasoning, and capable of flexible integration with other information. The central philosophical question is whether anything in the theory addresses the hard problem — why there is something it is like to be in a globally broadcast state.

Baars and Dehaene believe their theories do address phenomenal consciousness as well as access consciousness. Dehaene's argument runs as follows: when information achieves global broadcast, it becomes available to all cognitive processors simultaneously. This integration is the hallmark of conscious experience — we do not experience isolated sensory fragments but a unified perceptual world in which different sensory modalities, emotional valences, memories, and conceptual associations are simultaneously available. The rich integration is not merely correlated with experience; it just is what phenomenal experience is. On this view, the workspace not only explains accessibility but is sufficient for phenomenal character: the character of experience is determined by which information is broadcast and how it is integrated.

Notice what this response commits GWT to. If phenomenal consciousness just is access consciousness — if the "what it's like" just is the global availability — then GWT implies that inverted qualia are impossible: two states with identical access-consciousness profiles have identical phenomenal character. It implies that absent qualia are impossible: any functional duplicate of a conscious system is automatically conscious in the phenomenal sense. And it implies that any system implementing the global workspace architecture — including silicon systems with the right connectivity — is conscious. Whether these implications are acceptable depends on prior views about phenomenal consciousness that GWT itself cannot settle.

## Chalmers's Challenge and Block's Distinction

Chalmers accepts GWT as an account of access consciousness but argues it leaves the hard problem untouched. Access consciousness is the property of a mental state being available for use in reasoning, report, and behavior. Phenomenal consciousness is the property of having a qualitative "what it's like." GWT explains the former in terms of global broadcast. Whether the latter requires more than global broadcast is precisely what is at issue.

The key argument is modal. One can conceive of a system with a fully functional global workspace — broadcasting information globally, integrating it across modules, generating accurate verbal reports — without there being any phenomenal experience. This "workspace zombie" is, Chalmers claims, conceivable in the relevant sense. If it is conceivable, global availability is logically compatible with the absence of experience. Therefore GWT, whatever its explanatory power, does not explain why global broadcast is accompanied by experience.

Ned Block's access/phenomenal distinction captures the same point. GWT explains access consciousness: it gives principled conditions under which a state is cognitively available, reportable, and behaviorally influential. The proposal that phenomenal consciousness just is access consciousness is a substantive identification, not an obvious truth. Block argues that conflating them generates an illusion of explanation: we explain the functional dimension and claim to have explained phenomenal consciousness, when in fact we have only explained what GWT was already designed to explain. The phenomenal dimension — the qualitative character — is not entailed by the functional story no matter how detailed that story becomes.

## The Functionalist Commitment and the Broader Debate

From the perspective of hard problem realists, GWT makes important progress on the easy problems while leaving the hard problem entirely untouched. This is not a criticism of GWT as an empirical theory of consciousness; it is a clarification of what kind of theory it is. GWT explains the distribution of consciousness across cognitive states — which states are conscious and which are not, and what neural mechanisms implement this distribution. It does not explain why the relevant mechanisms are accompanied by experience rather than occurring "in the dark."

GWT thus stands as a major empirical-theoretical framework philosophically committed to a functionalist resolution of the hard problem. The functionalist commitment is not incidental — it is built into the identification of consciousness with global broadcast. Whether this resolution is adequate, whether the hard problem dissolves when we recognize that consciousness just is access consciousness, or whether the hard problem survives any such functional identification, is the central fault line between GWT and its hard-problem-realist critics. That fault line is not where the neuroimaging data runs — it is where the philosophical analysis runs. And on that question, GWT provides the occasion for a decision rather than making the decision itself.
