# Integrated Information Theory

What makes one physical system conscious and another not? Giulio Tononi's answer, developed across a series of papers and now in its fourth major formulation (IIT 4.0), is that consciousness is identical to integrated information — measured by the quantity Φ (phi). A system is conscious to the degree that it instantiates integrated information: information that is generated by the whole system above and beyond what is generated by its parts. Consciousness is not binary but graded; it is not a property of particular brain regions but a structural property of causal organization; and it is not limited in principle to biological systems, since any system — biological, silicon, or otherwise — that instantiates the right pattern of information integration has some degree of consciousness.

Two intuitions motivate the theory. First, informativeness: being in a particular conscious state is highly informative — it rules out an enormous number of alternative states. Experiencing a blue circle on a red background is highly specific; it is not a green square, not a pure tone, not a pain. Conscious states individuate experience very finely. Second, integration: conscious experience is unified; it cannot be decomposed into independent parts. You do not experience the visual field and the auditory field separately; they are integrated into a single perceptual whole. These two features — high information and strong integration — are jointly captured by Φ, which measures the information generated by a system that cannot be attributed to any partition of the system into independent parts.

The mathematical formulation proceeds by examining all possible bipartitions of a system, finding the bipartition that minimizes information loss (the "minimum information partition"), and measuring the amount of information generated by the whole above and beyond the two sides of this partition. High Φ means the system's causal power is irreducible — it cannot be factored into independent parts without significant information loss. IIT also specifies the *content* of consciousness: the specific pattern of integrated information — the "conceptual structure" or "qualia space" — corresponds to the specific phenomenal character of the experience. Two systems with identical Φ values but different integration patterns would have different conscious experiences.

## Distinctive Predictions and Philosophical Status

IIT generates predictions that sharply distinguish it from other theories. Purely feedforward systems — deep learning networks that process information in one direction only — have zero Φ and are therefore not conscious despite their sophisticated processing. The cerebellum, although it has more neurons than the cortex, has a modular architecture that limits integration, giving it low Φ; the cortex, highly integrated, contributes much more. Even simple physical systems — photodiodes, logic gates — may have some nonzero Φ and thus some minimal degree of consciousness. This gives IIT panpsychist implications. And the theory predicts that a complex but modular system like the internet, or a large feedforward neural network, may have low or zero Φ — complexity per se does not guarantee consciousness; integration does.

What makes IIT philosophically distinctive is its aspiration to address the hard problem rather than merely circumvent it. Most theories of consciousness — Global Workspace Theory, HOT theories, representationalism — explain which states are conscious (access consciousness) without explaining why those states feel like anything. IIT attempts an identity: consciousness is identical to integrated information. The axioms of IIT — existence, composition, information, integration, exclusion — are held to be self-evident from the first-person perspective, and the mathematical formalism is derived from these axioms. The claim is that phenomenal properties are identical to intrinsic physical properties (integrated information) rather than relational or functional ones.

Whether this constitutes a genuine solution to the hard problem or a sophisticated redescription is contested. The identity between Φ and consciousness is claimed to be as direct as the identity between heat and mean kinetic energy — not a mere correlation but an identity grounded in first principles. Critics argue that the correspondence between phi-space and qualia-space is assumed by the design of the formalism rather than derived from it, and that a system could have high Φ with no phenomenal experience — the zombie objection applied to phi. Tononi's response is that Φ is an intrinsic property of a system's actual causal powers, not merely a functional or relational property, and that the identity is therefore not vulnerable to zombie-style conceivability arguments in the way that functional identities are. Whether this response succeeds depends on whether integrated information is genuinely intrinsic in the relevant sense — which is itself a contested question about the nature of causation and physical properties.
