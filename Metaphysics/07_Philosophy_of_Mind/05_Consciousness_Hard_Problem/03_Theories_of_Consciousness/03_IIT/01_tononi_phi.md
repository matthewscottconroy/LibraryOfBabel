# Tononi and Integrated Information Theory

What makes consciousness scientifically tractable, if it is tractable at all, is the existence of systematic relationships between the structure of experience and the structure of the underlying physical system. Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory (IIT), developed beginning in the 2000s, pursues this thought more rigorously than any prior theory. Its central concept, phi (Φ), is a measure of integrated information: the extent to which a system's causal power as a whole exceeds the sum of the causal powers of its parts.

Phi is computed as follows. Consider all possible bipartitions of a system — all ways of dividing it into two parts. For each bipartition, measure the information "lost" by treating the two parts as independent — that is, the information generated by the whole that cannot be attributed to the parts separately. Phi is the information lost at the minimum information partition (MIP): the bipartition that loses the least information, and therefore the bipartition where the system is "most nearly" decomposable. A system with high phi cannot be decomposed into independent subsystems without significant information loss — its parts work together in an irreducible way. IIT's core claim is that consciousness is identical to integrated information: a system is conscious to the degree that its phi is nonzero, and the character of its experience is determined by the specific pattern of integrated information it instantiates.

Notice what this makes IIT: not standard functionalism, but something closer to a structural identity theory. Standard functionalism identifies consciousness with input-output relations or causal roles — external relations between the system and its environment. IIT identifies consciousness with the system's *intrinsic* causal powers — what it does to itself, independent of external relations. A system could have the same input-output relations as a conscious system but low phi if it achieves those relations via a modular architecture, and on IIT it would not be conscious. This makes IIT's functionalism distinctively "inside-out": it is about intrinsic organization, not extrinsic function.

## Phenomenological Grounding and Specific Predictions

Tononi grounds IIT in phenomenological axioms — introspectively self-evident properties of every conscious experience. Existence: experience exists (the Cartesian starting point). Composition: experience has multiple aspects that can be differentiated. Information: each experience is specific and highly informative, excluding a vast number of alternatives. Integration: experience is unified, not decomposable into truly independent parts. Exclusion: experience has a definite grain — a definite spatiotemporal resolution, not more and not less.

From these axioms, IIT derives corresponding physical postulates. The integration axiom yields the postulate of intrinsic causal power: any substrate of consciousness must have irreducible causal power, measured by phi. The information axiom yields the postulate of high specificity in causal powers. The exclusion axiom yields the postulate that only the maximum-phi system at a particular grain is conscious — which generates the controversial "exclusion problem" discussed in the criticisms file.

The theory makes several empirically distinctive predictions. Purely feedforward systems have zero phi and are therefore not conscious — large language models with predominantly feedforward architectures are not conscious on this view. The cerebellum has low phi despite its large number of neurons because its architecture is modular, not integrated; clinical evidence supports this (cerebellar damage does not typically impair consciousness). Simple physical systems — photodiodes, basic logic gates — have nonzero phi and thus some minimal degree of consciousness, giving IIT panpsychist implications. And complex but modular systems like the internet or large feedforward neural networks may have very low phi despite vast complexity.

IIT is philosophically distinctive for attempting to explain the hard problem by grounding consciousness in an intrinsic property of physical systems rather than a relational or functional one. The ambition is that the identity between phi and consciousness explains not just *that* a system is conscious but *why* it has the specific phenomenal character it does: the structure of phi-space mirrors the structure of qualia-space, and phenomenal description is just physical description of the same structure from the inside. Whether this bridging of the explanatory gap is genuine or merely relocates the mystery in more elaborate mathematical language is the central philosophical question about IIT.
