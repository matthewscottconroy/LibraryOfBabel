# IIT and Phenomenology

One of the distinctive and ambitious features of Integrated Information Theory is its attempt to derive the structure of conscious experience from first principles — from phenomenological axioms that are held to be self-evident from the first-person perspective. Tononi and his collaborators argue that IIT does not merely identify a neural correlate of consciousness; it explains *why* experience has the specific character it does.

The approach begins with five axioms. Existence: conscious experience exists — this is the Cartesian starting point, the one thing that cannot be coherently doubted. Composition: experience is compositional, having multiple aspects that can be differentiated — a visual experience contains colors, shapes, locations, and movements as distinct though integrated elements. Information: each experience is specific and highly informative, excluding a vast number of other possible experiences — experiencing a blue circle on a red background is not experiencing a green square, a pure tone, or a pain. Integration: experience is unified, not decomposable into truly independent parts — we do not experience a separate color dimension and a separate shape dimension but a single integrated perceptual whole. Exclusion: experience has a definite grain — a definite set of distinctions, a definite spatiotemporal resolution, not more and not less.

From these phenomenological axioms, IIT derives corresponding physical postulates — conditions any physical substrate supporting consciousness must satisfy. The integration axiom yields the postulate of intrinsic causal power: the substrate must have irreducible causal influence that cannot be fully decomposed into the influences of its parts, measured by phi. The information axiom yields high specificity in causal powers: the system must make many distinct causal contributions. The exclusion axiom yields the postulate that consciousness is realized at a specific grain — only the maximum-phi system at the appropriate spatiotemporal scale is conscious. The derivation from phenomenological axioms to physical postulates is IIT's core methodological claim: it begins where the hard problem says we must begin, with the structure of experience itself, and then asks what physical structure must correspond to it.

## Qualia Space and the Bridging Claim

The theory then makes the bold claim that the specific mathematical structure of a system's integrated information — its "conceptual structure" or "qualia space" — corresponds to the specific phenomenal character of its experience. If experience is always structured (red is more like orange than like blue; pain differs from pleasure along multiple dimensions), then the structure of experience should be readable from the structure of phi. This means IIT aspires to be a theory of the *content* of consciousness, not just its presence: given a complete description of a system's integrated information structure, one could in principle determine what the system is experiencing. The redness of red would correspond to a specific region of qualia space; the ache of a headache would correspond to another.

This is philosophically ambitious. Most theories of consciousness explain which states are conscious — why certain states are available for report or behavioral control — without explaining why those states have the specific qualitative character they have. IIT attempts to bridge the explanatory gap by showing that the mathematical structure of phi-space mirrors the structure of phenomenal experience. The gap between physical and phenomenal description is bridged by identifying the two: phenomenal structure just is the structure of integrated information, described from the inside rather than from the outside.

Whether this bridging is genuine is contested. Critics argue that the correspondence between phi-space and qualia-space is not derived from the axioms but assumed by the design of the formalism: the mathematical framework is constructed to match the phenomenological structure, which makes the "derivation" circular. The phenomenological axioms are selected because they characterize obvious features of experience — it is informative, integrated, specific — and the mathematics is built to mirror these features. Whether this constitutes illuminating explanation or sophisticated redescription of the same facts in a different vocabulary is a fundamental philosophical question about IIT. If the answer is that it is redescription, then IIT leaves the hard problem exactly where it found it, despite the formidable mathematical apparatus. If the answer is that it is genuine explanation, then IIT has accomplished something no previous theory of consciousness has: it has not only identified but *explained* why certain physical structures have the phenomenal properties they have, by showing that phenomenal properties and physical properties of integrated information are not two things but one.
