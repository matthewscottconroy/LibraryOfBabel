# Criticisms of IIT

IIT has attracted both enthusiastic support and sharp criticism from multiple directions — philosophical, mathematical, and empirical. The criticisms illuminate not just weaknesses in IIT but what it would take for any mathematical theory of consciousness to succeed.

Begin with computational intractability. Computing phi exactly for even modest systems requires examining all possible bipartitions — an exponentially hard problem. For a system with billions of neurons, exact computation is not merely difficult but infeasible in any practical sense. This limits IIT's empirical testability and raises questions about whether phi is a biologically meaningful quantity or a mathematical abstraction without operational grip on real systems. Approximations have been developed — including the perturbational complexity index (PCI), used clinically to assess disorders of consciousness — but these are acknowledged approximations that may not track the theoretically defined phi. The gap between the theoretical ideal and the measurable proxy creates uncertainty about what empirical results with IIT-based measures actually demonstrate.

The exclusion axiom generates a distinct problem. IIT holds that only the maximum-phi system at the appropriate grain is conscious — if you are a subsystem inside a larger system with higher phi, you are not separately conscious. This implies that any brain region inside a conscious person would not be separately conscious, even if it has high phi by itself. Where exactly does one conscious system end and another begin? The exclusion axiom is meant to address this by identifying the unique maximum-phi system, but determining where boundaries fall is non-trivial and generates counterintuitive verdicts in cases involving nested or overlapping systems.

## The Grid Problem, Panpsychism, and the Zombie Objection

Scott Aaronson raised what became known as the grid problem: a simple grid of XOR logic gates can be arranged to have arbitrarily high phi — potentially higher phi than any human brain. IIT would then attribute to this grid richer consciousness than any conscious being we know of. Aaronson argued this is a reductio ad absurdum. Tononi and collaborators have revised the formulation of phi in later versions of IIT (now at IIT 4.0) to address this specific objection. Critics argue that the revisions amount to post-hoc adjustments motivated by avoiding counterintuitive verdicts rather than by independent theoretical considerations — a troubling methodology if the theory is meant to be derived from phenomenological axioms rather than adjusted to match our intuitions.

IIT implies that many simple physical systems have some degree of consciousness. Even a photodiode has some minimal nonzero phi. Critics find this implausible and argue that consciousness is a specific, rare phenomenon associated with biological brains, not a universal feature of all physical systems. IIT proponents respond that this objection presupposes a folk psychological concept of consciousness that may be inappropriate for fundamental theorizing. If phi is the right measure, the panpsychist implication follows necessarily, and our folk intuitions about the distribution of consciousness may simply be wrong. Whether this response is satisfying depends on whether one thinks the folk intuitions are tracking something real or merely a cognitive parochialism about biological minds.

The most fundamental philosophical challenge is a version of the absent qualia objection. Two systems could have identical phi yet differ phenomenally — or one could have high phi with no phenomenal character. Phi is a structural property of a physical system, and one can conceive of systems with high phi but no experience. Tononi's response is that phi is not merely a functional or relational property but an intrinsic property: it characterizes the system's actual causal powers, not just its input-output relations, and the identity between phi and consciousness is as direct and non-conventional as the identity between temperature and mean kinetic energy. Whether this deflects the zombie objection or merely restates the hard problem at the level of phi is disputed. The zombie argument is designed to show that any physical or functional property — including intrinsic causal power properties — is conceivably present without consciousness. If that intuition survives reflection, phi does not escape it.

Despite these criticisms, IIT continues to be a productive research program. It generates precise empirical predictions, stimulates rigorous debate about the measurement and explanation of consciousness, has influenced clinical practice through phi-based measures for disorders of consciousness (PCI, zap-and-zip protocols), and makes sharp empirical predictions that distinguish it from competitors — particularly GWT, with which it conflicts over whether the cerebellum is conscious and over whether feedforward systems contribute to experience. The debate over IIT is, in many ways, a microcosm of the broader debate about what it would take to explain consciousness scientifically: the standards are not yet agreed upon, and IIT has at least forced that disagreement to become explicit.
