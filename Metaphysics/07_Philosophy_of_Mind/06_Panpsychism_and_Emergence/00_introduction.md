# Panpsychism and Emergence

If consciousness cannot be derived from the physical, and if dualism is unsatisfactory, a third option presents itself: perhaps consciousness is not something that emerges from the physical at some level of complexity, but a fundamental feature of the world present in some form everywhere. This is panpsychism — the view that mentality, or at least some proto-mental property, is ubiquitous in nature, present even in elementary particles, and that the complex consciousness of human minds is not an emergence from nothing but a combination or integration of simpler mental features.

Panpsychism has a distinguished philosophical pedigree — Leibniz's monads have experiential features, Whitehead's process philosophy posits experiential occasions as fundamental — but it was largely marginalized in twentieth-century analytic philosophy. The hard problem has brought it back. If physics cannot explain consciousness, and if we accept that consciousness is real, then perhaps consciousness must be added to the ontology at the fundamental level rather than derived from it. Galen Strawson, Philip Goff, and others have developed sophisticated contemporary versions of panpsychism.

The main challenge for panpsychism is the combination problem: how do micro-experiential entities combine to produce the unified macro-experience of a human being? If electrons have proto-experiential properties, what combination of electrons yields the rich, unified experience of seeing a landscape? The combination problem is the hard problem restated at the micro-level, and it is not clear that panpsychism solves it.

Emergence theory offers a different response: consciousness emerges from physical processes at a certain level of complexity — not by being identical to those processes, but by arising from them in a way that is genuinely novel. Strong emergence (where the emergent phenomenon is not in principle derivable from its base) is philosophically significant but contested; weak emergence is less problematic but may not do the work required.

Illusionism — the radical alternative associated with Frankish and Dennett — bites the bullet: there is no hard problem because phenomenal consciousness, as ordinarily conceived, does not exist. The "experience" of qualia is itself a kind of representation or self-misunderstanding, not a real feature of the world. This chapter develops these three positions and evaluates what each costs and delivers.
