# Panpsychism Defined

The term derives from Greek — *pan* (all) and *psyche* (soul or mind) — but the contemporary view is more precise and less mystical than the etymology suggests. Panpsychism is not the attribution of full human consciousness to rocks and rivers. It is the view that some minimal form of experiential or proto-experiential property is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the universe — that wherever there is matter, there is some form of mentality, however primitive. The quarks, electrons, or whatever turns out to be fundamental do not just have mass, charge, and spin; they have something it is like, in however attenuated a sense, to be them.

It is worth being precise about what panpsychism is not, since it is easily confused with neighboring views. Pantheism attributes divinity to the universe; panpsychism says nothing about divinity. Animism attributes full-blooded consciousness or spirits to all natural objects; panpsychism typically makes far more modest claims about basic experiential properties. Hylozoism holds that all matter is alive; panpsychism is about mentality rather than life, and the two need not co-occur. Idealism holds that only minds exist, and matter is either mental or reducible to mental; panpsychism holds that matter exists but has mental or proto-mental properties, which makes it compatible with scientific realism about the physical world.

Contemporary panpsychism comes in several forms. Micropsychism attributes experiential properties to fundamental physical entities — particles, fields, or other basic constituents of matter. These micro-level experiential properties are thought to combine (in ways to be specified) to produce the rich experience of complex organisms. Cosmopsychism inverts this: the universe as a whole is the fundamental conscious entity, and the experiences of complex organisms are aspects or modifications of cosmic consciousness. Individual minds are derivations from the universal mind, not compositions of micro-minds. This avoids the combination problem (how micro-experiences combine into macro-experience) but faces a corresponding decombination problem (how cosmic consciousness breaks into individual experiences). Panprotopsychism holds that fundamental entities have properties that are neither fully experiential nor fully physical in the standard sense — proto-phenomenal properties that give rise to experience when appropriately organized without being experiential themselves.

## The Contemporary Revival

The revival of panpsychism in recent decades reflects growing dissatisfaction with the standard alternatives. Substance dualism faces the interaction problem and conflicts with the causal closure of physics. Reductive physicalism faces the hard problem: functional and neural descriptions cannot explain why there is subjective experience at all. Panpsychism offers a potential third way: consciousness is a fundamental feature of reality, present in all physical entities at some level — not reducible to non-experiential properties (which addresses the hard problem) and not a separate substance (which avoids the interaction problem). The hard problem is addressed by denying that consciousness ever emerged from non-conscious matter; it was always there, at every level of physical reality.

The central challenge for panpsychism — the combination problem — is the mirror image of the hard problem for physicalism. Where the physicalist faces the question "how does consciousness emerge from non-conscious matter?", the panpsychist faces the question "how do primitive experiential properties at the micro-level combine to produce the rich, unified, structured experience of a human mind?" Micropsychism must explain subject combination (how many micro-subjects give rise to a single macro-subject), qualitative combination (how colorless, characterless micro-experiences give rise to the vivid qualitative richness of human experience), and structural combination (how micro-experience gives rise to temporal continuity, self-awareness, and unified intentionality). Panpsychists argue that their combination problem is less severe than the emergence problem facing physicalism: at least they start with some experiential properties at the base level. Whether that starting advantage is enough to make the combination problem tractable is the central dispute in contemporary panpsychist philosophy.
