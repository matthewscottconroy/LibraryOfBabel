# What Is Panpsychism?

Not only human beings and animals have experience. The basic constituents of matter — electrons, quarks, or whatever turns out to be fundamental — have some form of proto-experiential or proto-mental properties. This is the claim of panpsychism, and it sounds strange at first. But consider the alternatives.

Substance dualism holds that consciousness is a non-physical substance or property distinct from matter. It faces the interaction problem: how does this non-physical consciousness causally interact with physical matter without violating causal closure? Reductive physicalism holds that consciousness reduces to physical or functional properties. It faces the hard problem: no functional or neural description seems to explain why there is subjective experience rather than none. Both alternatives face severe difficulties. Panpsychism offers a third way: consciousness is not reducible to non-experiential physical properties, but it is not a separate substance either — it is woven into the fabric of matter from the ground up. The hard problem is addressed by denying that consciousness ever emerged from non-conscious matter; it was always present. The interaction problem is avoided because mentality is not added to matter from outside; it is intrinsic to matter.

The view has a long philosophical history. Thales, Plato, Giordano Bruno, Leibniz, Schopenhauer, and William James all held versions of it. In the twentieth century it was largely dismissed as mysticism. In the twenty-first century it has undergone a dramatic rehabilitation, driven primarily by the apparent failure of physicalist accounts of consciousness. Philosophers including Philip Goff, Galen Strawson, William Seager, and Timothy Sprigge defend it; Chalmers takes it seriously as a consequence of Russellian monism. It is no longer a fringe position.

## The Central Challenge

The central challenge for panpsychism is the combination problem: how do the rudimentary proto-experiential properties of fundamental particles combine to produce the rich, unified conscious experience of a human mind? The combination problem has several dimensions that are worth distinguishing.

Subject combination: how do many micro-subjects combine to produce a single unified macro-subject? Many separate experiences do not obviously add up to one unified experience. When billions of neurons fire, each with some minimal experiential property, what makes the result a single subject rather than billions of tiny subjects? Qualitative combination: the qualities of human experience — the redness of red, the painfulness of pain — seem radically unlike anything we would expect micro-experiences to contribute. How do perhaps-colorless, perhaps-characterless micro-experiences generate the vivid qualitative richness of macro-experience? Structural combination: human consciousness has specific structural features — temporal continuity, self-awareness, unified intentionality — that seem to require more than the mere summing of micro-experiential properties.

Panpsychists argue that their combination problem is less severe than the emergence problem facing physicalism. At least panpsychism starts with some experiential properties at the base level, and the combination problem is about how these properties aggregate and transform. The physicalist has nothing experiential to start with — the emergence of consciousness from wholly non-experiential matter seems mysterious in a deeper way. Whether this argument succeeds — whether the combination problem is actually easier than the hard problem — is the central dispute between panpsychism and its critics. Cosmopsychism (the view that the universe as a whole is the fundamental conscious entity, and individual minds are derivations from cosmic consciousness) attempts to sidestep the combination problem by starting at the top rather than the bottom, but faces a corresponding decombination problem. Neither the bottom-up nor the top-down panpsychist approach has a fully satisfactory solution to the structural question of how the appropriate level of experiential organization is achieved.
