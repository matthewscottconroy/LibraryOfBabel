# Russellian Monism

Bertrand Russell observed in *The Analysis of Matter* (1927) that physics describes the world in purely structural-relational terms: it tells us about causal relations and mathematical structure but says nothing about the intrinsic nature of what underlies these structures. Physics tells us everything about the relational and causal properties of electrons — their charge, mass, spin, and how they interact with other things. But there is something the electron is intrinsically — the categorical basis that grounds these dispositions — that physics does not specify. As Russell put it: "Physics is mathematical not because we know so much about the physical world, but because we know so little: it is only its mathematical properties that we can discover."

This observation points to a structural feature of physical knowledge: we know the causal-structural profile of the physical world but are ignorant of its intrinsic nature. Standard physicalism, committed to identifying everything with what physics describes, is committed to the claim that there is nothing more to physical properties than their structural-causal profiles. But this leaves the intrinsic nature of physical properties uncharacterized. Chalmers, Goff, Strawson, and others have developed this observation into a positive metaphysical proposal: phenomenal properties are good candidates for the intrinsic nature of physical properties. If electrons have intrinsic properties, and those intrinsic properties are proto-experiential or experiential, then consciousness is not separate from the physical world but is its intrinsic nature — what the physical world is like from the inside.

The proposal has elegant features. No interaction problem: consciousness does not need to causally intervene from outside, because consciousness is the intrinsic nature of the physical. Mental causation is physical causation. The hard problem is addressed: we cannot derive consciousness from physical descriptions because physical descriptions capture only causal-structural properties, and consciousness is the intrinsic filler of these structural roles. The explanatory gap reflects the structural character of physical knowledge, not a genuine absence of consciousness from the physical world. Compatible with physics: Russellian monism adds nothing to what physics says — it only characterizes the intrinsic nature of what physics describes structurally. Adding experiential intrinsic natures to physical properties does not revise physics; it supplements it.

## Panpsychism vs. Panprotopsychism and the Objections

Within the Russellian framework, two versions are distinguishable. Panpsychist Russellian monism (Galen Strawson, Philip Goff) holds that the intrinsic nature of fundamental physical properties is genuinely experiential: electrons have experiences, however primitive. Panprotopsychist Russellian monism (Chalmers, in some moods) holds that the intrinsic nature of fundamental physical properties is proto-experiential — not yet experience, but the material from which experience is composed. This avoids attributing full experience to electrons while still grounding phenomenal consciousness in something non-structural.

Several objections press the view. The combination problem: even if micro-level properties are experiential or proto-experiential, how do they combine to produce unified macro-level consciousness? Russellian monism inherits this problem from general panpsychism. The intrinsic nature objection: some philosophers deny that physical properties have intrinsic natures beyond their structural-causal profiles. If physical properties are purely dispositional — constituted entirely by what they do rather than what they are — the Russellian observation identifies a pseudo-gap. The multiple intrinsic natures problem: different physical properties (charge, mass, spin) would have different intrinsic natures; why should any of these intrinsic natures be experiential rather than some other kind of non-structural property? There is nothing in the structural characterization of charge that would suggest its intrinsic nature is experiential rather than, say, haecceitistic or colorational in some non-phenomenal way. And the structural coherence problem: if physics is complete in specifying all causal-structural facts, intrinsic properties do no causal work and would be epiphenomenal — a consequence the Russellian monist must address. Whether these objections are decisive or merely set the agenda for further development is the central question for contemporary Russellian monism.
