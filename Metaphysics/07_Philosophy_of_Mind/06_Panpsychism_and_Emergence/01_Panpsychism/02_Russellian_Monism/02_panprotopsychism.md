# Panprotopsychism

It seems implausible that electrons and quarks have genuine experiences. Our concept of experience is closely tied to biological organisms with complex neural systems, and attributing experience to subatomic particles seems to stretch the concept beyond recognition. Standard panpsychism embraces this implication; panprotopsychism is an attempt to capture the Russellian insight — that the intrinsic nature of the physical must be characterized in terms other than purely structural-causal properties — while avoiding the attribution of full experience to the elementary constituents of matter.

Chalmers introduced the term "panprotopsychism" to mark this distinction. Both views share the Russellian starting point: physics describes only structural-causal properties, and something must constitute the intrinsic nature of what physics describes. Where panpsychism identifies those intrinsic properties with experience, panprotopsychism characterizes them as proto-phenomenal — properties that are neither straightforwardly physical (in the sense of purely structural) nor straightforwardly experiential, but that give rise to experience when organized in the right way. Fundamental particles have some non-structural intrinsic properties, and these properties — whatever they are — generate experience when arranged as they are in complex conscious organisms. This retains the Russellian insight while avoiding the counterintuitive attribution of sentience to electrons.

The advantage is a certain modesty: we need not commit to electrons having experiences. We need only commit to electrons having intrinsic properties that are the precursors to experience — the "raw material" from which experience is constructed when particles are appropriately organized.

## The Central Dilemma and the Placeholder Problem

Philip Goff has argued that the distinction between panpsychism and panprotopsychism may be less sharp than it appears, and this reveals the central challenge for panprotopsychism. If proto-phenomenal properties are genuinely different from phenomenal properties — if they are truly non-experiential — then we face a combination problem not so different from the emergence problem facing non-panpsychist views. How do genuinely non-experiential proto-phenomenal properties give rise to genuine experience? The emergence of experience from proto-experience seems as mysterious as the emergence of experience from purely structural properties. On the other hand, if proto-phenomenal properties are close enough to phenomenal properties to make the combination tractable — if they are experiential precursors in a robust sense — the view collapses back into standard panpsychism with a different vocabulary. Either the distinction from non-panpsychism is illusory (the combination problem is just as hard) or the distinction from panpsychism is illusory (the proto-phenomenal properties are effectively phenomenal).

A deeper challenge concerns the positive characterization of proto-phenomenal properties. What are they? If they are not experiential and not purely structural-causal, what positive description can we give them? The negative characterization leaves them as a theoretical placeholder without positive content. Panprotopsychists respond that this situation is analogous to early physics, when we could refer to properties like mass and charge without a complete positive characterization of their intrinsic nature. We can refer to proto-phenomenal properties as the intrinsic nature of physical properties that gives rise to experience — fixed by their explanatory role — even without a full positive description. The reference is fixed by the role the properties play, not by an intrinsic characterization. Whether this is satisfying depends on whether we think a property can do explanatory work without being positively characterized, and on whether the role-reference strategy genuinely picks out anything determinate.

Panprotopsychism sits between standard physicalism and full panpsychism: more than standard physicalism (adds intrinsic non-structural properties to matter), less than full panpsychism (does not attribute genuine experience to all physical things), and less than standard dualism (does not require separate substances). This position in conceptual space is what makes panprotopsychism attractive to philosophers who find both standard physicalism — because of the hard problem — and full panpsychism — because of the implausibility of electron experience — unsatisfying. Whether the position is stable or collapses into one of its neighbors is the central question about the view.
