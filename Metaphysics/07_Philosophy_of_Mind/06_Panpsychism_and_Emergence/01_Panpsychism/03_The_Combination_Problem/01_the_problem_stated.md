# The Combination Problem Stated

State the problem precisely. Suppose panpsychism is true, and particle p₁ has experience e₁, particle p₂ has experience e₂, ..., particle pₙ has experience eₙ. When these particles compose a human brain B, what grounds the claim that B has a unified experience E that is not merely e₁ + e₂ + ... + eₙ? Many micro-subjects do not obviously add up to a single macro-subject. If every electron has a tiny experience, why is there one unified perspective associated with a human brain rather than billions of micro-perspectives? The parts seem to have separate experiences; the whole seems to have a unified one. How does unity arise from multiplicity?

You do not experience your visual field and your auditory field as separate experiences — they are integrated into one unified experiential whole. But if this unity is a feature of the macro-subject that is not present in the micro-subjects, panpsychism must explain how it arises from them. This is the subject-summing problem, and it is perhaps the deepest dimension of the combination problem.

The qualitative combination problem presses differently. Even if micro-experiences exist, their qualities do not obviously give rise to macro-qualities. If electrons have proto-experiential properties, why do these combine to produce the specific qualitative character of the smell of coffee or the visual experience of red? The micro-level and macro-level properties seem radically incommensurable: electrons have no obvious visual properties, yet we have visual experiences; no obvious auditory properties, yet we hear music; no obvious hedonic properties, yet we feel pleasure and pain. How does the combination of properties that must be radically simpler than any human experience produce the specific richness of qualitative character?

## The Structural Mismatch and the Symmetry with Emergence

A third dimension concerns mathematical structure. For physical combination, we have well-developed theories: quantum mechanics, statistical mechanics, and thermodynamics describe how micro-physical properties give rise to macro-physical properties. These theories work because physical properties are structural-relational and we have mathematical tools for describing how structures combine. For experiential combination, we lack analogous tools. Experiential properties, if they exist, are intrinsic and qualitative, and we do not have a mathematics of intrinsic qualities. Without such a mathematics, we have no principled account of how micro-experiential structure maps to macro-experiential structure.

The combination problem is structurally analogous to the emergence problem facing non-panpsychist views. The emergence problem: how does consciousness arise from non-conscious neural matter? The combination problem: how does unified macro-consciousness arise from micro-conscious particles? In both cases there is an explanatory gap between base level and higher level. Sam Coleman has pushed this symmetry sharply: if panpsychists are entitled to posit a brute fact about how micro-experiences combine into macro-experience, why can't physicalists posit a brute fact about how neural activity gives rise to experience? If the combination problem is genuinely as hard as the emergence problem, panpsychism loses its primary motivation — it was supposed to explain consciousness without requiring a mysterious emergence of the mental from the non-mental. If panpsychism merely relocates the mystery, the case for preferring it over physicalism is weakened.

Panpsychists must therefore show either that the combination problem is genuinely easier than the emergence problem — because at least some experiential properties are present at the base level — or that it is no harder and that panpsychism has other advantages that still make it preferable. The former argument appeals to the thought that starting with experience and asking how it combines is more tractable than starting with nothing experiential and asking how experience arises. The latter argument appeals to the Russellian insight that panpsychism better accommodates what we know about the structure of physical description. Whether either argument succeeds is what the contemporary debate about the combination problem turns on.
