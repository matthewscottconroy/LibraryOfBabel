# Goff and Cosmopsychism

Philip Goff is the most prominent contemporary defender of panpsychism, and his work represents the most sustained effort to develop the view rigorously in the face of its challenges. His defense proceeds along two tracks: a historical-diagnostic argument about how the hard problem arose, and a constructive metaphysical proposal — cosmopsychism — designed to sidestep the combination problem that confronts standard bottom-up panpsychism.

The historical argument, developed in *Galileo's Error* (2019), locates the source of the hard problem in a methodological choice at the origin of modern science. Galileo's scientific revolution defined matter purely in quantitative structural terms, treating only the quantitative as part of the physical world and relegating qualities to the subjective mind. This was scientifically fruitful — it gave us mathematical physics — but philosophically consequential. It created the hard problem by stipulation: if matter is defined as purely quantitative and consciousness is qualitative, then of course consciousness cannot be explained scientifically. The "Galilean error" is to think we can get consciousness out of matter by adding more quantitative complexity. Panpsychism corrects this by recognizing that the intrinsic qualitative nature of matter is experiential. Consciousness was in matter all along; Galileo's methodological move made it invisible.

The standard panpsychist picture is bottom-up: elementary particles have micro-experiences, and these combine to produce the macro-experiences of complex organisms. The combination problem asks how this combination works. Cosmopsychism reverses the direction: the universe as a whole has a fundamental experience — cosmic consciousness — and individual organisms have experiences because they are aspects or modifications of the cosmic experience, not because their components combine.

## The Cosmopsychist Framework and the Decombination Problem

In Goff's framework, the universe has a fundamental experiential nature, and what we call physical objects are abstractions from or aspects of this cosmic whole. Individual organisms have experiences not because their components combine but because they are the universe experiencing itself through a particular perspective or mode of organization. My experience is not the sum of my neurons' experiences; it is an aspect of the universe's experience, determined by the physical organization of my brain. The brain does not produce experience from below; it selects or limits the cosmic experience to produce an individual perspective.

This sidesteps the combination problem: if consciousness is already whole at the top, there is no problem of combining micro-experiences into macro-experience. But it generates a corresponding decombination problem: what determines how the cosmic experience differentiates into individual experiences? Why does this process generate my experience rather than yours? What determines that the physical organization of my brain carves out a perspective corresponding to mine rather than some other?

Goff acknowledges this difficulty and regards it as ongoing research. He suggests that the physical organization of a brain "carves out" a portion of the cosmic experience and that the principles governing this carving are analogous to physical principles of composition. But the details remain underdeveloped. His response to the decombination objection is that the decombination problem is more tractable than the combination problem: building consciousness bottom-up requires explaining how a unified experience arises from a plurality of independent micro-experiences — generating unity from multiplicity. Building consciousness top-down requires explaining how a unified experience differentiates into individual perspectives — generating structure within a whole. The latter task, Goff argues, is at least intelligible in a way the former is not, because we begin with something unified rather than trying to construct unity from scratch.

Critics point out that cosmopsychism makes the metaphysics of consciousness depend on the metaphysics of the cosmos — it requires that the universe as a whole be a unified entity with a determinate experiential nature, which is itself a highly non-trivial commitment. Whether the universe has the kind of intrinsic unity that cosmopsychism requires, and whether the cosmic experience is a coherent notion, are open questions that cosmopsychism inherits before it can address the decombination problem. Goff's work has nevertheless revitalized the panpsychist project and brought it into serious dialogue with analytic philosophy of mind, making panpsychism a live option in contemporary philosophy of mind in a way it was not twenty years ago.
