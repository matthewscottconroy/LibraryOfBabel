# Consciousness and Fundamental Reality

The question of whether consciousness is fundamental is at the heart of contemporary debates between panpsychists, physicalists, and dualists. "Fundamental" has several senses: an entity is ontologically fundamental if it is not constituted by or reducible to anything more basic; explanatorily fundamental if it appears in the most basic laws of the relevant theory; grounding-fundamental if it grounds other facts without itself being grounded. Physicalists typically hold that consciousness is not fundamental in any of these senses: it is constituted by physical states, it does not appear in basic physics, and physical facts ground phenomenal facts. The challenge for physicalism is explaining why physical constitution or realization gives rise to consciousness — the hard problem.

Dualists hold that consciousness is ontologically fundamental — not reducible to physical states — and that psychophysical laws connect the physical and phenomenal without reduction. But dualism faces the causal closure challenge: if physical events have sufficient physical causes, where does consciousness fit in the causal order? Epiphenomenalism makes consciousness causally inert; overdetermination multiplies causes unnecessarily; interactionism violates causal closure. Each option faces serious objections.

Panpsychists, particularly Russellian monists, offer a third account: consciousness or proto-consciousness is fundamental in the sense that it constitutes the intrinsic nature of physical properties. Physical science describes the structure of reality; experiential properties are its intrinsic texture. Consciousness is as fundamental as mass or charge — indeed, experiential or proto-experiential properties *are* the intrinsic nature of these properties. Causal closure is preserved because consciousness does not add causal inputs beyond what physics describes; it constitutes the intrinsic nature of what physics describes. The reality of consciousness is preserved because it is not reduced away. The interaction problem does not arise because no separate substance needs to interact with matter from outside.

## Goff and Seager on Fundamentality and Explanation

Philip Goff's argument in *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality* (2017) turns on an epistemic asymmetry: consciousness is the only entity we know directly, through first-person acquaintance. We know the physical world only indirectly, through theoretical description. Given this epistemic situation, consciousness has a better claim to fundamentality than physical properties, which are known only through their structural-relational profiles. We know our own experience most directly; the physical world most indirectly. Panpsychism recognizes this by making consciousness fundamental rather than derivative.

William Seager's approach adds historical depth and scientific engagement. He traces panpsychist thought from antiquity through Fechner, James, and Royce to the contemporary revival, situating current panpsychism within a longer intellectual tradition. Seager is particularly interested in whether panpsychism can account for the specific qualitative character of experience or merely for the existence of experience in general — a more demanding requirement that the combination problem directly bears on. He also notes what he calls "the significance of experience": panpsychism is not just motivated by the hard problem but by a more general intuition that experience is too significant a feature of reality to be relegated to derivative status, arising only in complex biological systems by coincidence.

A natural worry about the fundamentality response: even if consciousness is fundamental, does this explain consciousness? If consciousness is a primitive feature of reality not derivable from anything more basic, have we explained it or merely labeled the mystery? Panpsychists respond that this is not uniquely their problem. Physicalism posits fundamental physical properties — mass, charge, spin — that are not further explained. They are the basic constituents of reality. Panpsychism simply includes experiential properties among the fundamental features of reality. This does not explain everything, but no theory explains everything — every theory bottoms out in brute primitives. The question is whether experiential properties are better primitives than non-experiential ones, given the failure of all attempts to derive consciousness from non-experiential properties. Whether the cost of accepting experiential primitives is lower than the cost of the hard problem for physicalism or the interaction problem for dualism is the central evaluative question.
