# Goff and Seager: Contemporary Panpsychism

Philip Goff and William Seager are among the most active contemporary defenders of panpsychism, and their work together represents the most sustained effort to develop the position rigorously in conversation with analytic philosophy of mind. Both argue that taking the hard problem seriously requires reconsidering the assumption that consciousness is non-fundamental.

Goff's central historical argument, developed in *Galileo's Error* (2019), locates the source of the hard problem in Galileo's methodological decision to exclude qualitative properties — color, sound, taste as experienced — from the mathematical description of nature. This was scientifically fruitful but left consciousness permanently unexplained by science, since consciousness is precisely the domain of qualitative experience. The solution is not to try harder to fit consciousness into the quantitative-structural framework but to recognize that qualitative and experiential properties are part of the fundamental furniture of the universe — always were, and would have been recognized as such had Galileo's methodological limitation not been mistaken for an ontological one.

Goff defends a form of "constitutive panpsychism": the experiential properties of complex systems like brains are constituted by the experiential properties of their physical parts. He acknowledges the combination problem but attempts to dissolve it rather than solve it, arguing that the apparent impossibility of combination reflects our limited concepts rather than a genuine metaphysical impossibility. His more radical response is cosmopsychism: the universe as a whole is the fundamental conscious entity, and individual experiences are aspects or modifications of the cosmic experience. This sidesteps combination by reversing direction — explaining individual experience top-down from cosmic consciousness rather than bottom-up from micro-experiences.

Goff's positive argument appeals to what he calls "simple knowledge": we have more direct, unmediated knowledge of our own conscious experience than of any physical fact. Our knowledge of physical properties is entirely structural-theoretical; our knowledge of experience is by acquaintance. Given this epistemic asymmetry, we should give priority to phenomenal properties in our ontology. Consciousness is what we know most directly; the physical world most indirectly. Panpsychism recognizes this asymmetry.

## Seager's Historical and Scientific Engagement

William Seager's approach is distinguished by its historical depth and scientific engagement. His *Theories of Consciousness* provides a comprehensive survey, and his more recent work defends a form of Russellian panpsychism particularly attentive to the relationship between panpsychism and emergence. Seager is interested in whether panpsychism can account for the specific qualitative character of experience or merely for the existence of experience in general — a more demanding requirement, since panpsychism's primary motivation is explaining qualitative character, not just postulating experience somewhere.

Seager's engagement with physics is distinctive. He takes seriously the possibility that consciousness might have a role in quantum mechanics — not in the naive sense that consciousness collapses the wave function, but in the sense that any satisfactory interpretation of quantum mechanics may need to incorporate experiential properties. He discusses the relational interpretation (Rovelli), the many-worlds interpretation, and other approaches with an eye toward what they imply for consciousness. He also notes what he calls the significance of experience: a universe in which experience was always fundamental is more comprehensible than one in which it arose from nothing experiential. Panpsychism is motivated not just by the hard problem but by a more general intuition that experience is too significant a feature of reality to be relegated to derivative status.

Goff and Seager converge on the key claims: the hard problem is genuine, Russellian monism is the most attractive response, and panpsychism faces the combination problem but is preferable to its rivals. They diverge in approach: Goff is more systematic and argument-driven, seeking to establish panpsychism by ruling out alternatives; Seager is more exploratory and historically informed, offering panpsychism as a promising research program rather than an established conclusion. They also diverge on the combination problem: Goff has proposed cosmopsychism as a route around it; Seager has been more willing to explore constitutive and emergentist alternatives within a broadly panpsychist framework. The revival of panpsychism they represent has had a significant effect on the field: panpsychism is now regularly discussed in mainstream philosophy of mind, a far cry from its marginal status twenty years ago.
