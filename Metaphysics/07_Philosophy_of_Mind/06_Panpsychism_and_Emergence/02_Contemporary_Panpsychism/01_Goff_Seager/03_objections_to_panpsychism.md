# Objections to Panpsychism

Panpsychism faces a number of serious objections beyond the combination problem, and working through them clarifies what is and is not at stake in the debate.

The implausibility objection presses first. The idea that electrons have experience seems simply incredible. Our concept of experience is tied to biological organisms with nervous systems; attributing experience to fundamental particles seems to stretch the concept beyond recognition. The objection has two components. The conceptual component: our concept of experience is so closely linked to biological, behavioral, and functional criteria that attributing experience to particles violates the meaning of the term. If experience requires the right kind of complex organization — as functionalists hold — then an electron's "experience" would not be experience in any meaningful sense. Panpsychists respond that this assumes functionalism is correct, which is precisely what panpsychism rejects. The argument for panpsychism begins with the observation that functional organization cannot explain phenomenal consciousness; to then insist that experience requires functional organization begs the question. The empirical component: there is no behavioral, functional, or scientific evidence that electrons have any form of experience. Panpsychists reply that this conflates evidence for access consciousness with evidence for phenomenal consciousness. The absence of behavioral evidence is not evidence of the absence of micro-experience — we would not expect behavioral evidence from entities at the fundamental physical level.

The causal redundancy objection raises a different challenge. If physics is causally complete, then adding experiential intrinsic natures to physical properties does not add any causal power. The intrinsic natures are causally inert — a form of epiphenomenalism. Russellian monists respond that the intrinsic natures are not additional properties alongside the structural-relational properties but are what those properties are "from the inside." There is not "charge (structural) + proto-experience (intrinsic)" but "charge = the structural aspect of a property whose intrinsic nature is proto-experiential." No epiphenomenalism results because there are not two separate properties, one doing causal work and one being inert. Critics find this response unclear: what does it mean for a causal power to have an intrinsic nature that "just is" experiential, if the experiential nature plays no distinctive causal role? The response may reduce the worry, or it may merely redescribe it.

## Individuation, Normativity, and Assessment

The individuation problem asks what determines the boundaries of experiential subjects. In a panpsychist world, every physical entity has some form of proto-experience — so what determines that I am one subject and you are another, rather than together being part of a single larger subject? For standard physicalism, subject-individuation is explained by functional or biological criteria: cognitive processing that is unified, integrated, and self-monitoring in a way that defines a single cognitive subject. If panpsychism holds that every physical entity has experience regardless of functional organization, those criteria cannot do the individuating work. Cosmopsychists face a version of this problem too: if the cosmos is the fundamental subject, what grounds the individuation of individual organisms as distinct subjects? The decombination problem is partly an individuation problem.

Panpsychism has potentially radical implications for ethics. If thermostats, rocks, and electrons have some form of experience — however minimal — they may have some moral status. Our practices of moral concern are calibrated to sophisticated behavioral, neurological, and evolutionary criteria; panpsychism threatens to disrupt these by making experience universal. Panpsychists typically respond that the moral implications of micro-experience are negligible: an electron's micro-experience is so impoverished compared to human or animal experience that it has no significant moral weight. Moral status is proportional to experiential richness, not to the bare fact of having experience at all. This response is plausible but leaves open where on the spectrum of complexity moral significance begins — and implies a continuous spectrum of moral status rather than the binary intuitions many philosophers find default.

These objections collectively raise the bar for panpsychism: the view must offer not just a diagnosis of the hard problem but a developed theory of consciousness, combination, individuation, and causation that outcompetes its rivals on the total cost of their respective commitments. Whether panpsychism's costs are lower than those of physicalism (the hard problem, the explanatory gap) or dualism (the interaction problem, epiphenomenalism) is the central comparative question, and it requires assessing not just individual objections but the overall theoretical burden each position carries.
