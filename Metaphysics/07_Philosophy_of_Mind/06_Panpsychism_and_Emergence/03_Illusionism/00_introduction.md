# Illusionism About Consciousness

The most radical physicalist response to the hard problem is not to solve it but to dissolve it: to argue that the seeming intractability of the hard problem is itself an illusion, generated by our confused introspective picture of our own mental states. Illusionism — associated with Frankish, Dennett, and Frankish's refinement of Dennett's position — holds that phenomenal consciousness as traditionally conceived does not exist. There are no qualia in the philosophically loaded sense — no intrinsic, non-representational, phenomenal properties of experience. What we take to be rich phenomenal consciousness is a representational construction — a model our brains make of themselves — that systematically misrepresents the nature of our mental states.

Dennett's heterophenomenology is the methodological expression of this view: instead of taking introspective reports about phenomenal experience at face value, we should treat them as data to be explained — as facts about how subjects represent their mental states, not as direct revelations of what those mental states are actually like. The "intuition pump" of the Cartesian theater — the inner stage where experience unfolds before an inner observer — is a persistent illusion, not a feature of the mental architecture.

Frankish's "illusionism" is more explicit: we are systematically deceived about the nature of our own mental states. We represent our pain as having an intrinsic phenomenal character — as being irreducibly painful — but this representation is false. What there really is, in the brain, is a complex functional state with various representational and causal properties. The "phenomenal character" is not an additional property over and above these functional properties; it is how we represent those properties to ourselves, falsely.

The standard objection is that illusionism seems to be self-refuting: isn't the illusion of phenomenal consciousness itself a phenomenal experience? Frankish's response is that even the seeming-to-have-phenomenal-experience is a functional state, not a genuine phenomenal state. Whether this response succeeds is a central question of contemporary philosophy of mind.

This section examines illusionism in depth, its motivations, its most developed forms, and the objections it faces.
