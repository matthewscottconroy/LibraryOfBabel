# Frankish's Illusionism

The hard problem is hard because it asks why there are phenomenal properties — why physical processes are accompanied by qualitative experience, why there is something it is like to see red. Keith Frankish's illusionism, developed primarily in "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness" (2016) and subsequent papers, takes a different approach: it denies the premise. There are no phenomenal properties of the kind that generate the hard problem. What we call phenomenal consciousness — the vivid, intrinsic, qualitative character of experience — is an illusion. The hard problem dissolves because its subject matter does not exist.

This requires careful statement. Frankish does not deny that we have mental states, that these states have cognitive effects, that we process information, or that there is something called "experience" in a thin functional sense. What he denies is that our introspective representations of those states are accurate. When we introspect and seem to find vivid redness — a qualitative property luminously present — this seeming is itself a cognitive event that represents our mental state as having properties it does not have. The state has functional properties; the representation attributes phenomenal properties. The representation is systematically misleading: we are "wired" to represent our cognitive states as having simple, intrinsic, ineffable qualitative properties because this representation is computationally useful for communication and self-monitoring, not because the states actually have these properties.

Frankish distinguishes his view from related positions. Eliminativism (Churchland) denies the reality of folk-psychological mental states. Frankish accepts the reality of representational mental states; he only denies phenomenal properties. Deflationism (Dennett in some moods) holds that consciousness is real but not the mysterious thing philosophers think it is. Frankish is more systematic: he accepts that there is a genuine intuition — we represent ourselves as having phenomenal properties — and provides an explanation of it via the illusory representation. Standard functionalism holds that phenomenal properties are identical to functional ones. Frankish holds that there are no phenomenal properties; the concept picks out a confused notion rather than identifying phenomenal with functional.

## The Illusory Representation and Its Compellingness

Frankish introduces the notion of "quasi-phenomenal properties" — properties that are attributed to mental states by introspective representations as if they were phenomenal properties, but which are actually functional. These are the real properties that play the role of phenomenal properties in our cognitive economy. The hard problem is generated by confusing quasi-phenomenal properties (functional properties that are represented as phenomenal) with genuine phenomenal properties (which do not exist). Once this confusion is cleared up, the hard problem dissolves.

Why is the illusion so compelling? Why does it seem so evident — to philosophers and non-philosophers alike — that experience has vivid qualitative character? Frankish's answer: introspective representations are computationally useful precisely because they represent mental states as having simple, atomic qualities rather than complex functional profiles. And we have no direct access to our own functional states — we are presented with introspective representations of those states, not with the states themselves. We see the map but not the territory, and the map is inaccurate. The compelling character of the illusion is a feature of the architecture: introspection presents its output directly, without any access to the representational process that generates it.

The natural objection is that this seems self-undermining: if my experience of red seems to have phenomenal properties, isn't that seeming itself a phenomenal property? Frankish bites the bullet: the seeming is itself a non-phenomenal functional state that represents (wrongly) that the system has phenomenal properties. The introspective representation is misleading, and there is no phenomenal residue. A related objection appeals to Cartesian certainty: the one thing we cannot doubt is the existence of our own experience. Frankish responds that the Cartesian point is about the existence of representation, not about the accuracy of what is represented. We cannot doubt that we are representing ourselves as having phenomenal properties. We can doubt whether the represented properties exist. Certainty of representation does not entail certainty of the represented.

For Frankish, the meta-problem of consciousness — why do we think there is a hard problem? — is the primary explanandum, not the hard problem itself. Once we explain why cognitive systems represent themselves as having phenomenal properties, we have explained everything there is to explain. This makes Frankish's approach distinctive: rather than trying to explain consciousness, he focuses on explaining why creatures like us believe we have consciousness of the phenomenal kind. That is a scientifically tractable question about cognitive architecture, introspective mechanisms, and evolutionary function — quite different from the apparently intractable hard problem.
