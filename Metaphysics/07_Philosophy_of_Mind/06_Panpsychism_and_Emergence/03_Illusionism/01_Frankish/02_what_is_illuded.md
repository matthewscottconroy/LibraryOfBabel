# What Is Illuded in Illusionism

When Frankish says that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, he needs to be precise about what the illusion is, what is doing the illuding, and what is being illuded about. The illusion is an introspective illusion — a systematic misrepresentation of our own mental states. We represent our mental states as having vivid, intrinsic, non-relational qualitative properties (qualia in the traditional sense) when those states have only functional properties. When you introspect your experience of red and seem to find a vivid redness — a qualitative property that seems luminously present, immediately given, ineffable — this seeming is itself a cognitive event that represents your mental state as having properties it does not have.

The illusion is not that you are not processing color information. You are. The illusion is that this processing has an intrinsic qualitative character over and above its functional role. Frankish calls this "illusory phenomenality": the representation of phenomenal properties that do not exist. The introspective system generates representations of mental states that are simplified, useful abstractions — representing states as having simple, atomic qualities rather than encoding their full functional complexity. This simplification is computationally efficient and useful for communication; it is also inaccurate.

The natural objection: if the seeming-to-have-qualia is itself a mental event, doesn't that seeming have phenomenal character? Frankish bites the bullet: the seeming is itself a non-phenomenal functional state that represents (incorrectly) that the system has phenomenal properties. There is no phenomenal residue. We have no direct access to our own functional states — we are presented with introspective representations of those states, not with the states themselves. We see the map but not the territory, and the map is systematically inaccurate in the specific way illusionism claims.

## The Positive Account and the Distinction from Functionalism

Frankish does not deny that there is something to consciousness. He introduces "quasi-phenomenal properties" — properties attributed to mental states by introspective representations as if they were phenomenal properties, but which are actually functional. These are real; they play the role of phenomenal properties in our cognitive economy without being phenomenal properties in the philosophically problematic sense. The structure of the positive account:

- Real states: cognitive and functional states that process information, integrate perceptual input, influence behavior, and generate introspective reports.
- Real representations: introspective representations that represent these states as having phenomenal properties.
- Illusory phenomenality: the phenomenal properties attributed by these representations do not exist.
- Quasi-phenomenal properties: the functional properties that introspective representations represent as phenomenal — real, but functional.

Frankish's position is importantly different from standard functionalism. Standard functionalism holds that phenomenal properties are identical to functional properties — the phenomenal character of pain just is the causal-functional role of the state. Frankish does not hold that phenomenal properties are identical to functional ones; he holds that there are no phenomenal properties. The concept of phenomenal property picks out nothing, or picks out a confused notion. This allows him to handle the inverted qualia challenge more easily than standard functionalists can: if phenomenal properties do not exist, there are no inverted qualia — the scenario is not merely functionally impossible, it is conceptually confused, since there is nothing to invert.

The zombie intuition reloaded: Frankish accepts that functional zombies are conceivable — creatures functionally identical to us but lacking phenomenal consciousness. But on his view, what the zombie lacks is not phenomenal consciousness, which does not exist, but the accurate-seeming representation of phenomenal consciousness. A zombie would have all the same functional states and all the same introspective representations; it just would not have phenomenal properties. But since we do not have phenomenal properties either, the zombie is exactly like us in every relevant respect. The zombie intuition does not establish that phenomenal consciousness exists; it establishes that we represent ourselves as having it — which illusionism can explain. Whether this is a satisfying treatment of the zombie intuition or merely redescribes it depends on whether one finds the illusionist's denial of phenomenal properties more credible than the zombie theorist's acceptance of them.
