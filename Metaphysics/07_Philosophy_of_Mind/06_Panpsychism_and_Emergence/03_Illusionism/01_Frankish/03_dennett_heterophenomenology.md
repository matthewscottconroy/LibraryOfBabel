# Dennett and Heterophenomenology

Daniel Dennett's approach to consciousness, developed since the 1980s, aligns closely with illusionism, though Dennett prefers the framing of "quining qualia" — eliminating the problematic concept altogether — over Frankish's more measured claim that qualia representations are systematically misleading.

The method of heterophenomenology, which Dennett uses as a methodological foundation, begins with subjects' verbal reports about their experiences and treats these reports as data without taking them at face value. Just as anthropologists study the beliefs of a community by attending to their verbal and behavioral outputs without assuming those beliefs are all true, heterophenomenology studies the "intentional objects" of experience — what subjects represent themselves as experiencing — from a third-person perspective. This contrasts with the standard phenomenological assumption that first-person reports are authoritative. Dennett argues that introspective reports are theoretical constructs that mix genuine information-processing events with confabulation, misattribution, and conceptual confusion.

The heterophenomenological procedure: collect verbal reports, construct from them the "intentional objects" subjects represent themselves as experiencing, then explain those representations in terms of information processing, cognitive architecture, and neural mechanisms — without assuming that any "intentional objects" are literally present in consciousness. When studying what a community believes, anthropologists do not simply accept those beliefs as true; they study what the community believes, why they believe it, how it functions. Heterophenomenology applies the same attitude to consciousness research.

*Consciousness Explained* (1991) develops the "multiple drafts" model. There is no single point in the brain where information becomes phenomenally present — no Cartesian theater in which a homunculus witnesses the show. Instead, information is processed in parallel streams; different aspects of a stimulus are processed in different regions on different timescales; these streams influence each other and behavioral outputs without any "final" conscious representation. What we call "conscious experience" is the result of probing: when a processing stream is queried by a behavioral or verbal probe, it produces a report, and this report constitutes the "conscious experience" at that moment. Dennett calls this the "fame in the brain" view — consciousness is not a place but a kind of influence. Information is conscious when it achieves the right influence on downstream processing and report-generation. There is no additional event called "becoming conscious."

## Quining Qualia and the Comparison with Frankish

"Quining Qualia" (1988) is a systematic argument that the concept of qualia is internally inconsistent. The traditional concept attributes to qualia properties that cannot all be jointly instantiated: ineffability (qualia cannot be communicated), intrinsicality (qualia are intrinsic, not relational), privacy (accessible only to the subject), and direct accessibility (the subject has privileged access to their own qualia). Dennett argues through thought experiments — including inverted qualia, absent qualia, and cases of "qualiaphilia" — that these properties are jointly incoherent, that no coherent concept underlies the term, and that we should eliminate the concept rather than theorize about it.

This is a more radical move than Frankish's illusionism. Frankish accepts that there are introspective representations of phenomenal properties — even if those properties do not exist, the representations are real and require explanation via the meta-problem. Dennett seems to be arguing that even the representations are confused: there is no coherent content to the claim that "experience has phenomenal properties," so the representation itself is defective, not just misleading.

Both Dennett and Frankish are type-A physicalists who deny the hard problem. They agree that phenomenal consciousness as traditionally conceived does not exist, that introspective reports about phenomenal properties are not authoritative, and that the apparent hard problem is a cognitive illusion or conceptual confusion. They differ in emphasis and strategy. Frankish is more willing to accept the phenomenological data as genuine representations, even if misleading ones, and engages with the meta-problem as a positive research program: explaining why cognitive systems generate the illusory representations they do. Dennett is more dismissive of the phenomenological standpoint, arguing that it is fundamentally confused rather than merely misleading. Frankish's illusionism has the dialectical advantage of being more clearly articulated and more responsive to phenomenal realist objections. Dennett's heterophenomenology is more methodologically ambitious but more vulnerable to the charge that it avoids rather than answers the hard problem by reframing the question. Both face the core objection that they explain everything except what was supposed to be explained: why experience has qualitative character at all.
