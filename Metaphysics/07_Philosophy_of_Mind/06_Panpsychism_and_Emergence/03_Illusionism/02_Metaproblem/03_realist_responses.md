# Realist Responses to Illusionism

Realists about phenomenal consciousness have developed several responses to illusionism, and working through them reveals not just objections to Frankish's specific view but the deeper fault lines in the debate about consciousness.

The self-refutation objection runs as follows. If illusionism holds that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, the illusion must itself be a mental event. But any mental event that genuinely seems a certain way has phenomenal character — there is something it is like for the mental event to occur. If there is something it is like to be deceived about one's own phenomenal states, then phenomenal states exist. Frankish's response is direct: P2 — that every mental event that "seems" a certain way has phenomenal character — begs the question. The question at issue is whether every mental event that plays a certain functional role has phenomenal character. The illusionist holds that the introspective representation is a functional state all the way down; it represents the system as having phenomenal properties without itself having phenomenal character. Block has pressed a different version: the introspective representation must represent something — it must have intentional content. The content of the representation (the "seeming red") involves phenomenal properties; to represent phenomenal properties, one must have some access to what they are like, which seems to require at least some phenomenal consciousness. Frankish responds that the representation involves phenomenal concepts that function to pick out states as phenomenal without requiring the representer to instantiate phenomenal properties.

The phenomenal certainty argument — the Moorean response Chalmers endorses — holds that the existence of phenomenal consciousness is the most certain fact about our mental lives: more certain than any philosophical theory, and more certain than the premises of any argument for illusionism. If an argument leads to the conclusion that phenomenal consciousness does not exist, the right response is to reject one of its premises rather than accept the conclusion. This is "tollensing the ponens": given P1 and P2 imply C, and C is false with greater certainty than either premise, deny the least certain premise. The argument does not identify which premise to reject; it establishes that one of them must be wrong. Its force is not merely epistemic but ontic — there is nothing more certain than the existence of one's own experience.

## Systematic Introspective Error and the Equilibrium

The objection that illusionism requires implausibly systematic introspective error deserves careful examination. There are different kinds of introspective error. Errors about causes and mechanisms are well-documented: Nisbett and Wilson (1977) showed that subjects confabulate the causes of their mental processes. Errors about the presence of mental states are documented in alexithymia and dissociative states. But illusionism requires a third kind of error: that when subjects report "a vivid redness," they are misrepresenting their mental state as having qualitative character it does not have. Realists find this hardest to accept. The first two kinds of error concern our knowledge of external or causal facts. The third concerns the immediate phenomenal character of experience — the very thing that seems most directly present to consciousness. How can we be wrong about what our experience is like when our experience is all we have direct access to?

The illusionist response: the question assumes that we have "direct access" to experience, which is precisely what illusionism denies. All access is mediated by the introspective representational system, which is subject to systematic distortion. There is no access immune to error, including introspective access to phenomenal character.

The debate has reached a kind of equilibrium. Each side explains the other's position as a cognitive error: the illusionist explains why phenomenal realists are in the grip of an introspective illusion; the phenomenal realist explains why the illusionist denies what is most obvious. Neither explanation can be assessed from a neutral standpoint — both sides appeal to their own phenomenal intuitions (or lack thereof) as evidence, making the debate partly self-referential. What is clear is that the debate has sharpened the issues considerably. The concept of phenomenal consciousness is now more carefully defined than before illusionism emerged as a systematic position. The meta-problem is now a recognized research program generating empirical predictions. And the question of introspective reliability — once treated as obvious on both sides — is now a central empirical and philosophical issue. The debate has been philosophically productive even if its verdict remains open.
