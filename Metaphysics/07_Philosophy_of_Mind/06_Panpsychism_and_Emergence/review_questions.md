# Review Questions — Panpsychism and Emergence

1. Panpsychism is motivated primarily by the apparent failure of both substance dualism (interaction problem) and reductive physicalism (hard problem). Articulate the specific form of each failure that panpsychism claims to avoid, and then explain why Russellian monism — the view that phenomenal properties constitute the intrinsic nature of physical properties — is the version of panpsychism best positioned to address both.

2. The combination problem asks how micro-experiential properties of fundamental particles combine to produce the unified, rich phenomenal experience of a human mind. Sam Coleman presses a symmetry: if panpsychists can accept a brute fact about how micro-experiences combine, physicalists can equally accept a brute fact about how neural activity gives rise to experience. Does this symmetry argument succeed, and does panpsychism genuinely have any explanatory advantage over physicalism?

3. Cosmopsychism (the view that the cosmos as a whole is the primary conscious entity, with individual minds as derivations) is proposed partly to sidestep the combination problem. Does it succeed? What "decombination" problem does it generate, and is this problem easier or harder than the original combination problem?

4. Frankish's illusionism denies that phenomenal properties — in the strong sense required by the hard problem — exist. What we call phenomenal experience is a functional state that introspective systems *misrepresent* as having ineffable qualitative character. The natural objection is that the misrepresentation itself has phenomenal character. How does Frankish respond, and does his response leave anything unexplained?

5. The meta-problem of consciousness asks why we *think* there is a hard problem. Chalmers introduces this as a distinct question that any adequate theory should answer. Illusionists claim that solving the meta-problem is solving the hard problem. Phenomenal realists deny this. What exactly is the disagreement, and what would settle it?

6. Weak emergence (in-principle derivable but requiring simulation) and strong emergence (not derivable even in principle) differ in their implications for physicalism. Why does Chalmers's hard problem commit him to the claim that consciousness exhibits strong emergence, and what would the existence of strong emergence imply for the relationship between physics and consciousness?

7. Both panpsychism and illusionism are radical responses to the hard problem, but they are radical in opposite directions: panpsychism inflates consciousness into a fundamental feature of all matter, while illusionism deflates it to an illusion generated by functional systems. Is there a way to adjudicate between them without simply assuming what each position contests — whether phenomenal properties are real?

8. Philip Goff argues that consciousness has a better claim to fundamentality than physical properties because we know consciousness directly through first-person acquaintance while knowing physical properties only through theoretical description. How does this epistemic asymmetry bear on ontological priority? Does knowing something more directly make it more fundamental, and are there problems with this inference?
