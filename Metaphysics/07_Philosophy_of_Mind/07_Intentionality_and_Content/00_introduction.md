# Intentionality and Content

When you think about Paris, your thought is *about* Paris — a city on the other side of the world. When you fear the tiger, your fear is *about* the tiger. When you believe that the meeting is on Thursday, your belief has a specific *content* — that the meeting is on Thursday — that determines what the world must be like for the belief to be true. This capacity of mental states to be about things, to have content, to represent the world as being a certain way, is what philosophers call intentionality. Brentano famously claimed that intentionality is the mark of the mental — what distinguishes mental states from physical states that merely are what they are without being about anything.

Intentionality raises two deep questions. The first is metaphysical: what is the nature of the relation between a mental state and its intentional object? When I think about Paris, what is the relationship between my mental state and Paris itself? Unlike ordinary physical relations (like touching or pushing), the intentional relation seems to hold even when the object does not exist: I can think about Santa Claus, fear a monster that is not there, desire things I have never experienced. This is the puzzle of intentionality with respect to non-existent objects.

The second question is about content: what determines what a mental state is about — what fixes its intentional content? The internalist holds that content is determined by what is inside the head — by the functional or neurological properties of the mental state itself. The externalist — following Putnam's famous Twin Earth argument — holds that content is partly determined by facts about the environment: your concept of water means H₂O because H₂O is what the stuff in your environment actually is, even if you have no idea that it is H₂O. Content, on this view, is "wide": it extends beyond the skin.

This chapter develops the theory of intentionality, the internalism/externalism debate about content, and the major accounts of how mental content is determined — teleological theories, causal theories, conceptual role semantics, and their competitors. These questions connect philosophy of mind to philosophy of language and to the foundational epistemological issues raised in every area of philosophy.
