# Brentano's Thesis: Intentionality as the Mark of the Mental

Franz Brentano proposed, in *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874), that intentionality — the directedness of mental acts toward objects — is the distinguishing mark of mental phenomena. Every mental act (perceiving, judging, desiring, loving, hating) is directed toward an object; this directedness is not an accidental feature but the defining property of the mental. A rock does not point at anything. A neuron does not represent anything, except perhaps in a derived sense. But a belief is always a belief *about* something; a desire is always a desire *for* something; a fear is always a fear *of* something. This aboutness or directedness is what Brentano called intentionality, reviving the medieval scholastic concept of *esse intentionale* — the intentional being that an object has when it is known by an intellect.

Brentano called the relevant phenomenon "intentional inexistence" — the object of a mental act "inexists" in the act, exists "immanently" within it. When I think of a unicorn, no real unicorn exists, but my thought is genuinely directed toward it. The unicorn has intentional inexistence: it is present as the object of thought. This contrasts with physical phenomena: stones and neurons do not point beyond themselves in this way.

The thesis has a formal structure:

- P1: Mental phenomena are distinguished from physical phenomena by a unique internal characteristic.
- P2: That characteristic is directedness toward an object — intentional inexistence.
- C: Intentionality is the defining mark of the mental.

Three features of Brentano's intentionality deserve noting. First, every mental act is directed toward an object — the thesis is exceptionless. Second, the intentional object may or may not exist outside the mind. Third, different mental acts can be directed toward the same object under different descriptions: one might admire Cicero while despising Tully without knowing they are the same person, showing that intentional content — the mode of presentation under which the object is given — can differ even when the object is numerically identical.

## Husserl's Development and the Two Challenges

Edmund Husserl, Brentano's most distinguished student, took intentionality as the founding concept of phenomenology. Husserl refined the analysis by distinguishing the *noesis* (the intentional act), the *noema* (the intentional content or "sense"), and the *object* intended. The noema is not the object itself but the sense through which the object is given — an abstract content that mediates between act and world. This allowed Husserl to account for the fine-grained structure of intentional experience, including the difference between veridical perception, hallucination, and imagination, all of which can share the same noema while differing in their relations to the world. His *Logical Investigations* (1900–01) and *Ideas I* (1913) develop intentionality into the center of a systematic transcendental philosophy.

The thesis faces two immediate challenges. The universality problem: do all mental states have intentionality? Moods — generalized anxiety, free-floating depression — seem to lack a specific object. Pains seem to be about bodily locations rather than having propositional content. Phenomenal qualities — the redness of red experience — seem to be features of experience rather than representations of something further. The extension problem presses in the other direction: do some non-mental states have intentionality? Thermostats respond differentially to temperature; biological systems represent their environments; photographs and traffic signs are about things. Brentano thought physical phenomena lacked intentionality, but biological and computational systems complicate this. Searle's distinction between original and derived intentionality attempts to address this: thermostats and photographs have only derived intentionality — intentionality assigned by conscious interpreters — whereas mental states have original or intrinsic intentionality. Whether this distinction can be made out without circular appeal to consciousness is a central debate. In contemporary analytic philosophy, Brentano's thesis functions as a constraint rather than a definition: any theory of mind must explain what makes mental states intentional, and the accounts offered by Fodor, Dretske, Millikan, and Brandom are direct successors of the question Brentano placed at the center of psychology.
