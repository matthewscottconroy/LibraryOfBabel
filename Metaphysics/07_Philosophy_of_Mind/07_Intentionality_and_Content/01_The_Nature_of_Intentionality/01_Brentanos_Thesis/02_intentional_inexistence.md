# Intentional Inexistence

Brentano's term "intentional inexistence" can mislead: "inexistence" here does not mean non-existence but the mode of immanent being that an object has *within* a mental act. The term is drawn from medieval scholastic theories of cognition, particularly Aquinas's distinction between the *esse naturale* of a form — its mode of being in a natural substance — and its *esse intentionale* — its mode of being in an intellect. When the mind knows fire, the form of fire exists in the mind intentionally, but the mind does not thereby become hot. Brentano modernized this by treating the mental act as the primary bearer of intentional properties and the object as what the act is directed toward.

The concept creates an immediate philosophical puzzle. Central is the problem of non-existent objects:

- P1: When I think of Sherlock Holmes, my thought has an intentional object.
- P2: Intentional objects are what mental acts are about.
- P3: Sherlock Holmes does not exist.
- C: A mental act can be about something that does not exist.

This conclusion is hard to avoid but difficult to explain. If intentional objects must in some sense be present to the mind, and if Sherlock Holmes does not exist, what exactly is present?

Three major strategies address this. Meinong's response was to posit a rich ontology: some objects *exist* (this cup), some *subsist* (numbers, universals), and some neither exist nor subsist (round squares, golden mountains) — they nonetheless have "being" as objects of thought. Meinong's Principle of the Independence of Sosein (being-so) from Sein (being) holds that an object can have properties regardless of whether it exists. This allows genuine aboutness in cases of empty reference and is developed by Graham Priest within a paraconsistent framework capable of handling contradictory objects. Most analytic philosophers follow Bertrand Russell's critique: Meinong's ontology is promiscuous and violates Occam's Razor.

Russell's theory of descriptions dissolves the puzzle by distinguishing grammatical form from logical form. Names like "Sherlock Holmes" are disguised definite descriptions — "the fictional detective created by Conan Doyle who lived at Baker Street..." — and thinking about Sherlock Holmes is thinking about whatever satisfies that description. When nothing satisfies the description, there is no absent object; there is only a quantificational claim that happens to be unsatisfied. Frege's sense-reference distinction offers a third route: the *sense* (*Sinn*) of an expression — the way it presents its object — is always present even when the *reference* (*Bedeutung*, the object itself) is absent. Mental acts always engage a sense, and it is via the sense that the act is about the world. When the object does not exist, the sense alone is what the act engages.

## Husserl's Noema and Contemporary Relevance

Husserl developed intentional inexistence into his theory of the *noema*: the intentional content of an act — the way the object is presented to consciousness. The noema is neither the act itself (the noesis) nor the object (which may not exist) but an abstract structure mediating between them. Every act has a noema even when it has no object; and two acts with different noemata can be directed at the same object, as when "the morning star" and "the evening star" present Venus under different modes of presentation.

Contemporary philosophy of mind tends to analyze intentionality in terms of mental content — representational states with propositional or conceptual content — and to locate the philosophical action in what fixes that content. The problem of intentional inexistence resurfaces in debates about mental files (singular representations of individuals), fictional characters in philosophy of language, and the extent to which thought about perceptually absent objects requires special resources beyond those used in present-directed thought. The scholastic concept has proven remarkably durable precisely because the problem it names — how a mental act can be genuinely about something that does not exist — does not yield to any simple solution.
