# Intentionality and Consciousness

Two major traditions in philosophy of mind have different accounts of the relationship between intentionality and consciousness, and the difference shapes one's entire philosophy of mind. The analytic tradition has historically treated them as orthogonal: a mental state can be intentional without being conscious — unconscious beliefs, dormant knowledge, tacit grammatical competence — and arguably conscious without being intentional, as in raw sensations with no representational content. The phenomenological tradition treats intentionality and consciousness as internally related: consciousness is always consciousness-of-something, and intentionality is always accompanied by phenomenal character.

The phenomenological view, developed most systematically by Husserl, holds that intentionality and consciousness are two aspects of the same phenomenon. Every act of consciousness has a noetic aspect (the intentional act), a noematic aspect (the intentional content or sense through which the object is given), and a phenomenal dimension (the felt character of the experience). These are analytically distinguishable but not ontologically separable: every conscious experience is an experiencing-of-something, and every intentional act is phenomenally saturated. The implication is that phenomenal consciousness is essentially intentional — consciousness without an object is not merely unusual but impossible. To be conscious is already to be conscious *of* something.

This phenomenological claim generates an important thesis in contemporary analytic philosophy: representationalism about consciousness. If all consciousness is intentional — always directed toward objects via intentional content — then the phenomenal character of an experience might be exhausted by its representational content. The redness of my red experience is its representing things as red; the painfulness of pain is its representing bodily damage. Tye's PANIC theory holds that phenomenal states are Poised, Abstract, Nonconceptual, Intentional Contents — they represent the world in a fine-grained, non-conceptual way, and their phenomenal character is constituted by this representational content. The argument for representationalism often appeals to the *transparency of experience*: when I introspect my experience of red, I seem to see through the experience to the red surface, not to detect a mental intermediary. Phenomenal character is "transparent" — it consists in representing external properties rather than in having intrinsic qualitative properties over and above those representations.

## The Residue of Qualia and the Central Tension

The opposing view holds that there is a residue of phenomenal character that cannot be reduced to intentional content. Even if pain represents tissue damage, the *hurting* quality of pain seems to be something over and above the representation. Block's distinction between access consciousness (availability for reasoning and behavior) and phenomenal consciousness (what it is like to have an experience) is directly relevant: representationalism may account for access consciousness — the availability of content for downstream processing — while leaving phenomenal consciousness unexplained. The inverted qualia thought experiment illustrates the gap: two subjects who are functionally and representationally identical but whose color experiences are systematically inverted both represent the same color properties and make the same discriminations. If inverted qualia are coherent, phenomenal character is not fully determined by representational content, and representationalism is false.

The analytic tradition is comfortable with unconscious intentional states — beliefs, desires, and tacit knowledge that influence behavior without reaching consciousness. Freudian unconscious beliefs, implicit attitudes revealed by social psychology, and the grammatical competence of fluent speakers are all candidates. This breaks the tight connection between intentionality and consciousness that phenomenology assumes.

The relationship between intentionality and consciousness therefore generates a central structural tension in philosophy of mind. If intentionality is logically independent of consciousness, then naturalizing intentionality — giving a causal, functional, or teleological account of how mental states come to be about things — does not automatically illuminate consciousness. The hard problem remains separate and unaddressed. If intentionality is essentially bound up with consciousness, as phenomenologists hold, then naturalizing intentionality requires naturalizing consciousness, and the two problems are not separable. How one resolves the intentionality-consciousness relationship shapes whether one thinks the hard problem is a distinct problem requiring a distinct solution, or whether solving one automatically addresses the other.
