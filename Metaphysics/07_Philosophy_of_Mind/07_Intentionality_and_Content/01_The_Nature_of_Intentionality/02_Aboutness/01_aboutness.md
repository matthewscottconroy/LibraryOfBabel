# Aboutness: The Structure of Intentional Content

Intentional states are "about" things. But "aboutness" involves several distinct dimensions that need to be kept separate. The intentional content of a state is what it represents — specified by a "that"-clause in natural language. My belief that it will rain has the content *that it will rain*. The intentional object is the thing the state is about. My fear of the spider is about the spider. Crucially, the intentional object can be non-existent — I can think about Sherlock Holmes — and a single object can be presented under different modes. I believe the morning star is bright without believing the evening star is bright, even though both names refer to Venus. The third dimension is the mode of presentation: how the object is given to the mental state. This is Frege's *Sinn* — the sense or descriptive content that determines which object a representation picks out and explains why two representations with the same object can differ informationally.

The mode of presentation explains why intentional contexts are intensional (with an "s") rather than extensional. In ordinary referential contexts, substituting a co-referential term preserves truth value: if Hesperus = Phosphorus, then anything true of Hesperus is true of Phosphorus. But in intentional contexts this fails. Formally:

- B(John, "the morning star is bright") can be true
- "morning star" = "evening star" (both co-refer to Venus)
- B(John, "the evening star is bright") can be false

Intentional states relate subjects not merely to objects but to objects-as-presented — to intensional contents. Any theory of mental content must explain this.

## Non-Existent Objects and Contemporary Theories

Mental states can be about non-existent objects: fictional characters, impossible objects, golden mountains. Meinong held that non-existent objects have a kind of being (*Sosein* — being-characterized) even without existence. Russell analyzed apparent reference to non-existents as disguised quantification over descriptive conditions. Frege held that the sense of a term exists even when its reference does not, and that mental acts engage senses directly. Contemporary philosophy addresses aboutness through several competing accounts.

Causal-informational theories (Dretske, Fodor) hold that a state S is about X if S carries information about X — if S's occurrence is lawfully correlated with or caused by Xs. This handles the core cases but faces the disjunction problem: horse-in-poor-lighting can also cause cow-representations, so the simple causal account makes the content "cow-or-horse-in-poor-lighting" rather than just "cow." Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory responds: the horse-in-poor-lighting case causes COW only because cows do; the cows are the basic case, the horses dependent. Teleosemantic theories (Millikan, Papineau) ground content in biological function: a state S is about X if S has the proper function of tracking X — if it was selected by evolution or learning because of its connection to X. This handles misrepresentation naturally, since misrepresentation is just failure to fulfill a proper function. Inferentialist theories (Brandom, Price) hold that the content of a concept is determined by its inferential role — what it licenses and what licenses it in the practice of giving and asking for reasons. Two-dimensional semantic theories (Chalmers) distinguish a primary intension (what the concept picks out as a function of how the actual world turns out) from a secondary intension (what it picks out in possible worlds given a fixed actual-world referent).

Each theory handles the three dimensions of aboutness — content, object, and mode of presentation — differently. Which theory handles the normativity of content (misrepresentation), the indeterminacy of content (what fixes which causally relevant thing is the content), and the problem of non-existent objects most satisfactorily is the central criterion for evaluation, and no theory currently achieves consensus on all three.
