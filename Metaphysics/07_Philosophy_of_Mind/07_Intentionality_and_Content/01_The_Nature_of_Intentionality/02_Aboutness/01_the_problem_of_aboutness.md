# The Problem of Aboutness

Consider a simple case: the thought that there is coffee in the kitchen. This thought is about coffee and about the kitchen. It is not the coffee — it does not smell or taste like coffee. It is not causally identical to the coffee — many thoughts about coffee are not caused by coffee, as when I think about coffee abstractly, and many coffee-caused brain states are not thoughts about coffee, as when coffee affects my alertness without being thought about. The causal and intrinsic properties of the thought seem insufficient to determine its object. So what makes this thought be about coffee?

This is sometimes called the "Brentano problem" and sometimes the problem of "original intentionality." It is one of the hardest problems in philosophy of mind precisely because aboutness does not seem to be a natural relation. It cannot be identified with spatial proximity, causal contact, or structural similarity — a photograph of Lincoln is structurally similar to Lincoln in some respects, but that is not what makes it represent Lincoln (the same photograph would represent someone else if we had different conventions). The relation of aboutness is irreducibly normative: it involves a standard of correctness, a notion of what the state is *supposed to* be about.

Three dimensions of difficulty are worth distinguishing. The normativity problem: thoughts can be wrong — I can think there is coffee when there is not. Misrepresentation is essential to intentionality, but what makes it the case that a thought *misrepresents* rather than simply represents something else? Any purely causal account struggles here. If a thought is caused by a horse-in-poor-lighting, why isn't it a correct thought about whatever caused it — that is, about horse-in-poor-lighting? To say the thought *misrepresents* requires a standard of correctness that the thought fails to meet. Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein on rule-following amplifies this: nothing in the natural facts seems to fix what the correct application of a concept is.

The indeterminacy problem: for any given thought, there are many things it might be about. My thought about coffee is causally related to coffee beans, to farms in Colombia, to caffeine molecules, to stimulation of my taste receptors. What determines which of these many causally relevant things is the "content" of the thought? Quine's argument from the indeterminacy of translation shows that behavioral dispositions underdetermine the content of linguistic expressions; a parallel argument applies to mental states.

The problem of non-existent objects: I can think about Sherlock Holmes, unicorns, past events, impossible objects like round squares. How can a thought be genuinely about something that does not exist? No causal or information-theoretic account straightforwardly handles this: there is no causal connection to Sherlock Holmes, and no information channel to him.

## The Disjunction Problem and Grice's Contribution

Fodor made the normativity and indeterminacy problems precise through the disjunction problem. Suppose a simple causal theory says that the concept COW is tokened whenever cows cause it. But horse-in-poor-lighting conditions can also cause tokenings of COW. So by the simple causal theory, COW would represent *cow-or-horse-in-poor-lighting*, not just cow. But COW represents cows — the horse-in-poor-lighting case is a misrepresentation. Fodor's proposed solution is asymmetric dependence: the horse-in-poor-lighting case causes COW only because cows do. Remove the cows, and horses would cease to cause COW. The cows are the basic case; the others are dependent on it. This asymmetry is supposed to select the right disjunct as content. Critics dispute whether asymmetric dependence solves the problem or merely redescribes it.

H.P. Grice's analysis of natural and non-natural meaning bears on aboutness at the linguistic level. Grice distinguished natural meaning (smoke means fire, spots mean measles — a causal-nomological relation) from non-natural meaning (what speakers mean by their utterances — involving the speaker's intention to produce a belief in the audience by means of the audience's recognition of that very intention). This analysis grounds linguistic meaning in mental intentionality — in the speaker's intentions — but does not solve the problem of mental aboutness. It shows that linguistic aboutness can be reduced to mental aboutness, making the problem of how brain states can be about things the primary target. That target remains incompletely hit by any currently available theory.
