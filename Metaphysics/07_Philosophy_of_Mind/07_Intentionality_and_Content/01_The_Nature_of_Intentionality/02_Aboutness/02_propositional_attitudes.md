# Propositional Attitudes

A propositional attitude is a mental state characterized by a subject's bearing a certain psychological relation — the "attitude" — to a proposition. The term is Bertrand Russell's. Examples: believing that it will rain tomorrow, hoping that the flight is on time, fearing that the project will fail, desiring that the world be better. Every such state is individuated partly by its propositional content — what the belief, hope, fear, or desire is *about*.

The structure is formally representable: **Att(s, p)**, where s is the subject, Att is the attitude type, and p is the proposition. Believes(Mary, that it is raining); Desires(John, that the project succeeds). This structure preserves the intensionality of attitude reports: substituting co-referential expressions in the position of p can change truth value even when the objects referred to are identical.

Propositional attitudes are the backbone of folk psychology — our everyday practice of explaining and predicting behavior by attributing mental states with content. She took an umbrella because she *believed* it would rain and *wanted* to stay dry. He left the meeting because he *feared* a confrontation. These explanations are powerful enough to be the foundation of much social and cognitive science. The relationship between folk psychology and scientific psychology is contested. Eliminativists (Churchland, Stich) argue that folk psychological categories like "belief" and "desire" are fundamentally inadequate and will be replaced by mature neuroscience. Defenders (Davidson, Dennett, Fodor) argue that folk psychology is either irreplaceable — because of multiple realizability and the normative constraints on rationality — or that its categories will be vindicated at some level of scientific explanation.

## Direction of Fit, Propositions, and Fodor's LOT

Functionalism distinguishes the attitude types by functional role: beliefs are updated by evidence, combine with other beliefs according to inference principles, and guide action together with desires. Desires are not directly evidence-sensitive but motivate action toward making their content true. Searle offers a complementary account based on direction of fit. Beliefs have a mind-to-world direction of fit: a belief aims to match the world and is defective if it does not. Desires have a world-to-mind direction of fit: a desire aims to change the world to match it and is satisfied when the world comes to match the content.

A deeper question concerns the propositions themselves. Fregean propositions are abstract, objective senses composed of modes of presentation, structured by logical relations among their components. Russellian propositions are complexes composed of the actual objects, properties, and relations they concern — "singular propositions" containing Mars itself (the planet). Possible-worlds propositions identify propositions with sets of possible worlds, treating them as unstructured; this creates difficulties because all necessary truths would be the same proposition — the set of all possible worlds.

Jerry Fodor's Language of Thought hypothesis offers a specific implementation. On Fodor's view, propositional attitudes are computational relations to mental representations — sentences in the Language of Thought (Mentalese). Believing that P is tokening a mental sentence with content P in the "belief box"; desiring that P is tokening the same mental sentence in the "desire box." Thought inherits productivity and systematicity from the combinatorial structure of the Language of Thought, just as language inherits them from syntactic structure. Whether thoughts have phenomenal character — whether there is something it is like to believe that it will rain — is contested. Husserl, Siewert, and Kriegel argue for cognitive phenomenology: even propositional attitudes have a distinctive phenomenal quality. Others hold that cognitive states are "phenomenologically thin" — their content is a theoretical posit of folk psychology rather than something presented in experience.
