# Derived vs. Original Intentionality

John Searle introduced a distinction that cuts to the heart of the naturalization project. A stop sign has intentionality — it means "stop" — but this intentionality is derived: it depends on conventions established by human minds. The sign does not mean anything intrinsically; it means what it means only because people have assigned it that meaning. By contrast, the beliefs and intentions of conscious agents seem to have original intentionality: they are about things in virtue of the agents' mental lives, not in virtue of being assigned meaning by others.

The distinction stated formally:

Derived intentionality: State S has intentionality derivatively if and only if S's aboutness is inherited from the original intentionality of conscious agents who have assigned content to S through conventions, stipulations, or practices.

Original intentionality: State S has intentionality originally if and only if S's aboutness is not inherited from any other intentional state but belongs to S in virtue of S's own nature — paradigmatically, in virtue of S's being a conscious mental state.

Examples of derived intentionality: written words, traffic signs, maps, computer outputs, photographs. Examples of original intentionality: beliefs, desires, perceptions, intentions. The distinction is central to Searle's Chinese Room argument against strong artificial intelligence. In the thought experiment, the room manipulates Chinese symbols according to formal rules and produces outputs that appear to a Chinese speaker outside as if they came from a fluent speaker. The room passes a Turing test for understanding Chinese. Yet Searle — who follows only syntactic rules and does not understand Chinese — argues that the room understands nothing. The formal symbol manipulation has only derived intentionality: the symbols mean what they mean only because designers and programmers have assigned them meanings. No original intentionality — and therefore no genuine understanding — is produced by computation. The argument is directed against the thesis that syntactic manipulation is sufficient for semantic content: if genuine mental states require original intentionality, and original intentionality requires consciousness, then no computational system can be genuinely intentional unless it is conscious.

## Naturalist Responses and the Debate's Stakes

Naturalists and functionalists push back on several grounds. The distinction is unclear: what makes intentionality "original" rather than "derived"? Searle says consciousness, but this either presupposes what needs explaining — why does consciousness generate original intentionality? — or defines original intentionality in terms of consciousness without a substantive account of either. All intentionality may be "derived" in the relevant sense: even conscious mental states have their content derived from evolutionary history, causal-informational relations to the environment, and functional organization. The relevant difference between minds and signs is not a metaphysical difference in kind but a difference in the type of causal or functional relation involved. Levels of description matter: on functionalist accounts, what makes a state have original intentionality is not an irreducible qualitative property but its functional role in a system of sufficient complexity. Derived intentionality in signs and computers operates at the level of the user who assigns content; original intentionality operates at the level of the processing system's own functional organization. Sufficiently complex and appropriately organized systems could then have original intentionality, including artificial ones.

The debate over derived versus original intentionality is tied to broader questions: whether consciousness is necessary for genuine mentality, whether the Turing test is a sufficient criterion for intelligence, whether biological organisms have a special metaphysical status with respect to intentionality, and whether naturalistic theories of content can succeed. Philosophers who accept that consciousness is necessary for original intentionality tend toward property dualism or Searle's own biological naturalism. Philosophers who think intentionality can be fully naturalized tend to dissolve the distinction or argue that sufficiently complex functional organization is sufficient for original intentionality regardless of substrate. The dissolution move is more compelling if one is already a functionalist; the resistance move is more compelling if one takes the hard problem seriously. The debate about derived versus original intentionality is thus continuous with the debate about whether there is a hard problem of consciousness at all.
