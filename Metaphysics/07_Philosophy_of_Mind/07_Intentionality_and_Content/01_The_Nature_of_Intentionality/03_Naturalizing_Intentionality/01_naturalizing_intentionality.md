# Naturalizing Intentionality

If mental states have intentional properties — aboutness, semantic content — and if intentionality is not reducible to physical properties, then intentionality is a non-physical property that supports dualism about the mind. Physicalists must show that intentionality is naturalizable: accountable in physical or biological terms without remainder. This is not merely a metaphysical concern; it affects cognitive science, which explains mental processes computationally, and philosophy of language, which grounds linguistic meaning in mental content. Fodor captured the stakes memorably: "If aboutness is real, it must be really something else" — something expressible in physical-biological vocabulary without remainder.

Causal theories are the most direct attempt. A mental state S represents X if S is caused by X, or by instances of the type X. The concept COW represents cows because it is causally connected to actual cows — cows cause the tokening of my cow-concept. The central difficulty is the disjunction problem: what makes it the case that COW represents cows rather than *cow-or-horse-in-poor-lighting*? Both cows and horses-in-poor-lighting cause tokenings of COW. A simple causal theory cannot distinguish the intended content from the wider causal disjunction. Any adequate causal theory must explain why the horse-in-poor-lighting case is a misrepresentation of a cow rather than a correct representation of horse-in-poor-lighting.

Informational theories (Dretske, *Knowledge and the Flow of Information*, 1981; *Naturalizing the Mind*, 1995) hold that a state carries information about X when its occurrence is nomically correlated with X — when X's occurring is a reliable signal for the state. Dretske's solution to the disjunction problem appeals to a learning period: during concept formation, it represents whatever is nomically responsible for its formation. After formation, the concept retains its content even when triggered by non-standard causes. Teleosemantic theories (Millikan, *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories*, 1984; Papineau, *Philosophical Naturalism*, 1993) hold that a state represents X because it has the biological function of tracking X — selected by evolution because of its connection to X. The frog's fly-detector represents flies because it was selected for reliable connection to flies; even when it fires in response to a small black bead, it misrepresents, because its function is defined by evolutionary history. Teleosemantics handles misrepresentation naturally: a representation can misfire while still having the function of representing X, just as a heart can fail to pump blood while still having the function of pumping blood.

## Inferentialism, Two-Dimensionalism, and the Meta-Question

Inferentialist theories (Sellars; Brandom, *Making It Explicit*, 1994) hold that the content of a concept is determined by its inferential role — what follows from it, what it follows from, what situations license its application. "Dog" represents dogs because of its inferential connections to other concepts (animal, four-legged, barks) and to perceptual and action states. This handles normativity naturally: the normative standards governing concept application are the standards of the inferential practice. But it faces the holism objection: if every concept's content depends on all other concepts in the inferential web, any change in belief changes all contents, making content comparison across belief systems impossible. It also faces a circularity worry: inferential role is itself a semantic notion and may not genuinely reduce intentionality.

Chalmers's two-dimensional framework distinguishes a primary intension (what the concept picks out as a function of how the actual world turns out to be) from a secondary intension (what it picks out in other possible worlds given a fixed actual-world referent). Naturalization proceeds via the primary intension, which can be understood functionally — determined by the cognitive role of the concept independently of which actual things it refers to.

There is a meta-question about the project itself: what would count as success? If the goal is strict reduction — a conceptual analysis of "represents" or "is about" in purely physical terms — success requires eliminating all intentional vocabulary. If the goal is merely ontological — to show there is nothing more to intentionality than natural facts, even if the vocabulary is irreducible — the bar is lower. Many philosophers now aim for the weaker goal, arguing that intentional properties can be realized in natural properties without being analytically reducible to them. None of the available approaches commands consensus, and each faces serious objections. The naturalization project is still active and unresolved — which is why it is still, after several decades of intensive work, one of the central open problems of philosophy of mind.
