# The Naturalization Project

The project of naturalizing intentionality aims to explain mental content — the aboutness of mental states — in terms acceptable to naturalism: causal, functional, evolutionary, or information-theoretic terms that make no irreducible reference to norms, meanings, or conventions. The underlying motivation is that a complete naturalistic theory of mind should not leave intentionality as a brute, unexplained phenomenon.

Intentionality appears to involve normativity: a thought can be correct or incorrect, a representation can be accurate or inaccurate. Norms are not obviously natural entities — they do not appear in the basic vocabulary of physics. If intentionality essentially involves norms, and if norms cannot be naturalized, then intentionality cannot be naturalized, and the naturalistic project faces a fundamental obstacle. The difficulty is not merely epistemic (we do not yet know how to do it) but potentially principled: norms may resist naturalization in kind.

Naturalization typically proceeds by identifying a naturalistically acceptable relation R such that the following holds:

Content Determination Thesis: State S has content C if and only if S bears R to C.

For the account to be genuinely naturalizing, R must be specifiable in non-intentional, non-normative vocabulary — causal relations, information-theoretic relations, biological functions, or computational relations that can themselves be characterized without implicit appeal to meaning or norms.

## Central Problems

The disjunction problem (or misrepresentation problem): how can a representation ever be false? Suppose state S represents C. If S can also be caused by C*, then by the simple causal theory, S represents *C or C*. The disjunction problem demands an account of why the "true" content is C rather than any causal antecedent. Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory responds: C* causes S only because C does; remove C from the causal picture, and C* would cease to cause S. C is the "normal" cause; C* the "deviant" one.

The indeterminacy problem: even granting that content is determined by relation R, there will often be multiple candidates satisfying R equally well. Quine's argument from the indeterminacy of translation shows this for language: behavioral evidence underdetermines translation between languages. The same argument generalizes to mental content. Is the content of my "rabbit" thought *rabbit*, *undetached rabbit-part*, or *rabbit-stage*? All three candidates are equally supported by causal-behavioral evidence. Something must break the tie, and it is not clear that any naturalistically acceptable property can do so.

The normativity problem: more fundamentally, misrepresentation presupposes a norm — a standard relative to which the representation is correct or incorrect. It is not clear that any purely descriptive natural fact can constitute a norm. Kripke's Wittgenstein argues that no fact about the past — dispositions, causal history, brain states — can determine what it is *correct* to do in a new case. If this is right, naturalistic content-fixing relations can at most identify patterns of actual application, not standards of correct application. The gap between what-is-done and what-ought-to-be-done may be ineliminable.

Strategies have been developed for each problem. Fodor's asymmetric dependence addresses the disjunction problem. Optimal-conditions theories (Goldman, Tye) hold that S represents X if S is caused by X under optimal or normal conditions defined by the evolutionary or functional role of the system. Teleological norms (Millikan, Papineau) ground norms in natural functions: misrepresentation is failing to perform an evolutionarily assigned function. Whether any of these strategies succeeds in genuinely grounding normativity — rather than describing patterns of actual use — is the central contested question. The naturalization project is still active, and it remains one of the central open problems of philosophy of mind.
