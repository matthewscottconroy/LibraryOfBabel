# Challenges to Naturalizing Intentionality

The project of naturalizing intentionality faces persistent challenges beyond the disjunction and indeterminacy problems. Several philosophers have argued that these challenges are not merely technical difficulties but point to a fundamental obstacle: intentionality essentially involves normativity, and normativity cannot be naturalized.

The most influential formulation of the normativity challenge comes from Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein's rule-following considerations in *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982). Kripke constructs a sceptical paradox about meaning: no fact about a person's past behavior, dispositions, or causal history determines which rule they were following. Consider addition. I have used the "+" sign many times and always given the sum. A sceptic asks: how do I know my past use was in accordance with the *addition* function, rather than some "quus" function — defined as x + y when x, y < 1000, and = 5 otherwise? My past behavior is compatible with both rules. My finite past use underdetermines infinitely many possible rules. My disposition to answer "5 + 7 = 12" rather than "5" is consistent with my following the quus rule all along and just not having encountered the relevant case. The conclusion is that there are no facts about what I mean by "+." More generally, there are no facts about meaning — and this extends to mental content. Kripke's sceptical solution abandons the demand for meaning-facts and instead analyses meaning-attributions as moves in a social practice: to say someone means addition by "+" is to say they belong to a community that will respond to their answers as correct or incorrect in the relevant ways. Meaning is social norm, not individual mental fact.

The holism challenge presses differently. The content of a belief depends on the contents of many other beliefs — beliefs are defined partly by their inferential relations to other beliefs. Believing that there is a cat on the mat requires having the concepts CAT and MAT, which in turn require having a host of other concepts. This conceptual holism makes content determination global: you cannot assign content to an individual state without assigning content to the entire web of beliefs. But causal and information-theoretic approaches tend to be atomistic: they assign content to individual states based on individual causal-informational profiles. Holism also creates problems for content comparison across minds and over time. If my concept RED has content partly in virtue of its inferential connections, and your concept RED has a somewhat different inferential profile, do we share the concept?

## The Background Challenge and the Consciousness Requirement

From phenomenology and embodied cognition, a deeper challenge concerns the pre-intentional background that makes intentionality possible. Intentional states are not isolated mental events but are embedded in practical skills, bodily habits, and social practices. Heidegger's analysis of tool-use is instructive: when I am hammering skillfully, the hammer is "ready-to-hand" (*zuhanden*) — I act through it without representing it as an object. Representation and intentionality emerge against a background of skillful, non-representational engagement with the world. Dreyfus developed this into a critique of classical cognitive science: computational models assume that intelligent behavior can be captured by explicit representations and rules, but skilled human behavior is not rule-governed in this sense. Naturalizing intentionality as a simple causal or informational relation ignores this constitutive background.

Searle argues that intentionality requires consciousness: only conscious states have original intentionality; signs, computers, and unconscious neural states have merely derived intentionality, assigned by conscious interpreters. If correct, naturalizing intentionality cannot proceed independently of naturalizing consciousness, and the hard problem is the true obstacle. This creates a potential circularity: if consciousness cannot be explained without intentionality (because consciousness is always consciousness-of something) and intentionality cannot be explained without consciousness, neither can be naturalized independently.

Defenders of naturalization respond that biological or computational facts are rich enough to fix determinate contents, even if behavioral dispositions alone are not; that two-factor theories can distinguish atomistic "core" content from holistic inferential role; that embodied and enactive theories can expand naturalization to include bodily and environmental factors; and that consciousness is itself naturalizable, making the dependency benign. Whether any of these responses succeeds depends on issues that are themselves contested. The challenges to naturalizing intentionality are not solved problems but ongoing debates that define the frontier of philosophy of mind.
