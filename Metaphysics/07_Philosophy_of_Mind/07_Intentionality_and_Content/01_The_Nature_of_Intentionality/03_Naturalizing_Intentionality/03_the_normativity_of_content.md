# The Normativity of Content

Mental states can be correct or incorrect, accurate or inaccurate, appropriate or inappropriate. A belief is correct if what it represents is the case; a desire is satisfied if the world comes to match its content. This normative dimension seems to be an essential feature of mental representation, not an optional add-on. And it creates the deepest challenge for naturalistic theories of content.

The argument against naturalization:

- P1: If mental states have content, they are subject to normative assessment — correctness and incorrectness.
- P2: Normative properties cannot be reduced to descriptive natural properties.
- C: Mental content cannot be reduced to natural properties.

P2 echoes the meta-ethical claim that "ought" cannot be derived from "is" (Hume's Guillotine, Moore's naturalistic fallacy). If it holds for mental content as it arguably holds for moral norms, then any naturalistic theory of content fails to capture content's essential normative character.

The misrepresentation problem makes this concrete. What makes it the case that my thought about a cat misrepresents the situation when I am looking at a fox in dim light? A simple causal account says the thought is caused by whatever causes it. If a fox-in-dim-light complex causes my CAT token, the causal account seems to say the thought represents fox-in-dim-light — not that it misrepresents a cat. Error requires a standard of correctness — a way things ought to be for the thought to be correct — that the representation fails to meet. Where does this standard come from?

## Teleological Approaches and Their Limits

Teleological approaches ground normativity in biological function. A representation has a teleofunction — a function assigned by natural selection or by learning. It is correct when it performs this function and incorrect when it fails to. Millikan's *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories* (1984) and *Biosemantics* (1989) argue that a perceptual representation functions to indicate a distal environmental feature, the function selected for by evolution. A representation triggered by an atypical cause — the fox, not the cat — thereby misrepresents, because it is not performing its normal function. Millikan distinguishes between a representation's Normal conditions (with capital N — the conditions in which it performs its function) and its actual conditions. Content is determined by the Normal conditions: what the representation is "supposed to" covary with given its evolutionary history.

Critics argue that this teleological move does not genuinely capture normativity. Natural selection explains why a mechanism exists and what it was designed to do, but it does not tell us what it *should* do in a normative sense. The link between evolutionary history and normative correctness is not self-evident. Furthermore, evolution selects for outcomes that increased reproductive fitness in ancestral environments — which may not be the same as selecting for truth-tracking. A representation that systematically misrepresents some feature of the environment in a fitness-enhancing way might still be evolutionarily "correct" while being semantically in error.

## Social Approaches and the Residual Difficulty

Brandom's inferentialist account (*Making It Explicit*, 1994) grounds normativity in the constitutive role of reason in social practices. A mental state's correct conditions are fixed by the inferential norms governing its use within the practice of giving and asking for reasons. To possess a concept is to have mastered the inferential commitments and entitlements associated with it. These norms are socially constituted — instituted and maintained by the community's practice of holding each other accountable. This approach takes normativity seriously by locating it in social practices, but it does not reduce normativity to non-normative natural facts — it explicates one kind of norm in terms of another. Whether this counts as "naturalizing" depends on how broadly one defines "natural": if social practices are natural phenomena, it may be naturalizing enough; if "natural" means "non-normative," the approach merely relocates rather than dissolves the normativity.

The deepest worry about all naturalizing approaches is that they presuppose what they are trying to explain. Causal theories presuppose that we can identify the "right" causal relation; but picking out the right relation seems to require normative concepts. Teleological theories presuppose that we can identify the proper function; but distinguishing proper function from malfunction seems to require normative concepts. Inferentialist theories are explicit that normativity is not further reducible. The normativity of content thus remains one of the most contested issues in philosophy of mind, and a primary challenge for any theory that aims to naturalize mental representation. Some take the non-naturalizability of normativity as a lesson about the limits of naturalism; others take it as a challenge to develop richer naturalisms that make room for normativity without treating it as irreducibly non-natural.
