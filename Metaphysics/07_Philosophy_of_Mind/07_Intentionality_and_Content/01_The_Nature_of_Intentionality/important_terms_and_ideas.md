# Important Terms and Ideas: The Nature of Intentionality

**Intentionality**
The "aboutness" or directedness of mental states—the property of being about, of, or directed toward something. Beliefs are about propositions, desires are about states of affairs, perceptions are about objects in the environment. Brentano proposed intentionality as the mark distinguishing mental from non-mental phenomena.

**Intentional Object**
The object toward which a mental state is directed. Crucially, intentional objects need not exist: one can believe in Santa Claus or desire a perpetual motion machine. The intentional object of a state is not necessarily the same as any real-world object.

**Intentional Inexistence**
Brentano's term for the mode of being of intentional objects: they "inexist" in the mental act—they are present in a peculiar, non-existential way within the mental state. The phrase captures the puzzling fact that intentional objects can be non-existent while still being genuinely "in" the act.

**Propositional Attitudes**
Mental states characterized by a relation to a proposition: belief that P, desire that P, hope that P, fear that P. The propositional content specifies what the state is about. Propositional attitude psychology is the commonsense framework of folk psychology.

**Opacity / Intensionality**
A feature of intentional contexts: substituting co-referring terms can change the truth value of sentences describing intentional states. "John believes Cicero was an orator" can be true while "John believes Tully was an orator" is false, even though Cicero = Tully. This opacity reflects the perspective-dependence of intentionality.

**Derived vs. Original Intentionality**
Searle's distinction: some things (books, computers) have intentionality only derivatively—borrowed from the minds of users. Mental states have original, intrinsic intentionality, not dependent on interpretation by others. This distinction motivates his rejection of computational accounts of mind.
