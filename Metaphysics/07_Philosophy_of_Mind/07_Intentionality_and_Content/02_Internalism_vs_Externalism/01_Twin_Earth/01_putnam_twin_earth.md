# Putnam's Twin Earth

Consider Oscar, an ordinary English speaker in 1750, long before anyone had discovered the chemical composition of water. Oscar uses "water" freely — he drinks it, collects it, thinks about it. Now consider Twin Oscar, his exact double on a planet otherwise indistinguishable from Earth, who does the same. The two are molecule-for-molecule identical: same functional organization, same beliefs, same phenomenal states. The one difference is hidden from both of them. What fills the rivers and falls as rain on Twin Earth is not H₂O but XYZ — a distinct chemical compound with the same macroscopic properties. When Oscar says "water," he refers to H₂O. When Twin Oscar says "water," he refers to XYZ. Their mental states are identical; the extensions of their terms are not.

This is the setup of Putnam's "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), and the conclusion Putnam draws from it is uncompromising: meanings just ain't in the head.

The argument can be stated precisely:

- P1: Oscar and Twin Oscar are in identical internal (psychological) states.
- P2: Oscar's word "water" refers to H₂O; Twin Oscar's word "water" refers to XYZ.
- P3: The reference of "water" therefore differs between Oscar and Twin Oscar despite their identical internal states.
- C: The reference of a term is not determined solely by internal psychological states — it depends on the external environment.

Notice that this is a *reductio* of internalism. If meaning were determined by internal states alone, Oscar and Twin Oscar would mean the same thing by "water." But they manifestly do not — their terms have different extensions. So the internalist thesis is false. Something external — the actual chemical constitution of the substance in the speaker's environment — partly determines what the speaker means.

## The Division of Linguistic Labor and Natural Kind Terms

Putnam adds a second mechanism that reinforces the externalist conclusion. Most ordinary speakers cannot distinguish H₂O from XYZ by inspection; they cannot perform the relevant chemical analysis. Yet their term "water" refers to H₂O rather than to whatever superficially watery stuff they happen to encounter. What explains this? Putnam's answer is the **division of linguistic labor**: competent users of a natural kind term defer to experts — chemists, in this case — for the standards of correct application. The term's extension is fixed by the community's practices, including the practices of specialists who investigate the nature of the substance. Meaning is not a private mental affair; it is distributed across a linguistic community and anchored to how the world actually is.

This connects Putnam's semantic externalism to Saul Kripke's work on names and natural kinds in *Naming and Necessity* (1972). Kripke argues that natural kind terms are rigid designators — they pick out the same kind in every possible world — and that their reference is fixed by an initial "baptism" involving a causal-historical connection to the kind, not by any description the speaker associates with the term. Water is H₂O in every possible world in which water exists, regardless of what anyone believes about its composition. For Putnam and Kripke alike, the extension of a natural kind term tracks the real essence of the kind, discovered empirically, not any cluster of properties associated with it in the speaker's head.

## Implications for Philosophy of Mind

The Twin Earth argument has consequences that reach well beyond philosophy of language. If the content of Oscar's "water" thought is partly constituted by the actual nature of the liquid in his environment, then mental content is **wide** — it cannot be read off from internal states alone. Two subjects who are internally identical can differ in the contents of their mental states. Content is individuated in part by environment.

This challenges every internalist program in the philosophy of mind. Mental content cannot be fully characterized by computational or functional description of the brain alone, because two functionally identical systems in different environments will have different contents. The individuation of mental states for the purposes of psychological explanation becomes environment-relative. And the relationship between internal processing and intentional action becomes more complex: what an agent's thoughts are about is determined not by internal states alone but by states-plus-environment.

Critics have pressed the **slow switching** problem. Suppose Oscar is transplanted to Twin Earth. Initially his "water" thoughts are about H₂O; gradually, as he interacts with XYZ, his thoughts shift to being about XYZ. But at what point does the shift occur? The gradual nature of the transition resists any sharp answer, suggesting that wide content may be indeterminate in switching cases — an awkward result for a theory that was supposed to fix content precisely by appeal to the environment. Wide-content theorists typically respond that indeterminacy in switching cases is a feature shared by many semantic relations, not a special defect of externalism. The internalist alternative — narrow content individuated by functional role — faces its own problems, as we consider when turning to the debate between wide and narrow content.
