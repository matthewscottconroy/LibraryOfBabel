# Twin Earth and Semantic Externalism

Suppose you and your exact physical duplicate — molecule-for-molecule identical, sharing every belief, every sensation, every functional state — are placed in environments that differ in one hidden respect. The liquid you each call "water" looks the same, tastes the same, and behaves the same in every observable way. But yours is H₂O and your duplicate's is XYZ, a distinct chemical compound unknown to either of you. Putnam's question, posed in "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), is simple: do you mean the same thing by "water"?

The intuitive answer is no. Your "water" refers to H₂O; your duplicate's refers to XYZ. If Twin Earth were discovered tomorrow, your duplicate would say, "I was mistaken — what I called 'water' was XYZ all along, not H₂O." The reference was always to XYZ, fixed by what the substance actually is rather than by any description associated with the term. And this means your mental states — identical in every internal respect — have different contents. Meanings just ain't in the head.

This founding case for **semantic externalism** undermines the descriptivist theory of meaning associated with Frege and Russell. If meaning were determined by the mental description associated with a term — the substance that is colorless, tasteless, quenches thirst, falls as rain — then you and your duplicate would mean the same thing, since you share all the relevant descriptions. But you do not. So descriptivism is false: the extension of a natural kind term is determined by the actual nature of the kind, not by the cluster of properties associated with it in the speaker's head.

## Wide Content, Narrow Content, and Indexicality

The Twin Earth argument extends naturally from language to mental content. If you believe "water is wet," your belief is about H₂O; if your duplicate has a molecule-for-molecule identical belief state, theirs is about XYZ. Identical internal states carry different contents depending on environmental context. This is **content externalism** — the view that mental content is partly constituted by the subject's external environment.

We can mark the distinction with a pair of concepts. **Wide content** (or broad content) is the content of a mental state as environmentally individuated: your water-thought has wide content about H₂O; your duplicate's has wide content about XYZ. Same internal state, different wide content. **Narrow content** (or functional content) is the content of a mental state as internally individuated — whatever the state shares between you and your duplicate. Both of you share the narrow content "the locally prevalent watery stuff," a description that picks out H₂O on Earth and XYZ on Twin Earth depending on context.

One illuminating way to understand wide content is as a form of **indexicality**. Context-sensitive expressions like "I," "here," and "now" refer to different things in different contexts of utterance even while the expression type and the speaker's internal state remain constant. "Water" functions similarly: its reference is fixed by the context — the local watery stuff — rather than by any internal description. This explains why you and your duplicate use the same word with the same internal associations but refer to different substances without either of you being in error about your own usage.

## The Significance of Natural Kinds

Putnam's argument applies most directly to **natural kind terms** — "water," "gold," "tiger," "heat" — that pick out kinds whose nature is determined by underlying constitution rather than superficial appearance. For such terms, reference is fixed not by the speaker's internal description but by the actual kind in the environment, discovered empirically. This connects semantics to metaphysics: what our terms mean constrains what the world must be like, and conversely, what the world is like — its actual microstructure — constrains what our terms mean. Kripke's parallel argument in *Naming and Necessity* (1972) establishes that natural kind terms are rigid designators, picking out the same kind in every possible world, with reference fixed by a causal-historical connection to the kind rather than by any associated description.

The Twin Earth argument generated the internalism/externalism debate that has dominated philosophy of mind and language for decades. It directly influenced Tyler Burge's extension of externalism to social and institutional factors, debates about psychological explanation and individualism, and the development of two-dimensional semantics as a framework for reconciling externalist intuitions with the cognitive significance of concepts. Whether narrow content can do the theoretical work that wide content's critics demand of it — explaining the connection between content and behavior, across contexts and switching scenarios — remains one of the central open questions in the theory of mental content.
