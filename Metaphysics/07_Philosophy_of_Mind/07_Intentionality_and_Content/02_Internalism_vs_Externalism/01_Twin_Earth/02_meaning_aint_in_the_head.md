# Meaning Ain't in the Head

There is a picture of meaning so natural that it goes almost unnoticed. On this picture — associated with Frege, Locke, and the broader analytic tradition up through the mid-twentieth century — meaning is individualistic. To understand "water" is to have a mental representation or description associated with the word: colorless, tasteless, odorless liquid that fills rivers and lakes and is essential for life. If two speakers have identical mental states associated with "water," they express identical meanings. Meaning is transparent to the competent speaker, accessible by introspection, fully determined by what is in the head.

This picture is foundational to classical epistemology and philosophy of language alike. It connects meaning to understanding, understanding to mental grasp, and mental grasp to the individual subject's mind. What Putnam's Twin Earth argument shows is that this picture is wrong.

Oscar and Twin Oscar have identical mental states associated with "water" — the same concepts, images, functional role, and descriptive associations. Yet their words refer to different substances (H₂O vs. XYZ). The extension of "water" therefore cannot be determined by the mental states shared between them. Something external — the actual nature of the substance in their environments — determines reference. Meanings, in the sense of extensions or referential content, are not in the head.

Notice that what is at stake here is primarily *extension* — the set of things the term picks out — rather than descriptive sense. Even if two speakers share all their descriptive associations, they may express different extensions. Extension is externally determined. Putnam's slogan marks a fault line in the entire individualistic picture of mind.

## The Extension to Mental Content

Putnam himself was primarily concerned with linguistic meaning, but the argument extends to mental content. Tyler Burge's work (*Individualism and the Mental*, 1979; *Thought and Object*, 2003) shows that the content of beliefs — not just words — is partly externally determined. If Oscar believes that the stuff in his glass is water, his belief has a truth condition determined by the actual nature of water: it is true if and only if the glass contains H₂O. The belief content — what the belief is *about* — is partly constituted by the external world.

This extension bears directly on the nature of propositional attitudes. If mental content is externally constituted, then the individuation of mental states for psychological explanation is not purely internal. Two subjects who are computationally or functionally identical might differ in their mental states in the sense that matters for intentional description. Fodor drew the consequence and resisted it. Though he accepted Putnam's semantic arguments for natural language, Fodor insisted that cognitive psychology should traffic in **narrow content** — the "internal" content shared by Oscar and Twin Oscar, the functional or conceptual role the state plays in the subject's psychology, abstracted from which environmental substance it picks out. His reason: psychology aims to explain behavior in terms of mental states, and behavior is determined by internal states, not by the environment. Two subjects who are internally identical will behave identically regardless of whether they are on Earth or Twin Earth. Wide content — the environmentally individuated content — cannot figure in causal explanations of behavior, because the external relatum that determines wide content is not what causes the behavior. Cognitive science, Fodor argued, should be methodologically solipsistic.

## The Cognitive Science Stakes

The debate between narrow-content individualism and wide-content anti-individualism continues to shape philosophy of cognitive science. The stakes are high. They determine what cognitive science is a science of — internal computations, or the organism-environment relation. They determine whether psychology can abstract from the subject's environmental embedding, or whether the embedding is constitutively relevant to the content of mental states. They have implications for the relationship between psychology and neuroscience, ecology, and social science.

Burge's response to Fodor is that folk psychology and scientific psychology do and should attribute mental states with wide content. The point of psychological attribution is to make sense of how subjects are related to their environment, and wide content is precisely what characterizes this relationship. Attributing beliefs with wide content explains why Oscar's behavior constitutes *fetching water* rather than merely fetching the clear liquid in his environment: it is because he has a belief with wide content about water that his action has the intentional description it does. The narrow content theorist can explain the physical motions; only wide content explains what Oscar was doing, in the sense that matters for understanding him as a rational agent.

We can put this more sharply. Narrow content, as a function from environments to extensions, has no intrinsic content of its own — it picks out different things in different contexts. If narrow content is what figures in psychological explanation, then psychological states are not really about anything in the world; they are only about whatever happens to be in the relevant environment. This threatens to dissolve the intentionality of mental states altogether, replacing it with a context-relative schema. The alternative — accepting wide content — preserves genuine intentionality at the cost of surrendering methodological individualism.

Putnam's slogan thus marks not just a point about natural language but a fundamental challenge to the individualistic picture of mind. Whether that challenge can be met — whether there is a coherent notion of narrow content that is both genuinely intentional and explanatorily adequate — is one of the central debates to which the internalism/externalism controversy keeps returning.
