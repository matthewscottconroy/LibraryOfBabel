# Wide Content

Oscar and Twin Oscar are internal duplicates, but their "water" thoughts are about different substances. This is the central datum that drives the distinction between wide and narrow content. **Wide content** is the content of a mental state that is partly constituted by factors external to the subject — features of the physical and social environment. It is not supervenient on internal states: two subjects can be internal duplicates while having mental states with different wide contents, because their environments differ. Oscar's water-thought has wide content about H₂O; Twin Oscar's has wide content about XYZ. The internal state is the same; the wide content is not.

The contrast with **narrow content** is equally important. Narrow content is the content of a mental state considered in abstraction from its environmental embedding. It supervenes on internal states: internal duplicates always share narrow content. Narrow content is often characterized as a function from possible environments to extensions — a two-dimensional content that picks out different extensions in different environments. In an H₂O environment, the narrow content picks out H₂O; in an XYZ environment, the same narrow content picks out XYZ.

We can illustrate this formally. Let W(Oscar, e) be the wide content of Oscar's water-thought in environment e (Earth), and W(Twin Oscar, e*) the wide content of Twin Oscar's water-thought in environment e* (Twin Earth). The externalist thesis is that W(Oscar, e) ≠ W(Twin Oscar, e*), despite Oscar and Twin Oscar sharing all their internal properties. Narrow content, by contrast, is whatever they share — the two-dimensional function that determines wide content once environment is specified.

## Three Arguments for Wide Content

The **Twin Earth argument** (Putnam, 1975) is the founding case. Internally identical subjects can have mental states with different extensions. Therefore, extension is not determined by internal states alone. Wide content is the externally individuated extension that differs between Oscar and Twin Oscar.

**Burge's social externalism** (*Individualism and the Mental*, 1979) extends the argument. Internally identical subjects embedded in communities with different concepts — different medical terminology, different conceptual frameworks — will have beliefs with different contents. A patient who has always deferred to the community's use of "arthritis" has beliefs about arthritis even if she has some misconceptions about the disease; a patient in a community where "arthritis" was used differently would have beliefs about something else. Content is socially as well as environmentally constituted.

The **referential argument** targets singular thoughts. When I am thinking *about water* — the very substance — my thought essentially involves that substance. Nothing purely internal can ground this de re aboutness. The thought is not merely about whatever happens to satisfy some description I associate with "water"; it is about the substance itself, and what the substance is determines what the thought is about.

## The Cognitive Science Debate

Wide content raises deep questions for cognitive science, and the debate about it has been among the most productive in philosophy of mind. Fodor's **autonomy argument** holds that psychological states should be individuated in a way that is autonomous from the environment, so that psychology can proceed without taking into account every feature of the agent's environmental and social context. Wide content violates this principle: individuating mental states by their wide content requires knowing the agent's environment, which a purely psychological theory should not need to do. Moreover, wide content is causally inert with respect to behavior. Two behaviorally identical subjects might have different wide contents; the internal state causes the behavior, and the external relatum that fixes wide content makes no causal contribution.

Burge's response is that folk psychology and scientific psychology do and should attribute mental states with wide content. Psychological attribution aims to explain how subjects are related to their environment, and wide content is precisely what characterizes this relationship. Attributing beliefs with wide content explains why Oscar's behavior constitutes *fetching water* rather than merely moving toward a clear liquid: he has a belief with wide content about water, and that is what makes his action intentionally describable as it is. The distinction between what Oscar's muscles do and what Oscar does — between physical motion and intentional action — is precisely the distinction between narrow and wide content.

## A Middle Path

One promising response to this disagreement distinguishes explanatory roles rather than insisting that one notion of content must do all theoretical work. Narrow content is relevant for explaining computational processes — the input-output relations that take place within the system, independent of what the system is tracking in the world. Wide content is relevant for explaining intentional action — how the agent is related to the world, what her thoughts are genuinely about. On this view, both notions are legitimate, and the conflict between internalism and externalism is partly an artifact of focusing on a single explanatory context.

This pluralism has been developed by Chalmers via two-dimensional semantics and by Gabriel Segal via the distinction between content and processing. The challenge is to show that the two notions are genuinely complementary — that narrow and wide content pick out different aspects of the same mental facts — rather than competing theories of a single phenomenon. If narrow content just is a schema for generating wide content given context, it is not clear that it has any intrinsic intentional character of its own, which threatens to collapse the distinction into the claim that there is only wide content and that some theories choose to study it from the inside. Whether that collapse is a reductio of the narrow content program or a vindication of it remains one of the live questions in the theory of mental representation.
