# Burge and the Arthritis Case

A patient visits his doctor with a complaint. He believes he has arthritis in his thigh. The doctor corrects him: arthritis is inflammation of the joints, not a condition that can occur in muscular tissue. The patient's belief is false — he has some rheumatic condition, but it is not arthritis by the community's standards of correct application.

Now consider a counterfactual variant. The same patient, with exactly the same internal states — the same neural organization, the same phenomenal experience, the same functional architecture — lives instead in a community where "arthritis" is used more broadly, covering certain muscular conditions as well as joint inflammation. In that community, the patient's belief that he has arthritis in his thigh would be true. The community's concept is different from ours.

This is Burge's case, introduced in "Individualism and the Mental" (1979), and the conclusion he draws from it is as stark as Putnam's: the content of a belief is not determined solely by the internal states of the believer. Something external — in this case, the social and linguistic practices of the community — partly constitutes what the believer believes.

The argument runs as follows:

- P1: The actual patient (A) and the counterfactual patient (A*) are internally identical.
- P2: A's belief that he has arthritis in his thigh is false — arthritis cannot occur in the thigh on his community's concept.
- P3: A*'s belief that he has "arthritis" in his thigh is true — his community's concept covers that condition.
- P4: Beliefs with different truth conditions have different contents.
- C: A and A*, though internally identical, have beliefs with different contents.
- C2: Mental content is not determined solely by internal states — it is partly constituted by the subject's social-linguistic community.

This is **social externalism** (also called **anti-individualism**): the social and linguistic practices of the community partly determine what the subject believes, and this determination is not overridden by what the subject individually understands or associates with the term.

## Why This Case Is More Radical Than Putnam's

Putnam's Twin Earth depends on a difference in the physical environment — H₂O versus XYZ. Burge's arthritis case depends on a purely social fact: the linguistic conventions of the community. The patient lacks complete mastery of the concept "arthritis" — he does not know it applies only to joints — but he nonetheless thinks using it, and the content of his thought is fixed by the community's standards. Most of our concepts are partly mastered in this incomplete way. We use terms like "mortgage," "contract," "chromosome," and "diabetes" without knowing all the conditions of their correct application. Our beliefs expressed with these terms have contents that depend on what experts and the community mean, not just on what we individually associate with the terms.

This pattern generalizes. I may use "solenoid" without knowing exactly what solenoids are; a patient may use "glaucoma" without knowing its clinical definition; a layperson may use "isotope" without knowing the relevant physics. In each case, the content of the thought is fixed by community standards, not by individual mental associations. Semantic deference is not the exception but the norm.

## Self-Knowledge and Its Limits

Social externalism has striking implications for first-person access. If the content of my beliefs is partly determined by my community's practices, then I cannot know with complete certainty what I believe simply by introspection. The arthritis patient believes something he would not assent to if he fully understood the content. This challenges the Cartesian picture of privileged first-person access — the view that one's own mental states are the objects of the most certain knowledge.

Burge's own response is measured. He holds that first-person authority is preserved with respect to the *type* of attitude one has — whether one believes something, desires it, fears it — even if the *content* of that attitude is not entirely self-transparent. What introspection reveals is that I believe-concerning-arthritis; what the content of that belief is depends partly on social facts that introspection cannot deliver. Far from this being an implausible consequence of externalism, Burge argues that it corrects an individualistic prejudice. We are social creatures who think and speak in ways embedded in and partly constituted by social practices. The idea that thought is a purely individual achievement, with content fully accessible from the armchair, is the picture that needs correction.
