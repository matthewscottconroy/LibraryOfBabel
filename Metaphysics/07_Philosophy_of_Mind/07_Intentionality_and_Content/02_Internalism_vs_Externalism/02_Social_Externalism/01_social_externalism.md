# Social Externalism: Burge and the Social Determination of Content

When I use the concept ARTHRITIS, I am thinking with a concept whose correct application conditions extend well beyond what I personally know. I may not know that arthritis is specifically inflammation of the joints, not muscles; I may not know that it is a disease with a particular pathophysiology. But the content of my thought — what I am thinking *about* when I think about arthritis — is fixed by the community's standards of correct application, not by my partial and perhaps mistaken understanding. This is Tyler Burge's central insight, developed in "Individualism and the Mental" (1979) and elaborated across decades of subsequent work: mental content is not a purely individual achievement.

The thought experiment that illustrates this is now familiar. A person believes he has arthritis in his thigh. His doctor corrects him: arthritis only occurs in joints. In a counterfactual variant, an internally identical person lives in a community that uses "arthritis" to cover certain muscular conditions as well. The internal states are the same; the beliefs are different. The actual person believes something false about arthritis; the counterfactual person believes something true about his community's concept. Since the internal states are identical and the contents differ, mental content cannot be determined by internal states alone. It is partly determined by the social-linguistic community.

This is **social externalism** — and it extends Putnam's natural kind externalism in a significant direction. Putnam's cases depend on differences in the physical environment (H₂O versus XYZ). Burge's cases depend on differences in social and linguistic conventions. A subject's content can differ from a counterpart's not because the physical world differs but because the community's practices differ. The mind is open not just to the physical world but to the social world.

## Content, Individuation, and Cognitive Science

Social externalism implies that mental states cannot be fully individuated by reference to internal states — brain states, functional organization, computational structure. To individuate a mental state fully, one must also specify the social-linguistic community in which the subject is embedded. To say Alf believes he has arthritis in his thigh is to say that Alf is in a certain internal state *and* that his community uses "arthritis" in the relevant way.

This has broad consequences. For philosophy of mind, mental state attribution is a relational claim about how the subject is situated in a social-linguistic environment, not just a report on the subject's internal state. For cognitive science, the computer science model — where the mind is understood as a program that can be analyzed by studying its internal code without reference to external context — is inadequate. The subject's social embedding is constitutively relevant to what the subject is thinking, and no purely internal description captures this.

For self-knowledge, subjects may not fully know the content of their own beliefs, since content depends on social facts they may be ignorant of. Burge holds that first-person authority is preserved with respect to the type of attitude one has, but that privileged access does not extend to guaranteed knowledge of precise content. This is not a surprising or counterintuitive result for Burge; it reflects the fact that most of our concepts are incompletely mastered. We use terms like "mortgage," "chromosome," "solenoid," and "glaucoma" without knowing all the conditions of their correct application, and our thoughts with these concepts have contents that outrun our individual understanding.

## The Parallel with Husserl and the Significance for Rationality

Burge's social externalism has a structural parallel in Husserl's account of intersubjectivity. Husserl recognized that our concepts are not purely individual but are constituted through intersubjective practices — we learn concepts through social interaction, and our concepts carry the normative standards of the community. What Burge adds is systematic rigor: social constitution of content is not merely a genetic story about how concepts are acquired but a constitutive story about what content *consists in* at any given time. I do not merely inherit the concept ARTHRITIS from my community; I think with a concept whose content is, at every moment of deployment, partly fixed by what the community means.

This has implications for rationality and belief revision. When I discover that my use of "arthritis" was mistaken — that the community's standard excludes muscular conditions — I revise not just a peripheral belief but the content of a whole range of thoughts. The revision is not merely a correction of false beliefs about arthritis; it is a refinement of the concept itself, bringing my individual use into alignment with community standards. Social externalism thus supports a picture of rational agents as embedded in and responsive to social-linguistic norms that partly constitute the content of their thought.
