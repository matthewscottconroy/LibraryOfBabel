# The Social Environment and Mental Content

The arthritis case is arresting in its particularity, but Burge's anti-individualism is a general thesis about the structure of thought. The claim is not that one unusual concept, imperfectly mastered, has its content socially determined — it is that most of our ordinary concepts work this way. Consider "mortgage." Many people have a rough and incomplete understanding of what a mortgage is: they know it involves borrowing money secured against property, but may not know the precise legal conditions required for an instrument to count as a mortgage. Yet when they think "I have a mortgage on my house," they are thinking about a mortgage — the legally defined instrument — not about whatever they happen to associate with the word. The content of their thought is fixed by the legal and social standards in their community, not by their individual mental associations.

The generalization Burge draws from this is radical. We are partial experts on most of the categories we think and speak about. We defer to the community's standards — to doctors, lawyers, chemists, and other specialists — for the precise conditions of correct application. This deference is not a deficiency of ordinary thinking but is partly constitutive of what it is to possess and use a concept in a social context. To think with the concept ARTHRITIS just is, in part, to deploy a concept whose content is fixed by the community's medical standards, whether or not one knows what those standards are.

This parallels Putnam's division of linguistic labor — the idea that linguistic communities distribute the cognitive labor of determining correct application conditions among different members. Some members determine the standard; most defer to the experts. Both the experts and the non-experts share the same concept, even though their mastery differs. What Burge adds is the constitutive claim: the sociological fact about how communities organize knowledge has metaphysical consequences for mental content.

## Consequences for Cognitive Science, Ethics, and Self-Knowledge

Standard cognitive science models the mind as an information-processing system whose structure can be understood by studying its internal computational organization, independently of the social and linguistic environment. Inputs and outputs are the interface with the world; everything in between is internal processing. But if mental content is partly socially constituted, then the content of mental states cannot be read off from internal computational structure alone. The same internal state can have different contents in different social environments. A cognitive science that ignores the social environment will misidentify the contents of the states it studies — not because it studies the wrong processes, but because it mischaracterizes what those processes are about.

This does not mean cognitive science must become sociology. But it does mean the social sciences and cognitive science are more deeply intertwined than the individualistic model suggests. Cognitive processes are embedded in social practices, and the social practices partly constitute the content of those processes.

The social constitution of content also has implications for ethics and moral responsibility. If our moral concepts — justice, rights, harm, duty — are partly socially constituted, then our moral beliefs have contents fixed partly by the standards of our moral community. Moral disagreement may sometimes be disagreement about concepts — about what justice or harm really means by the community's standards — not just disagreement about how to apply agreed concepts to cases. And moral responsibility may involve a social dimension: to be responsible for an action may require not just having the right internal states but being embedded in a social practice that recognizes those internal states as the relevant conditions of responsibility.

For self-knowledge, the consequences are perhaps most philosophically striking. If the content of my beliefs is partly determined by social facts I may not know, then introspection does not give me complete access to what I believe. The content of my thought about arthritis is fixed by medical standards; the content of my thought about mortgages is fixed by legal standards; the content of my moral beliefs is fixed by the community's normative practices. In each case, I may be wrong about what I believe — not in the sense of having beliefs whose existence I fail to report, but in the sense of mischaracterizing their content. First-person authority is preserved with respect to attitude type; it does not extend to full knowledge of what the attitude is about. Far from being a surprising or troubling consequence, Burge argues this reflects the correct picture of cognition: we are social beings whose minds are constitutively open to the community in which we think and speak.
