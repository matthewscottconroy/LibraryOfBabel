# Replies to Burge's Anti-Individualism

The main lines of reply to Burge's anti-individualism divide into challenges to the thought experiment itself, challenges to the conclusion drawn from it, and challenges to the significance of the conclusion for philosophy of mind and cognitive science. None of these has proved fully decisive, which is one reason the debate over social externalism remains live.

The **deviance reply** holds that the patient in Burge's counterfactual community is not using "arthritis" to express the same concept as the patient in the actual world. The counterfactual patient's term "arthritis" expresses a different concept — call it arthritis* — and so of course his belief differs. But this is not surprising or anti-individualistic: people in different communities who use words differently express different concepts. The apparent externalism is an artifact of allowing the same word to express different concepts across communities without distinguishing the concepts.

Burge's response cuts to the heart of the matter. This reply concedes precisely what the anti-individualist is arguing: that internal identity does not guarantee conceptual (content) identity. The deviance reply explains why the beliefs differ by saying the concepts differ — which just is the externalist thesis. Two people can be internally identical and yet differ in what they believe because content is not determined by internal states. The reply restates the conclusion rather than resisting it.

The **description theory reply** is more technically resourceful. Perhaps the patient's belief is best analyzed as a belief about *whatever condition the doctors call "arthritis."* On this view, the patient's concept is an internally represented description — a narrow content that the actual world's experts would fill in. The content is in the head (as an indexical description of expert usage), and deference to experts is captured by the description's structure.

Burge's response again: this mischaracterizes the patient's belief. When the patient reports "I have arthritis in my thigh," he is not thinking about a description but about a specific condition — the condition itself, not a description of it. Description theory faces the same difficulties as all internalist accounts of natural kind terms: descriptions can be empty, and the reference of medical condition terms (like natural kind terms) is determined by the nature of the condition, not by the associated description. Moreover, the description "whatever the doctors call arthritis" is itself a concept whose content is socially determined — the reply does not eliminate social constitution but relocates it.

## Fodor's Narrow Content Reply and Burge's Counter

Fodor argues that narrow content is the right explanatory notion for psychology. Yes, Oscar and Twin Oscar, Alf and Alf*, have different wide content — but they have the same narrow content. Since psychology explains behavior in terms of computationally relevant internal states, and since wide content is irrelevant to how internal states cause behavior, psychology should traffic in narrow content. Wide content is an artifact of how we describe mental states from outside, not of how minds actually work. Two computationally identical subjects will behave identically; adding "wide content" to the description changes nothing about the causal transactions inside the mind.

Burge's reply is that this argument assumes behavior can be described independently of content — as mere physical motion rather than intentional action. But intentional action descriptions — Oscar *fetched water*, not merely *moved a container from the kitchen* — are not eliminable in favor of purely physical descriptions without loss of explanatory power. It is precisely the wide content that makes the action fetching water. Cognitive science that ignores wide content cannot explain the relationship between thought and world-directed action. The internal state explains what Oscar's body did; wide content explains what Oscar did.

## The Compatibilist Resolution

The most widely accepted response accepts Burge's externalism but argues that it is compatible with the important commitments of cognitive science and epistemology. By distinguishing the level at which cognitive science operates (internal functional states, computational processes) from the level at which content-attribution operates (the organism-environment relation), one can acknowledge that content is partly externally constituted while insisting that a science of internal processing remains a coherent enterprise.

On this view, both narrow and wide content are legitimate theoretical constructs serving different explanatory roles. Narrow content explains computational processes; wide content explains intentional action and the organism's relationship to its environment. First-person authority is preserved at the level of attitude type (that one believes, desires, fears) even if not at the level of precise content. Cognitive science proceeds with internally-characterized states while philosophical content-attribution adds the external dimension.

The trend in philosophy of mind has been toward exactly this nuanced pluralism. Burge is widely credited with establishing that content individuation has a social dimension that pure internalism cannot capture. Fodor is widely credited with establishing that there is a legitimate explanatory role for an internally-characterized notion of mental state. The debate has not so much been resolved as absorbed into a richer picture in which the question is not whether content is internal or external but which notion of content is appropriate for which explanatory purpose.
