# Internalist Responses to Externalism

The natural motivation for internalism has always been causal. When Oscar reaches for a glass of water, it is his internal neural states — his belief that water is in the glass, his desire to drink — that causally explain his action. What the water actually is (H₂O or XYZ) makes no difference to the causal pathway from mental state to action. If wide content is not causally relevant to behavior, it has no role in a properly causal-explanatory science of psychology. This is Fodor's **methodological individualism**: psychology should individuate mental states without reference to the external environment, because only internally individuated states are causally relevant to behavior. A cognitive science that individuates states by their wide content will fail to formulate the causal generalizations that explain why people do what they do.

The most influential internalist response to Putnam and Burge therefore accepts externalism as applied to one kind of content while defending internalism about another. **Wide content** is the content of a mental state as environmentally individuated — what Oscar's thought is about given the facts of his environment. **Narrow content** is the "internal component" — the functional role the state plays in the subject's psychology, independent of environmental context. On this dual-content view, both internalism and externalism are partially right: there are two legitimate notions of content serving different explanatory roles. The challenge for narrow content is to be specifiable without reference to the environment yet rich enough to explain the role of beliefs in behavior.

Fodor proposed that narrow content is a function from contexts (possible environments) to wide contents — a two-dimensional conception that maps different environments to different extensions. The same narrow content applied to an H₂O environment yields one wide content; applied to an XYZ environment, it yields another. But critics pressed the point that this "content" is too abstract to play the explanatory role belief-content needs. What psychological explanations require is a determinate content, not a function from environments to contents. Laurence and Margolis offer a related response: concepts have both a referential component (varying with the environment) and a cognitive component (an internal representation grounding the reference). The cognitive component — something like a mental representation of the stereotypical properties of water — is what causes the subject's behavior. The referential component is determined externally but is not what does the causal work.

## Challenging the Thought Experiments

Some internalists challenge the thought experiments themselves. Devitt argues that the Twin Earth case depends on a question-begging description: when we describe Twin Earth water as "not water," we import the assumption that water is H₂O. But there is an equally good sense in which the Twin Earther's "water" is the same stuff — the watery stuff in their environment. Described neutrally, the intuition that the two speakers mean different things dissolves.

Two-dimensional semantics, developed by Jackson and Chalmers, offers a different accommodation. It distinguishes the **primary intension** of a term — what it picks out in different contexts — from its **secondary intension** — what it picks out in counterfactual worlds given the actual reference. The primary intension of "water" is the same for Oscar and Twin Oscar: both pick out the local watery stuff. Only the secondary intension differs — what H₂O versus XYZ pick out in counterfactual scenarios. If internalists care about the primary intension, they can have it without denying externalism about the secondary intension. The question, of course, is which intension matters for the relevant psychological explanations.

## McDowell's Rejection

Not all responses to externalism defend narrow content. McDowell, from a broadly Kantian and Wittgensteinian direction, rejects the very idea of narrow content as philosophically incoherent. Genuine thought is always world-directed; there is no "world-independent" mental content that could float free of its referential connections. The Cartesian picture of an inner content that is then connected to the world by an external relation is precisely what needs to be abandoned. A thought that P just is the thought that P is the case in the world. There is no layer of purely internal content beneath or behind this world-directedness, and positing narrow content simply reinstates the Cartesian picture at a new level.

The contemporary consensus has moved toward pluralism. Both narrow and wide content are recognized as legitimate theoretical constructs, each serving different explanatory purposes — narrow content for explaining the causal role of mental states in behavior, wide content for explaining intentional action and the subject's relation to the world. Whether this pluralism is a stable resolution or merely a deferral of deeper issues about what content ultimately is remains contested.
