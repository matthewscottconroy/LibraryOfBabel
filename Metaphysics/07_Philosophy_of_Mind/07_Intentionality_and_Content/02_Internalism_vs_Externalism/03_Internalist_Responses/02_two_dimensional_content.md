# Two-Dimensional Semantic Content

Before anyone discovered the chemical composition of water, "water is H₂O" was not knowable. Yet once discovered, it turned out to be necessarily true — water could not have been anything other than H₂O. This combination of epistemic contingency and metaphysical necessity puzzled philosophers until Kripke pointed out that the puzzlement presupposes an identification between what is knowable a priori and what is necessary — an identification that should be rejected. Two-dimensional semantics, developed by Stalnaker, Kaplan, and most influentially Chalmers, provides a systematic framework for this insight and uses it to reconcile internalist and externalist intuitions about mental content.

The framework assigns every expression two kinds of intension. The **primary intension** (or A-intension) is a function from possible worlds, understood as ways the actual world might turn out to be, to extensions. It captures what the expression would pick out if a given world were actual — the expression's epistemic role, what it picks out before any empirical discoveries fix its reference. The **secondary intension** (or C-intension) is a function from possible worlds, understood as counterfactual scenarios given that the actual world has already been fixed, to extensions. It captures the expression's extension in counterfactual scenarios, holding the actual reference fixed — the expression's metaphysical or alethic role.

The water case is the paradigm. The primary intension of "water" picks out, in any context, whatever substance plays the watery role in that context — the clear drinkable liquid that fills the rivers and quenches thirst. In an H₂O world (the actual one), it picks out H₂O; in an XYZ world, it would pick out XYZ. The secondary intension picks out H₂O in all possible worlds, because water is actually H₂O and it is metaphysically necessary that water = H₂O. This is why "water is H₂O" is epistemically contingent (not knowable a priori — empirical discovery was required) but metaphysically necessary (true in all possible worlds given the actual facts).

## Reconciling Internalism and Externalism

Two-dimensional semantics provides a principled way to accommodate both sides of the internalism/externalism debate. The primary intension of "water" is the same for Oscar and Twin Oscar — both pick out the locally prevalent watery stuff. This represents the narrow content: what is shared between the two subjects, what is "in the head," what explains their identical epistemic situations. If we specify the possible environment (Earth or Twin Earth), the same primary intension yields different extensions. The secondary intension differs: for Oscar, "water" picks out H₂O in all possible worlds; for Twin Oscar, it picks out XYZ. This represents the wide content — what the expression actually refers to given the actual environment.

Chalmers extends the framework to mental content directly. Concepts have both primary and secondary intensions. The primary intension of a concept corresponds to its narrow content — the functional or cognitive role it plays, which determines what it would pick out in any possible environment. The secondary intension corresponds to its wide content — what it actually picks out in this environment. Both are genuine components of mental content, serving different explanatory purposes. The primary intension explains epistemic and cognitive matters: why Oscar and Twin Oscar are in the same epistemic position, why "water might not be H₂O" was coherent before discovery, how thought experiments about possible worlds work. The secondary intension explains semantic and metaphysical matters: which substance is actually picked out, whether "water is necessarily H₂O" is true, how reference is preserved across possible worlds.

## Objections and Assessment

Three objections press on the framework. The **scrutability objection** challenges Chalmers's claim that primary intensions are accessible a priori — that one can work out what one's concept would pick out in any possible actual world by reflection. Critics argue that the primary intension of many concepts is not so accessible. What would "water" pick out in a world where the watery stuff were a plasma or a molecular aggregate with unusual properties? The boundaries of the primary intension may be just as vague and empirically sensitive as the concept's actual reference.

The **one-concept objection** (Soames) holds that "water" is just one concept with one intension, not two. The two-dimensional framework produces the appearance of two intensions by conflating the context of utterance (what world the speaker is in) with the circumstance of evaluation (the world at which the statement is evaluated). These are features of the theoretical framework, not of the concept itself. Attributing two intensions to "water" gives a false picture of semantic complexity.

The **metaphysics objection** asks why primary intensions matter if secondary intensions are what determine truth and reference. If water = H₂O in all possible worlds, the metaphysical facts are settled; the primary intension is a useful heuristic for tracking epistemic accessibility, but it does not track a genuine semantic value independent of the secondary intension.

Despite these objections, two-dimensional semantics remains the most sophisticated framework for analyzing the relationship between narrow and wide content, and is widely used in both philosophy of mind and philosophy of language. Whether it vindicates narrow content as a psychologically real kind or merely systematizes the ways in which the same expression can be evaluated from different perspectives is one of the ongoing questions about its ultimate significance.
