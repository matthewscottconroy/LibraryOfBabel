# Phenomenal Internalism

Consider two subjects who are physical duplicates — identical atom for atom, in every internal respect. Is it possible for one to have a red experience while the other has a green experience? The intuition that this is incoherent is the core of **phenomenal internalism**: phenomenal character, the felt quality of experience, the redness of a red experience and the painfulness of pain, is determined by the internal state of the organism. It is not constituted by anything external, not by what the organism is tracking, not by its evolutionary history, not by what substances happen to be in its environment. Two physical duplicates must have the same phenomenal experiences.

This supervenience claim — that phenomenal properties supervene on internal physical properties at the level of the individual organism — sits in sharp contrast to the externalism about intentional content that Putnam and Burge established. Intentional content may depend on the environment; phenomenal character, the internalist holds, does not. The redness of my red experience is mine, determined by my neural state, fully present regardless of what the external world happens to be.

The combination view that many philosophers have found attractive follows directly. Phenomenal properties are internally determined while the representational content that phenomenal states carry is externally determined. The felt quality of my red experience is a function of my neural state; the fact that my red experience is an *experience of blood* rather than *of paint* is determined by what my perceptual state normally covaries with, or was selected to track. Experience has two components: a phenomenal character (internal) and a representational content (partly external). Ned Block's distinction between **phenomenal consciousness** (P-consciousness: what it's like) and **access consciousness** (A-consciousness: availability for reasoning, report, and behavior control) is relevant here. Phenomenal consciousness might be internally determined even if access consciousness depends partly on external context.

## Phenomenal Externalism and the Representationalist Challenge

The most challenging case for phenomenal internalism arises when intentional content and phenomenal character are tied closely together. Representationalists like Tye (*Ten Problems of Consciousness*, 1995) and Dretske hold that the phenomenal character of an experience just is its representational content — specifically, its fine-grained, nonconceptual representational content. If representing-red is externally determined, and if phenomenal character equals representational content, then phenomenal character is also externally determined. This is **phenomenal externalism**: the phenomenal character of experiences varies systematically with the environment.

Dretske defends a version of this directly. Color experiences are experiences of environmental reflectance properties, and what counts as a correct representation of those properties is an external matter. The same internal state could produce different phenomenal characters in different environments, if the environments determine different representational contents. The redness of my experience is not a bare intrinsic quality but the property of representing a certain type of reflectance — and what that type is depends on the history and context of the representing system.

Against phenomenal externalism, internalists invoke the **conceivability of zombies**. A philosophical zombie is a physical duplicate of a conscious subject who has no phenomenal consciousness. If zombies are conceivable — and many philosophers find them conceivable — then phenomenal consciousness is not necessitated by the physical-relational structure that externalists cite. The gap between the functional-representational and the phenomenal suggests that phenomenal character is something over and above representational content: something internal, intrinsic, and not reducible to relational properties.

## Significance for the Hard Problem

The debate between phenomenal internalism and externalism is not merely technical. Its resolution has far-reaching consequences for the hard problem of consciousness. If phenomenal properties are internally determined, they are local features of the brain and can in principle be explained by neuroscience — once we understand the neural correlates of consciousness, we will understand what gives rise to phenomenal character. If phenomenal properties are relational — partly constituted by the environment — then they are not local to the brain, and neuroscience alone cannot explain them; explanation would require tracing the relational properties that constitute phenomenal character, which might take us all the way to facts about evolutionary history, environmental structure, and the organism's embedding in the world.

The internalist picture is more conservative but faces the standard hard problem challenge: even if phenomenal character supervenes on internal states, we still face the question of why any internal state gives rise to any phenomenal character at all. The externalist picture is bolder but faces the question of whether it is really phenomenal character that is being explained — whether the relational properties that fix representational content are sufficient to account for what it is like to be in a state, or whether the felt quality escapes the relational account as surely as it escapes the internal one. This is one of the deepest open questions at the intersection of philosophy of mind and philosophy of perception.
