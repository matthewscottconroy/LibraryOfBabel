# Theories of Mental Content

The externalism debate established that mental content is not determined solely by internal states; the environment plays a role. But this leaves open the further question: what specific features of the environment and the mind's relation to it determine the content of mental states? Theories of mental content are theories about what makes a mental state have the content it has — what fixes which state of affairs or object the thought is about.

Causal theories hold that a mental state represents whatever caused it to exist or to be tokened in a systematic way. Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory is the most developed causal theory: a thought represents horses if (a) horses cause horse-thoughts, and (b) anything else that causes horse-thoughts does so only because horses cause horse-thoughts. The asymmetry — other causes depending on the horse-horse-thought connection — is what ensures that the thought genuinely represents horses and not horse-pictures or horse-impressions.

Teleosemantic theories hold that mental states represent what they have the biological function to represent — what they were selected (by natural selection or learning) to track. Millikan's biosemantics grounds representation in proper function: the proper function of a mental representation is to represent the conditions that caused the evolution or learning of the representational system. This gives intentionality a naturalistically respectable grounding in evolutionary and developmental history.

Conceptual role theories hold that the content of a mental state is determined by its inferential and practical role — its relations to other mental states and to behavior. What makes a state the belief that it will rain is its tendency to be caused by rain-experiences, to combine with the desire to stay dry to produce umbrella-seeking behavior, and so on. The content is the role, not an internal intrinsic property or a causal-historical fact.

This section examines these and other theories of mental content, assessing their theoretical advantages, their compatibility with externalism, and the challenges each faces.
