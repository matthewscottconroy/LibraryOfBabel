# Causal Covariance Theories

A weathervane tracks wind direction. A tree ring tracks a year of growth. In each case, there is a reliable causal connection between the indicator and what it indicates — not because anyone assigned the indicator its meaning, but because of how the physical world works. Causal covariance theories of mental content extend this idea to mental representations: the content of a mental state is determined by what it reliably covaries with. If state M is reliably caused by features of type F, then M represents F. Content falls out of cause-and-effect relations that are themselves explicable in terms of physics. No mysterious normative facts need be invoked.

Dennis Stampe (*Toward a Causal Theory of Linguistic Representation*, 1977) was among the first to develop this systematically. On his view, a symbol S represents a state of affairs P if S would occur in response to P under conditions in which S would reliably indicate P. The concept RED represents red things because red things, under normal conditions, reliably cause RED-concept tokenings. Dretske's information-theoretic version (*Knowledge and the Flow of Information*, 1981) gives this mathematical precision: S carries information about source F if there is a nomic (law-like) connection between F and S such that the conditional probability P(F | S) = 1 under natural conditions. Information carrying is defined without reference to intentional or normative vocabulary — it is a structural feature of the causal network.

## The Disjunction Problem

The most immediate and serious challenge is the **disjunction problem**, pressed with particular force by Fodor (*Psychosemantics*, 1987; *A Theory of Content*, 1990). Suppose my HORSE concept is sometimes caused by horses and sometimes by cows in dim light. Both horses and cows-in-dim-light reliably cause HORSE-concept tokenings. Simple causal covariance implies the concept represents {horses or cows-in-dim-light} — since both are reliable causes. But clearly the concept represents horses and *misrepresents* cows-in-dim-light. The theory cannot distinguish the intended content from the disjunctive content, and so it cannot account for misrepresentation — for the concept being wrong when triggered by non-paradigm causes.

The problem is general and unavoidable on simple causal grounds. Any concept can be tokened by non-paradigm causes in unusual conditions. The simple causal account always implies a disjunctive content wider than the intended content. Since misrepresentation — the possibility of a representation being wrong — is essential to intentionality, a theory that cannot ground it has not explained content at all.

The **normal conditions response** holds that content is determined by what the representation covaries with under normal conditions. Cows-in-dim-light are not normal causes of HORSE tokenings; dim light is abnormal. So HORSE represents horses, not the disjunction. The problem is circularity: normal conditions must be specified independently of content. If normal conditions are just those under which the representation tracks the right content, we use content to define normality and normality to define content.

The **optimal conditions response** (Stampe) holds that content is determined by the cause under conditions in which the representation is most reliably correlated — the conditions under which the system works best. Under optimal visual conditions (good lighting, typical distances, normal perception), HORSE is tokened only by horses. The challenge remains: optimal for what purpose? And how do we specify optimal conditions for abstract concepts, whose tokens have no obvious optimal perceptual conditions?

## Teleological Supplement

The most promising response supplements causal covariance with teleological considerations. Normal conditions are those under which the representing system was designed — by evolution or by learning — to function. Normal causes are the causes for which the system was built; deviant causes (cows in dim light) are causes for which it was not. This hybrid approach is the basis of teleosemantic theories, which ground normativity in biological function rather than in purely causal-covariance relations. Millikan and Papineau take precisely this path. What the causal covariance framework contributes is the basic insight that mental content is grounded in systematic causal connections to the world; what it cannot provide, without supplementation, is the normative structure that distinguishes the intended cause from the deviant one. Most philosophers now hold that bare causal covariance is insufficient for determining content and must be augmented by either teleological or normative considerations — which is why causal theories have developed into a family of successor theories rather than standing on their own.
