# Causal Theories of Mental Content

Kripke (*Naming and Necessity*, 1972) and Putnam (*The Meaning of "Meaning"*, 1975) established, against the Fregean-Russellian tradition, that the reference of names and natural kind terms is grounded in causal contact with the world, not in the descriptions speakers associate with those terms. A name refers to its bearer because speakers are causally connected — through a chain of uses — to the original act of naming. A natural kind term refers to the kind because speakers are causally connected to instances of that kind. The project in philosophy of mind was to extend this insight to the content of mental representations themselves. The concept DOG represents dogs not because the concept is associated with a description that dogs satisfy, but because dog-instances caused the formation and tokening of the DOG concept. The content of a mental state is determined by its causal history.

The appeal is clear. Causal relations are physical relations, part of the natural order. If content is determined by causal history, intentionality is naturalizable — it is just a species of the causal relations that physics already recognizes. The concept COW represents cows because cow-instances cause COW-tokenings through a lawful nomic connection. No norms, meanings, or intentional primitives need to be invoked at the ground level.

The simplest version: mental state M represents X if and only if M-tokenings are causally produced by X-instances in the appropriate lawful way. The connection must be nomic (law-like), not merely accidental. A single historical coincidence between an X and an M-tokening does not suffice for M to represent X; the connection must be a reliable, law-governed dependency.

## The Misrepresentation Problem and Fodor's Response

The simple causal theory faces the **misrepresentation problem** (a version of the disjunction problem) immediately. If my DOG concept is sometimes caused by dogs and sometimes by cats at night from a distance, the simple theory implies the concept represents "dogs or cats (under certain conditions)." But this disjunctive content is not the right content — the concept is supposed to represent dogs, and the cat-activation is a misrepresentation. Without an account of misrepresentation, the theory has not explained genuine intentionality.

Fodor's **asymmetric dependence theory** (*A Theory of Content*, 1990) attempts to solve this. A concept C represents X if: (1) X-caused tokenings of C are nomologically necessary — there is a genuine law connecting Xs to Cs; and (2) the other causes of C (Ys, Zs) are asymmetrically dependent on the X-C connection — if the X-C connection broke, the Y-C and Z-C connections would also break, but not vice versa. Cats-cause-DOG-tokenings only because dogs cause DOG-tokenings and cats visually resemble dogs under certain conditions. Remove the dog-dog connections, and cats would not trigger DOG either. The asymmetric dependence of the cat-connection on the dog-connection grounds the dog-content: the cat is a deviant cause precisely because its causal role depends on the system being calibrated for dogs.

Critics press the **circularity objection**: specifying which connection is the "intended" one — the one on which others asymmetrically depend — seems to require already knowing the content. Which connection is foundational depends on which object the concept is "supposed to" represent, and that is precisely what the theory is trying to explain. Fodor's response is that asymmetric dependence is specified in purely counterfactual causal terms, without presupposing content — we ask what would happen to the causal dependencies if we surgically removed specific laws. Whether this counterfactual characterization is genuinely non-circular remains contested.

## Further Difficulties

Causal theories face persistent difficulty with **abstract concepts**. The concept JUSTICE, THE NUMBER SEVEN, or THE SQUARE ROOT OF -1 has no obvious causal connection to instances in the environment — there are no justice-instances walking around that caused the formation of my JUSTICE concept. Fodor acknowledged this difficulty and did not offer a satisfying solution; it represents a genuine gap in the causal program.

Millikan presses a related concern: causal theories conflate the cause of a representation with its content. A frog's fly-detector fires in response to small dark moving spots — which may be flies, but also BBs and small stones. The causal theory implies the content is the disjunction. Teleosemantics resolves this by grounding content in the function the representation serves — what it was selected to track — not the range of causes that trigger it. This critique motivates the shift from purely causal to teleosemantic theories.

The core insight of causal theories survives these criticisms: mental content is not arbitrary but is systematically connected to the world through reliable causal relations. What the bare causal account cannot supply is the normative structure that distinguishes the intended cause from the deviant one. That is why causal theories have evolved into a family of successor theories — supplemented by teleological, informational, or normative constraints — rather than standing as autonomous accounts.
