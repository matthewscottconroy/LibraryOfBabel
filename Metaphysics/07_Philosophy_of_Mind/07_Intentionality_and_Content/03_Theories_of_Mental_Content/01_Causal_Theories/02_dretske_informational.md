# Dretske's Informational Semantics

Claude Shannon's mathematical theory of communication defines information in terms of reliable causal-nomic connections between signals and sources. A signal carries information about a source when its occurrence is a reliable indicator that the source is in a certain state — when the conditional probability P(source-state | signal) = 1 under normal channel conditions. Shannon was not thinking about minds or meaning. But Dretske, in *Knowledge and the Flow of Information* (1981) and *Naturalizing the Mind* (1995), recognized that this relation — nomic reliability between mental state and distal cause — is precisely the structure that naturalists need for a theory of mental content.

The idea is elegant. My perception of red carries information about the presence of something red in the environment because the perception reliably occurs when and only when, under relevant conditions, something red is present. The information-carrying relation is a physical, nomically grounded relation; it requires no reference to norms or meanings. Mental representations carry information about the world in virtue of standing in such reliable causal-nomic relations, and this information-carrying is the basis of their intentional content.

This gives the informational approach several virtues. It is naturalistically respectable: information is a well-defined quantity in physics and engineering. It is mathematically precise: information-carrying is characterized in terms of conditional probability, not vague appeals to "tracking" or "aboutness." And it connects fruitfully to epistemology: to know that P is, roughly, to have a belief that carries the information that P, giving a unified naturalistic account of both content and justification.

## The Disjunction Problem and the Learning Period Solution

The disjunction problem applies to informational semantics as directly as to causal covariance theories. A smoke detector responds to smoke under normal conditions, but also to steam under abnormal ones. The conditional probability P(smoke or steam | alarm) = 1, not just P(smoke | alarm). The informational theory seems to imply the detector represents the disjunction.

Dretske's response is the **learning period story**. During the learning period in which a perceptual system is being calibrated, its representations are associated with a specific class of distal causes. After the learning period, the representation continues to represent that class even when caused by other things — in which case misrepresentation occurs. Content is fixed by the causes operative during learning, not by all the causes that can later trigger the state. A child learning "dog" by exposure to dogs acquires a concept that represents dogs even when the child later mistakes a cat for a dog in dim light.

The solution has intuitive appeal — learning does seem to fix content in a way that subsequent errors do not — but it faces objections. What determines which features of the learning environment fix the content? A child learning "dog" is exposed to many features simultaneously — shape, size, behavior, context — and there is no principled account of which features are content-determining. How does the account extend to abstract concepts like JUSTICE or INFINITY, which are not learned through direct causal exposure to instances? And even during the learning period, the system may be caused by multiple things; specifying which is content-determining seems to require invoking content.

## The Transition to Teleosemantics

Dretske recognized these limitations and in later work (*Explaining Behavior*, 1988; *Naturalizing the Mind*, 1995) incorporated teleological elements. The learning period succeeds not just because of what happens during it but because of what the learning is *for* — what function the resulting representation serves in the system. This moves Dretske toward a hybrid theory where information-carrying is constrained by the function of the representing system, a position that overlaps significantly with Millikan's teleosemantics.

The informational approach remains a landmark in the naturalization project. It demonstrated that semantic properties of mental states could be characterized in terms of measurable physical relations — information flow — making genuine progress even if the resulting theory requires supplementation. The difficulties Dretske encountered illuminate exactly what any adequate causal theory needs to supply: not just a characterization of the causal relation, but an account of which causal relation is the content-determining one when multiple candidates exist. That is the structural problem that teleosemantics addresses by grounding content in biological function rather than informational history.
