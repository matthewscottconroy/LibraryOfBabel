# Fodor's Asymmetric Dependence Theory

The disjunction problem is not merely an embarrassment for simple causal theories — it threatens to show that no causal theory can account for misrepresentation. If a concept is caused by X and also by Y (in unusual conditions), and if content is determined by cause, then the concept represents {X or Y}, and there is no misrepresentation. But misrepresentation is essential to genuine intentionality. A state that cannot be wrong does not represent anything; it merely responds. Any adequate causal theory of content must solve the disjunction problem.

Fodor's **asymmetric dependence theory** (*Psychosemantics*, 1987; *A Theory of Content*, 1990) is the most technically developed attempt to solve it from within a causal framework. Fodor's key observation: the different causal connections between world and concept are not on a par. Some are basic; others depend on the basic ones. This asymmetric structure is what grounds content.

The formal definition: a mental representation type M means X (rather than Y) if and only if:

1. There is a nomic (law-like) connection between X-instances and M-tokenings: necessarily, X → M.
2. There is also a connection between Y-instances and M-tokenings: necessarily, Y → M.
3. The Y-M connection is **asymmetrically dependent** on the X-M connection: if the X-M connection broke, the Y-M connection would also break; but if the Y-M connection broke, the X-M connection would not.

In the horse case: horses cause HORSE-tokenings, and cows-in-dim-light also cause HORSE-tokenings. But the cow-HORSE connection is asymmetrically dependent on the horse-HORSE connection: cows in dim light only trigger HORSE because they visually resemble horses. Remove the horse-HORSE law, and cows-in-dim-light would not trigger HORSE either. Reverse the counterfactual: even if cows in dim light never triggered HORSE, horses would still do so. The horse-connection is basic; the cow-connection derives from it. So the concept represents horses, and the cow-activations are misrepresentations.

## Misrepresentation and the Language of Thought

This gives Fodor the structure needed to accommodate error. Misrepresentation occurs when a representation is tokened by a non-basic cause — a cause whose connection to the representation is asymmetrically dependent on the content-determining connection. When cows-in-dim-light cause my HORSE concept to token, the concept is tokened by something whose causal connection to it is derived, not basic. The concept was calibrated to track horses; the cow-triggering is a deviation. Normativity is grounded in asymmetric causal structure.

The theory is embedded in Fodor's broader **Language of Thought** (LOT) framework. Mental representations are syntactically structured symbols in a mental language; propositional attitudes are computational relations to these symbols. The asymmetric dependence theory gives the semantic content of the primitive symbols in LOT — the basic lexical entries — while the semantic content of complex representations is determined compositionally from the content of their parts. The theory thus targets the foundational level: content for primitive concepts, with compositionality handling the rest.

## Objections and Fodor's Defense

The **opacity objection** challenges the counterfactual characterization of asymmetric dependence. "If the X-M connection broke..." — but counterfactuals are notoriously context-sensitive. Which features of the counterfactual world are held fixed? In a world where horses never caused HORSE-tokenings, what else changes? The notion of asymmetric dependence may be too vague to yield a determinate account of content without smuggling in content-relative considerations through the counterfactual's interpretation.

The **circularity objection** presses harder. To determine which causal connections are basic versus derived, we seem to need a prior grasp of which one represents the intended content. But which is the intended content is precisely what the theory is supposed to explain. Fodor's response is that asymmetric dependence can be specified in purely non-intentional terms — in terms of laws and counterfactuals — without presupposing content. Whether this is correct depends on how the relevant counterfactuals are to be evaluated, which is itself a contested question.

**Abstract concepts** remain recalcitrant. JUSTICE, THE NUMBER FIVE, and QUARKS have no obvious direct causal connection to their referents. How is the asymmetric dependence structure established for concepts not learned through causal exposure to instances? Fodor acknowledged this as a genuine limitation without offering a satisfying solution.

Millikan's **functional critique** identifies a different problem: asymmetric dependence tracks the accidents of a particular learning history, not the function that the concept is supposed to serve. Even if cows-in-dim-light are asymmetrically dependent on horses for triggering HORSE, this is a contingent feature of how this particular system was calibrated. The content of a concept should be determined by what it was selected to track — what function it serves in the cognitive system — not by the structure of its accidental causal dependencies.

Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory remains one of the most careful and explicit attempts to naturalize intentionality within a causal framework. Its difficulties have been productive in clarifying exactly what any such naturalization must accomplish: it must specify, without circularity and in non-intentional terms, which among the many causes of a mental state is the content-determining one.
