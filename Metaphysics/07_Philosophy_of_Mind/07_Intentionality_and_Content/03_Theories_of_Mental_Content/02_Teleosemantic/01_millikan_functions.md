# Millikan and Biological Functions

A heart that is failing to pump blood has not acquired the function of failing. It has the proper function of pumping blood — and it is malfunctioning. This observation, unremarkable when applied to hearts, turns out to carry significant consequences for the theory of mental content. Millikan, in *Language, Thought and Other Biological Categories* (1984), builds a theory of intentionality on exactly this distinction between what a mechanism does and what it was designed to do.

The central concept is **proper function**. A trait or mechanism has a proper function if it was selected for by natural selection (or by an analogue such as learning or reinforcement) because it performed that function in ancestors. Proper functions are historical: they are determined not by what a token mechanism currently does but by what the mechanism type was selected for over evolutionary time. The heart's proper function is to pump blood because hearts that pumped blood contributed to fitness and were therefore selected. Immunological mechanisms have the proper function of fighting infection. A warning call has the proper function of alerting conspecifics to predators. A trait can fail to perform its proper function — this is what malfunction is — precisely because its proper function is defined by history, not by current performance.

Mental representations have proper functions in this sense. A frog's visual system has the proper function of detecting flies because ancestors with such systems that detected flies caught more flies, thereby surviving and reproducing. Millikan's theory of content: a mental representation R has content X if R's proper function is to covary with X in the environment — if R was selected because its tokening when X is present contributed to adaptive behavior.

## Misrepresentation and the Disjunction Problem

This handles the disjunction problem in one stroke. A frog's fly-detector is also triggered by small dark moving things generally — BB pellets, falling leaves, and so on. What is the content: FLY, or {SMALL DARK MOVING THING}? On a simple causal view, the content is the disjunction. On Millikan's view, the content is FLY, because that is what the mechanism was selected to track. When the frog snaps at a BB pellet, its visual system misrepresents: it tokens a representation whose proper function is to be caused by flies, but which is in fact caused by something else. The representation was designed to track flies; it is malfunctioning when caused by other things. Content is determined by evolutionary history; deviations from that history are misrepresentations, not content-expansions.

Millikan distinguishes between **direct proper functions** (functions of a mechanism type directly selected) and **derived proper functions** (functions of particular tokens deriving from the direct proper functions of the type plus the circumstances of their production). She also distinguishes **biologically determined** proper functions (from evolution) from **reproductively established** proper functions (from learning or practice). This extends the theory beyond innate cognitive structures to acquired concepts and beliefs: a learned concept acquires its content through a history of reinforced associations, analogous to natural selection at the individual level.

## Objections and Millikan's Responses

The **Swampman objection** (Davidson) targets the dependence on evolutionary history directly. A spontaneously assembled human duplicate — Swampman — would have no selection history, so by Millikan's theory, it would have no mental content. Yet Swampman seems to think and represent. Millikan bites this bullet: Swampman does not have genuine intentional states, or has them only derivatively through its physical similarity to a creature with proper functions. Critics find this counterintuitive; Millikan holds that the intuition reflects a failure to appreciate how much of our ordinary concept of intentionality is bound up with natural history.

The **indeterminacy objection** presses on the content-fixing role of evolutionary history. Evolution selected for frog visual systems that tracked flying insects, small dark moving things, and things that were food. All three descriptions are accurate of the ancestral selection environment. Which one fixes the content? Millikan provides a theory of "most proximate" proper function to address this — the function most specific to the mechanism's actual causal structure — but the indeterminacy concern persists at the margins of the theory.

Millikan's teleosemantics remains one of the most developed and systematic naturalizations of mental content. Its biological grounding makes it uniquely suited to the cognitive science project of understanding the mind as a product of evolution, and its treatment of misrepresentation is widely acknowledged as the most principled response to the disjunction problem within the naturalist program.
