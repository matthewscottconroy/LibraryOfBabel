# Teleosemantic Theories of Mental Content

The frog's visual system is triggered by flies, but also by small dark moving objects generally — BB pellets, falling seeds, anything sufficiently fly-like. A simple causal theory implies the system's content is the disjunction: {flies or small dark moving things}. But that seems wrong. The system represents flies — it was built to track flies, calibrated to flies over countless generations of natural selection. When it fires at a BB pellet, it is making a mistake, not representing a broader class of objects.

This intuition is the starting point for **teleosemantic theories of mental content**. What distinguishes a representation that means FLY from one that means SMALL-DARK-MOVING-THING is not their current causal properties — which might be identical — but their history: the fly-representing state was selected because it tracked flies. Content is determined by function, and function is determined by selection history. This moves the locus of content-determination from the present to the past — from the current causal profile of the state to its historical selection profile.

The result handles misrepresentation gracefully. A state can misfire — be caused by the wrong thing — without thereby changing its content, because its content is fixed by history, not by its current cause. The frog's fly-detector has the proper function of tracking flies; when it fires at a BB pellet, it is misrepresenting. Just as a heart is malfunctioning (not performing a different function) when it fails to pump blood, the visual system is malfunctioning (not tracking a different content) when it fires at something other than a fly.

The formal structure: a state M has content X if and only if M's proper function is to track X. M is correctly tokened if its occurrence is caused by an X-instance in the circumstances for which M's proper function is specified. M misrepresents if its occurrence is caused by something other than an X-instance — it represents X when there is no X. This gives teleosemantics a structural advantage over simple causal theories: misrepresentation is not the tokening of the wrong content but the failure of a state to perform its proper function.

## Millikan and Dretske: Two Versions

Millikan's **biofunctions and proper functions** (*Language, Thought and Other Biological Categories*, 1984) are the most developed teleosemantic account. Every biological structure or behavior has a proper function — the function it was selected for — even if it currently fails to perform it. A mental state's proper function is to track X, even if it currently misfires. Millikan provides a detailed theory of how proper functions are inherited, derived, and extended, covering both innate representational structures and acquired concepts and beliefs.

Dretske's later work (*Explaining Behavior*, 1988) develops a related view where intentional content is fixed by **learning history** rather than evolutionary history. The state was formed during a period when it was reliably caused by X, and its current function is to track X. This handles the Swampman objection slightly better: a spontaneously assembled human duplicate would acquire content through its learning history, however short. But it faces the question of what determines which features of the learning environment are content-fixing, since the learner is caused by many things during any learning episode.

## Difficulties

The **indeterminacy problem** asks what the evolutionarily selected-for content actually is. The frog's fly-detector was selected to help catch food; does it represent flies, food, or small dark moving things? All three descriptions are accurate of the ancestral selection environment, and evolutionary history underdetermines which one is content-fixing. Millikan's theory of "most proximate" proper function addresses this in many cases, but the indeterminacy concern persists at the margins.

The **Swampman objection** (Davidson) is the most discussed. A physical duplicate of a human being assembled by cosmic accident in a swamp has no evolutionary history. Teleosemantics implies Swampman's "representations" have no content. Most philosophers find this counterintuitive: Swampman seems to think about water, dinosaurs, and arithmetic exactly as we do. Millikan and Papineau accept the result; others take it as a reductio.

**Novel content** poses a structural challenge. What determines the content of beliefs about atoms, prime numbers, the French Revolution, or fictional characters? These have no evolutionary history and no direct role in ancestral fitness. Teleosemantics must be supplemented by an account of how content extends beyond evolutionarily ancient categories — through learning, inference, social transmission — and developing this extension without circularity is a significant theoretical challenge.

Despite these objections, teleosemantics remains among the most defensible naturalistic approaches to mental content. It provides the most principled available solution to the misrepresentation problem, grounds intentionality in biological facts that are themselves unproblematically natural, and connects philosophy of mind to evolutionary biology in a way that enriches both.
