# Papineau's Teleosemantics

Papineau's teleosemantic theory (*Philosophical Naturalism*, 1993) shares Millikan's basic framework — mental content is determined by biological function, where function is defined by evolutionary or learning history — but places more explicit emphasis on both the upstream causal history and the downstream behavioral output of mental states. The content of a belief is fixed jointly by what it was selected to track and what it was selected to produce.

The core theory: a mental state M has content X if (1) M was selected (evolutionarily or by learning) because M-tokenings tend to be caused by X-instances, and (2) M's downstream effects — its behavioral dispositions — are appropriate to the presence of X, tending to produce adaptive responses when X is present. Both conditions are required. Content is determined by the full functional role of the state: the normal cause that explains its selection (input side) and the appropriate behavior it generates (output side).

This handles the disjunction problem via a distinction between what makes a mechanism reliable in the actual world and what explains its selection. A frog's fly-detector is also triggered by BB pellets in the actual world, but BB pellets did not exist in the ancestral environment in which the mechanism evolved. The mechanism was selected because it tracked flies, not because it tracked {flies or BBs}. The selection story fixes the content determinately at "flies" even though the actual causal history includes BB-triggers as deviations.

Papineau is more explicit than Millikan about the relationship between content and truth conditions. A belief is true if and only if it is caused by (or would be caused by) a situation of the type that fixes its content. Truth is naturalized: to say a belief is true is not to invoke a mysterious correspondence relation between representations and facts, but simply to say that the belief is caused by the right kind of situation — the kind that its selecting mechanism was designed to track. Truth is the successful performance of a biological function. This deflationary account of truth connects naturally to the naturalization project: if content is a biological fact and truth is successful content-performance, then truth is a biological fact too.

## Novel Content and Comparison with Millikan

The problem of **novel content** — beliefs about atoms, prime numbers, the French Revolution, fictional characters — is one that any teleosemantic theory must address. These are not categories our ancestors evolved to track; they are theoretical and cultural constructs. Papineau argues that the biological selection story can be supplemented by a story about general cognitive mechanisms that extend content to novel domains. We evolved capacities for forming hypotheses, combining concepts, and following inferences, and these capacities allow us to form beliefs about things beyond our evolutionary past. The content of these beliefs is fixed not by direct selection for tracking the novel objects but by the cognitive mechanisms that generate them, supplemented by the inferential and conceptual structures within which they are embedded. Critics argue this move is too permissive or requires an independent account of the content of the cognitive mechanisms themselves — a circularity worry that affects all hybrid approaches.

Papineau's teleosemantics differs from Millikan's in emphasis. Millikan's theory is primarily upstream — focused on what the mechanism was selected to track — though she also develops a detailed theory of "consumer systems" that exploit representations. Papineau weights the downstream functional role more explicitly, and his account is structured more directly around truth conditions than around the internal hierarchy of biological functions. He is also more explicit about the naturalization of normativity: normative correctness for beliefs is their having the truth conditions their selecting mechanisms were designed to generate. This makes normativity a matter of biological function rather than an irreducible property. Whether this constitutes a genuine naturalization of normativity or merely a redescription of it is one of the central contested questions about the teleosemantic program.
