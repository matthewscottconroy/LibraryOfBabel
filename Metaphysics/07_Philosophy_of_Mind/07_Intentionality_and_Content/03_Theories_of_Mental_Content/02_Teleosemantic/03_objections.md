# Objections to Teleosemantics

Teleosemantic theories ground mental content in biological function, and function in selection history. The objections probe whether this grounding is adequate — whether evolutionary history can really do the work required of a theory of content without leaving essential normative and semantic structure unexplained.

The **wrong level objection** questions whether natural selection operates at a fine enough grain to fix individual representational content. Selection operates on whole organisms and their fitness contributions; it selects for organisms that survive and reproduce. Whether it selects for individual neural representations — a specific belief about the color of a predator, as opposed to the entire cognitive system that produces predator-tracking behavior — is far from clear. Attributing selective fitness to a single belief, isolated from the cognitive network in which it is embedded, may mischaracterize how natural selection works. Millikan and Papineau must either show that selection operates at sufficient grain, or embrace a holistic account of content-fixing that treats entire cognitive systems as the units of selection. Millikan's detailed theory of proper functions attempts the former; critics remain unconvinced that the theory assigns determinate content to individual representations without arbitrary stipulation.

The **new species objection** creates a related difficulty. A species that has just evolved, whose members have never been selected for any specific behavior, would still seem to have mental representations with determinate content. Teleosemantics implies they don't — there is no selection history yet. Teleosemanticists typically respond that the new species evolved from earlier species, and the relevant selection history is inherited from ancestral lineages. This works when the new species's cognitive mechanisms are continuous with those of its ancestors, but the response may not extend to genuinely novel cognitive capacities.

## Swampman and the Normativity Gap

Davidson's **Swampman objection** is the most discussed. Imagine a physical duplicate of a human being assembled by cosmic accident in a swamp. Swampman has no evolutionary history — it came into being moments ago. Yet Swampman seems to have thoughts with determinate content: it immediately recognizes people, uses language, and behaves intentionally. If Swampman is physically identical to a human being, surely it has the same mental states.

Teleosemantics implies that Swampman's "representations" have no content, since they have no selection history. Millikan accepts this result: Swampman does not genuinely think; it merely behaves as if it thinks. Genuine intentionality requires proper functions, and proper functions require history. Swampman's internal states may be functional analogs of representations, but without the history, they lack genuine content. Critics find this prioritizes theoretical commitment over phenomenological plausibility: whatever the theory says, Swampman seems to be a genuine thinker, and a theory that implies otherwise should be revised.

The **indeterminacy of selection history** is a structural problem for all teleosemantic views. The frog's fly-detecting system was selected in an environment full of flies — but also full of small dark moving things, since flies are small dark moving things. Both descriptions are accurate of the ancestral environment. Which one fixes the content — flies or small dark moving things? Millikan's theory of "most proximate" proper function and the distinction between direct and derived functions addresses many cases, but the indeterminacy concern persists at the margins. For many concepts, multiple descriptions of the ancestral environment are compatible with the selection history, and no principled method selects among them.

## The Normativity Gap

The deepest objection is the **normativity gap**. Teleosemantics grounds normativity in natural selection, but natural selection is a descriptive process — the differential reproduction of traits. What a mechanism was selected for is a fact about the past. The normative force of content — the sense in which a representation is *correct* or *incorrect* — seems to be a present-tense fact about standards of accuracy. The heart should pump blood now, not merely because it did so in the past. The normative "should" seems stronger than the descriptive "was selected because."

Teleosemanticists respond that the relevant normativity is exactly the kind of functional oughtness at issue. The sense in which a representation ought to track X is just the sense in which a mechanism designed to track X has failed when it tracks something else. This is not a moral or rational ought but a functional ought — and functional oughts are naturalistically respectable.

Critics press back: this deflates normativity by equating it with functional failure. But the normativity of mental content seems to involve more than functional failure — it involves the capacity to be *mistaken* in a way that involves a subject who can recognize the error and correct it. A heart that fails to pump blood has not made a mistake; a thinker who forms a false belief has. Functional failure does not imply subjective error-recognition, and the teleosemantic cannot close this gap without invoking further normative resources that the biological framework does not supply on its own.

Despite these objections, teleosemantics remains among the most developed naturalistic approaches to mental content, with a principled solution to the misrepresentation problem that simple causal theories lack. The objections have motivated increasingly detailed theories within the teleosemantic framework and have driven the search for hybrid approaches that combine teleological considerations with informational, inferentialist, or normative elements.
