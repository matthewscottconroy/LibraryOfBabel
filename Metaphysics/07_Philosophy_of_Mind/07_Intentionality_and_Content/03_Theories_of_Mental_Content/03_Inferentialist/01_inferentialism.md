# Inferentialist Theories of Mental Content

What makes something the concept BACHELOR? Not its causal history — there are no bachelor-events that cause BACHELOR-tokenings. Not its biological function — evolution had no special interest in tracking the unmarried. The concept is the concept BACHELOR because of how it is used in reasoning: from "x is a bachelor," one infers "x is male" and "x is unmarried"; from "x is a married man," one infers "x is not a bachelor." The concept's identity is its inferential position in the space of reasons. **Inferentialist theories** generalize this: the content of any mental concept is determined by its inferential role — its relations of entailment, compatibility, and incompatibility with other concepts and propositions.

The tradition runs from Kant's claim that concepts are rules for synthesis — not images or copies of objects but patterns of judgment governing how perceptual material is taken up into thought — through Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), where meaning is use: what a word means is determined by how it is used in the practices and forms of life of a community. Sellars's *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (1956) systematizes this into the view that to be in a cognitive state is to be in a position within the "space of reasons" — to be able to make inferences, respond to reasons, and be held responsible for one's commitments. Brandom's *Making It Explicit* (1994) is the most systematic development.

Brandom argues that meaning is fundamentally a matter of normative inferential roles — the norms governing what a speaker is committed to, entitled to, and incompatible with when they use a term. Concepts are inferences-in-use. Brandom distinguishes **substitution-inferential roles** (relations of compatibility and incompatibility among concept applications) from **material inferences** (inferences valid in virtue of conceptual content rather than logical form alone: "This is red; therefore this is colored" is a material inference, valid because of the concepts RED and COLORED). Conceptual content is constituted by these material inferential relations. To grasp what a concept means is to master the material inferences it is involved in.

Crucially, Brandom adds two kinds of transitions that connect the inferential network to the world. **Language entry moves** are non-inferential transitions from perceptual states to conceptual applications — perception of a red surface grounds the non-inferential application of RED. **Language exit moves** are transitions from beliefs and desires to action — from the belief that the surface is red and the desire to avoid red to the action of avoiding it. These moves anchor the inferential network in perception and action without reducing content to causal relations.

## The Myth of the Given and the Normativity of Content

Sellars's contribution is his critique of the "Myth of the Given" — the idea that perceptual experience can provide a non-inferential foundation for knowledge. What makes something a cognitive state with content is precisely its location within the space of reasons: its connections to other cognitive states via inferential norms. Brute sensory states — states with no inferential connections — have no content. Content is not given by the world directly; it is instituted by normative practices of holding each other to inferential standards — what Brandom calls the practice of "deontic scorekeeping."

This means content is essentially normative and social. For something to be a concept is for it to be governed by norms of correct use — inferential rules distinguishing the correct from the incorrect application. These norms are constituted by social practices of inferential accountability.

The most telling objection from causal theories is that inferentialism explains content in terms of inferential role but cannot explain truth conditions — what makes a thought true or false. Internal inferential connections do not by themselves determine which external facts satisfy the concepts. Brandom's language entry and exit moves are designed to address this, but critics question whether non-inferential perceptual transitions can anchor genuine aboutness without smuggling in causal or informational relations under another name.

Inferentialists counter that causal theories get the order of explanation backwards. Causal theories explain content by appeal to causal relations to the world, but identifying the relevant causal relations — the content-determining ones among all the actual causes — already requires understanding what the representation is about. Reference presupposes content, not the other way around. And for theoretical terms — electrons, quarks, the unconscious, utility — inferential role really does seem to be what gives the concept its content, since there are no observable instances to which these concepts are causally connected in any direct way.

Inferentialism remains a live position representing an alternative to causal and teleological approaches, one that takes seriously the normative, social, and rational dimensions of mental content in ways that purely natural-causal theories cannot easily accommodate.
