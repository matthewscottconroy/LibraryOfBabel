# Sellars and Brandom on Inferentialism

Sellars's *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (1956) opens with a demolition that has shaped philosophy of mind ever since. The "Myth of the Given" is the assumption that perceptual experience provides a non-inferential epistemic foundation for knowledge — that sensory data are simply given to the mind with their content already determined, prior to any conceptual activity. Sellars argues against this on two grounds. Empirically: the idea of unconceptualized sensory data is a philosophical fiction; actual experience is always already conceptually structured. Epistemically: a state can justify beliefs only if it is itself in the "space of reasons" — connected to other states by inferential relations. A brute sensory impression, causally connected to the brain but not to the inferential network, justifies nothing. To be cognitively relevant, an experience must already be a conceptual state — one with inferential commitments and entitlements.

This leads directly to the inferentialist account of content. To be a cognitive state with content is to be in a position within the space of reasons — to be subject to inferential norms, to be the kind of thing that can serve as evidence for or against other states, to be the kind of thing for which one can be held epistemically responsible. The space of reasons is normative: it is governed by standards of what follows from what, what is compatible with what, and when applications of concepts are appropriate.

Brandom's *Making It Explicit* (1994) extends this into a comprehensive theory of language, thought, and intentionality. To possess a concept is to master a set of **material inferences** — inferences correct in virtue of the concepts involved, not merely their formal structure. The inference from "It is raining" to "It is wet outside" is a material inference involving RAIN and WET. Mastering the concept RAIN involves knowing this inference — being committed to its validity and responsive to challenges. Brandom's theory is built on a model of **deontic scorekeeping**: participants in a linguistic community keep track of each other's commitments and entitlements. When Alice asserts "It is raining," she undertakes a commitment to "It is wet outside" and to other inferential consequences. Bob can hold Alice to this commitment or challenge it. The norms governing this scorekeeping — what licenses what, what is incompatible with what — constitute the inferential content of the concepts used. Content is essentially social: concepts are not private mental items but positions in a shared normative practice.

## Language Entry, Language Exit, and the Anti-Naturalist Stance

Brandom does not leave the inferential network floating free of the world. **Language entry moves** are transitions from perceptual states to conceptual applications (seeing red grounds the non-inferential claim "That is red"). **Language exit moves** are transitions from conceptual states to action (the belief that the stove is hot and the desire not to be burned grounds the action of not touching the stove). These non-inferential moves connect the network to perception and action without reducing content to causal relations.

Brandom explicitly denies that intentionality can be naturalized. The space of reasons is not part of the causal order; normative statuses — commitments and entitlements — cannot be identified with any natural property. The relationship between the causal and the normative is one of expression and commitment, not reduction. This puts Brandom in fundamental tension with Fodor, Dretske, and Millikan, who seek a naturalistic account of the same phenomena.

Brandom argues that objective representational content — content about mind-independent facts — is explained within inferentialism. The objectivity of a concept consists in the fact that its application is governed by norms not merely expressing individual preference but responsive to how things are in the world. The world enters through the language entry moves (perceptual applications) and through the practice of offering observations as reasons.

McDowell's *Mind and World* (1994) shares Sellars's concern with the space of reasons and the critique of the Myth of the Given. McDowell's view is that perceptual experience is already conceptual — it presents the world as being a certain way — and that this conceptual perceptual content grounds the rational connection between experience and belief without reducing it to a merely causal connection. The Sellars-Brandom tradition thus offers a sophisticated account of the normative dimensions of mental content that purely causal and teleological approaches struggle to capture — at the cost of remaining outside the naturalist program that dominates much of philosophy of mind.
