# Problems for Conceptual Role Semantics

The problems for conceptual role semantics (CRS) and inferentialism are not incidental technical difficulties — they probe the limits of the inferentialist framework and clarify what any account of content must supply that purely internal inferential structure cannot.

The **holism problem** is the most pressing. If meaning is determined by inferential role, and inferential role includes all the inferential connections a concept has to other concepts, then any change in belief changes the inferential roles of all related concepts and thereby changes their content. Consider: Mary believes that all whales are fish, John believes that no whales are fish. Their concept WHALE has different inferential roles. On thoroughgoing CRS, they mean different things by "whale." But this seems wrong — their disagreement only makes sense if they are disagreeing about the same thing.

The holism problem has three related faces. The **individuation problem**: two people who disagree about any substantive matter will mean different things by their words, making genuine disagreement impossible (since disagreement requires sharing the concept at issue). The **communication problem**: if meaning changes whenever beliefs change, genuine communication requires complete agreement — an impossible standard. The **diachronic problem**: if my beliefs change over time, my concepts change their meaning continuously; the word "planet" meant something different before and after Pluto was reclassified.

CRS defenders respond by distinguishing *constitutive* from *peripheral* inferential connections. Only some connections are meaning-constituting (the "analytic" ones); others are merely contingent beliefs (the "synthetic" ones). But this is precisely the distinction Quine attacked in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951): no principled method can identify the meaning-constituting inferences independently of the full web of belief, without presupposing a prior grasp of meaning. The Quinean challenge makes the constitutive/peripheral distinction look unavailable to CRS without circularity.

## Three Further Problems

The **truth-conditional problem** challenges whether inferential role can determine extension. My RED concept applies to red things and not green things. CRS says: because RED has certain inferential connections — to COLORED, to ORANGE (nearby), to GREEN (incompatible), and to perceptual applications. But these connections are internal; they do not by themselves reach out to the world and determine which external facts make RED-applications true. A concept could have exactly the inferential role that RED has while applying to green things if the world were systematically different. Brandom's response appeals to language entry moves — perceptual transitions from world to concept — as the grounding. But critics note that these perceptual applications are themselves intentional, requiring content to be explained by the same inferentialist account, generating a regress.

The **learning problem** presses on concept possession. Children acquire concepts before mastering the full inferential apparatus. A child can possess the concept DOG before knowing that dogs are mammals, related to wolves, or anatomically characterized in particular ways. If concept possession requires full inferential mastery, children and novices have no concepts. One response — only core or constitutive connections are required — runs into the Quinean difficulties again: specifying which connections are core without appealing to content is a problem the theory has not solved.

The **naturalization problem** is explicitly acknowledged by Brandom and Sellars. Inferentialism holds that intentionality is essentially normative — constituted by norms of the space of reasons — and that norms cannot be reduced to natural facts. This makes inferentialism incompatible with the broadly naturalistic project of cognitive science, which seeks to explain mental processes in physical and biological terms. Brandom's position is that cognitive science explains the causal mechanisms underlying mental processes while inferentialism explains the normative content of those processes — complementary but distinct explanatory projects. Critics find this unsatisfactory: a complete account of mind should show how the normative and the causal relate, not simply assign them to separate explanatory domains with no bridge principles between them.

## Hybrid Responses

These problems have led to hybrid accounts combining inferential role with causal or teleological elements. Peacocke's theory of possession conditions (*A Study of Concepts*, 1992) holds that concept possession requires both inferential commitments and appropriate connections to perceptual and action-guiding states, the latter partly determined by the world. Two-factor theories (Devitt and others) distinguish inferential role (narrow content) from referential role (wide content). The emerging consensus is that inferential role is a necessary but not sufficient condition for content determination and must be supplemented by causal or functional connections to the world. CRS captures something essential about the rational and normative structure of thought; what it cannot supply, alone, is the grounding of that structure in the world.
