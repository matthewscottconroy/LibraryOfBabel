# Review Questions — Intentionality and Content

1. Brentano identified intentionality — the directedness of mental acts toward objects — as the mark of the mental, distinguishing it categorically from anything physical. Does Brentano's thesis survive the observation that Merleau-Ponty attributes a form of "motor intentionality" to the body itself, and that thermostats can be said (trivially) to be "directed at" temperature? What would it take for intentionality to genuinely distinguish the mental from the physical?

2. The problem of intentional inexistence — how a mental act can be genuinely *about* Sherlock Holmes when no such person exists — is handled differently by Meinong (subsistent non-existents), Russell (disguised descriptions), and Frege (senses without referents). Which approach best preserves the intuition that thought is genuinely directed at its object rather than merely characterized by a description? And what does the choice between these approaches imply for theories of mental content more broadly?

3. Putnam's Twin Earth argument shows that Oscar and Twin Oscar, who are molecule-for-molecule identical, differ in the content of their "water" thoughts because their respective environments contain H₂O and XYZ. Does this establish that mental content is partly constituted by the *actual* physical environment, or only that the *reference* of natural-kind terms is determined externally? Is there a distinction here, and does it matter?

4. Burge's arthritis case extends externalism from the physical environment (Putnam) to the social-linguistic environment: what you believe is partly determined by your community's standards of correct concept application. Burge argues that first-person authority is preserved with respect to *attitude type* even if not with respect to *content*. Is this a satisfying rescue of first-person authority, or does it concede the most important epistemic asymmetry between self-knowledge and knowledge of others?

5. Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory attempts to solve the disjunction problem within a purely causal framework: representation M means X because the Y-M causal connection depends asymmetrically on the X-M connection. The circularity objection holds that determining which connection is "basic" requires already knowing which is content-determining. Does this objection succeed, and is there a non-circular way to specify asymmetric dependence?

6. Teleosemantics grounds content in evolutionary history: what a state represents is determined by what it was selected to track. This handles misrepresentation (a frog's fly-detector misfires when triggered by a pellet) but faces the indeterminacy problem and the Swampman objection. Compare teleosemantics and Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory on these two challenges. Which theory is in better shape, and why?

7. Semantic externalism (Putnam, Burge) implies that two subjects who are functionally identical may have different mental contents. This threatens functionalism: if functional organization does not determine content, then mental states defined by functional role may lack determinate content. How serious is this threat, and is the two-dimensional approach (narrow content + context) an adequate response?

8. The naturalization of intentionality — explaining content in purely physical and causal terms — has been the central project of the chapter. If none of the proposed naturalizations (causal, teleosemantic, inferentialist) fully succeeds, what follows? Does the failure to naturalize intentionality support dualism, or might it instead support a view on which intentionality is a primitive feature of a broader naturalistic picture?
