# Mental Causation

We like to think that our mental states cause our behavior: that my belief that it is raining causes me to carry an umbrella, that my desire for coffee causes me to brew a pot, that my fear of the approaching car causes me to jump back from the road. This causal efficacy of the mental is not merely theoretically important; it is what makes the mind seem relevant to the world, what makes actions different from mere bodily motions, what gives psychology and folk psychology their explanatory purchase.

The problem is that the physical world appears to be causally closed. Every physical event has sufficient physical causes — there is no gap in the physical causal chain into which mental causes could insert themselves. If my brain's moving my arm has a sufficient neurological cause, what further causal work is there for the mental state of intending to raise my arm to do?

This is the exclusion problem, and it threatens the causal relevance of the mental under any non-reductive view of mind. If mental properties are identical to physical properties (type identity theory), there is no problem: the mental cause just is the physical cause. But if mental properties are distinct from physical properties — if being in pain is not the same as having C-fibers firing — then the mental property faces exclusion: the physical property already does all the causal work, leaving nothing for the mental property to cause.

Responses to the exclusion problem include accepting epiphenomenalism (mental properties cause nothing but are caused by physical events), accepting reductionism (mental properties are identical to physical properties after all), redefining causation so that the same event can have causes at multiple levels without exclusion, or arguing that the causal closure of physics is not as strong as the argument assumes.

The chapter develops the exclusion problem with care, examines the major responses, and connects mental causation to the debate about reasons and causes: are rationalizing explanations (she left because she wanted to) genuinely causal explanations, or a different kind of explanation altogether?
