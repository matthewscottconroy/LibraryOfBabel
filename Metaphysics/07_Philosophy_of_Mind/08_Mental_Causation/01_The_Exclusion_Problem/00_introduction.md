# The Exclusion Problem

Jaegwon Kim's exclusion problem is the most precise and most influential formulation of the problem of mental causation. The problem arises from a tension between four claims that each seem independently plausible: (1) mental events have causal effects on physical events (mental causation); (2) every physical event has a sufficient physical cause (physical causal closure); (3) mental properties are not identical to physical properties (non-reductivism); (4) the effects of mental events are not systematically overdetermined by mental and physical causes. These four claims are inconsistent, and any satisfactory theory of mind must explain which one to reject and why.

If mental properties are distinct from physical properties, and if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, then whenever a mental event M causes a physical event P, there is also a physical event that is the sufficient cause of P. Either M = the physical event (which contradicts non-reductivism), or M causes P independently of the physical cause (which generates overdetermination), or M is causally inefficacious with respect to P (which contradicts mental causation). No option is satisfactory.

Kim's own resolution is to accept a form of reductionism: mental properties that are causally efficacious must be reducible to physical properties, and those that are irreducible (like phenomenal properties) are causally inefficacious — epiphenomenal. This is a radical conclusion: it implies that our phenomenal experiences — our pains, our seeing-as-red, our felt emotions — make no causal difference to anything. Kim accepts this for phenomenal properties while arguing that functional properties can be reduced.

Non-reductivists have responded in several ways: by challenging the causal closure premise, by arguing for "emergent" causal powers that are not reducible but are genuinely causal, or by invoking a notion of causation (interventionism) that allows higher-level properties to be causally efficacious without competing with lower-level physical causes. This section examines the exclusion problem and its proposed solutions with care.
