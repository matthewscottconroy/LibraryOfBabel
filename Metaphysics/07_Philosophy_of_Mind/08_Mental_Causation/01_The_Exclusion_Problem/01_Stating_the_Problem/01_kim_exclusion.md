# Kim's Exclusion Problem

Non-reductive physicalism is the dominant position in contemporary philosophy of mind. It holds that mental properties are real properties — not mere façons de parler — while denying that they are identical to physical properties. Mental events are identical to physical events (token physicalism), but mental properties are not identical to physical properties (type non-reduction). Mental properties supervene on physical properties — no mental difference without a physical difference — and are multiply realizable in different physical substrates. This position, associated with Davidson's anomalous monism and Fodor's token physicalism, has the appeal of accommodating scientific naturalism while preserving the autonomy of psychology. Jaegwon Kim's exclusion problem is the argument that this comfortable middle ground does not exist.

The argument operates through four principles:

**Causal efficacy of the mental**: Mental events have causal effects on the physical world — my desire for coffee causes my arm to move toward the cup. This is a platitude of folk psychology and is assumed by all explanatory psychology.

**Causal closure of the physical**: Every physical event has a sufficient physical cause — the arm movement is fully caused by neural events. Physical causation is complete: no physical event requires a non-physical input.

**Non-reduction**: Mental properties are not identical to physical properties — there is a fact about the intentional content of my desire that cannot be captured in purely neurophysiological terms.

**No overdetermination**: The arm movement is not overdetermined by both the mental event and the physical neural event. Systematic overdetermination is implausible.

These four generate a contradiction. Mental events are causally efficacious (1), but every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause (2), and the mental cause is distinct from the physical cause (3). So the mental cause would have to be a second sufficient cause — which is overdetermination (excluded by 4). One of the four principles must be rejected.

## Kim's Diagnosis

Kim argues that rejecting overdetermination (4) is implausible for systematic cases — a world saturated with causal overdetermination is unlike the actual world, and it makes mental causes redundant. Rejecting causal closure (2) is not a serious option given the evidence from physics and neuroscience. Accepting epiphenomenalism — rejecting (1) — makes our rational self-understanding an illusion and undermines the explanatory project of psychology. The only stable resolution, Kim concludes, is to reject non-reduction (3): to identify mental properties with physical properties, giving reductive physicalism.

Kim considers the **functionalization response** in detail: mental properties might be causally efficacious as second-order properties — as the property of *having some physical property P that plays a certain functional role R*. Mental property M is then the second-order property *having a physical realizer that causes E*. M's causal efficacy is inherited from P's.

His objection cuts precisely: if M's causal contribution is just the contribution of the physical realizer P, then M qua M is causally redundant. It is P that does the causal work. The second-order property adds nothing to the causal story; it is a description of P's causal work, not an independent causal contribution. If this is right, functionalism does not solve the exclusion problem — it merely redescribes it.

Kim eventually argued that intentional mental properties (beliefs, desires, etc.) can in principle be reduced to functional-physical properties, but that phenomenal properties (qualia, what-it-is-like-ness) resist functional reduction. If so, phenomenal consciousness may be epiphenomenal — a deeply troubling result that motivated Kim's later work on the philosophy of mind. Most non-reductive physicalists have responded by contesting one of the four principles — most often by challenging causal closure, the no-overdetermination principle, or by reconceiving the nature of causation — generating the large literature on responses to the exclusion problem.
