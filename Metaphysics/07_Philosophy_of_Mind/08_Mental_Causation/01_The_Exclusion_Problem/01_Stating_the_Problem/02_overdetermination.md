# Overdetermination and Mental Causation

Consider the firing squad: two rifles fire simultaneously, either shot sufficient to kill. The death is overdetermined — it has two independent sufficient causes. This is genuine but rare. The worry that the exclusion argument raises is whether mental causation is systematically like this: every mental effect (arm movements, verbal reports, actions) overdetermined by both a mental cause and a physical cause. Kim's argument holds that systematic overdetermination of this kind is implausible. If it were true, mental causes would be universally present but universally redundant — the physical causes doing all the work. The exclusion problem is therefore not just the abstract observation that overdetermination is odd, but the specific concern that accepting it makes mental causation vacuous.

Causal overdetermination, stated precisely, occurs when: (1) event E occurs; (2) event C₁ is a sufficient cause of E — E would have occurred even if C₂ had not; (3) event C₂ is a sufficient cause of E — E would have occurred even if C₁ had not; and (4) C₁ and C₂ are numerically distinct events. In the two-bullet case, each bullet's penetrating the heart independently suffices for death. Had either not struck, the other would have caused death anyway. This is genuine causal duplication.

Applied to mental causation: if my desire for coffee (mental event M) and the neural firings implementing it (physical event P) are both sufficient causes of my arm's movement (E), then E is overdetermined. Non-reductive physicalism holds that M and P have distinct types even if their tokens are identical. The question is whether this structural situation involves the problematic kind of overdetermination.

## The Overdetermination Response

Some philosophers have accepted overdetermination as the price of non-reductive physicalism and argued that it is not as implausible as Kim suggests. The **macro-micro analogy** is the most discussed: when a vase breaks because it was struck by a rock, this event is explained both by the macro-level event (rock striking vase) and by the micro-level physical events (molecular collisions at the surface). We routinely accept that macro-events and micro-events both causally explain the same physical effects. If this ordinary macro-micro overdetermination is acceptable, why not mental-physical overdetermination?

The response also appeals to a distinction between **constitutive** and **additive** overdetermination. The two-bullet case involves two separately operating causal processes that converge on the same effect. In the mental-physical case, the mental process and the physical process are not separately operating — the mental is implemented in the physical. The overdetermination is not a doubling of independent causal power but something more like a higher-level description of the same causal process.

## The Response to the Overdetermination Response

Critics argue that the macro-micro case does not involve genuine overdetermination because the macro-event just is the micro-event described at a different level. The vase's breaking is the molecular collision — not a separate event alongside it. If that is so, there is no overdetermination; there is one event described twice. But mental events, on non-reductive physicalism, are genuinely distinct in type from their physical realizers even if their tokens coincide. The distinctness that non-reductive physicalism insists on is exactly what generates the overdetermination problem.

The counterfactual test is revealing. Whether overdetermination is present can be tested: in the two-bullet case, if bullet 1 had not struck, death would still have occurred via bullet 2. Apply this to mental causation: if the mental event had not occurred — if the desire for coffee had been absent while the neural firing remained — would the arm movement still have occurred? If physical closure holds and the physical cause is sufficient, the answer is yes. This makes the mental cause not just overdetermining but causally redundant. The Davidson strategy of arguing that mental events cause qua physical (not qua mental) concedes this point — it accepts that mental *properties* are not causally efficacious and only mental *events* (as physical events) are. Whether this preserves genuine mental causation — the causal relevance of what a desire is *about* — is the **irrelevance of content** objection, and it is what has driven much of the subsequent debate.
