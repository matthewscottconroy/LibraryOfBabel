# Physical Causal Closure

The thesis of physical causal closure — sometimes called the completeness of physics — holds that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause. The physical world is causally closed: no physical event requires a non-physical cause. Every physical effect can, in principle, be fully explained in terms of prior physical states plus the laws of physics. This thesis is what makes the exclusion problem so pressing. If the physical world is causally closed, there is no "room" for mental causes to make an additional causal contribution unless mental events are identical to (or constitutive of) physical events.

The thesis admits of different formulations. **Strong closure** holds that every physical event is nomically sufficient for its physical effects — given the laws of physics and the prior physical state of the world, all physical events are determined. This is classical determinism, denied by quantum mechanics. **Probabilistic closure** holds that every physical event has a complete probabilistic physical explanation — physical antecedents plus physical laws fix the probability of every physical event. This is compatible with quantum indeterminacy. Most philosophers accept weak or probabilistic closure; strong closure is now rarely defended.

## The Empirical Case for Closure

The empirical argument for physical causal closure is cumulative and powerful. Before modern biology, it was thought that living organisms required a special "vital force" not found in inorganic matter. Chemistry and molecular biology showed this to be false: biological processes are entirely explicable in terms of chemical and physical processes. Before modern neuroscience, it was thought that mental events might require intervention from a non-physical mind. Neuroscience has traced mental processes to neural processes with extraordinary precision. In neither case did physics need to be revised to include a non-physical causal factor.

The laws of physics do not include a "mental" term. They work entirely in terms of physical quantities — mass, charge, position, momentum. The extraordinary success of physical science in explaining physical phenomena without invoking non-physical causes motivates the inductive inference: physical causation is complete. The convergence of evidence from multiple independent domains — physics, chemistry, biology, neuroscience — points in the same direction.

## Challenges to Closure

Several philosophers have challenged causal closure directly. Popper and Eccles (*The Self and Its Brain*, 1977) argued for gaps in physical causation leaving room for mental causation. Eccles proposed that the mind interacts with the brain at the level of individual synaptic vesicles, exploiting quantum indeterminacy to influence neural firing. Critics responded that quantum-level interventions are too small to influence macroscopic neural behavior and that quantum events are random, not susceptible to intentional direction. The gap that quantum indeterminacy creates is a gap in the sense of randomness, not a gap in the sense of room for rational guidance.

More recent emergentist arguments hold that higher-level causation — at the level of organisms, computational processes, or social structures — is genuinely novel and not reducible to or explained by lower-level physical causation. Downward causation from higher levels to lower ones might be real, creating room for mental causation without violating closure strictly understood. Kim himself developed powerful objections to downward causation, arguing that genuine downward causation either reduces to same-level causation or involves the problematic causal overdetermination that the exclusion argument targets.

## Closure Within Naturalism

Most philosophers of mind accept causal closure as a constraint and build their accounts of mental causation within it. The debate then centers on how to accommodate mental causation given closure — through reductionism, through compatibilism about causation at multiple levels, or through reconceptions of the causal relation — rather than on whether closure holds. Accepting closure rules out interactionist dualism: the view that mental events can directly cause physical events as distinct non-physical events making their own causal contribution. Once closure is granted, the question becomes how mental causation is possible within a closed physical system — and that question is the exclusion problem.
