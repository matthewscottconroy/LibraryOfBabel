# Denying Physical Causal Closure

One response to the exclusion problem goes to its foundation: reject the premise that physical causation is complete. If physical events do not always have sufficient physical causes, there may be room for mental causes to contribute causally to physical effects without thereby overdetermining them. This is the strategy of interactionist dualists and some emergentists, and it traces directly to Descartes.

Descartes held that mind and body are genuinely distinct substances that interact causally at the pineal gland — the point of contact between the non-physical soul and the physical brain. Mental events cause physical events, and this requires that physical causation be open to non-physical inputs. Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia pressed Descartes on the mechanism immediately: how can a non-extended, non-spatial substance cause physical motion? Descartes's replies were unsatisfying. The interaction problem — how a non-physical substance can causally interact with a physical one — is the classical Achilles heel of substance dualism and has motivated many philosophers toward physicalism despite the exclusion problem.

Popper and Eccles (*The Self and Its Brain*, 1977) attempted to make interactionism scientifically respectable by locating the mind-body interface at the level of quantum indeterminacy. Eccles proposed that the mind influences the brain at individual synaptic vesicles, tipping the balance in quantum-probabilistic neurotransmitter release. Since quantum events are genuinely indeterministic, the mind can make a causal contribution without violating any deterministic physical law.

This faces three serious objections. The **decoherence objection**: quantum decoherence occurs extremely rapidly in warm, wet biological systems — the quantum superpositions that would be needed for mental intervention decohere into classical states before they could influence neural computation. The **randomness objection**: quantum events are genuinely random, not directed by causal agents. Even if the mind could influence which quantum outcome is realized, it is not clear how mental intentions could constitute a non-random constraint on quantum probabilities — how the gap created by quantum indeterminacy could be intentionally filled rather than randomly filled. The **small-scale objection**: quantum fluctuations at individual synapses are too small and too local to explain the large-scale neural and behavioral effects that mental causation is supposed to produce.

## Assessment

Most philosophers of mind accept causal closure as a constraint and seek responses to the exclusion problem within this constraint. Denying closure remains a minority position. The empirical evidence for physical causal completeness is strong and cumulative: the physical sciences have not found any physical events that require non-physical causes, and neuroscience has made extraordinary progress explaining neural processes without invoking non-physical mental properties. Denying closure appears ad hoc — motivated by the philosophical desire to create room for mental causation rather than by independent scientific evidence.

The strategy of denying closure does have a constructive legacy, however, in motivating more sophisticated emergentist positions. These positions try to show that higher-level causation — including mental causation — is genuinely irreducible to lower-level physical causation without requiring gaps in physical law. Whether genuinely novel higher-level causal powers can be coherent without such gaps is the central question that the emergentist tradition continues to address.
