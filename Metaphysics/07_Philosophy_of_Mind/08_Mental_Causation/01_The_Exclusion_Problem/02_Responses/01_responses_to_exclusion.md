# Responses to the Exclusion Problem

The exclusion problem has generated a large literature of responses, each targeting one of the four premises or challenging the inference structure of Kim's argument. None has achieved consensus, and the debate remains one of the most active in philosophy of mind. The responses divide into six main strategies.

**Reject causal closure.** If physical causation is not closed, there is room for mental causation without overdetermination. This is the interactionist strategy. Critics respond that the empirical evidence for causal closure is strong: the physical sciences have not found any physical events requiring non-physical causes, and denying closure requires accepting gaps in physical law that current evidence does not support. The quantum indeterminacy version — that the mind can influence which quantum outcome occurs — faces the objections that quantum-level influences are too small for macroscopic effects and that quantum randomness is not intentionally directed.

**Accept overdetermination.** Some philosophers bite the bullet: physical events are systematically overdetermined by both physical and mental sufficient causes. If the mental supervenes on the physical, the two "causes" are not genuinely independent, so systematic overdetermination is less troubling than in the two-bullet case. Kim's counter is that this effectively makes mental causes redundant. Since mental properties supervene on physical ones, there is no possible world in which the mental cause is present and the physical cause absent. The mental cause cannot make a counterfactual difference — which undermines its causal status entirely.

**Reject non-reduction.** The reductionist response accepts causal closure and denies the distinctness of mental and physical properties: mental properties just are physical properties. On type identity theory or realizer functionalism, the mental cause is the physical cause, described at a different level. There is no overdetermination because identity is not overdetermination. The cost is giving up multiple realizability and the explanatory autonomy of psychology.

## Realization, Proportionality, and Interventionism

**The realization view** (Yablo, Shoemaker) holds that the relationship between mental and physical causes is realization rather than competition. A statue and a piece of marble are not two separate objects competing for the same spatial location; the marble realizes the statue. Similarly, the physical cause realizes the mental cause — they are not two separate causes but one cause described at two levels of abstraction. Yablo's **proportionality criterion** refines this: the cause of an effect should be the description under which the cause makes a counterfactual difference proportional to the effect. On this criterion, mental causes can be genuinely causally relevant because the mental description is the appropriate level for explaining the effect under the relevant counterfactuals. The mental cause is not redundant because it captures real counterfactual dependencies — what the agent would have done if the desire had been different — that the physical description alone does not reveal.

**The interventionist account** (Woodward) holds that X causes Y if an ideal intervention on X would change Y. Mental properties are causally relevant to physical effects if an ideal intervention on mental properties would change the physical effects. Changing beliefs through argument changes behavior — this establishes counterfactual dependence at the mental level. Physical causal closure ensures that any intervention on mental properties is also an intervention on physical properties (since mental supervenes on physical), but Woodward's pluralistic account holds that the mental-level explanation provides genuine causal information not captured by the physical-level description alone.

**The constitutive approach** holds that mental causation and physical causation are not competing explanations at the same level. Mental events do not add a second causal chain alongside the physical; they are the physical events, described differently. The exclusion problem assumes that mental and physical causes are two distinct entities competing for the same causal role, but this assumption — that cause-talk is univocal across levels — is not obviously correct. The most widely held view among non-reductive physicalists is some version of the realization or constitutive response, which argues that mental causation is genuine because mental properties are realized in physical properties that are causally sufficient for the effects in question, without being a second sufficient cause on top of them.
