# Non-Reductive Solutions

Non-reductive physicalists want both: genuine mental causation and genuine distinctness of mental properties. The exclusion problem says they cannot have both. The non-reductive solutions are attempts to show that they can — either by showing that mental properties are causally efficacious without being separately sufficient causes, or by arguing that the exclusion problem rests on a mistaken conception of causation or causal explanation. None has achieved consensus, but each illuminates something important about the structure of the problem.

**The realization view** (Yablo, *Mental Causation*, 1992; Shoemaker, *Physical Realization*, 2007) holds that the relationship between mental and physical causes is realization rather than competition. A shape and the arrangement of metal that realizes it are not two competing objects in one location; they are one object characterized at different levels. Similarly, the mental property and its physical realizer are not two competing causes for the same effect — they are one cause, described at two levels. Yablo's **proportionality criterion** refines this: the correct description of a cause should be proportional to the effect — neither too specific (mentioning causally irrelevant details) nor too general (losing causally relevant distinctions). When a belief causes behavior, the proportional description is the mental one — "believing that water is nearby" — not the specific neural characterization, which is too fine-grained for the behavioral effect in question. Mental descriptions pick out the causally relevant properties in a way that physical descriptions at the micro-level do not.

**Woodward's interventionist account** holds that X causes Y if an ideal intervention on X would change Y. Applied to mental causation: mental properties are causally relevant to physical effects if intervening on mental properties — changing beliefs through argument, perceptions through experience — makes systematic differences to behavior. This supports pluralism about causal explanation: both mental-level and physical-level descriptions are genuinely causal, since interventions at each level produce counterfactual differences. Physical causal closure does not eliminate mental causation because mental-level interventions are genuine interventions. The objection — that any intervention on mental properties is also an intervention on physical properties, making physical causation prior — Woodward answers by insisting that the explanatory information provided by mental-level causal claims is not captured by physical-level claims. Mental-level explanations identify the conditions under which behavior changes in response to reasons, and that information is not conveyed by specifying neural states.

## Causal Relevance, Constitutive Views, and Anomalous Monism

An important strategy distinguishes **causal sufficiency** from **causal relevance**. Physical causal closure concerns causal sufficiency: the physical cause is sufficient for the physical effect. But mental properties can be causally relevant to effects without being separately sufficient. My belief that the stove is hot is causally relevant to my avoiding it — it explains why I avoided it, not merely that some physical event sufficient for avoidance occurred. Causal relevance and causal sufficiency come apart: the physical cause is sufficient, but the mental property is relevant in the explanatory sense that matters for understanding the agent.

The **constitutive view** holds that mental events do not add a second causal chain alongside the physical; they are the physical events, characterized in terms of their psychological profile. There is no more a problem of mental causation than there is a problem of statue causation: when a statue causes its shadow, the statue is not competing with the bronze that constitutes it. The exclusion problem wrongly assumes that mental and physical causes are two distinct entities competing for the same causal role.

Davidson's **anomalous monism** (*Mental Events*, 1970) preserves token identity while denying type reduction. Mental events are identical to physical events, but mental descriptions are irreducible because mentality is governed by normative principles of rationality that physical descriptions cannot capture. Mental events cause physical events not qua mental but qua physical: the causal connection is secured by the physical description. This preserves mental causation at the event-token level while denying that mental *properties* are separately causally efficacious.

The **content irrelevance objection** (Fodor, Baker, McLaughlin) is the sharpest challenge to anomalous monism and to constitutive views generally. If mental properties are not causally efficacious — if only physical event tokens are — then the content of a mental state makes no causal difference. Whether I believe it will rain or that the sun is shining makes no causal difference if only the underlying neural state matters. This eliminates the explanatory significance of psychological explanation: what a desire is *about* never causes anything. Most non-reductive physicalists take this to be an unacceptable result, and it is why the search for a non-reductive solution that preserves the causal relevance of mental content continues.
