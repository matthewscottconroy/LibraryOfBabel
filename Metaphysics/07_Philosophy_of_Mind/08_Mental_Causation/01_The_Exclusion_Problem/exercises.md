# Exercises

1. **Reconstructing the argument**: Write out Kim's exclusion argument as formally as possible (with numbered premises and conclusions). Identify the weakest premise and the strongest response to it. Does the argument succeed?

2. **Overdetermination response**: Suppose mental causation involves systematic overdetermination (every neural event has both a neural cause and a mental cause). Is this philosophically acceptable? Compare to cases of genuine overdetermination (a wall being hit by two balls simultaneously).

3. **Levels of explanation**: Some philosophers argue that mental causation and physical causation operate at different explanatory levels, so there is no competition. Is this a coherent position? Does it preserve genuine mental causation or merely "mental explanation"?

4. **Downward causation in biology**: Biologists often describe higher-level biological processes as causally influencing lower-level chemical processes (regulation of gene expression, for example). Does this show that higher-level causation is real? Or is this always reducible to lower-level chemical causation?

5. **The Kim dilemma**: Kim argues that non-reductive physicalism must choose between reduction and epiphenomenalism. Is there a third option? What would it look like to have genuinely irreducible mental properties that are nevertheless causally efficacious?
