# Important Terms and Ideas: The Exclusion Problem

**The Exclusion Problem**
Kim's challenge: if every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, and mental events are not identical to physical events, then mental causes are systematically excluded by physical causes. Adding a mental cause would overdetermine every physical effect, which is implausible. So either mental events are physical (reduction) or they have no causal efficacy (epiphenomenalism).

**Causal Closure of the Physical**
The principle that every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause—causes at the physical level are complete in explaining physical effects. This principle, combined with the distinctness of mental events from physical events, generates the exclusion problem.

**Overdetermination**
A causal situation where an event has more than one independent sufficient cause. Kim argues that if mental causation is genuine and physical causation is complete, every mental event's physical effect is overdetermined—by both a mental cause and a physical cause. Since overdetermination is rare and special, this conclusion is implausible.

**Causal Relevance vs. Causal Efficacy**
The distinction between a property being relevant to causation (figuring in the causal explanation) and being efficacious (doing the causal work). Non-reductive physicalists attempt to show that mental properties are causally relevant even if physical properties are causally efficacious.

**Proportionality**
Yablo's criterion: the right cause of an effect is the one whose presence and absence perfectly matches the effect's presence and absence—the most proportionate cause. Mental causes are often more proportionate than neural causes because they specify only the relevant level of description.

**Downward Causation**
Causation from higher-level to lower-level properties or events. Mental causation on a non-reductive view requires downward causation: mental properties causally influence physical events. Kim challenges whether genuine downward causation is coherent given the closure of the physical.
