# Davidson: Reasons as Causes

In 1963, the prevailing view in analytic philosophy of action was neo-Wittgensteinian: reasons for action are not causes of action but a categorically different kind of explanation. Ryle argued that mental explanations describe an agent's dispositions, not antecedent events. Anscombe (*Intention*, 1957) held that explaining an action by giving reasons places it in a normative framework — showing the action as intelligible given the agent's beliefs and desires — which is logically distinct from causal explanation. Reason-giving and cause-giving are different forms of explanation; one rationalizes, the other identifies producing conditions. Davidson's "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" argued that this is wrong, and changed the field.

Davidson's argument: for reasons to be the actual reasons for which an agent acted, they must be causes. Consider Jones, who believes the gas is left on and wants to prevent a fire. She turns off the stove. Did she turn off the stove *for those reasons*? Davidson argues yes, only if those beliefs and desires causally produced the action. If they were causally irrelevant — if she turned off the stove because of a nervous tic that happened to coincide with her having those reasons — she did not act for those reasons. The "acting for a reason" relation is constituted by causal connection. Without it, the reasons are merely post-hoc rationalizations, not the actual motivation.

Davidson introduces the concept of a **primary reason** for an action:

> A primary reason for an action consists of a pro attitude toward actions of a certain kind and a belief that the action is of that kind.

A primary reason is a desire-belief pair. The agent wants some outcome (a pro attitude) and believes the action will bring it about. The primary reason both rationalizes the action and causes it. The same mental state that makes the action intelligible is also the causal antecedent that produced it.

## Deviant Causal Chains and Anomalous Monism

Davidson recognizes that causal connection between reason and action is necessary but not sufficient for acting for a reason. **Deviant causal chains** intervene: the desire to turn off the stove might make the agent so anxious that she frantically sweeps her arm, accidentally turning it off in a way she had not planned. The desire caused the behavior, but not in the right way — not through the normal mechanism by which intentions guide voluntary action. Specifying what counts as the right causal pathway is a major ongoing project in philosophy of action. Proposals include: the causation must proceed through a conscious intention that governs the action; the agent must monitor progress toward the goal during the action; the normal mechanism for voluntary action must be operative throughout.

Davidson embeds reasons-as-causes in his broader **anomalous monism**: mental events are identical to physical events (token physicalism), but there are no strict laws connecting mental event types to physical event types. This allows mental events to cause physical events (as physical events, under their physical descriptions) while maintaining the irreducibility of the mental. The **principle of charity** supports the irreducibility: in interpreting another person's actions and beliefs, we must maximize the rationality of their beliefs and desires. What makes something a belief or desire is partly that it is embedded in a rationally coherent network. Physical descriptions carry no such rationality constraint, which is why mental descriptions cannot be captured by physical ones.

The **content irrelevance objection** follows. If mental events cause physical events under their physical descriptions but not under their mental descriptions, then the content of a belief — what makes it a belief about rain rather than sunshine — makes no causal difference. The mental properties are causally idle; only the underlying physical events cause anything. Davidson's response is that mental descriptions serve a different explanatory role: to explain an action by citing reasons is to show it as rational, to place it in the logical space of justification. This is a legitimate explanatory project distinct from the causal project of specifying sufficient conditions. Both projects are valuable, and neither reduces to the other. Whether this response preserves genuine mental causation — the causal efficacy of intentional content — or merely redescribes the problem remains one of the central contested questions in philosophy of action.
