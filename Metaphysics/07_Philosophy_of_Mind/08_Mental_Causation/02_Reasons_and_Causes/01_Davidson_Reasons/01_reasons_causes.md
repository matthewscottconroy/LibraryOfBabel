# Davidson: Reasons and Causes

To explain why Jones turned off the stove, we say she believed the gas was on and wanted to prevent a fire. This is a reason-giving explanation. It shows the action as rational — it makes the behavior intelligible given Jones's mental states. The question Davidson poses in "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963) is whether this kind of explanation is also causal, or whether it is something else: a description of dispositions (Ryle), or a placement of the action in a normative framework (the Wittgensteinians), categorically distinct from causal explanation.

Davidson argues it is both. Specifically: for a reason to be the reason *for which* Jones acted — not merely a reason that rationalizes the act in retrospect — the reason must have caused the action. If Jones's belief and desire were causally irrelevant and she turned off the stove for some neurological accident that happened to coincide with her having those reasons, she did not act for those reasons. The causal connection is constitutive of the "acting for a reason" relation.

A **primary reason** for an action consists of a pro attitude toward actions of a certain kind and a belief that the action is of that kind. The primary reason for turning off the stove is the pair: (wanting to prevent a fire) + (believing that turning off the stove will prevent the fire). This pair rationalizes the action — it shows why turning off the stove is intelligible given these mental states — and causes it: these are the mental states that produced the behavior.

Davidson argues by **counterfactual dependence**. Suppose Jones has all the beliefs and desires that rationalize an action, but the action occurs for some unrelated reason. The reasons did not explain the action in the relevant sense — the action was not done *for* those reasons. The "for those reasons" condition requires that the reasons causally produced the action. Rationalizing explanation without causal connection is merely post-hoc reconstruction.

## Deviant Causal Chains and Anomalous Monism

Even granting that reasons must cause actions, Davidson recognizes that causal connection is not sufficient. **Deviant causal chains** intervene: a belief and desire can cause behavior through an abnormal pathway — through anxiety, trembling, or accident — without the action counting as done for those reasons. The desire to avoid embarrassment might produce such panic that the agent's behavior becomes erratic and happens to produce the intended result through luck. The reason caused the action, but not in the right way.

Davidson's solution: the right kind of causation runs through the agent's intentions and their normal implementation in voluntary action. What "normal" means here is contested, and specifying it without circularity — without appealing to the notion of intentional action itself — is an ongoing problem in philosophy of action.

Davidson embeds all of this in his broader **anomalous monism**. Mental events are identical to physical events (token physicalism). But there are no strict psycho-physical laws connecting mental types to physical types (anomalism). Mental descriptions are governed by the **principle of charity** — we interpret agents as maximally rational, maximally coherent — and this rationality constraint has no counterpart in physical descriptions. The mental vocabulary is irreducible because it answers to a normative standard (rationality) that physical vocabulary does not.

This allows Davidson to preserve mental causation at the level of event tokens: the reason (as a physical event) causes the action (as a physical event). But it generates the **content irrelevance objection**: if the causal work is done by the physical event under its physical description, then the mental properties — the content of the belief, the intentional structure of the desire — are causally inert. Whether I believe it will rain or that the sun is shining makes no causal difference, since only the underlying neural states cause anything. Davidson's response is that rationalizing explanation serves a different legitimate purpose from causal explanation, and neither reduces to the other. Most philosophers find this too concessive — genuine agency requires that what the desire is *about* makes a causal difference — which is why the content irrelevance problem remains unresolved.
