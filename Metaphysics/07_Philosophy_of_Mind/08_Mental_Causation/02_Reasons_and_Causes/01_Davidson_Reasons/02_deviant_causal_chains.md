# Deviant Causal Chains

Davidson established that reasons must cause actions for the actions to be done for those reasons. This is necessary for intentional action. But it is not sufficient. A striking class of counterexamples shows that reasons can cause actions through the wrong pathway — deviant causal chains — and when they do, the action is not intentional, however clearly the agent had the reason.

Davidson's own example (*Freedom to Act*, 1973): a climber holds a rope attached to a companion. The desire to loosen his grip on the rope — and thereby rid himself of his companion — makes the climber so nervous that he loses control of his hands and accidentally drops the rope. Here there is a reason (the desire to let go), and the reason causally contributes to the dropping (via the nervousness). But the climber did not intentionally drop the rope or drop it for the reason he desired. The causal chain between reason and action exists but is deviant: the reason influenced the action through an unintended mechanism (anxiety → loss of motor control) rather than through the standard mechanism by which intentions produce voluntary movements.

A second example: an agent intends to kill someone and drives carefully to the house. While driving, startled by a child crossing the road, she swerves and accidentally kills a pedestrian who turns out to be the intended victim. The intention caused the driving, which causally contributed to the death. But she did not kill the victim for the reason and did not do so intentionally. The causal chain runs through an accident, not through the normal process by which intentions guide deliberate action.

The standard causal theory of action holds: Agent A performs action X for reason R if and only if R causes A's X-ing. Deviant causal chains show that this biconditional fails in the forward direction — causal connection is not sufficient. Something further is required: some condition on the structure or manner of causation that rules out deviant chains.

## Proposed Solutions and Their Difficulties

The **right mechanism condition** holds that the causation must proceed through the mechanisms that standardly mediate between intentions and voluntary movements — specifically, the system for voluntary motor control. Deviant cases involve mechanisms (anxiety, reflexes, chance events) that are not part of the normal intention-to-action pathway. The problem: what determines the "right" mechanism? If we say it is the mechanism that normally mediates between reasons and actions, we presuppose a notion of "normal" that is difficult to specify without circularity. We seem to need to appeal to intentional action to identify the normal pathway — which makes the analysis non-reductive.

The **monitoring condition** holds that the agent must be continuously monitoring and adjusting her action in light of her intention throughout the process. Deviant cases involve actions the agent is not guiding in light of her intention. The problem: some intentional actions — skilled performances, automatic behaviors — do not involve conscious monitoring. Skilled pianists do not consciously monitor each finger movement; yet their playing is intentional.

The **appropriate process condition** (Mele, Bishop) holds that the causation must proceed through a process that constitutes the agent's practical reasoning — the process by which the agent deliberates, forms intentions, and translates them into movements. Deviant processes are those that bypass or distort this practical reasoning structure. This is more plausible but faces the challenge of specifying practical reasoning in terms that are neither circular nor purely physical.

Deviant causal chains reveal something important: intentional action cannot be reduced to a mere causal-mechanical process. There must be something about the way reasons cause actions — not just that they cause them — that distinguishes intentional from non-intentional causation. This distinction seems to require a normative or structural condition on the causal process. Specifying that condition without appealing to intentionality threatens circularity; allowing an appeal to intentionality may make the analysis non-reductive. This is one of the central open problems in the philosophy of action.
