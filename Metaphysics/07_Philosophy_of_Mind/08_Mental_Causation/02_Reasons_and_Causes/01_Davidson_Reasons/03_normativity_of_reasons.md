# The Normativity of Reasons

A reason is not merely a psychological state that produces behavior. A reason is something that counts in favor of an action — something that makes an action appropriate, justified, or rational. This normative dimension distinguishes reasons from causes in the ordinary sense, and it creates a persistent tension with causal accounts of mental causation.

Philosophers distinguish between **motivating reasons** — the psychological states that actually motivate an agent, the desire-belief pairs that cause and rationalize action in Davidson's sense — and **normative reasons** — facts or considerations that count in favor of an action independently of whether any particular agent is motivated by them. That it would save a child's life is a normative reason to act whether or not the agent is motivated by child-saving. An agent can act on motivating reasons that are not normative reasons (acting from bad or mistaken reasons) and can have normative reasons without motivating reasons (there is reason to act but no motivation). The gap between motivating and normative reasons is the gap between psychological causes and rational justifications.

Davidson held that reasons are causes, and that explaining an action by citing reasons is a form of causal explanation. But he also recognized the normative dimension through his **principle of charity**: in attributing mental states to an agent, we must maximize the rationality of the belief-desire system. This is not merely a methodological principle but a partially constitutive one — what makes something a belief or desire is partly that it is embedded in a rationally coherent network. The normative standards of rationality are constitutive of the mental. Since physical descriptions carry no rationality constraint, mental descriptions cannot be captured by physical ones.

## McDowell's Challenge

McDowell argues that reasons for action are constitutively normative — they belong to the "space of reasons," which is irreducible to the "realm of causes." When we explain an action by citing a reason, we are not identifying a prior psychological event that produced the action; we are showing the action as the rational response to a situation as perceived by the agent. The causation involved in reason-explanation is not the "pushing and shoving" causation of physical events but *rational causation* — the kind of causal relation that obtains when an agent responds to reasons as reasons. This cannot be captured by purely physical descriptions, because rationality is an irreducibly normative concept. Reducing rational causation to physical causation loses the normative dimension — which is what the explanation was explaining in the first place.

## The Naturalistic Response and the Normativity Gap

Naturalists argue that the normativity of reasons is itself naturalizable. If reasons are mental states with representational content, and if representational content can be naturalized via causal or teleosemantic theories, then reasons inherit their normativity from the normativity of content — which is explained by selection history or causal-functional organization. The fact that a reason counts in favor of an action is grounded in the content of the mental states: the desire has content (making P true), the belief has content (that A will make P true), and together they give the agent a reason to do A.

Critics press the **normativity gap**: even if the content of a desire can be naturalized, the fact that having a desire with content C counts in favor of A is not a naturalistic fact. It is a normative fact — and normative facts cannot be derived from naturalistic facts (Hume's Guillotine). Naturalizing content explains what the desire is *about*, not why having it gives the agent a reason to act.

This debate connects directly to the exclusion problem. If reasons are normative and cannot be reduced to physical states, they seem unable to be causes in the physical sense — since physical causation requires nomically connected physical states. If they can be fully naturalized, the normative dimension seems to evaporate. The challenge is to explain how reasons can be both causally efficacious and genuinely normative — how the rational and the causal coincide. This is the central tension in the metaphysics of mental causation: between the image of the human being as a rational agent governed by reasons, and the image of the human being as a physical system governed by causal laws. Whether these two images can be reconciled or describe complementary aspects of the same reality is one of the deepest unresolved questions in philosophy of mind.
