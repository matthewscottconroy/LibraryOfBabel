# Action Theory

What is the difference between my arm rising and my raising my arm? Both involve the same physical movement — the same muscle contractions, the same displacement of the limb. Yet one is an action and one is not. A reflex arc produces movement but not action. A spasm produces movement but not action. When I intentionally raise my arm, something is present that is absent in the reflex case, even if we cannot detect it by measuring the physical movement. Action theory is the philosophical study of that something: what transforms a mere bodily movement into a genuine action, what distinguishes intentional from unintentional action, and how reasons, intentions, and bodily movements are related.

This connects directly to mental causation. If actions require mental causes — reasons, intentions, desires — what kind of causation is involved, and how do mental states produce physical movements? But action theory also raises a deeper question: whether the mental-causal story is sufficient to explain agency, or whether agency involves something irreducible to any causal account.

**Volitionism** holds that what makes a movement an action is that it is caused by a prior mental state — a volition, desire, or act of will. The challenge is the regress problem: if every action requires a prior mental cause, what causes the act of will? If the act of will is itself an action, it requires a prior mental cause, and so on. Volitionism faces either an infinite regress or a primitive "pure act of will" that is itself unexplained.

**Agential guidance accounts** hold that actions are distinguished not by their causal history alone but by their ongoing structure. An action is a movement subject to continuous guidance by the agent — she monitors, adjusts, and controls the movement in light of her intention throughout its execution. What distinguishes intentional action from reflex or accident is that the agent is continuously regulating the movement: correcting deviations, adjusting to feedback, maintaining the movement on track. This explains the deviant causal chain cases more naturally than pure volitionism.

**Anscombe's intentional description account** (*Intention*, 1957) makes intentionality conceptually prior to causation. An event is an action under a description when the agent can be asked "Why are you doing X?" and give a rational answer. An arm rising is an action under the description "signaling" if it makes sense to ask why the agent is signaling. The action is identified by its rational structure, not its causal antecedents.

## The Belief-Desire Model and Its Extensions

The dominant contemporary account is the **belief-desire model**, developed by Davidson and subsequent philosophers. An action A is performed intentionally if it is caused by a belief B and a desire D in the right way, where D is a pro-attitude toward some outcome O, B is the belief that doing A will produce O, and the B-D complex causes A through the agent's practical reasoning through the non-deviant pathway. This model is psychologically tractable, connects to functionalism in philosophy of mind, and provides a basis for the computational modeling of action.

Bratman's **planning theory** (*Intention, Plans, and Practical Reason*, 1987) extends the model. Intentions are not mere belief-desire combinations but planning states — settled commitments that structure future reasoning and coordinate behavior over time. Intentions are stable (they persist through deliberation), coordinative (they constrain future planning), and carry genuine commitment (not merely behavioral disposition). This planning structure explains the temporal structure of human agency: we form plans in advance, act on them, and adjust them in light of new information — a structure that cannot be captured by simple belief-desire pairs.

**Weakness of will (akrasia)** presses on the belief-desire model. An agent might judge that A is all-things-considered best, have the desire to do A, and yet do B. Davidson argued that the akratic action is intentional — performed for a reason — but not performed in accordance with the agent's all-things-considered judgment, requiring a distinction between different standings of reasons in the agent's psychology.

Libet's neuroscience experiments (1983) introduced an empirical challenge: the readiness potential — the neural preparation for voluntary movement — precedes conscious awareness of the intention to act by approximately 350 milliseconds. This has been taken to challenge the view that conscious intentions cause voluntary actions. The debate about its implications — whether it shows that conscious will is an illusion, a post-hoc narrative, or something consistent with conscious causation — is one of the most active areas at the intersection of philosophy and neuroscience.
