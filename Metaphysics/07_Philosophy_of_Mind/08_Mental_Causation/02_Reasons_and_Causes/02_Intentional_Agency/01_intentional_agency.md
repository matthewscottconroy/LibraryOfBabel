# Intentional Agency and the Explanation of Action

Intentional agency involves acting for reasons — performing actions because of beliefs and desires, in light of goals, in a way that the agent can reflectively endorse and explain. The explanation of intentional action is central to philosophy of mind and action theory alike. What distinguishes intentional action from mere behavior? How do mental states cause actions? And what is the relationship between intentional explanation — citing beliefs and desires — and causal-mechanical explanation?

The **belief-desire model**, associated with Davidson and developed extensively by subsequent philosophers, is the standard account. An action A is performed intentionally if and only if it is caused by a belief B and a desire D in the right way, where D is a pro-attitude toward some outcome O (the agent wants O), B is the belief that doing A will produce O, the B-D complex causes A through the agent's practical reasoning, and the causation is of the appropriate, non-deviant kind. This model unifies the causal and rational dimensions of action: the belief-desire pair both explains the action (shows why the agent did it) and causes it (as the psychological antecedent of the behavior). To explain an intentional action by citing reasons is to exhibit the agent's practical syllogism — the implicit reasoning from desires and beliefs to action.

**Akrasia** presses on the model. An agent might judge that action A is all-things-considered best, have the desire to do A, and yet do B. Explaining akrasia requires distinguishing the agent's all-things-considered judgment (a cognitive state) from the operative desire (the motivational state that actually produces behavior). Davidson argued that the akratic action is intentional — performed for a reason — but not performed in accordance with the agent's all-things-considered judgment. This requires a richer psychology than simple belief-desire pairs can represent, because the agent's own assessment of her reasons conflicts with what she does.

## The Planning Theory and Collective Intentionality

Bratman's **planning theory** (*Intention, Plans, and Practical Reason*, 1987) extends the belief-desire model with a more sophisticated account of the temporal structure of human action. Intentions are not mere desire-belief combinations but planning states — settled commitments to act that structure future reasoning and coordinate behavior over time. Intentions are stable (they persist through deliberation and resist revision except for good reasons), coordinative (they constrain future planning, preventing the agent from simultaneously planning incompatible actions), and carry genuine commitment (not merely behavioral disposition). This planning structure explains how we form plans in advance, act on them over time, and adjust them in light of new information — something that cannot be captured by belief-desire pairs that represent only the agent's current state.

Many significant actions are performed by groups. Committees deliberate, armies attack, corporations act. These collective actions seem to require something beyond the intentions of individual members. Bratman holds that collective action is constituted by a set of interlocking individual intentions meeting conditions of meshing subplans and common knowledge — no group mind is required. Searle, by contrast, holds that collective intentionality is a primitive form of intentionality — the "we-intention" — irreducible to individual I-intentions. When soldiers attack together, each soldier has a we-intention that is genuinely collective, not merely a similar individual intention. This dispute reflects a more general question about whether social facts are constituted by individual facts or are irreducibly social.

## Agency, Free Will, and Neuroscience

The explanation of intentional agency connects directly to debates about free will. If intentional actions are caused by prior mental states, and those states are themselves caused by prior neural and environmental states, is the agent genuinely free? Compatibilists argue that causal determination is compatible with freedom if the agent's beliefs and desires are among the relevant causes and the agent is responsive to reasons — she would act differently if the reasons were different. Incompatibilists argue that causal determination precludes genuine freedom regardless of what the relevant causes are.

Libet's experiments (1983) introduced a neuroscientific challenge: the readiness potential — the neural preparation for voluntary movement — precedes conscious awareness of the intention to act by approximately 350 milliseconds. This has been taken to show that conscious intentions are causally downstream from the neural processes that initiate action, making conscious will an epiphenomenal narrative rather than a genuine cause. The debate about implications is extensive: some argue that Libet shows free will is an illusion; others argue that the experimental paradigm is too impoverished to test the relevant notion of intentional agency; others argue that the conscious awareness tapped by the experiment is not the same as the planning and deliberation that constitute genuine agency.
