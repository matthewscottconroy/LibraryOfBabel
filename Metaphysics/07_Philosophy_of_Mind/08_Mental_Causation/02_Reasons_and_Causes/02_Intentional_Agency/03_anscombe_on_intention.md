# Anscombe on Intention

Anscombe's *Intention* (1957) is widely regarded as the most important work on the philosophy of action in the twentieth century. Its approach is unlike causal theories in a fundamental respect: it does not analyze intention in terms of prior mental states that cause behavior, but in terms of the logical structure of intentional description and a distinctive kind of knowledge — practical knowledge — that agents have of their own actions.

Anscombe's central analytical tool is the "Why?" question. An action is intentional under a description D if it makes sense to ask "Why are you doing D?" — where "Why?" is understood as a request for a reason (not merely a cause), and where the agent can give a reason that shows the action as rational or purposive. This criterion is description-relative: the same bodily event may be intentional under one description and not under another. Moving one's finger across a trigger is intentional under "shooting the gun" but not under "causing the bullet to travel at 900 m/s" — the agent may not know the bullet's velocity, and if asked "Why are you causing the bullet to travel at 900 m/s?", the question would be inapplicable in the relevant sense. Intentional actions can be described at multiple levels — "moving my finger," "pulling the trigger," "firing the gun," "shooting the intruder," "defending my home" — all applicable to the same physical event, forming a nested series of intentional descriptions organized toward a goal.

## Practical Knowledge

The most philosophically original contribution in *Intention* is the concept of **practical knowledge** — the knowledge an agent has of what she is doing without observation. When I raise my arm intentionally, I know that my arm is rising not by seeing it (though I might) but by being the agent of the raising. This first-person practical knowledge is constitutive of intentional action, not derived from it.

This contrasts with **speculative knowledge** — knowledge derived from observation or testimony, which fits the facts: it is correct when it corresponds to how things are. Practical knowledge, by contrast, creates or guides the facts: the agent's practical knowledge of what she is doing is not answerable to the movement but is partly constitutive of the movement's being the action it is. Anscombe's example: the man who plans to build a house and then builds it according to his plan has practical knowledge of the building; the anesthesiologist who observes the operation has speculative knowledge of what is happening. The difference is in the direction of fit: practical knowledge has world-to-mind fit (the world is made to conform to the agent's plan); speculative knowledge has mind-to-world fit (the mind conforms to the world).

Anscombe also develops a teleological analysis of desire. A desire is not a mere internal agitation but an orientation toward an object under a description — toward eating, say, as (in some respect) good or valuable. What we desire are things presented as desirable under some description. This is importantly different from treating desires as mere pushes or drives: desire has intentional content, and its role in action is to present an object as worth pursuing. The rational structure of action is thus built into the analysis of desire from the beginning.

## Against Causal Theories

Anscombe is skeptical that the explanation of an action by citing reasons is a causal explanation. The question "Why did you do X?" has a distinctive structure that cannot be reduced to "What caused your X-ing?" Reason-giving shows the action as rational — as made sense of by the agent's beliefs and desires — not as the effect of a prior psychological event. The logical form of intentional explanation is different from the logical form of causal explanation, even if the mental states cited in intentional explanations are causally related to the actions they explain.

This is not a denial that mental states cause actions. It is a claim about the form and purpose of intentional explanation: it places actions in the space of reasons and goals, not in the causal order of antecedent events producing effects. Davidson's theory of reasons as causes was partly a response to Anscombe, insisting that what makes a reason the reason for which the agent acted is precisely its being the cause — that without causal connection, the reason is merely a post-hoc rationalization. Anscombe's work remains the reference point for this debate, and her insistence on the description-relativity of intentionality, the concept of practical knowledge, and the teleological structure of desire continue to shape contemporary philosophy of action.
