# Important Terms and Ideas: Reasons and Causes

**The Causal Theory of Action**
Davidson's thesis: an action is intentional only if it is caused (in the right way) by the agent's beliefs and desires that rationalize it. A reason explains an action by showing how the agent's mental states made it rational; this explanation is a causal explanation.

**Rationalization**
Davidson's term: a reason rationalizes an action if the agent's belief and desire together make it intelligible why the action was performed—they show it was reasonable from the agent's perspective. Rationalization is both normative (showing intelligibility) and causal (the belief-desire pair causes the action).

**Deviant Causal Chains**
Cases where an action is caused by the right beliefs and desires but in the wrong way: e.g., nervousness about intending to steal causes a shoplifter's hands to shake, accidentally knocking the goods into their bag. The causal theory must exclude such deviant chains to capture genuinely intentional action.

**The Wittgensteinian View**
The view (associated with Anscombe, von Wright, and others influenced by Wittgenstein) that reasons are logically rather than causally related to actions—explaining an action by citing a reason is showing what it means, not identifying its cause. Davidson famously argued against this.

**Humean Theory of Motivation**
The view that motivation requires a desire (or conative state) as well as a belief: beliefs alone cannot motivate action; only desires can. The Humean theory implies that the fundamental springs of action are non-rational—desires that are not themselves subject to rational assessment.

**Anti-Humeanism**
The view that beliefs alone (including normative beliefs) can motivate action, without requiring a separate desire. If I believe I should do X, this belief itself motivates me, without needing an independent desire to do my duty. Dancy, McDowell, and others defend versions of this view.
