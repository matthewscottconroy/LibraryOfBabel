# Strong Emergence and Downward Causation

Weak emergence is relatively uncontroversial. A property is weakly emergent from lower-level properties if it is an unexpected consequence of their dynamics but is in principle derivable from complete knowledge of those lower-level properties. Conway's Game of Life exhibits weak emergence: complex global patterns arise from simple local rules, and the patterns are derivable in principle from those rules, even if they are practically unpredictable. **Strong emergence** is a different and far more contested claim: a property is strongly emergent if it is not derivable in principle from complete knowledge of the lower-level properties — if it is genuinely novel, not predictable even by an unlimited computational mind.

In the philosophy of mind, the strong emergence claim is that phenomenal consciousness is strongly emergent: no complete account of the physical basis of the brain would allow a theorist to derive the fact that there is something it is like to have certain brain states. Chalmers uses this to argue for property dualism: phenomenal properties are genuinely novel, irreducible to physical properties, appearing at a level of organization where nothing in the lower-level description anticipates them.

**Downward causation** follows naturally if mental properties are strongly emergent. If mental properties are genuinely novel causal powers, they may exercise causal influence back on the physical level — consciousness (if strongly emergent from neural activity) might cause specific patterns of neural activity in ways not predictable from neural dynamics alone. Downward causation is formally characterized as follows: a property P at level L exercises downward causation on level L-1 if there are causal relations between instances of P and lower-level properties that cannot be fully captured in terms of lower-level causal relations alone.

## The Exclusion Problem Applied

Strong emergence with genuine downward causation faces Kim's exclusion argument directly. If physical events have sufficient physical causes — if causal closure holds — then higher-level emergent properties are causally excluded. The lower-level story is complete, and there is no causal work for the higher-level properties to do. Either the emergent property M is identical to its neural basis (reduction) or M is causally inert (epiphenomenalism). Strong emergence with genuine downward causation appears to be a third option, but it is not coherent within a physicalist framework that accepts causal closure.

O'Connor defends a version of strong emergence attempting to accommodate this objection. Complex systems can have intrinsic dispositional properties not possessed by their parts, and these properties can causally influence the system's behavior without violating causal closure — because they are properties of the physical system, not separate from it. On O'Connor's view, emergent properties are genuinely novel but not non-physical: they are physical properties of complex systems not deducible from the properties of the system's parts. Kim is skeptical: even on O'Connor's account, if the physical basis of the emergent property is causally sufficient for the physical effects, the emergent property is still causally excluded. The question is whether emergent properties can be causally efficacious in a way not reducible to the causal efficacy of their physical basis — and Kim argues they cannot, within a physicalist framework.

Whether strong emergence is coherent has high stakes for the philosophy of mind. If it is coherent and downward causation is real, there is a principled way to defend the irreducibility and causal relevance of mental properties without accepting substance dualism. If strong emergence is incoherent — as Kim argues — then the only options are reduction or epiphenomenalism. Whether weak emergence, supplemented by the interventionist account of causation, is sufficient to vindicate mental causation without the metaphysical extravagance of strong emergence is the central question that remains open.
