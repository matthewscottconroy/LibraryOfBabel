# Is Downward Causation Coherent?

The coherence challenge for downward causation is straightforward to state and difficult to answer. If lower-level physical processes are causally sufficient to produce lower-level physical effects, how can higher-level properties add further causal influence without introducing overdetermination? The constituents are already interacting causally; any additional causation from above seems either redundant or in conflict with causal closure.

Kim's exclusion argument applies directly:

- P1: Every neural event that has a cause has a sufficient neural (and ultimately physical) cause.
- P2: If mental events exercise downward causation on neural events, mental events are causes of neural events.
- P3: Neural events already have sufficient neural causes.
- C: Mental events would overdetermine neural events — or are causally excluded.

If the physical level is causally closed, no level above it can exert downward causal influence without introducing overdetermination. The exclusion problem is not a special problem for mental causation; it is the general problem for any claim of downward causation in a physically closed world.

A related problem is **causal drainage**: if higher-level properties are causally excluded by the lower level, then higher-level explanations are not genuine causal explanations but descriptions of patterns in lower-level causation. The higher level is causally "drained" of its apparent causal power. Applied to mind: if mental properties are causally excluded by their neural bases, psychology does not identify distinct causal factors — it describes patterns in neural causation. Whether this is a reductio of the exclusion view or an acceptable consequence depends on one's views about the autonomy of psychology.

## The Interventionist Response and the Metaphysics of Levels

The interventionist account (Woodward) responds: higher-level properties are causally relevant if intervening on them would change the outcome. If manipulating a mental state — through argumentation, persuasion, or pharmacology affecting mental states — changes subsequent behavior in ways not predictable from the lower-level description alone, then the mental property is genuinely causally relevant. The objection that any intervention on a mental property is also an intervention on a physical property (given supervenience) does not collapse the levels, Woodward argues, because the mental-level intervention and the physical-level intervention track different causal structures. Causal relevance is level-relative and plural.

The deeper challenge is metaphysical. If higher-level entities just are arrangements of lower-level entities — nothing over and above them — then higher-level properties are not separate from lower-level properties: they are lower-level properties described at a higher grain. In that case, there is no "downward" causation because there is no separate higher level to cause from. What appears to be downward causation is same-level causation of physical properties, redescribed for explanatory convenience.

Defenders of downward causation respond by appealing to multiple realizability. If mental properties are realizable by many different physical configurations, they cannot be identical to any single physical property. A multiply realizable mental property picks out a genuine pattern across multiple realizations — a pattern that has its own causal significance. The common pattern can be causally efficacious in ways that the individual physical realizations are not, precisely because it abstracts from the particular physical realization.

## Strong Emergence and Its Costs

The strongest version of downward causation requires strong emergence: genuinely novel causal powers at higher levels, not predictable from lower-level dynamics. The coherence challenge is sharpest here: if physical causal closure holds, strong emergence with downward causation entails overdetermination or exclusion. If physical causal closure is relaxed, strong emergence might be coherent but at the cost of departing from naturalism.

Most philosophers conclude that strong emergence with robust downward causation requires either revising causal closure or accepting property dualism. Within a framework that accepts closure, downward causation can at most be understood in the weak, interventionist sense — as genuine causal relevance at multiple levels, not as a distinct layer of causation over and above the physical. Whether weak, interventionist downward causation is sufficient to vindicate the autonomy of psychology and the genuine causal efficacy of mental properties is the central unresolved question.
