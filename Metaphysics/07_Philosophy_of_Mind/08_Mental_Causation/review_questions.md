# Review Questions — Mental Causation

1. Kim's exclusion argument deploys four principles — causal efficacy of the mental, causal closure of the physical, non-reduction, and no overdetermination — and shows they are collectively inconsistent. Which principle is most vulnerable to rejection, and what is the cost of rejecting each? Is there any position that rejects one principle without incurring an unacceptable cost?

2. The constitution response to the exclusion problem (Yablo, Bennett) argues that mental and physical causation do not compete because they are not distinct contributions: the same causal work is described at two levels. Kim replies that if mental causation just *is* the physical realizer's causation, the mental property is causally redundant. Assess whether the constitution response genuinely dissolves the exclusion problem or merely relocates it.

3. Davidson argued in "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963) that reasons are causes — the belief-desire pair that rationalizes an action also causes it. This was controversial because neo-Wittgensteinian views held that reason-explanation and causal explanation are categorically different. What is Davidson's argument that they must be unified, and how does his later anomalous monism create a tension with this earlier view?

4. Deviant causal chains are a persistent problem for the reasons-as-causes account: a desire to summit a mountain might cause the climber such anxiety that they accidentally stumble to the top, rather than climbing intentionally. The causal connection holds but the action is not done *for* the reason. Why has specifying the "right kind" of causal connection proved so difficult, and what does this difficulty suggest about whether reasons are causes in the ordinary physical sense?

5. The concept of basic action identifies the point at which intentional control interfaces with the physical world. Libet's readiness potential experiments suggest that neural preparation for voluntary movement begins before conscious intention. Does this show that the "interface" is below the level of conscious intention, or does it undermine the very concept of a basic action? What would each conclusion mean for the picture of intentional agency?

6. Downward causation — the causal influence of higher-level organizational properties on lower-level constituents — is offered as a way to make mental causation real without violating causal closure. Critics argue that what appears to be downward causation is just same-level physical causation redescribed in higher-level terms. Is there a way to show that higher-level causal generalizations are not merely convenient shorthand, and what would that demonstration require?

7. Mental causation connects directly to the metaphysics of laws of nature. If mental properties are genuinely distinct from physical properties and exercise their own causal influence, then mental-level causal generalizations appear to be laws that are not derivable from fundamental physics. Does this constitute a reason to prefer reductive physicalism (which preserves the unity of natural law) over non-reductive physicalism or property dualism?

8. If Kim is right that non-reductive physicalism leads either to reduction or to epiphenomenalism with respect to phenomenal properties specifically, what are the implications for moral responsibility? If my conscious deliberation has no causal impact on my behavior, can I be held responsible for what I do in the normal way? And does the same problem arise for agent-causal accounts of free will?
