# Philosophy of Perception

Open your eyes and look at the world. You seem to see the world directly — the tree, the building, the sky — as if perception were a window through which reality presents itself immediately to the mind. Yet there are good reasons to think that perception is a far more complex achievement than this naive picture suggests. What you see is mediated by the eye, the retina, the optic nerve, and an elaborate processing hierarchy in the visual cortex. The image on the retina is two-dimensional; depth is inferred. Colors as experienced are not properties of surfaces as physics describes them; they are responses of the visual system to patterns of electromagnetic radiation. Illusions, hallucinations, and perceptual constancies all suggest that perception is construction rather than passive reception.

The philosophy of perception asks: what exactly is the nature of perceptual experience, and what is its relationship to the external world? The naive realist holds that in ordinary perception we are directly aware of the external world — not of representations of it, not of sense data, but of the mind-independent things themselves. This preserves the phenomenology of perception but must explain how perception can go wrong. The representative realist holds that we are directly aware of mental representations (sense data, percepts), which in turn represent external objects; what we immediately perceive is the representation, not the thing represented. This explains misperception but generates the veil-of-perception problem: how do we know that our representations correspond to anything external?

Disjunctivism holds that veridical perception and hallucination are fundamentally different experiences — not just different in what they represent, but different in their intrinsic nature. In veridical perception, the world itself is present to the mind; in hallucination, it is not. The two experiences may be causally and phenomenally indistinguishable to the subject, but they are nonetheless fundamentally distinct.

Enactivism challenges the entire framework, proposing that perception is not representation at all but a form of skillful engagement with the environment: to perceive is to know how to interact with what surrounds you, not to form an inner model of it. This chapter develops these competing theories and evaluates them against the phenomena of perception, illusion, and cross-modal experience.
