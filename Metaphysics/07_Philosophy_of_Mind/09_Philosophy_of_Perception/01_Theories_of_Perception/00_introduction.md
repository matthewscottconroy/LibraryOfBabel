# Theories of Perception

When you look at an apple and see that it is red, what exactly is happening? This question has an obvious neurological answer — light enters the eye, the visual system processes it, and a perceptual representation is formed — and a philosophical answer that is far less obvious. The philosophical question is not about the mechanism of vision but about the nature of the cognitive achievement: when you perceive something, what is the relationship between you, your visual experience, and the external world?

Naïve realism (or "direct realism") holds that perception is a direct, immediate relation between the perceiver and the external object. When you see the red apple, you are directly aware of the apple itself — the apple and its redness are immediate objects of your visual experience. There is no "veil of perception," no intermediate representation, no sense-data between you and the world. The perceived object is the immediate object of your awareness.

The representative theory of perception (or indirect realism) holds that what we are immediately aware of in perception is an internal mental representation — a sense-datum, a percept, an idea — not the external object itself. The external object causes the representation, and we infer or postulate the external object from the representation. Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities was part of this tradition: secondary qualities (color, taste, smell) are not in the objects but are powers to produce experiences in minds; primary qualities (shape, size, motion) are genuinely in the objects.

Idealism — associated with Berkeley — holds that there are no material objects independent of minds; objects just are bundles of experiences. To be is to be perceived (esse est percipi). The external world is constituted by mental contents, not merely represented by them.

This section examines these theories of perception — naïve realism, representative realism, and idealism — with attention to the arguments that motivate each and the difficulties each must face.
