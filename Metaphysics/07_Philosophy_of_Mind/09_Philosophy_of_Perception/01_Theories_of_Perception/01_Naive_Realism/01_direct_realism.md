# Direct Realism

When I look at the coffee cup on the table, I seem to see the cup itself — not an image of it, not an impression of it, not a mental surrogate, but the actual cup. The phenomenology of perception is one of direct encounter with the world: the world presents itself. **Direct realism** takes this phenomenology at face value. In ordinary, veridical perception we are directly and immediately aware of the external world — of mind-independent physical objects and their properties — without any intermediary layer of mental objects standing between perceiver and world.

This contrasts sharply with **indirect realism**, associated with Descartes and Locke: what we directly perceive are ideas or representations caused by the external world. The external world is inferred from the ideas; the ideas form a veil separating us from reality. On indirect realism, we never actually perceive physical objects — we perceive mental items that (we hope) correspond to them. Direct realism denies this entirely. Perception is not a two-stage process of having an experience and then inferring an external cause; it is a direct, unmediated encounter with the world.

Direct realism comes in two forms. **Naive (or common-sense) realism** holds that the objects of perception are ordinary mind-independent objects and that the phenomenal character of perceptual experience is constituted by those objects and their properties. There is no phenomenal residue beyond what the perceived world provides. The redness I see is the redness of the apple; the shape I see is the shape of the table. **Epistemological direct realism** holds that we have non-inferential, direct epistemic access to the external world in perception — no argument from appearances to reality is needed — even if the phenomenology of perception involves representational content. The emphasis is on epistemic directness rather than on the metaphysical constitution of experience.

Contemporary naive realists (Campbell, Martin, Snowdon, Brewer) defend a relational conception of perception: external objects are constituents of perceptual experiences, not merely causes of representations. The experience just is a relation between perceiver and perceived object. If the object were absent — as in hallucination — the subject would not have the same type of experience; she would have a different, derivative mental state. Aristotle anticipated this: in perception, the sense organs receive the sensible form of the object — its color, shape, sound — without receiving its matter. The form is directly received; there is no intermediary mental object. Reid revived this against Hume's skepticism: perception involves immediate awareness of external objects, not inference from sensory ideas.

## Challenges

The challenges to direct realism are well known. The **argument from illusion**: a straight stick looks bent in water; white walls look pink in red light. If we are directly perceiving the object, why does it appear as it is not? **The argument from hallucination**: hallucinations can be phenomenally indistinguishable from veridical perceptions but lack the relevant object. If the same phenomenal character is present in both cases, phenomenal character cannot be constituted by the perceived object. **The causal argument**: perception involves a complex causal chain from object to brain; anything that generates the same neural effects produces the same experience, suggesting that what we experience is the result of the causal process, not the object that initiated it.

Direct realists have developed sophisticated responses. Most prominently, **disjunctivism** (McDowell, Martin, Snowdon) denies the common-kind assumption: veridical perception and hallucination are fundamentally different kinds of mental state, not two instances of a common type. In veridical perception, the subject is genuinely acquainted with external objects; in hallucination, the subject has a different experience that merely mimics perception from the inside. The phenomenal character is not the same in both cases; they only seem indistinguishable to the subject because she cannot tell from the inside which state she is in. Whether disjunctivism is successful — or whether it resolves the challenge by stipulation — remains contested.
