# Naive Realism

The "naive" in naive realism does not mean unsophisticated. It means the view captures the natural, unreflective phenomenology of perceptual experience. Perception presents itself as contact with the world: we seem to see the apple, not a picture of the apple. In hearing a violin, we hear the violin — not an auditory representation of vibrations. In smelling coffee, we smell the coffee — not an olfactory image. **Naive realism** honors this phenomenology by holding that the objects and properties we seem to perceive directly are indeed the objects and properties we do perceive. The phenomenology of directness reflects a metaphysical fact about the structure of perceptual experience: it is not mediated by mental intermediaries.

Contemporary naive realism — associated with Campbell, Brewer, Johnston, Martin, and Snowdon — holds that the objects of perceptual experience are genuinely mind-independent objects in the world, and that these objects are **constituents** of the experience itself, not merely causes of an experience that represents them. This is the **relational view**: perception is a relation between perceiver and perceived object, not a state in which the perceiver has an inner representation caused by the object. The experience just is the perceiver's relation to the world.

The structure is: Perceptual experience = Perceiver PERCEIVES Object (a genuine two-term relation). The object is not the cause of an inner state; it is a constituent of the experiential state itself. The phenomenal character of the experience is partly constituted by the properties of the perceived object. This makes the experience dependent on the existence of the perceived object: if the object were absent — if I were hallucinating — I would not have the same perceptual experience but a different, derivative mental state.

One of the key supports for this view is the **transparency of experience** — when I try to attend to my visual experience as such, I seem to "see through" it to the objects in the world. Moore first noted this: in trying to introspect the experience of seeing blue, one's attention passes through the experience to the blue object. This suggests that the experience has no intrinsic phenomenal character beyond the properties of what it represents. Naive realism explains this naturally: the experience is constituted by the object, so attending to the experience just is attending to the object.

## Disjunctivism and the Challenge of Illusion

The classical challenges to naive realism are illusion and hallucination. When a straight stick looks bent in water, or when I hallucinate a pink elephant, what am I perceiving? There is no bent stick and no pink elephant to be directly aware of. If phenomenal character can occur without the relevant object (as in hallucination), it seems phenomenal character cannot be constituted by the object.

**Disjunctivism** (McDowell, Martin, Snowdon) is the contemporary naive realist's main response. Veridical perception and hallucination are fundamentally different kinds of mental state, not two instances of a common type. In veridical perception, the subject is genuinely acquainted with external objects; in hallucination, the subject has a different experience that merely mimics perception from the inside. The phenomenal character is not the same in both cases; they only appear similar to the subject because she cannot tell from the inside which state she is in. The "common factor" assumption — that veridical perception and hallucination share a common phenomenal core — is precisely what disjunctivism denies.

The **selective attention response** addresses illusion more directly: when the straight stick looks bent in water, the subject does not directly perceive a bent stick but perceives the straight stick as appearing bent given the optical properties of water. The appearance of bentness is a relational property of the perception — a way of perceiving the straight stick under these conditions — not a mental object standing between subject and world. Whether these responses are successful, or whether they merely reframe the challenge without resolving it, is the central ongoing debate in the philosophy of perception.
