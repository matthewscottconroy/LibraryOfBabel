# Naive Realism Today

Contemporary naive realism — associated with Martin, Campbell, Brewer, and Johnston — turns on a sharp contrast between **representation** and **presentation**. Representationalist and sense-datum theories hold that what gives experience its phenomenal character is either the representational content the experience carries or the sense-data it involves — something internal, something on the perceiver's side of the perceptual relation. Naive realism holds that what gives veridical experience its phenomenal character is the direct presentation of the world itself: the phenomenal properties of the experience just are (partly) properties of the perceived object, instantiated in the experience through the perceptual relation.

Martin develops the relational account in detail. Perceptual experiences are **relations** between perceivers and perceived objects, not merely states of the perceiver with representational content. The relata of this relation are essential to the experience's identity: a visual experience of a red tomato could not be numerically the same experience if the tomato were absent. The position is **object-dependent**: the particular experience had in seeing the tomato depends essentially on the tomato. This contrasts with representational views, on which the same internal state (with the same representational content) could occur in hallucination, and the object's presence or absence makes no difference to the type of experience.

The view has several epistemological benefits. Perception does not infer the external world but encounters it. If external objects are constituents of perceptual experiences, then perceptual knowledge is not knowledge derived from internal evidence about an external reality but knowledge acquired by direct acquaintance with reality. There is no further question about how the experience "hooks onto" the world — the hooking-on is built in. And there is a natural explanation of the first-person/third-person asymmetry: the first-person perspective is that of a subject genuinely acquainted with the world; the third-person description is the causal description of the process producing that acquaintance. The asymmetry is real, not an illusion.

## Disjunctivism and the Hallucination Challenge

The main challenge for contemporary naive realism is the **argument from hallucination**: hallucinations can be phenomenally indistinguishable from veridical perception, but they lack the relevant object. If the same phenomenal character is present in both cases, and in hallucination there is no object to constitute the experience, then phenomenal character cannot be constituted by the object. This is a direct threat to the relational view.

Martin and Snowdon respond with **disjunctivism**: veridical perception and hallucination are not instances of a common phenomenal type. They are disjunctive states — either the subject perceives the world (veridical case) or the subject has a mental state that merely mimics perception (hallucination case). The "indistinguishability" of hallucination from veridical perception is an epistemic fact about what the subject can tell from the inside, not a metaphysical fact about the states sharing a phenomenal type. The disjunctivist slogan: being appeared to F-ly does not pick out a single inner state — it picks out a disjunction of possible situations, either perceiving an F-object or having a subjective state that resembles perceiving one. The two disjuncts are metaphysically distinct even if the subject cannot distinguish them.

The pressing objection: if the two disjuncts are metaphysically distinct, why are they subjectively indistinguishable? What explains the indistinguishability if not a common phenomenal element? Martin's response: indistinguishability does not require identity or even similarity in phenomenal character. It only requires that the subject lacks the discriminatory capacity to tell the cases apart from the inside. This is a cognitive limitation, not a phenomenal equivalence. Whether this is satisfying — or whether it explains the indistinguishability too easily — is the central debate about disjunctivism.

Campbell defends naive realism from a different angle: the most fundamental function of consciousness is to put the subject in contact with objects in their environment, making those objects available for reasoning and action. Colors are mind-independent properties of surfaces; seeing red is acquaintance with that surface property. This grounds color experience in the world rather than in the mind, and supports the relational view's claim that the phenomenal character of seeing red is constituted by the redness of the object, not by an internal quale or representation.
