# Problems for Naive Realism

Naive realism faces three classical arguments that have driven philosophers toward indirect or representational theories of perception. Each argument exploits some version of the difference between how things appear and how they are, or between veridical perception and perception-like experiences that lack an object. Together they form the backdrop against which contemporary naive realist responses must be evaluated.

The **argument from illusion** begins with familiar cases. A white wall looks pink in red light; a straight stick looks bent in water; a circular coin looks elliptical from an angle; railway tracks appear to converge in the distance. The object has fixed mind-independent properties — the wall is white, the stick is straight — but it appears to have different properties. The classical argument (Price, Broad, Ayer): in illusion, what we perceive (the apparent pinkness, the apparent bentness) does not exist in the mind-independent object. If what we perceive in illusion does not exist in the mind-independent object, we must be perceiving a mental object — a sense datum. Since illusory and veridical experiences are qualitatively similar or indistinguishable, we must be perceiving sense data in both. Therefore we never directly perceive mind-independent objects.

The naive realist response: the argument moves too quickly from "appears bent" to "perceives a bent thing." Appearing bent under certain conditions is a relational property of the stick — a fact about how the stick appears from certain angles and in certain media. This is a mode of presenting the (unbent) stick, not a fact about a mental object with a bent property. The apparent properties are properties of how the stick is presented, not properties of a mental intermediary.

The **argument from hallucination** is more threatening:

- P1: Hallucination H is phenomenally indistinguishable from veridical perception VP.
- P2: In H, no mind-independent object is perceived.
- P3: If H and VP are phenomenally indistinguishable, they share a phenomenal type.
- C: The phenomenal type shared by H and VP is not constituted by a mind-independent object.

This directly threatens naive realism's claim that phenomenal character is constituted by the perceived object. The **disjunctivist response** (Martin, Snowdon, McDowell) rejects P3. Veridical perception and hallucination do not share a phenomenal type; they are disjunctive cases. Phenomenal indistinguishability — the subject cannot tell from the inside which case she is in — does not imply phenomenal identity — the two cases have the same phenomenal type. The disjunctivist accepts the epistemic symmetry (both cases are indistinguishable from the inside) while denying the metaphysical symmetry (both cases have the same phenomenal character). The objection: if the cases are metaphysically distinct, what explains their subjective indistinguishability? Martin's answer: indistinguishability reflects a cognitive limitation, not a phenomenal equivalence.

## The Causal Argument and the Time Lag Problem

The **causal argument** holds that perception involves a complex causal chain from the object to the brain, and anything that generates the same neural effects produces the same experience. My experience is determined by my neural state, not by the object — so the object cannot be a constituent of my experience.

The naive realist response: the causal argument shows that the object is causally necessary for the experience in the normal case; it does not show that the object is not a constituent of the experience. In the normal veridical case, the object is both the cause and a constituent of the experience. The possibility that the same neural state could be produced by something other than the object does not change the metaphysical fact about the normal case — it shows what would happen in the abnormal case, which the disjunctivist treats as a different kind of experience (hallucination, not perception).

The **time lag argument** presses a related point: we see stars as they were thousands of years ago. We perceive light that left the star long before we perceive it; some stars whose light we see no longer exist. If naive realism holds that we perceive the star itself, we are directly perceiving an entity that may no longer exist — a strange claim about direct access. The naive realist response distinguishes perceiving the star from perceiving the star as it currently is. We perceive historical stages of stars — the star as it was when the light left it. This shows that the perceptual relation has an inherently temporal structure, not that direct perception is impossible.

These responses are philosophically sophisticated and have changed how the debate is conducted. Whether they are ultimately successful — or whether they explain away the challenges too quickly — is what makes this one of the most productive ongoing debates in the philosophy of perception.
