# Sense Data Theory

The argument from illusion put pressure on common-sense realism that philosophers felt needed to be answered seriously. When I see a white wall in red light, I directly experience something pink. No physical object is pink. So what I directly experience must be something mind-dependent — a mental particular with the properties it appears to have. This argument generalizes: if in the illusory case what is directly experienced is a mental particular, then given the phenomenal similarity between veridical perception and illusion, in all perception what is directly experienced must be a mental particular. The immediate objects of perceptual awareness are not mind-independent physical objects but **sense data** — private, mind-dependent entities that are immediately given to consciousness and that mediate our awareness of the external world.

This was the dominant view in early analytic philosophy. Russell (*The Problems of Philosophy*, 1912) argued that the immediate objects of perception must be sense data rather than physical objects: the same penny looks different shapes from different angles, while the physical object remains constant. Something is immediately given that varies with perspective; this cannot be the physical object; therefore it must be a mind-dependent sense datum. Moore, Broad, and Price developed and refined the theory. The motivation was partly epistemological — to provide a foundation for knowledge in something directly given — and partly phenomenological — to explain the qualitative character of perceptual experience.

Sense data have several alleged features. **Epistemic certainty**: I cannot be wrong about what my sense data are like. I can be wrong about what causes them, but not that something pink is directly given. This incorrigibility was supposed to make sense data epistemically privileged. **Privacy**: sense data are accessible only to the perceiver — private mental particulars analogous to Cartesian ideas. **Determinacy**: a sense datum is determinately some specific shade, some specific shape. **Direct givenness**: sense data are not inferred or constructed but immediately given to consciousness.

The relation between sense data and physical objects is then inferential. Perceptual knowledge is reconstructed from the certainty of sense data plus inferences to the physical causes of those data.

## Varieties and Decline

The theory admits varieties. **Phenomenalism** (Berkeley, Mill, Ayer) holds that physical objects are just permanent possibilities of sense data — to say there is a table in the room is to say that if one were to have certain sensory experiences, one would have certain further ones. **Representative realism** (Locke, Descartes) holds that physical objects exist mind-independently and cause sense data, with some properties of sense data (primary qualities: shape, size, motion) resembling physical properties and others (secondary qualities: color, smell, taste) not doing so. **Neutral monism** (Russell in some periods) holds that both mental and physical objects are constructions from the same neutral elements — sense data or "sensibilia" that are neither intrinsically mental nor intrinsically physical.

Sense data theory declined in the second half of the twentieth century. Sellars's critique of the Myth of the Given (*Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind*, 1956) argued that to know something requires having it under a concept, and having a concept requires being in the space of reasons. There is no pre-conceptual epistemic foundation; sense data cannot play the foundationalist role assigned to them. Wittgenstein's private language arguments undermined the idea that sense data language could have determinate meaning. And representationalist theories of perception offered an alternative account of phenomenal character without committing to private mental particulars. Contemporary philosophy of perception has largely moved beyond sense data theory, though the problems it was designed to solve — the phenomenal character of experience, the epistemological role of perception, the challenges of illusion and hallucination — remain central.
