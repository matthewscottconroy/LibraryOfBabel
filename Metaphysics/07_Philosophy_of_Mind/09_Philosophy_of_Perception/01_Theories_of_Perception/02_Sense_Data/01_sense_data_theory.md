# Sense Data Theory

When I see a white wall as pink in red light, I directly experience something pink. There is no pink physical object. So what I directly experience must be something mind-dependent — a private, mental particular that has the properties it appears to have. **Sense data theory** holds that the immediate objects of perceptual awareness are such entities: not mind-independent physical objects but sense data, standing between the perceiver and the external world.

The theory was most influentially defended by Russell, Moore, and Price in the early twentieth century. Sense data are thought to have several distinctive features. **Epistemic certainty**: I cannot be mistaken about what my sense data are like — I can be wrong about what causes them, but not that something pink is directly given. **Privacy**: my sense data are accessible only to me; they are private mental particulars. **Determinacy**: a sense datum has perfectly determinate properties, without the observer-relative variability of physical object properties. **Direct givenness**: sense data are not inferred or constructed but immediately given to consciousness.

The relation between sense data and physical objects is inferential. From the regularities in my sense data — their patterns, correlations, predictability — I infer the properties of physical objects that cause them. This reconstruction of the external world from sense data is the foundationalist epistemological program in its classical form. If sense data are epistemically certain, they can serve as the foundation from which knowledge of physical objects is built. Perceptual knowledge is secure because its foundation — direct acquaintance with sense data — is incorrigible.

The theory admits varieties. **Phenomenalism** (Berkeley, Mill, Ayer) holds that physical objects just are permanent possibilities of sense data — to say there is a table in the room is to say that if one were to have certain sensory experiences, one would have certain further ones. Physical objects are constructions from sense data, giving the view an idealist character. **Representative realism** (Locke, Descartes) holds that physical objects exist mind-independently and cause sense data; some properties of sense data (primary qualities: shape, size, motion) resemble physical properties while others (secondary qualities: color, smell, taste) do not. **Neutral monism** (Russell in some periods) holds that both mental and physical objects are constructions from the same neutral elements — sense data or "sensibilia" — that are neither intrinsically mental nor physical.

## The Epistemological Role and Its Critics

The foundationalist picture motivating sense data theory was attacked from several directions. Sellars (*Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind*, 1956) argued that the Myth of the Given — the idea that sense data are known pre-conceptually and non-inferentially — is incoherent. To know something requires having it under a concept; having a concept requires being in the space of reasons. There is no pre-conceptual epistemic foundation; sense data cannot play the foundationalist role. Chisholm and others responded that Sellars confuses the conditions for reporting experience with the conditions for having it: what is given in experience can be pre-conceptual in the relevant sense. The debate remains live.

Wittgenstein's private language arguments undermined the idea that sense data language could have determinate meaning independently of public practice. And the development of representationalist theories of perception offered an alternative account of phenomenal character without committing to private mental particulars. The problems sense data theory was designed to solve — the phenomenal character of experience, the epistemological role of perception, the challenges of illusion and hallucination — remain central to philosophy of perception, even as the specific theoretical apparatus of sense data has been largely abandoned.
