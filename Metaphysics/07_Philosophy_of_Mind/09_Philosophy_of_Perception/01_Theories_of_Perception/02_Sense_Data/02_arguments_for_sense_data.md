# Arguments for Sense Data

The main arguments for positing sense data share a common strategy: they identify a case where the phenomenal character of experience diverges from the properties of physical objects, and conclude that the phenomenal character is carried by an intermediate, mind-dependent entity. Whether these arguments succeed depends on whether the inference from "experience as of X" to "direct perception of an X-like mental entity" is legitimate.

The **argument from illusion** is the founding argument. A straight stick looks bent in water. The physical stick is straight, but something bent is presented to experience. The argument:

- P1: In the water illusion, the stick appears bent.
- P2: The physical stick is not bent.
- P3: I am directly aware of something bent.
- P4: The physical stick is not bent, so what I am directly aware of is not the physical stick.
- C: I am directly aware of a bent mental entity — a sense datum.

The generalization step runs: if in illusory cases we are directly aware of sense data, and if illusory experiences are qualitatively similar to veridical ones, then by parity, veridical experiences are also experiences of sense data.

Direct realists challenge P3. The argument assumes that "appearing bent" entails "being directly aware of something bent." But the stick's *appearing bent* in water may be a way of *perceiving the straight stick* — a way the perceptual system responds to refracted light. Appearing bent is not presenting a bent object; it is presenting the straight stick under conditions that produce a bent appearance. The argument moves illicitly from the adverbial ("the stick appears bent") to the accusative ("I perceive a bent thing").

The **argument from hallucination** is generally considered the most powerful:

- P1: In hallucination, the subject has an experience phenomenally indistinguishable from veridical perception.
- P2: In hallucination, no physical object is perceived.
- P3: If two experiences are phenomenally indistinguishable, they have the same immediate object of awareness.
- C: In both hallucination and veridical perception, the immediate object of awareness is a mental entity — a sense datum.

Disjunctivists (McDowell, Martin) reject P3. Phenomenal indistinguishability does not entail sharing an immediate object. Hallucination and veridical perception are radically different kinds of experience even if the subject cannot tell them apart from the inside. The indistinguishability is an epistemic fact about the subject's discriminatory limitations, not a metaphysical fact about the experiences sharing a type.

The **argument from perceptual variation** presses that the same physical object looks different from different distances, angles, and lighting conditions. A circular coin appears circular from straight on but elliptical from an angle. Physical objects have fixed properties, so these variations must be variations in how things appear — variations in sense data.

- P1: The coin's apparent shape varies with the angle of view.
- P2: The coin's physical shape does not vary.
- P3: What varies is what I am directly aware of.
- C: What I am directly aware of is not the physical coin but a sense datum whose apparent shape varies.

Critics challenge P3 again: the argument confuses the coin's shape with its apparent shape under various conditions. Appearing elliptical from an angle is a way of perceiving a circular object from that angle, not a perception of an elliptical mental object. The adverbial modification of the perception-report is not a commitment to a distinct elliptical mental entity.

All three arguments rest on the same structure, and their success or failure turns on the same question: whether appearances or looks constitute a class of mind-dependent objects distinct from physical objects, or whether they are modes of perceiving those objects under various conditions. This question connects directly to the broader debate between sense data theory, representationalism, and naive realism.
