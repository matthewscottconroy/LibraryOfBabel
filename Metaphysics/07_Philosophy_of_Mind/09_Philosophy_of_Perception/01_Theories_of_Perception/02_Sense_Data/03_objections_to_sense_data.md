# Objections to Sense Data

The objections to sense data theory are multiple, interconnected, and have been widely regarded as decisive. Together they challenge both the ontological posit and the epistemological role that sense data were designed to play.

The **regress of perception** is the most structurally damaging. If perceiving a physical object requires perceiving a sense datum that mediates awareness of it, then perceiving the sense datum should also require something — perhaps another sense datum mediating awareness of the first. The regress begins. Defenders reply that sense data are **directly given** in awareness without requiring a further act of perception — they are immediately present to consciousness, not "perceived" in the same way physical objects are. But this reply undermines the original argument for sense data. If sense data can be immediately given without mediating intermediaries, why cannot physical objects be immediately given without mediating sense data? Either all perception requires intermediaries — leading to regress — or some objects are directly given, in which case the argument for introducing sense data over-generalizes.

The **epistemic role problem** attacks the foundationalist motivation. Sense data are supposed to be known with certainty and to provide a secure basis for knowledge of the external world. But moving from sense data to external objects requires inference — and the inference introduces the very uncertainty sense data were supposed to avoid. My sense data may be the same whether I am perceiving a real table, a hologram, a dream, or a hallucination. To know which is the case, I must infer from sense data to their causes, an inference that is never certain. If the inference is uncertain, sense data cannot provide the epistemic security they were designed to deliver. And it is not even clear what would constitute a good inference from sense data to physical objects: the "best explanation" move is itself uncertain, and phenomenalism — reducing physical objects to sense data to avoid the inference — faces its own massive difficulties.

The **privacy objection** targets the mismatch between what sense data theory predicts and how perception is actually experienced. Sense data are private in the sense that only the subject can directly perceive them. But ordinary perception is experienced as public: we see the same table, hear the same music, smell the same coffee. When two people look at a red apple, they are looking at the same thing, not at numerically distinct private entities with similar properties. Sense data theory misrepresents the essentially public, world-directed character of perception by forcing it into the model of a private theater of inner appearances.

## Sellars and Wittgenstein

Sellars's critique of the **Myth of the Given** (*Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind*, 1956) is the most influential philosophical objection. The idea that sense data provide a pre-conceptual, non-inferential foundation for knowledge is, Sellars argues, incoherent. To justify a belief — to provide epistemic support — something must itself be in the space of reasons: it must be the kind of thing that can stand in logical relations of entailment and evidential support to other beliefs. But sense data, as pre-conceptual raw feel, are not in the space of reasons. They can cause beliefs but not justify them. Justification requires concepts; sense data are supposed to be pre-conceptual; therefore sense data cannot justify beliefs. If Sellars is right, the primary motivation for the theory collapses.

Wittgenstein's private language arguments apply indirectly. If sense data language — "I am now having a red, round sense datum" — were genuinely private in meaning, fixed by private ostension, there would be no stable semantics for the language. There would be no fact of the matter about whether a subsequent use of "red" refers to the same shade as the current sense datum. The semantics of sense data terms must ultimately be grounded in public practice, not private ostension — which undermines the foundationalist picture of sense data as the basic semantic atoms.

**Ontological extravagance** rounds out the objections. Representationalist theories offer to explain everything sense data explain — the phenomenal character of experience, the phenomenology of illusion and hallucination — without the ontological commitment to private mental particulars. The redness of my red experience, on representationalism, is the experience's representing things as red, not an inner red object. If representationalism is correct, sense data are posited entities whose explanatory work can be done without them — which is a strong reason to reject them. Whether representationalism can successfully account for everything that motivated sense data theory is the central ongoing question.
