# Representational Theory of Perception

Representationalism occupies a distinctive position in the logical space of theories of perception. Like naive realism, it holds that we perceive the external world rather than inner mental entities: the content of perception is about external objects, not sense data. Like indirect realism, it holds that perceptual experience is mediated by representational states that can misrepresent the world. Unlike sense data theory, it does not posit a new class of mental particulars but instead posits intentional states with content. Perceptual experience, on this view, just is a species of intentional state — directed toward the world in virtue of its representational content.

To perceive a red tomato is for one's perceptual system to be in a state with the representational content "there is a red round object here." The phenomenal character of perception is determined by this representational content. This proposal is strongly supported by cognitive science and computational theories of vision. Marr's three-level theory of visual processing analyzes vision as the construction of increasingly abstract representations of the visual scene — from retinal image to primitive sketch to 2.5D sketch to 3D model. At each stage, the visual system constructs a representation of some aspect of the scene. The output is a rich representation of the 3D structure of the world relative to the viewer. Perceptual experience is the subject's access to the representational output of this processing; phenomenal character reflects the content of these representations.

The representational theory explains the **intentionality of perception** — its aboutness — by analyzing perceptual experience as a species of intentional state. Like beliefs and desires, experiences are directed toward the world: directed toward objects and properties in the environment in virtue of their representational content. This connects perception to the broader theory of intentionality: the general theory of intentional content (causal, teleosemantic, inferentialist) applies to perceptual content as well as to the content of beliefs and desires. Perceptual content is just one form of intentional content.

A key advantage is the account of **perceptual error**. Perception can misrepresent the world: the stick looks bent but is straight; the wall looks pink but is white. These are cases of misrepresentation — the experience has content that does not match the actual state of the world. This parallels false belief: a belief can have content that does not match the facts. Representationalism treats perceptual error and cognitive error as the same kind of phenomenon — misrepresentation — explained by the same general theory.

## Varieties and the Main Challenge

**Strong representationalism** (Tye, Dretske) holds that phenomenal character is fully determined by representational content. Two experiences with the same representational content have the same phenomenal character, and vice versa. Phenomenal properties are identical to representational properties. **Weak representationalism** holds that phenomenal character supervenes on representational content but is not identical to it — there may be phenomenal residue beyond content.

The main challenge comes from cases where the same representational content seems compatible with different phenomenal characters. Inverted qualia: two subjects might represent red in the same way (correctly, with the same functional role) but have systematically different phenomenal experiences. If two subjects correctly represent "red" and yet their experiences have different qualitative characters, phenomenal character is not fully determined by representational content. Strong representationalists respond by questioning whether genuine qualia inversion is conceivable — whether it would require different functional organization and hence different representational content. Whether this response succeeds depends on the relationship between phenomenal character and functional role, which is itself one of the central unresolved questions in the philosophy of mind.
