# Intentionalist Accounts of Perception

Consider what happens when you try to attend to your visual experience itself — to its inner qualitative texture rather than to the things you are seeing. Most people find they cannot do it. Attention keeps passing through the experience to the red apple, the rough table surface, the blue sky. There seems to be no inner layer of mental stuff to find; the experience is, as Moore first put it, "diaphanous." This phenomenological datum is the starting point for intentionalism about perception, and it is more significant than it first appears.

Intentionalism holds that the phenomenal character of perceptual experience is constituted by — or at least wholly supervenes on — the intentional content of that experience. "Intentional" here carries no suggestion of deliberateness; it means simply that the experience is about something, that it has representational content directed at features of the world. The claim is that what it is like to have a visual experience of red is nothing over and above what the experience represents — the redness is in the content, not in some further inner mental property. Moore noticed the transparency datum without fully accepting this conclusion. The intentionalists — Dretske (*Naturalizing the Mind*, 1995), Tye (*Ten Problems of Consciousness*, 1995), Harman, Byrne — argue the datum compels it.

## Strong and Weak Intentionalism

Notice that there are two directions one might press the supervenience claim. **Downward supervenience**: if two experiences share their intentional content, they share their phenomenal character. **Upward supervenience**: if two experiences share their phenomenal character, they share their intentional content. **Strong intentionalism**, defended by Tye and Dretske, accepts both. This makes phenomenal character and intentional content necessarily co-extensive — they rise and fall together, though they may differ in sense. **Weak intentionalism** accepts only downward supervenience, or accepts upward supervenience only relative to additional factors: the mode of presentation, the particular phenomenal concepts the subject deploys, or the vehicle of the representational state. Phenomenal character is partly, but not wholly, determined by representational content.

The difference matters. Strong intentionalism promises to naturalize phenomenal character via the naturalization of intentional content — if we can explain what it is for a mental state to represent redness, we have thereby explained what it is like to see red. Weak intentionalism concedes that phenomenal residue remains after content has been fully specified, which reopens the hard problem it was supposed to close.

Dretske's version ties representation to natural information relationships. The perceptual system carries information about external properties — surface reflectance, for example — and the phenomenal character of the experience is constituted by these informational relationships. Crucially, what the subject experiences as phenomenally present is the surface property, not the experience itself. The experience is a transparent window onto the property represented, which is why introspective attention keeps passing through it to the world.

Tye's PANIC theory is a careful specification of what the relevant content is. PANIC content is Poised, Abstract, Nonconceptual, and Intentional. The "poised" component places the content at the right architectural location — the interface between early perceptual processing and higher cognition — so that it is available for reasoning and action in the way that characterizes conscious perception. "Abstract" means the content specifies types rather than particular objects, so the same content can be tokened in different encounters with different particulars. "Nonconceptual" is the most philosophically loaded component: the content is finer-grained than any concepts the subject possesses, which is why we can experience determinately specific shades we have no names for. The phenomenal character of experience is constituted by this fine-grained, nonconceptual, poised content — not by any further inner properties beyond it.

## The Inverted Qualia Challenge

We can put the central difficulty sharply. Imagine two subjects, A and B, who are functionally identical — same input-output behavior across all conditions — and who both correctly represent red things as red. Their representational content, by hypothesis, is the same. But their phenomenal characters are systematically inverted: what is reddish for A is greenish for B and vice versa. If this scenario is genuinely possible, strong intentionalism is false, because two experiences can share their intentional content while differing in phenomenal character.

Strong intentionalists typically deny the possibility. Tye's response is that genuinely inverted qualia would require different functional organization — the two subjects cannot be functionally identical if their qualia are inverted, because the functional role of the qualia is partly constitutive of what it is they represent. Inversion at the phenomenal level would show up as functional difference, and functional difference would generate representational difference. The thought experiment does not actually succeed in holding content fixed while varying phenomenal character; it merely seems to, because we have the intuitive sense that phenomenal character could float free of function. Whether that intuition is trustworthy is exactly what is at issue.

The alternative is weak intentionalism: accept that phenomenal character is not fully determined by representational content, and add a further condition to close the gap. Phenomenal concepts, or modes of presentation, or the intrinsic properties of the representational vehicle might supply what content alone leaves underdetermined. This preserves the core insight of intentionalism — that experience is essentially world-directed, not a private theater of inner appearances — while conceding that the hard problem cannot be dissolved by the move to content alone.

There is also a position that combines elements of intentionalism with naive realism: hold that experience represents external objects as constituents of the experience itself, so that the red tomato is genuinely present in the experience rather than merely represented by it. Whether this combination is coherent, or whether it simply relocates the problem rather than solving it, remains disputed. What is not in dispute is that the transparency datum with which we began requires an explanation — and intentionalism offers the most direct one available.
