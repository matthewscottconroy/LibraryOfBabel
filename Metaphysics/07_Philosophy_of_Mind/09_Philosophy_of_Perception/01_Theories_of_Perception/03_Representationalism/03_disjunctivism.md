# Disjunctivism

Here is an assumption that almost everyone in the philosophy of perception has accepted without argument: when veridical perception and hallucination are phenomenally indistinguishable, they must share a common phenomenal factor. The sense data theorist used this assumption to infer that both cases involve direct awareness of a mental particular. The representationalist uses it to argue that both cases instantiate the same type of representational state. Disjunctivism rejects the assumption outright. What looks like a single natural kind — "being appeared to F-ly" — is actually a disjunction of radically different states: seeing the tomato (a genuine relational state involving the world) and hallucinating a tomato (a purely internal state with no external relatum). They are not instances of a common type that differs only in its causal origin; they are different types of mental state through and through.

The name "disjunctivism" reflects this: experience is not a single kind but a disjoint pair. The philosophical payoff is significant. If the common factor assumption is rejected, the argument from hallucination to the existence of sense data — or to the priority of internal representational states — simply fails. We cannot conclude that veridical perception and hallucination share a highest common factor if there is no such factor to be found.

## McDowell, Martin, and What Indistinguishability Explains

John McDowell (*Mind and World*, 1994) develops disjunctivism in an epistemological key. In genuine veridical perception, the fact that the world is thus-and-so is itself part of the content of the experience — not merely represented but present. The world is directly available to the perceiving subject, which grounds a non-Cartesian epistemology: the veridical perceiver's knowledge does not depend on inference from inner states to external causes. McDowell's slogan is that "the highest common factor" between perception and hallucination is an illusion. The subject who sees the red tomato has a different kind of access to the world than the subject who hallucinates one, even though neither can tell from the inside which situation she is in. This is precisely what makes the epistemology externalist: epistemic status depends partly on how things actually stand in the world, not only on the character of inner states.

M. G. F. Martin presses the metaphysical point with particular force. Veridical perception is a relational state: the perceiver genuinely stands in a perceptual relation to the perceived objects, and the state is partly individuated by those objects as its relata. Hallucination is not a relational state; it is an inner mental state individuated purely by its intrinsic properties. These are not merely different causal histories attaching to states of the same fundamental type — they are different kinds of state at the most basic level of metaphysical characterization.

Now consider the objection that presses hardest: if perception and hallucination share no positive phenomenal element, what explains their indistinguishability? Surely the reason I cannot tell from the inside whether I am perceiving or hallucinating is that both states have some common qualitative feel — some shared positive feature in virtue of which they seem alike. The disjunctivist's reply is that this inference, though initially compelling, is mistaken. Indistinguishability is an epistemic relation between states — a fact about the subject's discriminatory limitations — and such a relation can hold between states that share no positive feature. What the hallucinating subject lacks is not a positive mark identifying her state as hallucinatory; she simply has no internal feature that would allow her to distinguish it from perception. The indistinguishability is explained negatively, by the absence of any discriminating mark, not positively, by the presence of a shared component.

Martin's formulation is the sharpest: the negative epistemic fact — that the hallucinator cannot know she is not perceiving — does not require that hallucination and perception share a positive phenomenal element. The common factor assumption smuggles in a positive reading of indistinguishability that the disjunctivist is entitled to reject.

## Consequences

The epistemological consequences are substantial. If the veridical perceiver genuinely has the world directly available, she can cite the perceptual fact — that she sees the tomato is red — as her reason for believing it is red. The hallucinator cannot cite a perceptual fact; she can only cite an apparent one. Their epistemic positions differ, not because of anything in their internal states, but because of the objective character of what they are (or fail to be) related to. This is epistemic externalism about perception: the justificatory status of perceptual beliefs depends partly on external conditions, not solely on how things are from the inside.

The strongest objection remains the explanatory gap. Naive realism of the disjunctivist variety has trouble accounting for why hallucinations have any phenomenal character at all, if they are not even the same kind of state as genuine perceptions. And the appeal to absence-of-discriminating-marks as an explanation of indistinguishability strikes many as explanatorily thin — surely there is something it is like to hallucinate, and that positive phenomenology demands a positive explanation. Whether the disjunctivist has the resources to provide it, without reintroducing something like a common factor, is the question on which the debate continues to turn.
