# Important Terms and Ideas: Theories of Perception

**Sense-Datum Theory**
The view that perceptual experience is always and directly of sense-data—mind-dependent intermediary objects (patches of color, sounds, smells) that represent external objects indirectly. We never directly perceive physical objects; we perceive sense-data that are caused by them.

**Naïve Realism (Direct Realism)**
The view that perceptual experience directly presents external mind-independent objects and their properties; there are no mental intermediaries between perceiver and world. When I see a red apple, I am directly acquainted with the apple and its redness, not a mental representation of them.

**Representationalism**
The view that perceptual experience has representational content—it represents the world as being a certain way. Veridical and illusory experiences differ in whether their representational content is accurate; the phenomenal character of experience is its representational content.

**The Argument from Illusion**
The argument for sense-datum theory: in perceptual illusions and hallucinations, we are directly aware of something (a bent-looking stick, a pink elephant) that does not correspond to external reality. What we are directly aware of must be a mind-dependent sense-datum. If illusions involve sense-data, then perceptions must too (by continuity).

**Transparency of Experience**
The phenomenological observation (Moore, Dretske, Martin) that when we attend to our perceptual experience, we find ourselves attending to the features of experienced objects, not to features of the experience itself. Experience is "transparent" to the world—we seem to see through it rather than at it.

**Disjunctivism**
The view that veridical perceptions and corresponding hallucinations are fundamentally different kinds of mental events—not different specifications of a common kind. A genuine perception of a cat and a hallucination of a cat share no common mental factor; they are radically disjoint experiences.
