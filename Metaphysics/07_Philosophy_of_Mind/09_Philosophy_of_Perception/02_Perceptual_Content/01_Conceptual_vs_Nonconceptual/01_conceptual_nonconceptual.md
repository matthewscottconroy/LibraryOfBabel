# Conceptual vs. Nonconceptual Perceptual Content

Look at the wall in front of you. There are dozens of subtly different shades of white and off-white — variations in cast shadow, reflected light, surface texture — that you perceive as distinct but could not name, retrieve from memory, or reliably identify again in a different context. You are perceiving content that exceeds your conceptual repertoire. Whether that observation supports a deep claim about the format of perceptual representation — or merely a superficial claim about the limits of our vocabulary — is the question that divides the conceptualist from the nonconceptualist.

A state has **conceptual content** just in case having that content requires the subject to possess the relevant concepts. For a belief to represent that there is a red tomato, the believer must possess the concepts RED, TOMATO, and EXISTENCE. Conceptual content is concept-dependent in this way: the concepts are constituents of the content, and content and concepts stand or fall together. A state has **nonconceptual content** if it can represent features of the world without the subject's being required to possess concepts for those features. The bat's echolocatory state represents the size and position of an insect without the bat deploying any concepts. Evans's point is that human perceptual experience may work similarly for the fine-grained detail that exceeds our conceptual grip.

Two readings of the nonconceptual content thesis (Heck's distinction) should be kept separate. **State nonconceptualism**: a state type is nonconceptual if creatures without the relevant concepts can be in states of that type. This is a thesis about which creatures can undergo the relevant states. **Content nonconceptualism**: the representational format itself differs from conceptual content, even when both represent the same features of the world. This is a stronger, more metaphysical claim about the kind of representation involved. Most defenders of nonconceptual content, including Evans and Peacocke, intend something like state nonconceptualism, but content nonconceptualism has also been defended.

## The Case for Nonconceptual Content

The **fine-grainedness argument** is the sharpest. I can perceive a precise shade of chartreuse tinged with yellow for which I have no available concept — no general term, no standing recognitional capacity, no ability to match it reliably against memory. Evans's formal point:

- P1: I perceive many shades that I cannot identify, name, or reliably re-identify.
- P2: Any content requiring concept-possession would be limited to the identifiable and nameable.
- C: Perceptual content exceeds what concept-possession would allow; it is at least partly nonconceptual.

The **animal and infant argument** broadens the evidential base. A crow discriminates tools by shape and material. A six-month-old infant tracks objects through occlusion. These creatures plausibly lack the full conceptual apparatus of adult human cognition, yet they perceive their environments in richly structured ways. If perception required concept-possession in the adult human sense, we would have to deny genuine perceptual content to creatures whose perceptual achievements are obvious, or stretch "concept" so far as to make the requirement trivial. Nonconceptual content provides a unified framework across the animal kingdom.

The **phenomenological argument** presses from the first-person side: the presented character of the perceptual scene — its dense, multi-dimensional array of color, texture, depth, and motion — seems to outrun anything capturable in the discrete categorical structure of concepts. When I look at a face, I perceive subtle features of expression, skin tone, and spatial arrangement that I could not fully conceptualize even with effort. The phenomenology suggests a representational format that is not concept-bounded.

## The Case for Conceptual Content

McDowell's argument in *Mind and World* (1994) proceeds from the requirements of epistemic justification. Beliefs are constitutively subject to rational evaluation; to justify a belief, experience must stand in rational, reason-giving relations to it. But rational relations — inferential, evidential, logical — hold only among items within the space of reasons. And only conceptually structured states belong to the space of reasons:

- P1: Experience can justify belief only if experience stands in rational (reason-giving) relations to belief.
- P2: Only conceptually structured states stand in rational relations.
- C: Experience is conceptually structured.

If experience were nonconceptual, it would be a brute causal input to cognition — like a blow to the head that causes a belief — rather than a rational consideration. McDowell calls nonconceptual experience a "mere external constraint" on thought, not a rational anchor. This violates what he takes to be the Kantian platitude that intuitions without concepts are blind: experience must already be conceptually shaped to genuinely deliver the world to thought.

McDowell handles the fine-grainedness objection by appealing to **demonstrative concepts**. I need not have a standing general concept for a precise shade of chartreuse; I can form the demonstrative concept *that shade*, which picks out the precise shade in the current experience. Demonstrative concepts are concept-exercisings, but they inherit their fine-grained specificity from the perceptual situation rather than from a prior standing concept. This allows conceptual content to be as fine-grained as perceptual experience.

## Evaluating the Debate

The Evans-McDowell debate hinges on what justification requires. Critics of McDowell challenge P2: nonconceptual states might justify conceptual beliefs through a different mechanism, one that does not require shared representational format. Peacocke has argued that scenario content (nonconceptual) can make beliefs correct or incorrect, and this normative relationship grounds justification without requiring that the experience itself be conceptual. Heck presses that the connection between experience and belief can be direct — a demonstrative concept exercised in perception suffices — without the experience's content thereby becoming fully conceptual.

The demonstrative concepts response faces pressure from the other direction as well. If demonstrative concepts inherit their specificity from the experience rather than providing it, the fine-grained content is at bottom experiential and nonconceptual — the demonstrative concept merely labels it. This suggests the experience is doing the representational work, and the concept is a cognitive response to it rather than its format.

Peacocke's **scenario content** attempts a hybrid: perceptual states have scenario content (nonconceptual, fine-grained, specifying ways space could be filled relative to the perceiver) and protoproposition content (closer to conceptual, encoding which objects have which properties). The two levels interact: scenario content grounds and constrains protopropositional content, while propositional content organizes the deliverances of scenario content into justificatory claims. This layered view preserves the fine-grainedness of experience without severing its connection to rational justification.

The debate connects to several broader issues. If perceptual content is conceptual, then concepts and beliefs can in principle penetrate all the way into experience — explaining why expertise and expectation alter what we see. If content is nonconceptual, perception is shielded from top-down conceptual influence by its different representational format, which bears on questions of modularity and cognitive penetrability. The nature of justification, the scope of rationality, and the architecture of cognition all turn on how this question is resolved.
