# McDowell vs. Evans on Perceptual Content

Gareth Evans and John McDowell were, in many ways, philosophical allies — both trained at Oxford, both deeply influenced by Frege, both opposed to the dominant computational picture of mind. Yet they reach strikingly different conclusions about the content of perceptual experience, and the disagreement is not merely technical. It concerns the most fundamental question about the relationship between mind and world: whether the deliverances of perception are already shaped by the rational, conceptual capacities that belong to thought, or whether they are prior to thought and merely inform it.

## Evans's Nonconceptualism

Evans, in *The Varieties of Reference* (1982), distinguished two components of mental life: the informational system, which includes perceptual states and their deliverances, and the conceptual system, which includes beliefs, judgments, and other states that belong to the space of reasons. This is not merely a distinction between two cognitive capacities; it is a distinction between two kinds of representational content.

Perceptual systems produce states with representational content that is more fine-grained than any conceptual repertoire the perceiver possesses. I can discriminate many more shades of green than I have concepts for. My perceptual state has content that goes beyond my conceptual resources — Evans called this **analog** representational content, a form of representation that is continuous and fine-grained rather than discrete and concept-bound. The content of a perceptual state is not carved at the joints of the subject's conceptual scheme; it is as fine-grained as the discriminatory capacities of the perceptual system itself. This reflects empirical facts about visual processing: the visual system constructs representations of spatial properties, surface properties, and object structure using formats that need not answer to any conceptual scheme the subject commands.

Perceptual states, on this view, provide input to the belief-forming system without being beliefs. The input can be richer than the conceptual content of any belief the perceiver would form on its basis. What perception delivers is information, and that information has a nonconceptual format before being taken up and transformed by the conceptual system.

## McDowell's Conceptualism

McDowell, in *Mind and World* (1994), argues against nonconceptualism on explicitly epistemological grounds. His concern is that nonconceptual content would make experience epistemically inert — unable to justify the beliefs it causes. Experience must be capable of serving as rational support for judgment and belief; for it to do so, it must stand in rational relations to belief. But rational relations — inferential, evidential, logical — hold only among items within the space of reasons. And only conceptually structured states belong to the space of reasons:

- P1: For experience to justify belief, experience must provide reasons for belief.
- P2: Reasons must be within the space of reasons — conceptually structured.
- P3: Only conceptually structured states are within the space of reasons.
- C: Experience must be conceptually structured if it is to justify belief.

If experience had nonconceptual content, the relation between experience and belief would be like the relation between a blow to the head and the belief it causes — a causal nudge, not a reason. McDowell finds this picture deeply unsatisfying because it severs the rational connection between world and thought. The result would be a form of coherentism in disguise: our beliefs would ultimately be constrained only by one another, with experience providing merely causal pressure rather than rational grounding.

The fine-grainedness objection Evans marshals is handled through **demonstrative concepts**. When I perceive a particular shade of green for which I have no standing concept, I can form a demonstrative perceptual concept: *that shade of green*. This concept is exercised in the moment of experience and anchors its content to the specific perceptual situation. It is conceptual — an exercise of a concept-forming capacity — but it does not require a prior standing concept for that shade. The demonstrative concept picks out exactly what the experience presents, so conceptual content can be as fine-grained as the experience itself.

On McDowell's view, conceptual capacities are not a filter that limits what can be experienced. They are the medium in which objective experience is possible. Only a creature with conceptual capacities can genuinely see objects as objects — can engage with the world rationally rather than merely respond to it informationally.

## The Sellarsian Background

McDowell's position is shaped throughout by Sellars's critique of "the Myth of the Given." Sellars argued (*Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind*, 1956) that experience cannot serve as a presuppositionless foundation for knowledge because nothing outside the space of reasons can justify claims within it. McDowell reads Sellars as establishing that experience must be conceptual if it is to play any epistemic role at all. But McDowell also finds that Sellars, having correctly rejected the Given, is pulled toward coherentism — the view that justification is entirely a matter of relations among beliefs, with experience providing no rational constraint from outside. McDowell's project is to thread between these pitfalls: experience is conceptual (avoiding the Myth of the Given) but genuinely world-involving (avoiding coherentism's isolation of thought from world).

## Key Objections

The nonconceptualist reply to McDowell (Peacocke, Heck) questions whether justification requires shared conceptual format. A nonconceptual state can justify a conceptual belief if the state's correctness conditions are related in the right normative way to the belief's truth conditions: a perceptual state representing *that shade* makes it correct to form the belief *there is a red patch here* under appropriate conditions, and this normative relation grounds justification without requiring the experience itself to be conceptual.

The challenge to the demonstrative concept response is deeper: if demonstrative concepts inherit their fine-grainedness from the experience — if *that shade* picks out a specific shade only because the experience already presents it as specific — then the fine-grained content is at bottom experiential and nonconceptual. The demonstrative concept labels the content that the experience delivers, rather than constituting that content. This suggests that even on McDowell's own account, the work is being done by the experience, and the concept is a response to it.

A further objection (Byrne, Logue): if perceptual content is fully conceptual, then creatures without the relevant concepts cannot perceive the corresponding features. But this seems empirically false. A congenitally color-blind person who acquires color vision perceives colors before acquiring color concepts, and the perceptual acquisition itself seems to precede and explain the conceptual one — suggesting a nonconceptual level at which color perception operates independently of concept-possession.

Contemporary philosophy of perception has largely moved toward hybrid positions: experience has nonconceptual content in the sense that concept-possession is not required for states of that type (state nonconceptualism), but the experience can also involve the application of concepts that shape what is perceived. The Evans-McDowell debate remains foundational because its resolution determines how we conceive the relationship between the subpersonal informational order of perception and the personal, rational order of thought.
