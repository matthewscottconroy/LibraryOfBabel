# Nonconceptual Content

There is something strange about the view that perceptual content must be conceptual. A crow selects the right tool for a task it has never encountered before, navigating a spatial and mechanical problem with apparent flexibility. A six-month-old infant tracks an object through occlusion, registering that it continues to exist behind the screen. We want to say — and we have good empirical reason to say — that these creatures are representing features of their environments. But attributing to them the rich conceptual resources that, on some views, are required for any representation at all seems to either credit them with too much or to stretch the notion of "concept" beyond recognition. Nonconceptual content is the theoretical posit that makes room for genuine representation without requiring concept-possession, and its motivation is as much empirical as philosophical.

A mental state has nonconceptual content when it can have that content even if the subject does not possess the concepts that would specify it. This is the contrast with belief: to believe that water is wet, one must possess the concepts WATER and WET. But to perceptually represent the wetness of a surface — to have a state that responds differentially to wet surfaces and guides behavior accordingly — no such concept is required. Infants and animals represent wetness, but they need not possess WET.

## Varieties and Motivations

Evans, in *The Varieties of Reference* (1982), drew the fundamental distinction between the informational system and the conceptual system. The informational system includes perceptual states and their deliverances; the conceptual system includes beliefs, judgments, and other states within the space of reasons. Evans called perceptual content **analog**: it varies continuously along dimensions corresponding to perceived features, rather than operating by the discrete, categorizing structure of concepts. The fine-grainedness argument follows directly — I can discriminate many more shades of green than I have concepts for, and this overabundance of discriminatory content over conceptual content is the mark of nonconceptual representation.

Peacocke (*A Study of Concepts*, 1992) formalizes this with the notion of **scenario content**. A scenario is a way of filling out space around the perceiver — a specification, at each point and direction, of what is there: which surface, which color, which texture, which distance. A perceptual experience has a scenario as its content when it represents the environment as conforming to that scenario. Scenarios are not conceptual; they are spatial, fine-grained specifications that need not be articulable by the perceiver. Peacocke also introduces **protoproposition content** at a higher level — a more structured format encoding which objects are perceived as having which properties — yielding a hybrid framework: nonconceptual content at the base, something closer to conceptual content above it.

Heck distinguishes **weak** from **strong** nonconceptualism. The weak version holds that we can ascribe nonconceptual content to perceptual states by using our concepts to specify what the states represent, without the states themselves depending on those concepts. The strong version holds that the representational format of perceptual content is categorically different from conceptual content — not just that the subject lacks the relevant concepts, but that the kind of representation involved is fundamentally unlike conceptual representation. Strong nonconceptualism has more radical implications, including that perception is to some degree cognitively encapsulated from top-down conceptual influence.

The cognitive science argument supports this encapsulation claim. Marr's theory of vision describes perceptual processing through a hierarchy of representations — from the primal sketch through the 2.5D sketch to the 3D model — none of which are formulated in terms of the subject's conceptual scheme. Early and intermediate vision constructs representations of edge orientations, binocular disparities, and object structure using computational operations that are subpersonal and pre-conceptual. This gives us reason to believe that perceptual content is, at its representational base, nonconceptual — and explains why the Müller-Lyer lines still look unequal to someone who knows perfectly well that they are the same length. The knowledge does not penetrate to the level at which the nonconceptual representation is fixed.

## McDowell's Objection and the Replies

McDowell's argument is that nonconceptual content cannot play the epistemic role that its proponents need it to play. For experience to justify belief — to provide reasons — it must stand in rational, inferential, normative relations to belief. Only conceptually structured states can stand in such relations. A nonconceptual state can causally produce beliefs, but it cannot be a reason for them; it is a brute causal input, like a blow to the head that causes a conviction. Nothing outside the space of reasons can justify claims within it.

The nonconceptualist replies are several. Peacocke and Heck argue that justification does not require shared representational format: a perceptual state with scenario content can justify a conceptual belief when the state's correctness conditions are related in the right normative way to the belief's truth conditions. If the experience accurately represents the scene and its content makes a certain conceptual belief true, the experience justifies the belief through a normative connection that is not itself inferential. The move is to broaden the conception of justification beyond the inferential model that McDowell assumes.

A second reply reconceives what the experience contributes: even if the experience is nonconceptual, the perceiver can recognize — in a distinct conceptual act — that the experience has a certain character, and this recognition then justifies the belief. The nonconceptual experience is the basis for the recognitional judgment; the judgment provides the in-the-space-of-reasons support. A third, more radical reply questions whether McDowell's narrow conception of justification is defensible: if only conceptually structured states can justify, then animal and infant cognition involves no justified representations at all, and this seems too strong a conclusion to build into an epistemological theory.

The current consensus, if there is one, accepts some form of state nonconceptualism — the animal and developmental arguments are difficult to resist — while remaining divided on whether the representational format of experience is categorically nonconceptual, and on what account of justification can accommodate nonconceptual evidence without abandoning the role of reasons entirely.
