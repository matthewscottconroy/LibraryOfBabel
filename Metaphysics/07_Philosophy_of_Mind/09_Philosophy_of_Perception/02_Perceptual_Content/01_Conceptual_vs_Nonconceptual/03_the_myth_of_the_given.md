# The Myth of the Given

Foundationalism about knowledge rests on a picture: somewhere below the inferential structure of our beliefs, there must be a bedrock — items that are known directly, without inference, without conceptual mediation, just given to consciousness with immediate certainty. Sense data were the canonical candidate. When I experience something pink in red light, my sense datum is known to me with certainty; it is immediately present to consciousness, requiring no inference, no interpretation, no conceptual apparatus. The rest of knowledge is built up from this bedrock. Sellars's argument in "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" (1956) is that this picture is incoherent — that anything capable of playing the justificatory role the Given was designed to play cannot be pre-conceptual, and anything genuinely pre-conceptual cannot play that role.

The argument is structural. The Given is assigned two tasks simultaneously. First, a **causal-explanatory task**: the basic items must explain why we form the beliefs we do. Sensory episodes, as natural events in the causal order, can do this; they cause the formation of beliefs, and there is no mystery about how. Second, a **justificatory task**: the basic items must provide reasons — they must justify beliefs, not merely cause them. Justification is a normative notion: it concerns whether a belief is appropriate or correct to hold given some consideration. For X to justify belief B, X must stand in logical or normative relations to B — the kind of relations that make B the right thing to believe given X.

Sellars's diagnosis:

- P1: For X to justify belief B, X must stand in rational (inferential/normative) relations to B.
- P2: Only conceptually structured states stand in rational relations.
- P3: The Given is supposed to be pre-conceptual — not itself structured by concepts.
- C: The Given cannot justify belief; it can at most cause it.

A sensory episode, as a pre-conceptual event in the natural order, can cause a belief. It cannot be a reason for a belief, because reasons belong to what Sellars calls the "space of reasons" — the logical space in which items stand in inferential, evidential, and normative relations to each other. Nothing outside the space of reasons can justify claims within it. This is not a rejection of experience's causal role; it is a rejection of the idea that raw, pre-conceptual experience can simultaneously play a justificatory role.

## The Scope of the Critique

Sellars's argument reaches further than its specific target of sense data. It challenges any view that locates epistemic foundations in pre-conceptual givens. **Infallible introspective reports** — "I am now seeing red" — if genuinely pre-conceptual, cannot justify the conceptual belief they are supposed to ground; if they are themselves conceptual acts, they are not the pre-conceptual bedrock foundationalism requires. **Qualia**, if genuinely pre-conceptual, can cause me to believe there is something red, but their merely qualitative character — prior to any conceptual uptake — cannot justify that belief. **Russellian acquaintance** — the idea that we are directly acquainted with sense data, and that acquaintance is a form of knowing that requires no further justification — is precisely the target: acquaintance, if it is to count as knowing at all, must involve conceptual capacities.

Sellars's positive view is that all genuine knowledge — including perceptual knowledge — is within the space of reasons. This means it is always inferentially and conceptually structured, always standing in the right relations to other items in the rational order. This does not mean all knowledge is inferentially derived (there is non-inferential knowledge), but it means that even non-inferential knowledge is apt for inference — it has the right logical and normative connections to everything else one knows. There is no presuppositionless epistemic bedrock; all knowledge presupposes a conceptual framework.

## McDowell's Use and Extension

McDowell, in *Mind and World* (1994), builds on Sellars's critique to motivate his own conceptualist account of perception. He accepts that the Myth of the Given must be avoided: if experience is to play an epistemic role, it cannot be pre-conceptual. But he finds that Sellars himself, having correctly rejected the Given, is pulled toward coherentism — the view that justification is entirely a matter of relations among beliefs, with experience providing merely causal pressure from outside the conceptual order. McDowell takes coherentism to be equally unacceptable: it severs the rational connection between world and thought, leaving our thinking without genuine rational constraint from how things stand.

McDowell's solution is to reconceive experience as conceptually structured: not a pre-conceptual brute given, but also not a mere internal state sealed off from the world. Experience is a rational openness to the world in which the world's features are present in conceptual form. The world itself is present in experience — but already conceptually shaped, already within the space of reasons. This allows experience to provide rational constraint from the world without being a pre-conceptual Given, threading between the two pitfalls.

The disjunctivist development of this position goes further: in veridical perception, the fact that the tomato is red is itself part of what the perceiver has access to. The justification for believing the tomato is red is not provided by an inner state at all — whether conceptual or nonconceptual — but by the worldly fact itself, which the perceiver stands in direct rational relation to. This bypasses the Sellarsian dilemma, though it raises its own questions about how facts can be reasons.

## Criticisms

The non-inferentialist response questions Sellars's assumption that all justification must be inferential. Reliabilist accounts of justification (Goldman) hold that a belief is justified if it is produced by a reliable process; the reliable causal production of a belief by a perceptual state can be sufficient for justification without requiring that the state itself be within the space of reasons. The externalist about justification simply denies P2: normative relations can hold between a nonconceptual perceptual state and a conceptual belief without the state's having conceptual structure, so long as the relation is of the right type (reliable, truth-conducive, appropriately connected to the world).

Sellars's legacy is substantial. The space of reasons framework he introduced has been developed most extensively by Brandom (*Making It Explicit*, 1994) into a full inferentialist semantics and normative pragmatics. In philosophy of perception, Sellars's argument moved the field away from classical sense data foundationalism toward representationalism and conceptualism — the central alternatives are now precisely those McDowell traces: a conceptually structured experience that avoids the Myth, or a nonconceptual content that requires a new account of how it earns its justificatory credentials.
