# Phenomenal and Representational in Perception

Here is a question we can sharpen precisely: is what it is like to see red the same thing as representing red? The representationalist says yes — the redness of the visual experience just is the experience's representing redness in the world. The phenomenal realist says no — phenomenal character is an intrinsic property of the experience, something over and above what it represents, something that could in principle be held fixed while the representational content varied. The stakes are high: the first view promises to naturalize consciousness along with content; the second view insists that phenomenal character is a genuine addition to the world that no functional or representational description can capture.

Both notions in the debate are real and intuitive. **Phenomenal character** is the subjective, qualitative dimension — what it is like, the felt redness or the felt pain. **Representational content** is the world-directed, intentional dimension — what the experience is about, what it purports to represent, the conditions under which it would be accurate. The question is whether these two dimensions are ultimately the same or genuinely distinct.

## Three Positions

**Strong representationalism** (Tye, *Ten Problems of Consciousness*, 1995; Dretske, *Naturalizing the Mind*, 1995) holds that phenomenal character is identical to, or fully constituted by, representational content. Two experiences with the same representational content have the same phenomenal character, and vice versa. The redness of my experience is nothing over and above the experience's representing redness. This connects to the transparency datum that Moore first noticed: when I try to introspect my experience of red, my attention passes through it to the external red surface; I find no further inner redness, only the redness attributed to the world. If phenomenal character were something over and above representational content, introspection should reveal it. It does not.

**Weak representationalism** accepts the supervenience claim — same representational content, same phenomenal character — but denies identity. Phenomenal properties are constituted partly by representational content and partly by additional factors: the mode of presentation of the content (visual vs. auditory), the phenomenal concepts the subject applies in introspection, or the intrinsic properties of the neural vehicle that carries the content. This allows phenomenal residue beyond content without abandoning the tight connection between the two.

**Anti-representationalism** (associated with Ned Block's phenomenal realism) holds that phenomenal character is an intrinsic property of mental states that has no essentially representational character. Block's distinction between **access consciousness** (A-consciousness — availability for reasoning, report, and rational control) and **phenomenal consciousness** (P-consciousness — what it is like) is the theoretical instrument here. The claim is that these dimensions can come apart: blindsight patients show A-consciousness for stimuli they deny seeing, suggesting that phenomenal character is not simply functional availability. If a system could have all the representational content required for A-consciousness while lacking P-consciousness, phenomenal character is not identical to representational content.

## The Arguments

The **inverted qualia argument** is the main challenge to strong representationalism. Imagine two subjects, A and B, who are functionally identical and who both correctly represent red things as red and green things as green — their representational content is the same. But their experiences are systematically inverted: what red is like for A is what green is like for B. If such inversion is metaphysically possible, two experiences can share their representational content while differing in phenomenal character, which refutes the strong view. Strong representationalists respond that genuine inversion would require different functional organization — and different functional organization generates different representational content — so the scenario is not actually possible. The inverted subjects would not be functionally identical, because phenomenal character is tied to functional role. Whether this response fully accounts for the intuition driving the thought experiment remains disputed.

The **absent qualia argument** presses similarly: a system might implement the full representational content of a conscious perceiver while lacking any phenomenal experience — a philosophical zombie with matching representations but no phenomenal character. If zombies are possible, representational content does not entail phenomenal character. Strong representationalists typically deny the metaphysical possibility of zombies even if they are conceivable — the conceivability gap does not establish a possibility gap.

The **phenomenal sorites** poses a more concrete challenge. Consider a series of shades where adjacent shades are indistinguishable in isolation but the endpoints are discriminable when compared directly. This suggests phenomenal differences that track distinctions too fine to register in any representational content the perceiver can deploy. If phenomenal character is more fine-grained than representational content, they cannot be identical.

## Vehicle Representationalism and the Middle Ground

A position worth noting: **vehicle representationalism** holds that phenomenal properties are properties of the representing vehicle — the neural substrate — rather than of the representational content alone. This preserves the basic representationalist insight that phenomenal character is a feature of how the mind represents, while acknowledging that the vehicle itself contributes something beyond the content it carries. The vehicle's intrinsic properties, including its biological character, may partly determine what it is like to be in the state.

The debate over phenomenal and representational character is ultimately a debate about the reducibility of consciousness. Strong representationalism promises a path through the hard problem: naturalize content, and phenomenal character comes with it for free. Anti-representationalism insists that no such path exists — that phenomenal character is a genuine addition to the natural world, irreducible to anything functional or representational, and that any adequate account of mind must acknowledge this irreducibly subjective dimension. Whether the transparency datum supports strong representationalism or merely shows the world-directedness of experience — without showing that phenomenal character just is representational content — is the question on which the debate turns.
