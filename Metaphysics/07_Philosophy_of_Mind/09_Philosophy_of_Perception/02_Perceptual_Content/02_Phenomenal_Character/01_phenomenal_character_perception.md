# Phenomenal Character in Perception

What is it like to see red? The question sounds oddly formed until you grasp what it is asking. Thomas Nagel (*What Is It Like to Be a Bat?*, 1974) used it to point at a dimension of experience that third-person descriptions systematically miss. We can give a complete neuroscientific account of what happens when wavelengths around 700nm stimulate the retina, how the signal propagates through the lateral geniculate nucleus to V4, what representational states the visual system constructs — and still leave something out. What it is like from the inside to see red: this is phenomenal character, and the fact that it seems to resist third-person description is the source of some of the deepest puzzles in philosophy of mind.

Ned Block's distinction between **access consciousness** (A-consciousness) and **phenomenal consciousness** (P-consciousness) gives these intuitions theoretical precision. A state is A-conscious if its content is available for use in reasoning, report, inference, and the rational control of behavior. A state is P-conscious if there is something it is like to be in it — if it has phenomenal character. The crucial claim is that these two dimensions can come apart. Blindsight patients lack P-consciousness for stimuli in their blind field — they sincerely report seeing nothing — yet show above-chance performance on forced-choice visual tasks, suggesting some A-consciousness. If this dissociation is genuine, phenomenal character is not simply functional availability for report and inference; it is an additional, independent dimension of conscious states, not reducible to any functional role.

## Phenomenal Character and Representational Content

The central philosophical question is how phenomenal character relates to representational content. **Strong representationalism** (Tye, Dretske) holds that phenomenal character is identical to representational content: what it is like to see red just is the experience's representing redness in the world. This view naturalizes phenomenal character by reducing it to content, and content to causal or teleosemantic relations — giving a fully naturalistic account with no phenomenal residue. **Property dualism** holds that phenomenal character is an intrinsic property of mental states that is not reducible to representational or functional properties: pain is painful in virtue of painfulness, a property that is real but not physical or functional. **Weak representationalism** occupies a middle position: phenomenal character supervenes on representational content but is not identical to it; additional factors — mode of presentation, biological substrate, phenomenal concepts — are needed to fix phenomenal character given content.

The **phenomenal sorites** offers a concrete challenge to the strong view. Consider a series of color shades — shade 1, shade 2, ..., shade n — where adjacent shades are indistinguishable in isolation but discriminable when directly compared. This is paradoxical: if phenomenal indistinguishability is transitive, shades 1 and n should be phenomenally identical, but they are not. One lesson is that phenomenal character may be more fine-grained than representational content. The perceiver cannot identify, name, or re-identify specific shades in the middle of the series — no representational content is available to capture the distinction — yet the phenomenal differences are real. If phenomenal character tracks distinctions that exceed representational resources, phenomenal character and representational content are not identical.

## Analyses of Phenomenal Character

Two structural analyses have been developed to characterize what phenomenal character is without reifying sense data. The **adverbial analysis** — developed by Chisholm among others — avoids commitment to mental objects by modifying the perceptual act rather than positing an object of perception. Instead of saying I perceive a red sense datum, we say I perceive redly — the adverb captures the qualitative character of the act without implying there is an inner red object. The **relational analysis** goes in the opposite direction: phenomenal character involves a genuine relation to the object perceived, and what it is like to have the experience is partly constituted by the character of that object. On this view, the tomato is not merely represented; it is part of the experiential relation, and its redness is partly constitutive of the experience's phenomenal character. This connects directly to naive realism.

## Introspection and the Higher-Order Question

When I attend to my experience of red, what am I accessing? **Higher-order thought (HOT) theories** (Rosenthal) hold that an experience is conscious if and only if the subject has a concurrent higher-order representation of being in that experience. Phenomenal character is not intrinsic but relational — it depends on being the target of a higher-order state. The implication is that creatures without higher-order representational capacities lack phenomenal consciousness. **First-order theories** hold that phenomenal character is an intrinsic property of the first-order perceptual state itself, given directly in experience without needing a higher-order gloss. The experience is conscious from the inside, not because it is represented by a further mental state.

## The Hard Problem

Phenomenal character is at the center of what Chalmers calls the hard problem of consciousness: why is there something it is like to be in certain mental states, rather than mere information processing in the dark? Physical and functional descriptions explain behavioral, cognitive, and physiological facts — the easy problems. They leave the hard problem untouched: why is the physical and functional activity accompanied by subjective experience at all? The conceivability of philosophical zombies — beings physically identical to us but lacking phenomenal consciousness — is Chalmers's instrument for pressing the point. If zombies are conceivable, phenomenal character is not logically entailed by any physical description, which suggests it is not reducible to the physical.

Strong representationalists typically deny that zombies are genuinely conceivable, or argue that conceivability does not establish metaphysical possibility. The debate remains unresolved, but its stakes are clear: phenomenal character in perception either falls within the scope of a naturalistic theory of representational content, or it requires something more — an irreducibly subjective dimension that no account in terms of information, function, or causal role can capture.
