# Qualia in Perception

Consider the specific shade of red on the tomato in front of you. Not red in general, but that particular warm, saturated red — the exact quality of that color as it appears right now. Frank Jackson's Mary, who has lived her entire life in a black-and-white room studying the neuroscience of color vision, knows everything physical there is to know about wavelengths, cone cells, opponent processes, and the functional role of color experiences. When she steps outside and sees red for the first time, does she learn something? Jackson's argument is that she does: what it is like to see red is not captured by any physical description, however complete. That extra knowledge — the felt quality, the phenomenal character, the "what it's like" — is what philosophers mean by qualia.

The knowledge argument is not universally accepted; physicalists have a range of responses. But the intuition it pumps is real and difficult to dismiss. There is a felt quality to perceiving red that seems distinct from the fact that I am in a functional state that discriminates red surfaces, and distinct from the representational content of the state. Whether this apparent distinctness reflects genuine ontological distinctness — whether qualia are real irreducible properties — is the central question.

## Theories of Qualia

The **phenomenal realist** account (Chalmers, Block, Levine) holds that qualia are genuine intrinsic properties of mental states, not reducible to functional or representational properties. Pain is painful in virtue of instantiating the property of painfulness; the redness of a red experience is a real phenomenal feature of the state. These properties are real but not physical: they are additional features of the world not captured by any purely physical description. The explanatory gap (Levine) follows: even if we identify pain with some neural state, we can explain why that neural state disposes a creature to withdrawal behavior without explaining why it feels like this — why there is a specific phenomenal character. The gap is not in the facts but in the explanation.

The challenge for phenomenal realism is causal: if phenomenal properties are not physical, how do they relate to physical causation? **Epiphenomenalism** holds they are caused by physical states but cause nothing in turn — making qualia causally inert, which seems to contradict the obvious fact that how things feel influences what we do. **Interactionist dualism** allows phenomenal properties to cause physical events — but then they must intervene in physical causal chains, threatening physical causal closure.

The **representationalist account** (Tye, Dretske) identifies qualitative character with representational content. The redness of my red experience just is its representing red things; painfulness is the experience's representing tissue damage or something functionally analogous. This naturalizes qualia: once representational content is naturalized via causal or teleosemantic theories, phenomenal character is naturalized with it. The challenge comes from inverted qualia: two subjects who represent the same colors in the same functional way might have different qualitative experiences — what one experiences as reddish, the other experiences as greenish. If inverted qualia are possible, representational content does not determine qualitative character. Strong representationalists deny this possibility, arguing that any genuine phenomenal inversion would require functional differences that generate different representational content.

The **adverbial theory** (Chisholm, Sellars) avoids commitment to mental objects by reformulating the structure of experience. Rather than saying I perceive a red sense datum — which posits an inner red object — we say I perceive redly: the adverb modifies the perceptual act rather than picking out an object of perception. Qualitative character is an adverbial modification of experiencing, not a property of anything. This sidesteps sense data theory's ontological commitments and its regress problems. The challenge is complexity: for rich multi-property experiences, the adverbial formulation becomes awkward, and it is not clear whether it eliminates qualia or merely redescribes them without inner objects.

The **eliminativist account** (Dennett, Frankish) holds that qualia as traditionally conceived — intrinsic, non-relational, non-representational phenomenal properties — do not exist. Dennett argues that when we try to specify what qualia are supposed to be, the concept dissolves: qualia are alleged to be ineffable, immediately apprehensible, intrinsic, and independent of functional role — but on examination no such properties can be coherently described. What we call qualia are just functional properties misdescribed in phenomenal terms. Frankish's **illusionism** is more precise: we do not have experiences with intrinsic phenomenal properties, but we are constitutively disposed to represent our experiences as having such properties. Phenomenal consciousness is a systematic illusion. The hard problem is transformed — not why there are qualia, but why we so thoroughly believe in them.

## The Inverted Qualia Thought Experiment

The inverted qualia scenario — traced to Locke and developed by Shoemaker and Block — imagines two subjects with systematically inverted phenomenal experiences but identical representational and functional organization. When A sees a ripe tomato, she has an experience that phenomenally resembles what B has when she sees the sky. Both represent the tomato as red and the sky as blue; their behavior is indistinguishable; but their phenomenal characters are inverted.

The thought experiment is designed to show that phenomenal character is not constituted by representational or functional properties — that there is an extra dimension of experience that can vary while everything functional holds fixed. Those who deny the possibility (Tye, Dretske) argue that genuine inversion would require different functional organization, which generates different representational content: the scenario assumes that functional and representational features can be held constant while phenomenal character varies, and this assumption is false. Those who accept the possibility draw the dualist conclusion that phenomenal character is a genuine additional feature of experience (Chalmers, Block). Those who eliminate qualia (Dennett) argue that the intuition driving the thought experiment relies on a confused concept that dissolves under examination.

Whether qualia are real, and if real whether they are reducible, determines what kind of theory of mind is ultimately possible — whether naturalism can be complete, or whether consciousness requires something more.
