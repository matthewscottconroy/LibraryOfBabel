# Transparency of Perception

Try to attend to your visual experience itself — not to the red apple on the table, but to the experience of the red apple. Notice what happens. Attention passes through the experience to the apple; you find the apple's redness, its roundness, its shiny surface — but you do not find any further inner mental redness, any private red property that belongs to the experience rather than to the world. The experience seems, in Moore's word, "diaphanous" — transparent as glass. You see through it to what it is about. This is the phenomenon of transparency, and it is the phenomenological starting point for one of the most influential arguments in the philosophy of perception.

Moore first articulated the observation in "The Refutation of Idealism" (1903): when you try to introspect the experience of blue, "your attention passes through the experience to the blue object." Gilbert Harman developed this into a systematic argument for representationalism in "The Intrinsic Quality of Experience" (1990). If phenomenal character were something over and above representational content — an intrinsic mental property distinct from the represented world-properties — introspection should reveal it. But introspection reveals only world-properties. What I find when I attend to my red experience is redness as a property of an external surface, not redness as an inner mental property. Therefore, phenomenal properties are not intrinsic mental properties; they are representational properties — how the world is represented as being.

## The Argument and Its Uses

The formal structure:

- P1: When a subject introspects her perceptual experience, she finds properties of the represented external world, not intrinsic properties of the experience itself.
- P2: If phenomenal properties were intrinsic properties of experience additional to representational properties, introspection would reveal them as distinct from world-properties.
- P3: Introspection does not reveal phenomenal properties as distinct from world-properties — the experience is transparent.
- C: Phenomenal properties are not intrinsic properties of experience over and above representational properties; they are representational properties.

Tye deploys the transparency argument as central support for his PANIC theory: the phenomenal character of experience is constituted by its poised, abstract, nonconceptual intentional content, with no residue of intrinsic mental properties beyond the representational. Dretske similarly uses it to motivate the claim that what is experienced phenomenally is always a property of the represented external world, never a property of the experience itself.

## Objections

The **phenomenological objection** questions whether transparency is universally true as a description of experience. Experiences can be sharp or fuzzy, vivid or dim, in ways that seem to characterize the experience rather than the world it represents. The emotional tone of anxiety or exhilaration seems to pervade experience in a way that is not simply a matter of what is represented. In afterimages and phosphenes, phenomenal character seems present without any clear representational target: the floating red circle of an afterimage has undeniable felt quality, and it is implausible to identify this with a representation of an absent external red property. Representationalists respond that even afterimages represent: they represent a property at a location, misrepresenting something as being there that is not. Critics find this move strained — the question is whether the felt quality of the afterimage can be adequately captured by the claim that it misrepresents.

The **conflation objection** (Block) argues that the transparency thesis conflates two things. The fact that introspective attention is world-directed shows that experience is intentionally directed at the world; it does not show that there are no intrinsic phenomenal properties. Block's distinction between A-consciousness (availability for reasoning and report) and P-consciousness (what it is like) is relevant here: A-conscious states are functionally transparent in the sense that their content is available for use, and introspective attention to A-conscious states is naturally object-directed. But P-consciousness may have intrinsic properties that are not revealed by ordinary introspective attention precisely because such attention is itself world-directed. Transparency may be a truth about how we introspect, not a window onto the fundamental nature of phenomenal character.

The **introspection objection** presses this: the transparency argument relies on the reliability of introspection, but perceptual processing is largely subpersonal and inaccessible to introspective attention. If phenomenal character partly depends on subpersonal neural properties, it may not be fully revealed by an introspective act that passes through to world-properties. The transparency of experience may be a feature of introspection's phenomenological orientation, not evidence about what the experience fundamentally is.

## Transparency and Naive Realism

Transparency has a natural home in naive realism as well as in representationalism — but the two interpretations of the datum differ fundamentally. For the representationalist, transparency shows that phenomenal properties are representational properties: the experience is transparent because its phenomenal character just is its representational content. For the naive realist, transparency shows that experience is a direct relation to worldly properties, not mediated by representations at all: the redness in experience is the actual redness of the external surface, present in the experience as a genuine relatum. Both views honor Moore's observation, but they draw different theoretical conclusions from it.

The question is which interpretation is better supported. The representationalist's version has the theoretical advantage of fitting within a broader naturalistic framework for mental content. The naive realist's version has the phenomenological advantage of taking transparency literally — the world is in the experience, not merely represented by it — but faces difficulty with illusion and hallucination, where the world is not available to be a relatum.

Most philosophers now accept transparency as a genuine phenomenological datum that counts against sense data theory: experience does not present inner mental objects with their own qualitative properties. But transparency does not settle the deeper question of whether phenomenal character is identical to representational content, genuinely distinct from it, or constituted by direct relational contact with the world. It illuminates the intentional structure of experience and constrains the range of acceptable theories; it does not decide among them.
