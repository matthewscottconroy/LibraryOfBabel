# Enactivism

The classical picture of perception runs roughly like this: the world impresses itself on the sensory organs; the sensory organs transduce this impression into symbolic representations; those representations are processed by central cognition; and motor commands are output as behavioral responses. Perception is the passive input stage of this process — the conversion of world to inner model. The mind is, in Ryle's phrase, a ghost in the machine, working on inner representations behind the input and output ports.

Enactivism rejects this picture at its foundation. The mind is not inside the head processing representations of an outside world. Perception is not passive reception but active exploration. Cognition is not the manipulation of inner symbols but the ongoing, skillful engagement of an embodied organism with its environment. The organism does not recover a pre-given world; it **enacts** a world — the label comes from Varela, Thompson, and Rosch (*The Embodied Mind*, 1991), and it captures what is most distinctive about this family of approaches.

## Origins and Core Claims

Three streams feed into enactivism. Merleau-Ponty's *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) argued that embodiment is not instrumental to consciousness but constitutive of it: the body is the perspective from which the world is perceived, the medium in which skills are acquired, and the "intentional arc" through which the subject is engaged with the world. We perceive things as graspable, walkable, threatening — in terms of the possibilities for bodily action they afford. Heidegger's analysis of the "ready-to-hand" in *Being and Time* (1927) reinforces this: tools in absorbed use withdraw from theoretical attention; the hammer is encountered as a moment of hammering, not as a bare object. This practical, absorbed engagement is more fundamental than the detached theoretical stance that traditional epistemology takes as its model.

Francisco Varela's work on autopoiesis, developed with Humberto Maturana, provided the biological framework. Autopoietic systems are self-producing and self-maintaining: they generate and maintain their own organization through internal processes. Cognition, on this view, is what an autopoietic system does in the course of maintaining itself — not information processing on a pre-given environment, but the process by which the organism enacts its own world while maintaining viability. The environment is not given independently of the organism; it is shaped by the organism's ongoing activity. And ecological psychology — Gibson's affordances, dynamical systems approaches (Thelen, van Gelder) — gave the program empirical backing, grounding enactivist themes in perception research and skill acquisition.

*The Embodied Mind* (1991) synthesized these streams into four core claims: that cognitive processes involve the whole embodied agent embedded in environment (embodiment), that cognition enacts a world rather than recovering a pre-given one (enaction), that cognitive capacities emerge from nonlinear coupling of brain, body, and environment (emergence), and that phenomenological analysis of lived experience is methodologically necessary, not replaceable by third-person description alone (experience).

## Perception as Action: Noe's Version

Alva Noe's *Action in Perception* (2004) develops the specifically perceptual dimension of enactivism. Perceiving is not having mental images or representations in the head; it is a form of practical knowledge — knowing how the sensory array would change as you move, act, or explore. Central to Noe's account is the concept of **sensorimotor contingencies**: the lawful relations between movement and sensory change. When I turn my head, the visual scene shifts in a characteristic way; when I move toward an object, it expands in the visual field; when I trace a surface with my fingers, tactile sensations change with my movement. To perceive an object is to know — implicitly, practically — what would happen to sensory experience if one moved in various ways. This knowledge is not stored in inner representations; it is embodied in sensorimotor skills.

The evidence from **sensory substitution** is striking support. Paul Bach-y-Rita showed that congenitally blind subjects wearing a tactile stimulation device conveying visual information could, with training, begin to experience the information as visual — as if seeing through the device. What determines the character of the experience is not the sensory channel but the sensorimotor skills the subject exercises in exploring with it. If perceptual character is constituted by sensorimotor skill rather than by sensory modality, the inner-representation model of experience is undermined at its roots.

Change blindness points in the same direction. Subjects fail to notice large changes in a visual scene when those changes coincide with a brief interruption. They believe they have a rich, detailed inner representation of the entire scene — but they do not notice that a person's jacket has changed color or that a major object has been swapped. The enactivist interpretation: the richness of perceptual experience is not pre-stored in an inner snapshot but is achieved through active exploration, available-on-demand through sensorimotor engagement. The scene is not inside the head; the perceiver has access to it through skillful activity.

## Varieties

**Autopoietic enactivism** (Varela, Thompson) stays close to the biological roots: all autopoietic systems have a form of cognition appropriate to their organization; human cognition is a particularly complex instance of the more general phenomenon of self-maintaining biological activity, not a special-purpose information-processing engine. **Sensorimotor enactivism** (O'Regan, Noe) focuses on the specific claim that perceptual experience is constituted by implicit knowledge of sensorimotor contingencies. **Radical enactivism** (Hutto and Myin's REC) takes the anti-representationalist commitment to its logical conclusion: even basic perception does not involve content-bearing representational states. Contentless sensorimotor patterns are enough for perception; content-involving representation arises only at the level of linguistically-mediated practice. This is controversial even among enactivists, many of whom accept that perceptual systems have representational states — nonconceptual content — while rejecting the intellectualist, internalist account of those states.

Varela and Thompson also developed **neurophenomenology** as a methodological program: using detailed first-person phenomenological reports, trained through careful introspective practice, to constrain and guide neuroscientific investigation, and using neuroscientific findings to illuminate the physical basis of phenomenological structure. The mutual constraint between first-person and third-person methods is central to the enactivist research program, and it represents a genuine departure from cognitive science as typically practiced.
