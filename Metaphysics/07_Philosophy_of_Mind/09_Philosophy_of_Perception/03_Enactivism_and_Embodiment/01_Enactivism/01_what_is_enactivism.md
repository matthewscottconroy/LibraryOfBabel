# What Is Enactivism?

The word "enactivism" is not self-explanatory. It was coined by Varela, Thompson, and Rosch in *The Embodied Mind* (1991) to capture a specific claim: that organisms do not passively receive a pre-given world but actively enact their worlds through patterns of sensorimotor engagement. The claim is not merely that action is important to perception, or that embodiment matters — though both are true. The claim is that perception and cognition are not inputs followed by internal processing followed by outputs. They are constituted by the ongoing sensorimotor loop itself. The organism's engagement with its environment is not the result of cognition; it is what cognition is.

To understand what this rejects, consider the classical computational theory of mind. Cognition is the manipulation of inner symbolic representations according to formal rules. Perception is the process of converting sensory inputs into symbolic representations of the environment; these representations are processed by central cognition; and motor commands are derived as output. The flow is world → sensory transduction → inner representation → computation → action. On this picture the mind is an inner representational engine, working on inner symbols, in principle disconnected from its body and environment except through input and output ports.

Enactivism denies this at three levels. It denies that the relevant cognitive states are representations; it denies that perception is passive; and it denies that the mind is inside the head. The three denials are connected. If perception is active exploration, then the relevant states are sensorimotor patterns and dynamic couplings, not representations of a scene. And if cognitive processes span brain, body, and environment in a continuous sensorimotor loop, individuating "the mind" as something inside the head is simply the wrong unit of analysis.

## Three Sources

Enactivism draws on three traditions that converged in the late twentieth century. **Phenomenology** supplies the critique of the detached, theoretical model of perception. Merleau-Ponty's *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) argued that the body is the perspective from which the world is perceived, not the instrument of a disembodied mind. We perceive affordances — graspable, walkable, threatening things — not neutral objects to which we subsequently add practical significance. Heidegger's ready-to-hand in *Being and Time* (1927) shows that absorbed, practical engagement with the world is more fundamental than the theoretical, object-directed stance that epistemology typically treats as primary.

**Biological systems theory** supplies the conceptual framework. Maturana and Varela's work on autopoiesis holds that living systems are self-producing, self-maintaining: they generate their own components and maintain their organization through continuous internal processes. Cognition, on this view, is what an autopoietic system does in the course of maintaining itself. The nervous system does not compute an inner model of a pre-given world; it maintains the organism's viability by producing appropriate sensorimotor patterns. The organism and its environment co-define each other: the world, as perceived, is partly constituted by the organism's sensorimotor repertoire.

**Ecological cognitive science** supplies empirical grounding. J. J. Gibson's ecological psychology showed that animals perceive action-relevant properties of the environment — "affordances" — rather than neutral physical properties that must be supplemented by cognitive processing before becoming actionable. The dynamical systems approach to cognition (Thelen, van Gelder) models cognitive processes as trajectories through continuous state spaces rather than symbolic computations, supporting the view that cognition is a continuous dynamical process rather than a sequence of discrete representational states.

## Core Commitments and Varieties

Enactivism is a family of positions, not a single theory. What unifies the family is a set of commitments: that the body is partly constitutive of cognition (not merely causally relevant), that action is the medium in which cognition is realized (not its output), and that the world as perceived is partly constituted by the organism's sensorimotor repertoire (not given independently of the organism).

Within this family there are significant differences. **Sensorimotor enactivism** (O'Regan, Noe) specifies that perceptual experience is constituted by implicit knowledge of sensorimotor contingencies — the lawful relations between movement and sensory change. **Autopoietic enactivism** (Varela, Thompson) stays closer to the biological foundation, treating cognition as the self-maintaining activity of an autopoietic system across the entire animal kingdom. **Radical enactivism** (Hutto and Myin's REC) pushes the anti-representationalist position to its limit: even basic perception involves no content-bearing representational states; contentless sensorimotor patterns are sufficient, and content-involving representation arises only at the level of linguistically-embedded practice.

More broadly, enactivism is typically grouped with embodied, extended (Clark-Chalmers), and embedded cognition as part of the family of "4E cognition" approaches that challenge classical internalism. What these approaches share is the rejection of the Cartesian picture of a disembodied mind working on inner representations — though they differ in how much of the classical picture they wish to retain and how much to replace.

The methodological implication is significant: Varela and Thompson's **neurophenomenology** holds that first-person phenomenological analysis is not supplementary to cognitive science but methodologically necessary. Phenomenology can reveal features of experience that third-person scientific description alone misses, and neuroscience can illuminate the physical basis of phenomenological structures. The mutual constraint between first-person and third-person methods is not a concession to subjectivism; it is a recognition that experience is a datum that cognitive science cannot bypass.
