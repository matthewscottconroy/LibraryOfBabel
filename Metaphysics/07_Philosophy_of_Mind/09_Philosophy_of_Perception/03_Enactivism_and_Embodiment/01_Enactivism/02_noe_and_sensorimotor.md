# Noe and the Sensorimotor Account

Alva Noe's *Action in Perception* (2004) offers the most carefully articulated and empirically connected version of the enactivist approach to perception. The central claim is simple to state but has wide-ranging implications: **to perceive is to exercise sensorimotor skills**. Not to have inner images, not to form representations, not to construct inner models of the visual scene. To perceive is to know — practically, implicitly, not necessarily propositionally — how one's sensory experience would change if one moved, turned, reached, or acted.

When I see a tomato, I perceive it as three-dimensional, as having a back surface I cannot currently see, as having a texture I could feel if I touched it. This is not a matter of beliefs I hold, or of inner models I carry. It is a matter of sensorimotor skills I exercise — an understanding that is written into my body's repertoire of interactions with the world. Noe calls the patterns underlying these skills **sensorimotor contingencies**: the lawful, characteristic relationships between movement and sensory change. Move toward the tomato and it expands in the visual field; tilt your head and the scene rotates in a characteristic way; run a finger across a surface and the tactile pattern changes with the motion of your hand. To perceive is to have mastered these contingencies — to be organized around them in a way that makes them the medium of your engagement with the world.

## Sensory Substitution and the Modality Question

What makes an experience visual rather than tactile or auditory? The standard answer is: the sensory channel — the transducer type, the neural pathway activated. Noe's account gives a different answer: the sensorimotor contingencies exercised in the experience. And Paul Bach-y-Rita's sensory substitution experiments provide striking empirical support for this.

Blind subjects wearing a device that converts camera images into patterns of tactile stimulation on the skin report, initially, only tactile sensations. With practice — active exploration, moving the camera to explore the environment — subjects begin to experience the information as visual. They report "seeing" shapes, distances, and movement. What has changed is not the sensory channel — tactile stimulation is still the proximate input — but the sensorimotor skills the subjects exercise in using the device. They have learned visual sensorimotor contingencies: how the pattern on the skin changes with camera movement. And learning those contingencies transforms tactile sensation into visual perception.

This is difficult to explain on the representationalist picture, which ties visual experience to the activation of visual representational systems in visual cortex. On Noe's account it is natural: what makes experience visual is not the hardware but the sensorimotor knowledge, and sensorimotor knowledge is learnable through any channel.

## Change Blindness and the Richness of Experience

The change blindness experiments of O'Regan and Simons provide the second major empirical pillar. In these experiments, subjects fail to notice large, dramatic changes in a visual scene — a change in the color of someone's jacket, the disappearance of a major object — when the changes coincide with a brief visual interruption. Subjects typically believe they have a rich, detailed inner representation of the entire scene. But they do not notice what would be obvious if they did.

The enactivist interpretation: there is no complete inner snapshot. The richness of perceptual experience is not pre-stored in an inner model; it is available on demand through skilled sensorimotor engagement with the scene. Noe's formulation is precise: perceptual experience is not like a photograph but like a skill. Having the skill of playing the piano does not mean having all the music pre-stored; it means being able to produce the music through skilled activity. Having the experience of a rich visual scene does not mean having a complete inner model; it means having practical access to the scene through active exploration. The richness is in the skilled relationship, not in any inner store.

## The Brain in a Vat Consequence

The sensorimotor account has a striking implication that Noe explicitly accepts. Could a brain in a vat, connected to a simulation providing all the normal sensory inputs, have genuine perception? On representationalist views, yes — what matters is the representational state. On Noe's account, no. The brain in a vat lacks a body and therefore cannot exercise sensorimotor skills; it receives sensory inputs that are not structured by any ongoing sensorimotor engagement. The inputs may match those of a normally embodied perceiver, but they arrive without the sensorimotor scaffolding that makes them experiences of objects.

Critics find this counterintuitive: if the brain receives exactly the same information, why deny it perception? Noe's response is that the inputs are not the same — a normally embodied perceiver's sensory array is structured by active exploration, while the brain in a vat receives inputs that happen to match this array without any underlying sensorimotor structure. The difference is constitutive.

## Phenomenal Character

The most persistent objection concerns phenomenal character. Sensorimotor skill describes patterns of behavioral competency. Why is the exercise of those competencies accompanied by subjective experience? A blind person with perfect sensorimotor knowledge of how visual stimulation would vary with movement would not thereby have visual experience. A well-designed robot with the right sensorimotor loops might have all the relevant competencies without any phenomenal character at all.

Noe acknowledges the challenge and argues that phenomenal character is not something added to sensorimotor engagement but partly constituted by it. The lived exercise of sensorimotor skills in a genuine perceptual situation is the experience; the demand for a further explanation of why the exercise is phenomenally conscious seems to presuppose the very internalist picture of experience that enactivism rejects. Critics find this response unsatisfying. The hard problem remains: why there is something it is like to exercise sensorimotor skills, rather than nothing. In *Strange Tools* (2015), Noe extends his framework to art as "reorganizing practice" — artworks disrupt habitual sensorimotor engagements and open new possibilities of experience. This extension suggests that the sensorimotor account is for Noe the basis of a broader theory of human experience and its transformation, but it does not close the explanatory gap between sensorimotor facts and phenomenal consciousness.
