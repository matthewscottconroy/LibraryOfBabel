# Objections to Enactivism

Enactivism makes bold claims. The mind is not inside the head; cognition is not the manipulation of inner representations; perception is constituted by sensorimotor skill rather than inner models. These claims are philosophically attractive as correctives to classical internalism, but they generate equally sharp objections. The major lines of criticism concern phenomenal character, mental representation, the architecture of cognitive science, and the scale of the framework's application. In each case there is a genuine enactivist reply, and in each case critics have found the reply insufficient.

## The Phenomenal Character Objection

Consider a hypothetical robot with perfectly organized sensorimotor loops — it exercises all the right sensorimotor contingencies when navigating its environment, correctly anticipating how its sensory array will change with every movement. There is no reason to think the robot has any phenomenal experience. Yet on the sensorimotor account, its perceptual competency would be indistinguishable from genuine perception. Or consider a congenitally blind person with perfect theoretical knowledge of visual sensorimotor contingencies — she knows exactly how the visual field expands when one moves toward an object — but who gains no visual experience from this knowledge alone. Sensorimotor skill and phenomenal character seem to come apart.

This is the hard problem applied directly to enactivism: even a complete enactivist story about sensorimotor engagement leaves unexplained why there is something it is like to engage in this way. Noe and Thompson respond that the objection assumes phenomenal character can be separated from sensorimotor engagement — that the engagement could be present without the experience — and they challenge this assumption. The lived exercise of sensorimotor skills in a genuine perceptual situation just is the experience; the demand for a further explanation presupposes the very internalist picture enactivism rejects. Thompson's neurophenomenological program suggests that treating experience as a self-enclosed inner domain disconnected from physical structure is itself the source of the hard problem; dissolving that picture may dissolve the problem.

Critics find these responses unconvincing. Challenging the separability assumption does not explain the phenomenon. The intuition that sensorimotor engagement could occur without phenomenal character — that a sufficiently sophisticated but non-conscious system could implement the same sensorimotor loops — seems to survive the enactivist response. Whether this intuition is reliable is exactly what the debate is about, but enactivists need more than the assertion that the internalist framework is presupposed.

## The Representation Objection

Contemporary cognitive neuroscience is rich in evidence for internal representations: retinotopic maps in early visual cortex, face-selective areas in the fusiform gyrus, object-recognition hierarchies, place cells and grid cells in the hippocampus functioning as a cognitive map, predictive coding mechanisms generating detailed predictions about incoming sensory signals. These structures are not merely sensorimotor patterns; they are representations in a robust sense — states that carry information about the world in a format that can be stored, retrieved, and operated upon offline.

The enactivist response has two variants. The moderate version (Noe, Thompson) accepts that brain states have representational properties but denies that cognition is adequately characterized as the rule-governed manipulation of classical symbolic representations. The neural states that cognitive neuroscience calls "representations" are often states defined by their role in ongoing sensorimotor coupling: place cells are active as a rat navigates an environment, not as it consults an inner map before moving. The radical version (Hutto, Myin) denies that basic perceptual and sensorimotor states have representational content at all.

Critics find both responses strained. The evidence for rich predictive models, stored object representations, and offline cognition — imagination, memory, planning — is substantial and is not easily reinterpreted as sensorimotor coupling. When we imagine a scene without any current sensorimotor engagement, something that functions as a representation seems to be active. The moderate version's distinction between "sensorimotor representation" and "classical symbolic representation" may prove too thin to do the required theoretical work.

## Predictive Processing and the Internalist Challenge

Predictive processing (PP) accounts (Clark, Friston) have emerged as the most empirically detailed framework for understanding perceptual processing. On the PP account, the brain maintains a hierarchical generative model that predicts sensory inputs; prediction error signals propagate upward, updating the model; the brain is fundamentally a prediction machine constructing world models to minimize prediction error. This is a richly internalist picture: the organism's perception is generated by its internal model, not by direct sensorimotor engagement with the world.

Andy Clark has argued that PP and enactivism are compatible — the generative model drives action (active inference) as well as perception, and sensorimotor contingencies are part of what the model predicts. The enactivist can read PP as describing the mechanistic basis of the sensorimotor engagement that constitutes perception, not a competing account. But this integration is unstable: if the generative model drives perception and action, it is the explanatorily fundamental entity, and sensorimotor engagement is its product rather than the constitutive basis of experience. Enactivism's claim that skillful action is primary, not the model, sits uneasily with PP's prioritization of the predictive hierarchy.

## The Scale Objection

Enactivism's most compelling examples are at the level of basic sensorimotor perception: tracking a moving object, exploring a surface, navigating a space. These are cases where sensorimotor engagement and experience seem tightly coupled. But human cognition includes abstract thought, mathematical reasoning, episodic memory, language, and planning — processes that seem to proceed offline, in the absence of any current sensorimotor engagement with the environment. When a mathematician reasons about abstract objects, or when a person retrieves an episodic memory from years ago, there is no obvious sensorimotor loop at work.

Noe's response in *Strange Tools* (2015) points to the linguistic and notational scaffolding of abstract thought: mathematical reasoning proceeds through skilled manipulation of notation, which is a genuinely sensorimotor, environmental engagement. But critics note that the cognitive work seems to be done by something over and above the manipulation of notation — by whatever processes generate correct mathematical inferences from the notation. The notation is a tool; what uses the tool is what requires explanation. Thompson allows that higher cognition involves internal simulation grounded in and structured by prior embodied experience, but this begins to look like a qualified acceptance of mental representation. Hutto's radical enactivism simply accepts that language-mediated cognition is a different case — enactivism applies to basic perception, not all of cognition — which raises the question of whether enactivism is a theory of cognition at all, or only a theory of a specific class of perceptual engagements.

The productive consequence of these objections is that the most interesting current work in enactivism involves precisely these challenges: the relationship between predictive processing and sensorimotor engagement, the nature of offline cognition, and the scope of the anti-representationalist program. Where enactivism has been most successful is in challenging snapshot-based pictures of perceptual experience and directing attention to the constitutive role of active exploration. Whether it can do more than this remains open.
