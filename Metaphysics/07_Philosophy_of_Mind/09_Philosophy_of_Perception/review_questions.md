# Review Questions — Philosophy of Perception

1. Naive realism holds that perceptual experience is constituted by the perceived objects themselves — objects are not merely causes of inner states but constituents of the experience. The classical challenge is from hallucination: a hallucinating subject has an experience phenomenally indistinguishable from veridical perception, yet there is no external object present. Why is this a problem specifically for the naive realist rather than for all theories of perception, and how serious is it?

2. Disjunctivism responds to the argument from hallucination by denying the common factor assumption — that veridical perception and hallucination share a positive phenomenal element. Martin argues that indistinguishability is an epistemic relation that does not require a shared positive phenomenal component. Explain the distinction between a positive explanation of indistinguishability (there is a common phenomenal factor) and a negative explanation (there is nothing that marks the difference from the inside). Does the negative explanation carry explanatory weight, or does it simply restate the problem?

3. Sense data theories posit mental particulars as the direct objects of perception — we see a sense datum of a tomato, not the tomato itself. The main advantages are explanatory (sense data are always as they appear, so illusion is not a problem) and the main disadvantage is that they introduce an extra ontological layer. Is the sense data theorist in a better or worse position than the naive realist with respect to the hard problem of consciousness?

4. McDowell argues that perceptual experience must be conceptually structured for it to stand in rational relations to belief — nonconceptual content could only causally nudge belief, not provide reasons for it. Evans responds that perceptual content is more fine-grained than any conceptual repertoire and represents this fine-grainedness through analog formats. Which view better accounts for (a) the richness of perceptual experience, and (b) the epistemic role perception plays in justifying belief?

5. The transparency of experience — when we introspect, attention passes through the experience to the world, rather than encountering a mental intermediary — is used to support both naive realism and representationalism. How can both theories appeal to the same phenomenological datum? What interpretation does each give of transparency, and which is more plausible?

6. Noe's sensorimotor account holds that to perceive is to exercise sensorimotor skills — to have practical mastery of how sensory experience would change with movement. The most persistent objection is that this explains sensorimotor competence without explaining phenomenal character: a blind person with perfect sensorimotor knowledge of visual contingencies would not thereby have visual experience. How does Noe respond, and is the response adequate given the hard problem of consciousness?

7. Enactivism and the extended mind thesis (Chapter 10) both challenge the internalist picture of perception and cognition. How do they relate to each other? Does adopting the sensorimotor account of perception support or require the extended mind thesis, or can they be held independently?

8. The debate between conceptualism (McDowell) and nonconceptualism (Evans, Peacocke) has implications far beyond philosophy of perception. If perceptual content is nonconceptual, this bears on (a) how cognitive development works in infants and animals, (b) whether the justificatory chain from world to belief bottoms out in something outside the space of reasons, and (c) whether the hard problem of consciousness is tractable. Trace at least two of these connections.
