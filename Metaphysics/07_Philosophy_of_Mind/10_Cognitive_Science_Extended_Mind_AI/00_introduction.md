# Cognitive Science, Extended Mind, and Artificial Intelligence

The last several decades have seen the philosophy of mind pulled simultaneously in three directions by the rapid development of cognitive science, the extended mind hypothesis, and artificial intelligence. Each development raises questions that are not merely empirical but deeply philosophical, and each requires the conceptual resources developed in the preceding chapters to be properly understood.

Cognitive science — the interdisciplinary study of mind that combines psychology, neuroscience, linguistics, and computer science — has produced a picture of mental processing that is both richer and stranger than folk psychology imagines. Computationalism holds that cognition is computation: mental states are computational states, and mental processes are computational processes. This framework has been enormously productive, generating theories of perception, memory, language processing, and decision-making. But it faces the same challenges as functionalism in general: does computation capture what is essential to mind, or does it leave out phenomenal consciousness?

The extended mind hypothesis, developed by Clark and Chalmers, challenges the assumption that the mind is bounded by the skull. If cognitive processes can extend beyond the brain — into notebooks, smartphones, and the environment — then the unit of cognitive analysis is not the individual brain but the agent-environment system. Otto's notebook functions cognitively just like Louise's hippocampus: both store information that is available for recall and action. If so, Otto's cognitive states extend into the notebook. This proposal has implications for the metaphysics of the self, the ethics of cognitive enhancement, and the philosophy of agency.

Artificial intelligence raises perhaps the most consequential questions. If a system processes information with sufficient sophistication, does it think? Does it understand? The Turing test — can the system's outputs be distinguished from a human's by a human interlocutor? — provides one operational criterion, but Searle's Chinese Room argument challenges whether operational competence implies genuine understanding. As AI systems become more capable, these questions move from philosophical thought experiments to urgent practical concerns: what moral status, if any, should a sufficiently sophisticated AI have? The chapter examines these questions with the seriousness they deserve.
