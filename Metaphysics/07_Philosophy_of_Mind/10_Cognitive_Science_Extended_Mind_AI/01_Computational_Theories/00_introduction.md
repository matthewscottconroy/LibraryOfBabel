# Computational Theories of Mind

The analogy between the mind and a computer is so pervasive in contemporary cognitive science that it has come to seem natural. But it is a specific, substantive theoretical commitment with philosophical roots and philosophical consequences. Computational theories of mind hold that cognitive processes are, at some level of description, computations — transformations of representations according to rules. Thinking is a form of symbol manipulation; believing is having a certain symbolic structure in one's cognitive system; reasoning is the manipulation of those structures according to inferential rules.

The founding document of computational cognitive science is Alan Turing's analysis of computation in terms of abstract machines (Turing machines) and his proposal that human intelligence can in principle be replicated by computation. Fodor's "Language of Thought" hypothesis — the hypothesis that thinking occurs in a mental language (Mentalese) with its own syntax and semantics — is the most developed philosophical expression of the computational view. Beliefs and desires are sentences in Mentalese; cognitive processes are syntactic operations on those sentences that are also sensitive to their semantic properties.

The computational picture has generated tremendous productivity in cognitive science: models of visual processing, language acquisition, memory, and problem-solving have been built on computational foundations and have made genuine progress. But the philosophical objections are serious. Searle's Chinese Room argument (examined in the functionalism section) challenges the claim that computation is sufficient for understanding. The frame problem challenges the claim that computation can replicate the contextual, flexible, common-sense reasoning that human cognition exhibits.

Classical computationalism — the view that cognition involves the sequential manipulation of explicit symbolic representations — has been challenged by connectionism: the view that cognition is best modeled by artificial neural networks that process information in parallel, without explicit symbols or rules. The cognition-computation relationship takes a different form in connectionist models, and the philosophical implications have been extensively debated.

This section examines classical and connectionist computational theories of mind, their theoretical commitments, and the challenges they face from both philosophy and cognitive science.
