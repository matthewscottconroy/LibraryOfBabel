# Classical Computational Theory of Mind

The idea that the mind is a kind of computer was not proposed as a metaphor. When Turing formalized the notion of computation in the 1930s, and when Newell and Simon demonstrated that symbol-manipulating programs could prove theorems and solve logic puzzles in the 1950s, it became possible to propose a literal identity: mental processes are computations, in exactly the same sense that a digital computer's operations are computations. The Classical Computational Theory of Mind (CCTM) takes this proposal seriously. The mind is a physical symbol system — a device that encodes, combines, transforms, and operates on discrete symbolic representations according to formal rules. Thinking is computing.

The core of CCTM rests on three commitments. First, **mental representations**: there exist internal symbolic states with both syntactic structure (formal, shape-like properties) and semantic content (they represent features of the world). Second, **computational processes**: mental operations are sequences of transformations on these symbols defined entirely by their syntactic properties — the semantic properties are not directly accessed by the computational mechanism. Third, **semantic tracking**: the syntactic operations are designed to mirror semantic relations, so that valid syntactic inference corresponds to truth-preserving semantic inference. This last point is crucial: it explains how the brain can be rational without requiring any spooky non-mechanical capacity. Rationality is mechanical if the right formal rules are in place.

## Fodor's Development

Jerry Fodor was the most systematic philosophical defender of CCTM, and his contributions shaped the field for decades. The **Language of Thought** (*The Language of Thought*, 1975) holds that thought is conducted in a structured, language-like system of mental representations — "Mentalese." Complex thoughts are built from simpler ones by combinatorial rules. This explains the productivity of thought: from finitely many primitive concepts, we can form indefinitely many distinct thoughts, just as a formal language generates infinitely many sentences from a finite vocabulary and grammar. It also explains the **systematicity** of thought: the ability to think "John loves Mary" is necessarily connected to the ability to think "Mary loves John," because these thoughts share constituent representations combined by the same structural rules. On an unstructured, atomistic picture of mental states, this connection would be inexplicable.

Fodor's **Modularity of Mind** (1983) introduced a structural distinction within CCTM that has proven enormously influential. Perceptual input systems are domain-specific, mandatory, fast, and informationally encapsulated: the visual module processes visual information without access to general background beliefs, which is why perceptual illusions persist even when we know they are illusions. Central cognitive processes — belief fixation, inference to the best explanation — are not modular: they are Quinean (sensitive to global coherence of the belief system) and isotropic (potentially sensitive to any available information). This asymmetry explains why AI has succeeded in building expert perceptual systems while failing to replicate flexible human central cognition. Building a modular input system is tractable; building an isotropic central process is not.

Newell and Simon's **Physical Symbol System Hypothesis** (1976) states the boldest version of CCTM: a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action. Any system capable of manipulating discrete symbols according to formal rules can, in principle, produce any behavior that counts as intelligent. This is the theoretical license for AI research: building intelligence is building the right symbol manipulations.

## Challenges

The frame problem cuts at CCTM's ability to model flexible updating. To act intelligently in a changing world, a system must update its representations appropriately in response to events. Specifying which representations are affected by any given change requires, in the classical framework, explicit enumeration of consequences — a computationally intractable task. Natural cognition handles this effortlessly; classical symbol manipulation has not.

The connectionist challenge showed that many cognitive capacities can be modeled by distributed, parallel processing across networks of simple units without discrete symbol manipulation. Connectionist models (*Parallel Distributed Processing*, Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986) exhibit graceful degradation, learning from examples, and performance patterns that more closely match human data than classical systems in perceptual and language tasks. Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988) argued that connectionist models could not account for systematicity without implicitly implementing a language of thought at some level — the implementationalist response — but this generated sustained debate rather than resolution.

Searle's Chinese Room argument (1980) presses the sufficiency direction: a system manipulating symbols according to formal rules may produce correct outputs without any genuine understanding of what the symbols mean. Syntax is not sufficient for semantics; computation is not sufficient for cognition. Strong representationalists dispute this, but the argument has remained one of the most discussed challenges to the claim that symbol manipulation is all there is to thought.

The emergence of large language models — transformer-based networks trained on text at scale — has reopened the debate. These systems achieve remarkable linguistic competence through non-classical, distributed statistical learning, without anything resembling Fodorean LOT. Whether this shows that CCTM is wrong, or shows that LOT can be implemented in non-classical architectures, or shows that these systems lack genuine cognition, is one of the central live questions in philosophy of cognitive science.
