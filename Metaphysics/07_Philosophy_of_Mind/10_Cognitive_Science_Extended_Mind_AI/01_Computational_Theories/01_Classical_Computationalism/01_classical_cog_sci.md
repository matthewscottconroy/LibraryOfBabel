# Classical Computationalism and Cognitive Science

Something remarkable happened in the late 1950s. Newell and Simon demonstrated that a computer program could prove theorems from Whitehead and Russell's *Principia Mathematica*, solve logic problems, and play chess — tasks that had seemed to require the distinctively human capacity for reasoning. Chomsky proposed that linguistic competence could be characterized by a system of formal rules — transformational grammars — operating on symbolic representations of sentences. And cognitive psychology, breaking from behaviorism, began to posit internal representations as the explanatory medium for mental processes. These convergences crystallized into a research program: cognition is the computational manipulation of symbolic representations, and the science of cognition is the science of the right computational architecture.

The **Computational-Representational Theory of Mind** (CRTM) gives this program its philosophical spine. Mental states are relations to internal symbolic structures with syntactic form and semantic content. To believe it is raining is to stand in a particular computational relation to a mental representation with the content "it is raining." Mental processes — inference, perception, problem-solving — are formally defined operations on these structures, operations that are sensitive to their syntactic properties but not directly to their semantic content. Crucially, the syntactic operations are designed to track semantic properties: valid inferences correspond to truth-preserving syntactic operations. This explains how the brain can be rational: rationality is not a mysterious extra-physical capacity but a consequence of the right formal organization.

## Fodor and Marr

Fodor's framework dominates classical computationalism philosophically. The **Language of Thought** (*The Language of Thought*, 1975) holds that thought is conducted in a compositionally structured mental language — "Mentalese" — in which complex thoughts are built from simpler constituents by combinatorial rules. This explains productivity (finitely many concepts generate infinitely many thoughts) and systematicity (the ability to think "John loves Mary" is necessarily connected to the ability to think "Mary loves John," because these thoughts share constituent representations governed by the same structural rules).

Fodor's **modularity thesis** (*The Modularity of Mind*, 1983) divides cognitive architecture into three levels. Transducers convert physical signals to neural representations. **Input systems** — perceptual modules — process transduced signals into representations available for central cognition; they are domain-specific, mandatory, fast, and informationally encapsulated, unable to access general background knowledge. **Central processes** — belief fixation, inference — are not modular: they are isotropic (potentially sensitive to any available information) and Quinean (sensitive to the global coherence of the belief system). The asymmetry between modular input systems and holistic central processes explains why AI has succeeded at building expert perceptual systems while failing to replicate flexible human central cognition. Modularity makes the former tractable; the frame problem makes the latter intractable.

David Marr's *Vision* (1982) provided the most rigorous implementation of the classical program for a specific capacity. Marr's three-level framework became standard methodology for analyzing cognitive systems: the **computational level** specifies what computation the system performs (the problem and its mathematical rationale); the **algorithmic level** specifies the representations and procedures used; the **implementation level** specifies the physical realization. Marr showed that vision, understood at the computational level, is the recovery of a description of the world from images — a task with a principled mathematical solution because images carry systematic information about geometry and surface structure. The algorithms he described — computing the primal sketch, the 2.5D sketch, the 3D model — became the template for computational cognitive science.

Newell and Simon's **Physical Symbol System Hypothesis** (PSSH) (1976) states the program's boldest claim: a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action. Building intelligence is building the right symbol manipulation. This drove AI research for decades.

## The Progressive Challenges

Classical computationalism faced sustained challenge from the late 1980s onward. **Connectionist** models (*Parallel Distributed Processing*, Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986) showed that many cognitive capacities could be modeled by distributed processing without discrete symbol manipulation. Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988) responded that connectionist models could not account for the systematicity of thought without implementing a language of thought at some level — but this response generated as much debate as it resolved. **Embodied and enactive** cognitive science (Varela, Thompson, Rosch; Clark; Noe) argued that classical computationalism fundamentally misrepresents cognition by abstracting away from the body and the environment: cognition is not disembodied symbol manipulation but is constituted by sensorimotor engagement with the world.

**Predictive processing** (Friston, Clark) represents the most sophisticated internal development of the computational approach. The brain is reconceived as a hierarchical prediction machine that constantly generates and updates probabilistic generative models of sensory causes. This retains the computational framework while replacing classical rule-governed symbol manipulation with continuous Bayesian updating. The success of transformer-based large language models has further complicated the landscape: these systems achieve remarkable linguistic performance through non-classical distributed learning, without anything resembling LOT, challenging Fodor's claim that compositional structure is necessary for language.

Contemporary cognitive science has not abandoned the computational framework, but it has been significantly pluralized. Symbolic computation plays an important role in higher cognition — language, logical reasoning, explicit planning — while other processes, particularly perceptual and sensorimotor, require non-classical models. Whether a unified computational account can accommodate both levels, or whether human cognition is irreducibly heterogeneous, is the central ongoing question in the field.
