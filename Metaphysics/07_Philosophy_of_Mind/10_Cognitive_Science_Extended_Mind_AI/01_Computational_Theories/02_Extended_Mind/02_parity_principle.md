# The Parity Principle

The parity principle states: "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process." The principle is the methodological core of the extended mind thesis, and its force depends entirely on what grounds cognitive attribution in the first place.

The argument has a straightforward structure. A process is cognitive in virtue of playing functional role R. An external process X plays functional role R in the same way as the corresponding internal process. Therefore X is cognitive. The argument presupposes functionalism: if mental states were defined by their intrinsic physical properties rather than their causal-functional roles, functional parity would not imply cognitive parity. The skull boundary would matter because the physical substrate matters. But if functional role is what individuates cognitive states, then a functional state realized in a notebook is a cognitive state, just as a functional state realized in silicon would be.

Consider the applications. Otto's notebook entries play the same functional role as Inga's neural memory traces — storing information, making it accessible on demand, guiding behavior. By the parity principle, they are memory states. If a person calculates in their head (cognitive), and another uses a calculator with the same functional effect, the calculator use is equally cognitive. Navigation using GPS plays the same functional role as internal spatial reasoning; by parity, it constitutes cognitive spatial reasoning. Writing as a cognitive scaffold — using notes to organize and extend thinking — is, by parity, part of the thinking process rather than merely a record of it.

## The Integration Conditions and the Bloat Problem

Not every external resource qualifies. Clark and Chalmers specify three conditions for genuine cognitive extension: reliable availability (the resource is there when needed); automatic endorsement (information from it is accepted without critical evaluation, as biological memory is); easy accessibility (it can be retrieved without effortful search). Otto's notebook meets these conditions; a library shelf does not.

The bloat problem challenges whether these conditions are sufficient to contain the extension. If cognitive processes extend into the environment whenever they play the right functional role, there seems to be no principled limit: the notebook extends to include the house, which extends to include the city's information infrastructure. Clark's response is that the integration conditions do the limiting work — the house does not meet them; the notebook does. Critics press that the conditions are too vague to do this work reliably. "Reliable availability," "automatic endorsement," and "seamless integration" admit borderline cases, and smartphones fail some conditions on some occasions. The conditions need sharpening if they are to constitute a principled mark of genuine cognitive extension rather than an ad hoc restriction.

## The Derived Intentionality Objection

Adams and Aizawa's most fundamental challenge: cognitive states have intrinsic intentionality — their content is fixed by causal history and biological function, independently of any interpretive stance. Notebook entries have only derived intentionality — they mean what they mean because we interpret them that way. This categorical difference disqualifies external resources from counting as genuine cognitive states.

Clark's response questions whether the intrinsic/derived distinction is as sharp as Adams and Aizawa require. Whether neural representations have truly intrinsic intentionality — content not dependent on any interpretive framework — is precisely what is contested in debates about naturalizing intentionality (Fodor, Dretske, Millikan). If teleosemantic intentionality, which derives content from biological function and selection history, counts as genuine intentionality, then the derived/intrinsic distinction may not track the internal/external boundary cleanly. A state whose content is fixed by its functional role in a biological system is, in the relevant sense, no more "intrinsically" meaningful than a state whose content is fixed by its functional role in an extended system. The objection assumes a specific view about what grounds intentionality — a view that is itself disputed.

## Parity and Functionalism

The parity principle stands or falls with functionalism about cognition, and two challenges to functionalism bear directly on it. If phenomenal consciousness is not constitutively determined by functional role — if zombies are metaphysically possible — then functional parity does not imply parity of conscious experience. Extended mind theorists typically concede this: cognitive extension does not imply the extension of phenomenal consciousness. The extended mind is a functionally extended cognitive system, not an extended locus of phenomenal experience. Whether this concession is a minor qualification or a fundamental limitation depends on how central phenomenal consciousness is to one's conception of mind.

Searle's biological naturalism presses the second challenge: cognitive states require specific causal powers found only in biological systems with appropriate neural structure. Computational or functional organization is not sufficient. Clark responds that biological naturalism is ad hoc without independent support for the claim that biology has special causal powers not reproducible by functional systems. But this leaves the question open: the debate between extended mind theory and biological naturalism is ultimately a debate about what grounds genuine cognitive states, and the parity principle cannot resolve it without an independent account of what makes something cognitive in the first place.
