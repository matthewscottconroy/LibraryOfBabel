# 4E Cognition: Embodied, Embedded, Enacted, Extended

Classical cognitive science treats cognition as a three-stage process: perception provides input, central processing computes on internal representations, action is the output. This "sandwich model" — action and perception as the bread, cognition as the filling — treats cognitive processing as fundamentally an internal affair, sealed off from the environment except through input and output ports. The environment provides the occasions for cognition; the cognition itself happens inside.

The 4E approaches — **E**mbodied, **E**mbedded, **E**nacted, **E**xtended — reject this model at every stage. Perception is not passive input but active, skilled exploration. Central processing is not isolated from action and environment but constitutively shaped by them. Action is not mere output but part of the cognitive process itself. The clean sandwich model misrepresents the continuous, reciprocal loop of brain, body, and environment that characterizes cognitive activity. What unifies the four approaches is not a single positive theory but a shared rejection of the classical picture and a shared insistence that cognitive science cannot understand cognition by studying the brain in isolation.

## The Four Es

**Embodied cognition** holds that cognitive processes are constitutively shaped by the kind of body the organism has — its perceptual organs, motor capacities, sensorimotor loops, and developmental trajectory. Lakoff and Johnson's work on conceptual metaphor (*Metaphors We Live By*, 1980; *Philosophy in the Flesh*, 1999) provides the systematic account: abstract concepts are structured by bodily experience through conceptual metaphor. "Argument" is structured by bodily experience of combat; "understanding" by the bodily experience of grasping; "quantity" by spatial verticality. These are not merely linguistic figures; they provide the inferential structure that abstract reasoning inherits from bodily experience. Barsalou's perceptual symbol systems (*Perceptual Symbol Systems*, 1999) give this a cognitive neuroscience basis: concepts are grounded in sensorimotor representations — partial simulations of perception and action — not in amodal arbitrary symbols. Evidence that reading action words activates motor cortex, that imagining grasping activates hand areas, and that spatial reasoning activates visual areas supports the thesis that central cognition is not isolated from sensorimotor systems.

**Embedded cognition** holds that cognitive processes are constitutively shaped by the environment in which they occur. Agents exploit stable environmental structures to simplify and offload cognitive tasks — what Hutchins's analysis of ship navigation (*Cognition in the Wild*, 1995) showed: navigation is accomplished not by individual cognition but by a distributed cognitive system encompassing crew, charts, instruments, and procedures. Kirsh and Maglio's epistemic actions — physical manipulations of the environment that simplify cognitive tasks, like rearranging Tetris pieces rather than mentally rotating them — show that manipulating the world is part of the cognitive process, not merely a precursor to it. Humans construct cognitive niches — writing, calendars, filing systems, organized workspaces — that shape what cognition is possible. The environment is not just a context for cognition; it is constitutive of it.

**Enacted cognition** — enactivism — holds that cognition is constituted by the sensorimotor engagement of an organism with its world, not by the internal manipulation of representations. Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's *The Embodied Mind* (1991) argues that organisms enact their worlds through ongoing sensorimotor activity. The world is not pre-given, waiting to be represented; it is shaped by the organism's activity. Noe's sensorimotor contingencies account (*Action in Perception*, 2004) specifies the mechanism: to perceive is to exercise practical knowledge of how sensory stimulation changes with movement, not to construct an inner model. The richness of experience is not pre-stored in inner representations but achieved through active exploration.

**Extended cognition** — Clark and Chalmers's thesis — holds that cognitive states and processes can extend beyond the brain and body to include tools, devices, and environmental resources. The parity principle: if an external process plays the same functional role as an internal cognitive process, it is cognitive. Otto's notebook functions like biological memory; it is part of his memory system. The mind can extend into notebooks, smartphones, and other information technologies to the extent that these satisfy conditions of reliable availability, automatic endorsement, and seamless integration.

## Tensions Within 4E

The four approaches are logically independent and sometimes in tension. Enactivists emphasize dynamic sensorimotor coupling and are skeptical of representational vocabulary even for external states; extended mind theorists accept a functionalist conception of mental representation that can be distributed into external resources — these pictures are not obviously compatible. Embodied cognition locates the cognitive system in the brain-body complex; extended cognition expands this to include the environment, raising the question of whether the body has privileged constitutive status that external notebooks do not.

The deepest division concerns representation. Radical enactivists (Hutto, Myin) reject mental representation entirely, even for basic perception; Clark accepts functional representation that can be realized externally; Barsalou accepts perceptual symbol systems grounded in sensorimotor processes. This disagreement about the nature and existence of mental representations cuts across the 4E family.

What the 4E approaches collectively accomplish, regardless of these tensions, is to expose the assumptions of classical cognitive science and show that many cognitive achievements require and involve more than internal computation. Whether they constitute a unified alternative paradigm or a family of distinct proposals that challenge the classical view from different directions remains an open question.
