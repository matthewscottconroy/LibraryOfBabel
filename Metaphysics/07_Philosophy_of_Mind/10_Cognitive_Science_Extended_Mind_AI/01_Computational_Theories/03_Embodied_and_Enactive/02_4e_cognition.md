# 4E Cognition

The four E approaches — **E**mbodied, **E**mbedded, **E**nacted, **E**xtended — are often treated as a unified family defined by opposition to classical cognitive science. But this framing obscures as much as it reveals. The four approaches are logically independent; they challenge the classical picture from different angles; and they are sometimes in tension with one another. Understanding 4E cognition requires understanding both what the approaches share and where they diverge.

What they share is a rejection of the sandwich model: the view that cognition is a three-stage process in which perception provides input, central processing computes on internal representations, and action is the output. The sandwich model treats cognitive processing as fundamentally an internal affair. The 4E approaches challenge this by showing, in different ways, that cognition is constitutively shaped by body, environment, and action — not merely contingently supported by them.

## The Four Approaches

**Embodied cognition** argues that cognitive structure is constitutively shaped by the body's form and dynamics. Abstract thought is grounded in bodily experience through conceptual metaphor (Lakoff and Johnson); perceptual categories are organized around affordances for embodied action (Gibson); social cognition is mediated by embodied simulation (mirror neuron research, Rizzolatti and Gallese). The body is not the delivery system for cognitive inputs and outputs; it is part of what cognition is.

**Embedded cognition** holds that cognitive processes are adapted to and partly constituted by features of the local environment. Kirsh and Maglio's epistemic actions (1994) show that people perform physical manipulations on the environment — rotating Tetris pieces on screen rather than mentally, rearranging physical objects to simplify pattern recognition — because the physical action is faster and cognitively cheaper than the internal computation it replaces. The cognitive process exploits the environment. Hutchins's analysis of distributed cognition (*Cognition in the Wild*, 1995) shows that complex cognitive achievements — ship navigation, aircraft piloting — are performed by distributed cognitive systems: human agents together with tools, instruments, records, and procedures. The cognitive system is not any individual agent but the organized whole. Humans construct cognitive niches — written language, numerical notation, organized workspaces — that shape what kinds of thinking are possible, building the environment into the cognitive process.

**Enacted cognition** — enactivism — holds that cognition is constituted by the sensorimotor engagement of an organism with its world, not by internal processing of representations. Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's *The Embodied Mind* (1991) argues that organisms enact their worlds through ongoing sensorimotor activity rather than representing a pre-given world. Noe's sensorimotor contingencies account (*Action in Perception*, 2004) specifies the mechanism: to perceive is to exercise knowledge of how sensory stimulation changes with movement — skilled, active engagement rather than passive reception and internal modeling.

**Extended cognition** — Clark and Chalmers's thesis — holds that cognitive states and processes can extend beyond the brain and body to include tools and environmental resources. The parity principle: if a process plays the same functional role outside the head as it would inside, it is cognitive regardless of location. Under conditions of reliable availability, automatic endorsement, and seamless integration, external resources — notebooks, smartphones, maps — can be genuine components of the cognitive system, not merely tools it uses.

## Tensions and Divergences

Extended and enacted approaches are in tension. Enactivists emphasize dynamic sensorimotor coupling — a looping, active process constituted by ongoing engagement. Extended mind theorists emphasize the distribution of functional cognitive states into external stores. These are different pictures: enactivism focuses on process, extended mind on state. Enactivists are typically skeptical of representationalism; the extended mind thesis presupposes that there are representational states that can be located externally. Whether external information storage is the kind of extension that genuinely fits the enactivist framework is unclear.

Embedded and extended approaches differ in kind, not degree. Embedded cognition holds that cognitive processes exploit environmental structure — the environment supports cognition from outside. Extended cognition holds that cognitive processes include environmental structure — the environment partly constitutes cognition. "Exploiting" and "constituting" are different relationships. The embedded approach is compatible with a broadly classical picture in which the cognitive system is internal but deploys external resources; the extended approach challenges the boundary of that system.

The deepest tension is about representation. Embodied and enacted approaches often reject mental representation in its classical, amodal, language-like form — cognition is sensorimotor engagement, not symbol manipulation. Extended cognition presupposes that there are representational states that can be realized externally. Radical enactivists (Hutto, Myin) and Gibsonian ecological psychologists reject representation entirely; Clark accepts functional representation distributable beyond the brain; Barsalou accepts perceptual symbol systems grounded in sensorimotor experience. This disagreement about representation cuts across the 4E family more sharply than any of the other divisions.

What the 4E approaches collectively accomplish is to force cognitive science to take seriously that many cognitive achievements require more than internal computation — that body, environment, and sensorimotor engagement are not mere context for cognition but are partly constitutive of it. Whether they constitute a unified paradigm or a diverse set of challenges, the conversation they have initiated has significantly enriched the theoretical landscape.
