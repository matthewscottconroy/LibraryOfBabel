# Predictive Processing

The classical picture of perception runs from world to sensor to inner representation: the world stamps its impressions on the senses, the senses transduce, the visual cortex represents. Predictive processing inverts this. The brain does not wait passively for sensory signals and then figure out what caused them. It continuously generates predictions — hypotheses about what is causing its sensory inputs — and uses the sensory signal to correct errors in those predictions. Perception is not a bottom-up read-off of sensory data; it is a top-down generative process constrained by sensory evidence. The world is always already anticipated.

This is the core of predictive processing (PP), developed into a comprehensive framework by Karl Friston and elaborated philosophically by Andy Clark (*Surfing Uncertainty*, 2016). The brain maintains **hierarchical generative models** that predict sensory inputs from the top down. When predictions match incoming signals, no error propagates. When they mismatch, prediction errors propagate upward through the hierarchy, triggering model updates. Perceptual experience is the brain's best guess about the hidden causes of its sensory inputs — the most probable state of the world given the current evidence and prior beliefs.

## The Bayesian Architecture

PP is sometimes called the "Bayesian brain" hypothesis because the inference involved is Bayesian: combining prior probability (what the brain expects based on its generative model) with likelihood (how well each world-hypothesis would predict the current sensory evidence) to yield a posterior (the most probable current state of the world).

Bayes' theorem: Posterior ∝ Prior × Likelihood

The brain's prior is its generative model — built from experience and encoding expectations about regularities in the world. The likelihood is the sensory signal. The posterior is the percept. This explains why perception is systematically influenced by expectations, context, and prior experience: the percept is not the sensory data but the inference from data plus prior. The hollow face illusion — a concave face appearing convex — illustrates this vividly: the prior expectation that faces are convex dominates the sensory evidence for concavity. The Ames room exploits the same mechanism. In both cases, a prior that is usually reliable overwhelms sensory information that conflicts with it.

The hierarchy ranges from primary visual cortex (detecting low-level features — edges, orientations, spatial frequencies) through intermediate areas (encoding shapes, textures, objects) to higher areas (encoding complex categories, faces, scenes, semantic content). Higher levels represent more abstract, slower-changing features; lower levels represent more concrete, faster-changing ones. Prediction errors — the mismatch signal — propagate upward; predictions propagate downward. What reaches consciousness is not the raw signal but the model's best interpretation.

## Active Inference

A crucial extension: action is not merely the output of cognition but a means of resolving prediction errors. When the brain predicts that the body will be in a certain posture (making proprioceptive predictions) and the prediction is wrong, it faces a choice: update the model to fit the actual state, or move the body to fit the predicted state. The second option — **active inference** — means that motor commands are issued to bring the world in line with the brain's predictions.

On the active inference picture, perception and action are unified under a single framework. Both minimize prediction error: perception does so by updating the model, action by changing the sensory input to match the model. This unification removes the sharp distinction between sensory and motor systems that classical cognitive science assumed. The organism is not a passive receiver that then computes and acts; it is continuously acting to confirm its predictions.

A further element: not all prediction errors are weighted equally. The brain assigns **precision** to prediction errors — a measure of the expected reliability of the signal. High-precision signals are given more weight in updating the model; low-precision signals, less. **Attention** is the brain's mechanism for dynamically adjusting precision: attending to a stimulus means increasing the gain on its prediction errors. This provides a unified account of attention as precision-weighting rather than spotlight selection, with implications for understanding disorders: autism spectrum conditions and schizophrenia may involve systematic miscalibration of precision estimation.

## PP and the 4E Approaches

Clark argues that predictive processing supports rather than undermines the 4E approach to cognition. Active inference means that the organism's actions are integral to the prediction-error minimization cycle: the system acts on the world to generate confirming sensory inputs, not merely to execute commands generated by internal computation. This is not a purely internalist picture — the organism-environment loop is essential to the process. Moreover, organisms construct and exploit environmental regularities precisely to reduce prediction error efficiently; the body's structure, the environment's structure, and habits of action are all parts of the prediction-minimization system.

However, critics (Hutto, Colombo, Kiefer) note that PP is fundamentally a representationalist theory. It posits rich internal generative models that are representations of the world in any reasonable sense. This places PP in tension with the more strongly anti-representationalist wings of the 4E movement — radical enactivism (Hutto and Myin) and Gibsonian ecological psychology — which reject the representational vocabulary entirely. The tension cannot be dissolved by pointing to active inference; the generative model remains an internal representation, and if the model drives perception and action, it is the explanatorily fundamental entity.

## PP and Consciousness

Anil Seth's work (*Being You*, 2021) develops PP's implications for consciousness. Conscious perception is a form of "controlled hallucination": the brain's generative model is always producing a perceptual hypothesis, and what we experience as veridical perception is the hypothesis that best fits the incoming evidence. Hallucination and veridical perception differ only in how well the model is constrained by sensory signals; in ordinary perception, the model is error-corrected, but the experience itself is the model's output. The "self" is also a predictive model — the brain's model of the organism and its bodily states, generated to predict and regulate the internal environment. Disorders of self-consciousness — depersonalization, dissociation — become disturbances of the self-model.

The **content problem** remains a genuine challenge for PP. The framework specifies the computational structure of cognitive processing but is less clear about how the content of predictive models is determined — what makes a model represent the world as being a certain way, rather than merely functioning as a mathematical device for reducing prediction error. PP needs an account of intentional content to supplement its computational description. This is the point at which the philosophy of mind and the predictive processing framework must engage more deeply than they have so far.
