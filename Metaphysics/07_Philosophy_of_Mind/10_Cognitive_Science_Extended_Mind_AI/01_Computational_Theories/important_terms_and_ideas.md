# Important Terms and Ideas: Computational Theories of Mind

**Computational Theory of Mind (CTM)**
The view that mental processes are computations—formal, syntactic operations over symbolic representations. The mind is a kind of information processor; thinking is manipulating mental symbols according to rules determined by their syntactic structure.

**Language of Thought (Mentalese)**
Fodor's hypothesis: thought is conducted in an internal language-like medium—a system of mental representations with syntactic structure that supports compositional semantics. Thinking is computing over sentences of mentalese.

**Physical Symbol System Hypothesis**
Newell and Simon's claim: any system that can manipulate physical symbols (according to a set of rules) is capable of general intelligent behavior—it is necessary and sufficient for intelligence. This grounds the AI research program in symbolic computing.

**Modularity**
Fodor's thesis that cognitive input systems (vision, language, audition) are informationally encapsulated, domain-specific, mandatory, and fast. Modular systems process information without access to central knowledge; their outputs are presented to a central system for integration.

**Classical vs. Connectionist Computation**
Classical computation (Fodor, Pylyshyn): cognition involves rule-governed operations over discrete, language-like symbolic representations. Connectionist computation (Rumelhart, McClelland): cognition involves activation spreading across networks of simple units—patterns of activation rather than discrete symbols.

**Systematicity**
Fodor and Pylyshyn's argument: cognitive abilities are systematically related (anyone who can think "A loves B" can think "B loves A"). Classical computation explains systematicity through compositionality—shared structure in mental representations. Connectionism has difficulty explaining systematicity naturally.
