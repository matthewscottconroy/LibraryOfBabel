# Strong vs. Weak AI

The distinction between strong and weak AI, introduced in Searle's 1980 paper "Minds, Brains, and Programs," separates two claims that are easy to conflate. Weak AI is uncontroversial: computers can simulate cognitive processes, perform tasks that require intelligence when done by humans, and serve as useful tools for studying cognition. No one disputes this. Chess engines beat grandmasters; image recognition systems outperform radiologists; language models generate coherent arguments. The cognitive work is being done. Strong AI is the far more ambitious claim: a suitably programmed computer does not merely simulate thought but genuinely thinks, understands, and has mental states. The right computational organization is sufficient for genuine mentality.

The distinction matters because strong AI, if true, would mean that silicon systems implementing the right program have genuine beliefs, desires, and possibly consciousness. It would mean that the substrate — biological neurons versus silicon — is irrelevant to whether mentality is present. Strong AI is a version of computational functionalism: mental states are defined by their functional roles, and any system implementing those roles has genuine mental states.

## Searle's Chinese Room

Searle rejects strong AI using the Chinese Room. A person locked in a room has a large set of Chinese symbols, rules in English for manipulating them, and input/output slots. She receives Chinese questions and produces Chinese responses by following the rules — without understanding a word of Chinese. From outside, the room appears to understand Chinese. But the person inside understands nothing.

The argument:
- P1: The person implements the same program as any AI system that simulates Chinese understanding.
- P2: The person does not understand Chinese.
- C: Implementing the program is not sufficient for understanding; syntax is not sufficient for semantics.

Computation is a syntactic activity — it manipulates formal symbols according to formal rules. Understanding requires semantics — genuine engagement with meaning. No syntactic process, however complex, can generate semantic understanding.

Searle anticipates the major objections. The **systems reply** argues that the person does not understand but the system — person plus rules plus symbols — does. Searle's response: internalize the whole system. Memorize all the rules and symbols so that the person *is* the entire system, carrying it all in her head. The person still does not understand Chinese. The **robot reply** suggests that if the Chinese Room were embodied in a robot with sensory and motor systems, it would understand because it would have causal connections to the things Chinese symbols refer to. Searle's response: the causal connections to the world do not penetrate the formal symbol-manipulation; the syntactic processes inside are still purely syntactic. The **brain simulator reply** imagines the program simulating actual neuron-by-neuron firing in a Chinese speaker's brain. Searle's response: if the same formal pattern is implemented in water pipes or tin cans, it clearly does not understand; the simulation of the causal pattern is not the same as having the causal powers.

## Biological Naturalism

Searle's positive view — **biological naturalism** — holds that mental states are caused by and realized in the biological brain, which has specific causal powers that produce consciousness and understanding. These causal powers are products of the brain's biological structure, not its computational organization. The same computation can be implemented by systems that are obviously not minded; computation, as a formal and abstract property, cannot in itself produce mental states.

The distinction between **intrinsic** and **observer-relative** intentionality is the deepest point. Mental states have intrinsic intentionality — they genuinely mean things, independently of any observer's interpretation. Programs have observer-relative intentionality — their symbols mean what they mean because human interpreters assign those meanings. No formal process can generate intrinsic intentionality; this is why symbol manipulation, however complex, cannot constitute genuine understanding.

## Defenders of Strong AI

Functionalists argue that the systems reply is correct and that Searle's internalization response fails. What matters is the functional organization of the whole system, not any particular component. If we attribute understanding to humans based on their functional organization, the same grounds apply to any system with suitably complex functional organization. Searle's biological naturalism is, on this view, scientifically unmotivated: there is no evidence that biological neurons have special causal powers not possessed by silicon systems implementing the same computational organization.

Dennett argues that Searle's intuition that the Chinese Room does not understand is itself the product of intuition-pumping calibrated for biological creatures. Our intuitions about understanding do not reliably extend to formal systems, and whether the system "really" understands is not a further fact over and above the facts about its functional organization and behavioral capabilities.

## The Distinction Today

Current large language models achieve weak AI objectives with remarkable success — they perform cognitive tasks at human or superhuman levels across vast domains. Whether they achieve strong AI — whether they genuinely understand — is precisely the question Searle's distinction poses. The question connects to debates about the nature of understanding (functional vs. semantic), the requirements for genuine intentionality (intrinsic vs. derived), and the relationship between syntax and semantics. Whether strong AI is achievable in principle depends on one's philosophy of mind; whether current systems approximate it remains one of the central empirical and philosophical questions of the moment.
