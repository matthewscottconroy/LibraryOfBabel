# Large Language Models and the Philosophy of Mind

Something philosophically interesting has happened. Questions that were once purely abstract — Does language processing require understanding? Can a disembodied system grasp perceptual concepts? What is the relationship between linguistic competence and genuine thought? — now have concrete empirical instantiations that demand analysis. Large language models are not thought experiments; they are real systems producing real outputs, and their existence forces a confrontation with the classical philosophy of language and mind.

LLMs are neural networks trained on vast quantities of text — much of the digitized text of the internet — to predict the next token given previous context. Through this training, they acquire the ability to generate fluent, contextually appropriate, and often sophisticated text on virtually any subject. They pass many informal versions of the Turing Test. Yet they have no perceptual access to the world, no embodiment, no biological drives, and no continuous history of acting in an environment. They process tokens. The dominant architecture — the **transformer** — uses attention mechanisms that dynamically weight the relevance of different parts of the input to each output, enabling sophisticated handling of contextual relationships across long sequences. The training objective is prediction; the emergent capacity is something that looks like linguistic understanding.

## The Central Question

Do LLMs understand language? This is the central philosophical question, and it admits no easy answer. Understanding language seems to require more than producing appropriate outputs to linguistic inputs. Searle's Chinese Room argument suggests that formal symbol manipulation — however sophisticated — does not generate semantic understanding. LLMs manipulate tokens; do they understand what those tokens mean?

The **stochastic parrots** critique (Bender et al., 2021) argues that LLMs are sophisticated pattern-matchers that reproduce linguistic patterns from training data without any genuine semantic engagement. They know the form of language — its statistical structure — without knowing its meaning, which requires grounding in non-linguistic experience. The **embodiment problem** gives this critique depth: LLMs have been trained on text generated by embodied agents — humans who perceive, act, and navigate the world. They have encountered linguistic descriptions of perceptual experience, bodily sensation, spatial orientation. But they have not had these experiences. If word meanings must ultimately be grounded in sensorimotor experience (Glenberg, Harnad), then LLMs might know the word "red" without knowing its meaning.

The **functionalist response** presses back: if understanding is constitutively characterized by functional capacities — answering questions appropriately, drawing relevant inferences, using language appropriately in context — then LLMs may exhibit a form of understanding, even if it differs from human understanding. The distributional hypothesis (Landauer and Dumais's latent semantic analysis, and its successors) suggests that the statistical relationships among words encode much of the meaning normally grounded in experience: learning the full statistical structure of language might be sufficient for a form of understanding without direct perceptual grounding.

## Classical Debates Renewed

The emergence of LLMs has renewed engagement with several classical debates. Turing's test: LLMs perform remarkably well in informal settings. Does this constitute evidence of genuine intelligence, or merely evidence that sophisticated pattern matching can mimic intelligence at the behavioral level? Searle's Chinese Room: LLMs are the largest and most sophisticated instantiation of the scenario Searle envisioned. The question of whether they understand or merely simulate understanding is directly posed.

Fodor's Language of Thought is challenged more directly. Fodor argued that language comprehension requires a compositional language of thought — that there must be, at some level, structured symbolic representations whose combinatorial properties explain systematicity and productivity. LLMs achieve sophisticated language behavior without any obviously compositional LOT architecture; their knowledge is distributed across billions of weights, not localized in symbol structures. This either challenges LOT's claim to necessity, or requires a reinterpretation of what LOT means in the context of distributed representations.

Dreyfus's critique (*What Computers Can't Do*, 1972; *What Computers Still Can't Do*, 1992) argued that human intelligence is constitutively tied to embodied, social engagement with the world and cannot be replicated by disembodied symbol manipulation. LLMs challenge this: they achieve sophisticated linguistic performance without embodiment. Whether this refutes Dreyfus — showing that language can be separated from the deeper cognitive capacities he had in mind — or merely shows that statistical text patterns can mimic the output of embodied understanding without replicating its basis, is a live question.

## The Capability-Failure Profile

LLMs exhibit a distinctive profile. Capabilities include generating coherent, stylistically varied text across virtually any topic; answering factual questions; summarizing, translating, coding; generating arguments; apparent contextual reasoning. Failures include confident confabulation — generating plausible-sounding but false information presented with no epistemic marking; sensitivity to superficial features of prompts, such that rephrasing a question changes the answer; difficulty with systematic, step-by-step reasoning in formal domains; susceptibility to adversarial inputs designed to elicit unexpected outputs.

This profile is philosophically significant. The failures may reveal something about the nature of LLM processing — its statistical, non-rule-governed character — or they may reflect limitations of current systems addressable by scaling or architectural change. Whether this capability-failure profile is consistent with genuine understanding, or definitively rules it out, is precisely what is contested.

Whether Dennett's intentional stance helps here: he argues that it is appropriate to attribute beliefs and desires to any system best understood by attributing them, regardless of whether the system "really" has inner states. On this view, attributing understanding to LLMs when this is the most predictively useful framework is legitimate without prejudice to deeper metaphysical questions. Whether the intentional stance can do this work without collapsing the distinction between genuine and simulated understanding is itself a live issue.
