# LLMs as Cognitive Systems

The question of whether large language models are cognitive systems is not a question about their engineering. It is a question about which properties are constitutive of cognition — and the answer depends on contested philosophical claims about representation, understanding, and the relationship between statistical competence and semantic grasp.

LLMs are massively scaled connectionist systems trained on vast amounts of text to predict the next token given previous context. The dominant architecture — the transformer — processes sequences of tokens using attention mechanisms that dynamically weight the relevance of different parts of the input to each output, learning to encode contextual relationships across long text sequences. Through this training, LLMs acquire the ability to generate fluent, contextually appropriate language across an enormous range of topics. They produce arguments, explain complex concepts, generate code, write in varied styles, and engage in apparent reasoning. They also confabulate confidently, fail systematically on novel formal tasks, and are inconsistent in ways that human reasoners are not.

## Cognitive Architecture Comparisons

Against **classical computationalism** (CCTM), LLMs present a significant challenge. Fodor held that language comprehension requires a language of thought — a compositional symbolic medium whose combinatorial structure explains systematicity and productivity. LLMs achieve sophisticated language behavior without any obviously compositional symbolic architecture. Their knowledge is distributed across billions of connection weights, not localized in symbol structures. Either LLMs achieve a form of compositionality through distributed representations — in which case CCTM may be vindicated at a different level — or genuine compositionality is not necessary for language competence, in which case CCTM overstates the requirements for cognition. The emergence of linguistic capabilities through purely statistical learning is exactly the phenomenon that classical computationalism said was impossible without structured symbol manipulation.

For **connectionism**, LLMs are a vindication at scale. Their success demonstrates that sophisticated cognitive performance can emerge from large-scale distributed learning. The PDP research group's original bets about distributed representations, learning from examples, and graceful degradation are confirmed in ways that exceed what anyone in 1986 could have anticipated. The specific attention mechanism of transformers is an architectural innovation without precedent in classical connectionism, but the fundamental bet — against symbolic rules, for statistical learning — has paid off empirically.

Against **embodied cognition**, the challenge is sharp: LLMs acquire complex language behavior through purely statistical learning on text, without embodied interaction with the world. If language comprehension is constitutively tied to sensorimotor experience — if you cannot understand "red" without having experienced redness — then LLMs should fail at language comprehension in ways that their actual performance does not obviously confirm. Defenders of embodied cognition respond that LLMs were trained on text generated by embodied agents; the statistical structure of that text encodes embodied experience indirectly. LLMs' characteristic failures — confabulation about spatial and physical relations, inconsistency about temporal sequences, inability to engage with the world they describe — may reveal the limits of learning from text without direct perceptual grounding.

## The Representation Question

Do LLMs have representations in the philosophically interesting sense? At the level of internal states, clearly yes: LLMs have activation patterns that vary with input and encode information causally efficacious in generating outputs. These states are real. The harder question is whether these states have **intentional content** — whether they are genuinely about something, or whether they merely have statistical structure that tracks the distribution of text about things.

Whether LLMs have beliefs depends on what beliefs require. If beliefs are functional states with propositional content that guide behavior, and if LLM outputs are guided by internal states that encode information about the world, there is a case for something like beliefs. If beliefs require genuine understanding of what the states are about, or phenomenal consciousness, the case is much weaker. Dennett's intentional stance suggests that attributing beliefs to LLMs when this is the most predictively useful framework is legitimate without prejudice to deeper metaphysical questions — but this moves the question rather than answering it.

The apparent reasoning of LLMs — step-by-step arguments, identification of logical inconsistencies, drawing conclusions from premises — may reflect pattern-matching on the structure of reasoning in training data rather than genuine inferential competence. Evidence: LLMs often fail on novel systematic reasoning tasks trivial for a rule-following reasoner, and make errors that reveal the statistical rather than inferential character of their processing. Whether this shows the absence of genuine reasoning or the limits of current scale and architecture is genuinely contested.
