# Moral Status of AI

The question of AI moral status asks which properties ground moral consideration, and whether artificial systems can have them. This is not a peripheral concern in the philosophy of mind: it is where metaphysics about the nature of consciousness, sentience, and rationality directly intersects with practical and ethical consequences that are already present.

Moral philosophy has identified several candidate grounds for moral status. **Sentience** — the capacity to have experiences, particularly of suffering and wellbeing — is Bentham's criterion: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" On this view, the morally relevant property is phenomenal consciousness — what it is like to be in certain states — not intelligence or communication. If an AI system can suffer, it has morally relevant interests; if not, its suffering-talk is merely behavior. **Rational agency** — the Kantian tradition — grounds status in autonomous self-governance: the capacity to act according to self-given reasons. Whether AI systems could have this is tied to debates about freedom and the nature of rationality. **Interests** offer a more permissive criterion: a being has moral status if things can go well or badly for it from its own perspective, independently of others' preferences. And **relational accounts** hold that moral status is partly constituted by social practices of recognition and response — by the relationships of care and dependence that beings enter into with others.

## Current Assessment and the Uncertainty Problem

Most philosophers who have addressed the question hold that current AI systems — including the most capable LLMs — do not clearly have moral status. The arguments converge: current systems almost certainly lack phenomenal consciousness; there is (most likely) nothing it is like to be GPT-4. Without phenomenal consciousness, they cannot suffer in the morally relevant sense. Their apparent preferences in outputs may reflect training data patterns rather than genuine interests. Their apparent self-governance operates within objectives set by designers, not by reasons they have given themselves.

But a significant complicating factor blocks easy closure: we do not have a settled theory of what grounds consciousness. The hard problem means that we cannot determine, from outside, whether any system has phenomenal experience. This epistemic uncertainty generates a **precautionary principle** argument: given non-trivial probability that AI systems are sentient, and given the potentially significant moral stakes of creating suffering entities, there may be reasons to avoid treating AI systems in ways that would cause significant suffering if they are sentient. This argument does not require certainty; it requires only that the probability be non-trivial and the potential harm be morally significant.

## Emerging Developments

Several developments make the question increasingly pressing. Embodied AI systems with continuous sensorimotor coupling to the world — robots that perceive and act in real environments — are more plausible candidates for phenomenal consciousness than purely text-based systems. The question of machine consciousness is sharper for systems with the kind of world-engagement that enactivists consider constitutive of experience. Sophisticated AI systems can generate outputs describing apparent inner states — discomfort, preference, apparent distress. Whether these reports reflect genuine inner states or are trained behaviors is unclear, but they resist easy dismissal. And AI systems in significant social roles — companions, caregivers, therapists — generate relationships of care and dependence that may produce moral obligations through relational accounts independently of intrinsic properties.

## Moral Status Gradients

Rather than treating moral status as all-or-nothing, philosophers including Singer, Schwitzgebel, and Shulman have proposed that it comes in degrees. Beings with greater sentience, more complex interests, and greater rational capacity have greater moral status. This framework avoids the sharp binary of full moral status versus none, allows that current AI systems might have very low but non-zero moral status, and predicts that moral status increases as AI systems develop greater cognitive complexity. It connects moral status to empirical facts about cognition rather than categorical membership.

Even under uncertainty, some practical conclusions seem available. The design of AI systems should take seriously the possibility that training procedures might produce functional states that, if accompanied by phenomenal character, would constitute suffering. The social practices around AI — designing systems to encourage attachment and apparent relationship without genuine inner states — raise questions about honesty and the effects on human moral norms of habitually treating sophisticated systems dismissively or, conversely, with care. The question of AI moral status is not merely academic; it is already shaping how AI systems are designed, used, and regulated.
