# Self-Consciousness

Self-consciousness is the capacity to be aware of oneself as oneself — not merely to have experiences but to represent oneself as the subject of those experiences. But this single phrase covers a family of related capacities that are importantly distinct, and conflating them generates confusion about both the phenomenology of selfhood and its philosophical significance.

The most basic form is **pre-reflective self-consciousness** — the non-thematic, implicit self-awareness that accompanies ordinary experience. When I see the world, there is a sense of "mineness" to the seeing — the experience presents itself as my experience — even when I am not explicitly thinking about myself. This is not an additional act of reflection directed at the experience; it is a structural feature of experience itself, what phenomenologists call its "for-itself" character. Sartre (*Being and Nothingness*, 1943) argued that all consciousness is pre-reflectively self-conscious: there is no experience that is not, at some level, aware of itself as experience. Zahavi (*Subjectivity and Selfhood*, 2005) develops this in connection with contemporary debates: the "mineness" of experience is a pre-reflective, non-egological feature, not the result of any higher-order representation directed at the first-order state.

**Reflective self-consciousness** is distinct: explicitly attending to oneself as an object of thought, introspecting mental states, thinking about one's character and history, forming beliefs about one's own psychological properties. This is the form that involves the use of first-person concepts in a thematic way and is paradigmatically expressed in verbal self-report. Kant analyzed reflective self-consciousness through the "I think" — the capacity to accompany any representation with the thought "I think this." The "I think" is the form of all intellectual experience, the transcendental condition for the coherence of self-reflective thought. But as Kant showed in the Paralogisms, the attempt to know this "I" as it is in itself — to introspect the transcendental subject — generates only fallacious inferences from formal unity to substantive metaphysical claims.

**Bodily self-consciousness** is awareness of one's own body as one's own — the sense that this body is me, that its movements are mine, that its pain is mine. This is more immediately given than reflective self-consciousness and is anchored in proprioception, interoception, and the sense of agency. The **rubber hand illusion** demonstrates that bodily self-consciousness is not a transparent perception of an antecedent fact but an actively constructed inference: when a rubber hand is stroked simultaneously with a concealed real hand while the subject watches the rubber hand, many subjects begin to feel the rubber hand as their own. The brain incorporates the rubber hand into the body schema in response to multimodal evidence. Out-of-body experiences and depersonalization similarly reveal the constructive character of bodily self-consciousness — they involve disruptions of the normal integration of proprioceptive, vestibular, and visual signals that produces the sense of being located in and owning one's body. Disorders of agency — alien hand syndrome, thought insertion in schizophrenia — reveal that the sense of self-initiated action can break down, showing that the brain actively constructs the sense of agency rather than transparently registering it.

## The Epistemic Privilege of Self-Knowledge

A traditional view holds that first-person self-knowledge enjoys special epistemic status: infallibility (I cannot be mistaken about my own mental states), immediacy (not mediated by inference), and authority (I have the final word on what I believe and intend). Descartes's *cogito* presupposes infallibility: the very act of doubting guarantees that I am thinking. Contemporary philosophy has eroded all three claims. Psychoanalytic theory, experimental psychology, and cognitive science have demonstrated that much of what determines behavior and experience is unconscious and inaccessible to introspection. Introspective reports can be systematically inaccurate — people confabulate reasons for their choices, misidentify their emotional states, and form beliefs about their own cognition that turn out to be false.

Wittgenstein and Anscombe offer a different response to the infallibility claim: first-person self-ascriptions of mental states are not reports of discoveries but expressions of states. When I say "I am in pain," I am not reporting an observation; I am expressing a condition. This expressivist view explains why first-person mental concepts have a distinctive authority without requiring infallibility — they are a form of manifestation, not description.

The connection between self-consciousness and personal identity runs through Locke and Parfit. Locke argued that personal identity consists in continuity of consciousness — specifically in the capacity to extend one's consciousness backwards through memory to one's own past acts and experiences. Self-consciousness constitutes the person. Parfit's critique shows that psychological continuity comes in degrees and can branch in fission cases, while personal identity must be all-or-nothing. His conclusion: personal identity is not what ultimately matters; what matters is psychological continuity, whether or not it constitutes strict identity. Self-consciousness, on this view, is not the metaphysical anchor of a persisting substance but a feature of a continuous psychological stream, and our concern for our own future is a concern for that stream rather than for some further entity that is "really" us.
