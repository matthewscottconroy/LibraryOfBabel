# The Narrative Self

What makes me the same person as the child in the photograph from thirty years ago? Not the same body — most of the molecules have turned over. Not quite the same psychology — beliefs, preferences, and character have changed substantially. Something persists, but it is not obvious what. The narrative theory of the self proposes an answer: what persists, and what constitutes the self, is a structure of story — the narrative that integrates past, present, and projected future into a coherent biography. This is not merely a description of a pre-existing self; it is, at least partly, what the self is.

The narrative theory is motivated by cases that challenge purely biological or psychological continuity criteria. A person who undergoes radical character change — religious conversion, recovery from addiction, severe brain injury — may maintain biological continuity while arguably becoming a different person in some narratively significant sense. Commitments and integrity — keeping promises, maintaining projects across time, taking responsibility for one's past — seem to constitute selfhood in ways that bare psychological continuity does not capture. The person who says "I am not the same person who did that" may be making a claim that has genuine content, even if they are biologically continuous with their past self.

## Ricoeur's Framework

Paul Ricoeur's *Oneself as Another* (1992) provides the most philosophically systematic narrative theory. Ricoeur distinguishes two types of identity: **idem-identity** (*mêmeté*) — numerical sameness, the persistence of a substance — and **ipse-identity** (*ipséité*) — selfhood, the kind of identity that involves keeping promises, maintaining character, being accountable. His central thesis is that narrative identity is primarily *ipse*-identity. What makes me the same person as my past self is not the metaphysical fact of substance continuity but my taking ownership of that past life, integrating it into an ongoing story.

Narrative identity is produced by the interplay of concordance and discordance: concordance is the unity and coherence that a narrative imposes on disparate events, giving a life the shape of a plot; discordance is the accidents, reversals, and disruptions that require the narrative to be revised and reconceived. A life is not a story already given — it is an ongoing interpretive project in which the person configures and reconfigures her past and projects her future. The narrative self is never complete.

MacIntyre (*After Virtue*, 1981) situates narrative identity within social practices and moral traditions. To be a person is to be an accountable participant in practices that extend over time. Actions are intelligible when they fit into a coherent story of who one is, what one is doing, and what one intends. Without tradition and community, without the narrative frameworks they provide, individual lives cannot cohere into a self. This connects personal identity to virtue ethics: virtues are the excellences of a person living a narrative life oriented toward a *telos* that gives the narrative its overall shape.

Schechtman's **narrative self-constitution view** (*The Constitution of Selves*, 1996) is analytically more precise: personal identity over time consists in possessing a narrative self-understanding — an implicit or explicit story of one's own life that coheres sufficiently to make actions and experiences intelligible as parts of a single life. The view is explicitly normative: the narrative must be coherent, roughly accurate (not purely confabulated), and recognizable to others as the narrative of a person. Pathological confabulation does not constitute a self.

## Objections

The **pre-narrative self objection** presses that there is a minimal self — the immediate, pre-reflective sense of being a subject — that is prior to and independent of any narrative. Infants have this minimal self, and so do adults in states of absorbed activity where narrative self-reflection is absent. The narrative theory may capture an important dimension of mature selfhood without capturing the most fundamental form.

The **authenticity problem** asks which narrative is authoritative when a person has multiple inconsistent self-narratives, or when the narrative distorts the past. If the self is constituted by narrative, selfhood is as reliable as the stories we tell — which is not very reliable at all.

The **narrative form objection** notes that trauma often resists assimilation into narrative; it remains as an intrusion rather than becoming a chapter. If narrative form is essential to selfhood, this may exclude or impoverish the selfhood of those whose experience resists narration.

The **circularity concern** asks who is doing the narrating. The narrative theory says the self is constituted by the narrative it tells about itself — but there seems to be a presupposed narrator whose existence the narrative is supposed to constitute. The default mode network — active during self-reflection, autobiographical memory retrieval, and prospective thinking — provides a neural basis for the kind of temporal integration the narrative self requires, and its disruption in Alzheimer's disease corresponds to the dissolution of narrative self-coherence. But identifying the neural substrate does not resolve the philosophical question of whether the narrative constitutes the self or merely expresses a self that was already there.
