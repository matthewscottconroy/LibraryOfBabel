# Domain Survey: Free Will and Agency

## Overview

The problem of free will is generated by an apparent conflict between two commitments: the commonsense conviction that agents are sometimes genuinely free — that they could have done otherwise and are therefore morally responsible for what they do — and the thesis that the world operates by deterministic (or at least fully natural) causal laws that leave no room for any alternative possibilities. If every event, including every human choice, is fully determined by prior events and the laws of nature, then nothing could have happened differently than it did. But if nothing could have happened differently, in what sense did the agent have a choice? And if the agent had no choice, how can praise, blame, reward, and punishment be anything other than arbitrary applications of power?

The philosophical literature articulates three main positions. **Hard determinism** (and its cousin, **hard incompatibilism**) accepts that determinism precludes free will and concludes that genuine free will does not exist — and, with it, that traditional notions of moral responsibility are confused or unjustified. **Compatibilism** holds that free will and determinism are not genuinely in conflict; the kind of freedom required for moral responsibility is compatible with everything being causally determined. **Libertarianism** (in the metaphysical sense, entirely unrelated to political libertarianism) holds that genuine free will exists and requires the falsity of determinism; agents have a special kind of causation — agent causation or undetermined choice — that cannot be captured by any deterministic mechanism.

The central argumentative structure is the **Consequence Argument** (van Inwagen 1983), which gives sharp form to the incompatibilist intuition: if determinism is true, then our actions are the consequences of the laws of nature and events in the remote past — facts entirely outside our control; the consequences of things outside our control are also outside our control; therefore, our actions are outside our control. This argument has transformed the debate by making the incompatibilist case precise enough to analyze carefully. Compatibilists must show either that "could have done otherwise" does not require what the Consequence Argument assumes, or that the relevant sense of "control" is compatible with everything being outside one's control in the specified sense.

The debate connects to deep questions in ethics (about moral responsibility, desert, punishment) and in the philosophy of action (about what distinguishes mere behavior from action, and what constitutes acting for a reason). It also connects to the metaphysics of causation: libertarians typically posit either agent causation (a fundamentally different kind of causation exercised by agents) or indeterministic quantum-level events that introduce genuine randomness into neural processing.

## Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

### The Compatibility Spectrum

**Hard incompatibilism**: Free will — in any sense robust enough to ground moral responsibility — is incompatible with determinism. If determinism is true, we have no genuine options; if indeterminism is true, our choices are random; neither option yields genuine freedom.

**Hard determinism**: Determinism is true; therefore, free will does not exist; therefore, traditional moral responsibility is incoherent.

**Hard indeterminism**: Determinism is false (as quantum mechanics suggests); but undetermined events are random, not free; therefore, free will still does not exist.

**Compatibilism**: Free will, properly understood, is compatible with determinism. The "freedom" required for moral responsibility is not the ability to do otherwise in a categorical sense (with all prior conditions held fixed) but something like the absence of compulsion, the responsiveness to reasons, or the capacity for reflective self-governance.

- *Conditional analysis* (classical compatibilism, Hume, Moore): "Could have done otherwise" means "would have done otherwise *if* one had chosen/wanted to." On this analysis, even in a deterministic world, people could have done otherwise — they would have, had they wanted to. *Objection* (Chisholm, Lehrer): this analysis fails for cases of compulsion — a person compelled to act may satisfy the conditional (they would have acted differently if they had chosen differently) but clearly lacked freedom.

- *Hierarchical compatibilism* (Frankfurt 1971): Freedom consists not in categorical alternatives but in the alignment between first-order desires (desires to do X) and second-order desires (desires to have certain first-order desires). An agent acts freely when their second-order will endorses the first-order desire that moves them. Compulsion involves a conflict between what one wants and what one is moved to do by one's second-order volitions.

- *Reason-responsive compatibilism* (Fischer and Ravizza 1998): An agent is free if their action-producing mechanism is reasons-responsive — the mechanism is such that the agent would have responded appropriately to a sufficient reason to do otherwise in a relevantly similar situation. This preserves moral responsibility without requiring categorical alternatives.

- *Sourcehood compatibilism* (Wolf, Watson): Freedom requires that the agent be the source of their actions in the right way — that the action trace back to the agent's own reasons, values, and character, without originating in manipulation, compulsion, or unresponsive processes.

**Libertarianism**: Free will exists and requires the falsity of determinism. 

- *Agent causation* (Chisholm, O'Connor): agents are irreducible substances that cause actions without their causation being fully determined by prior events or mental states. Agent causation is a genuinely different kind of causation from event causation. *Problem*: agent causation seems mysterious and physically unexplained; if agents are not subject to causal laws, agent causation seems arbitrary.

- *Event-causal libertarianism*: Free actions involve causation by the agent's reasons-states, but this causation is indeterministic — the reasons probabilistically cause the action without necessitating it. Kane (1996) develops this via "self-forming actions" at moments of torn decision, exploiting quantum indeterminacy at the neural level. *Problem*: if the agent's action is probabilistically caused by their reasons but not necessitated, the final outcome seems a matter of luck, not freedom.

- *Non-causal libertarianism* (Goetz, McCann): Free actions are not caused at all (causation and freedom are incompatible); they are intelligible through reasons without being causally produced by them. *Problem*: this seems to make free actions causally inert — uncaused events — which undermines the agent's ability to be responsible for them.

### Moral Responsibility

**The basic desert thesis**: A person deserves praise, blame, reward, or punishment in a fundamental sense — not merely as a forward-looking tool for behavior modification — only if they acted freely. Free will is the metaphysical precondition for basic desert.

**Strawson's reactive attitudes** (1962): Moral responsibility does not require the metaphysics of free will; it is grounded in the practice of holding people responsible — the "reactive attitudes" of resentment, indignation, gratitude, and love. Whether determinism is true is irrelevant to whether it is appropriate to maintain these practices. Hard determinists confuse a theoretical stance (considering someone "from the outside," as a natural mechanism) with the participant stance (engaging with someone as an agent who can be reasoned with).
- *Objection*: Strawson's appeal to reactive attitudes seems to confuse the justification of the practice of holding responsible with the presuppositions of that practice. If the presupposition (that the agent was genuinely free) is false, the practice may be irrational even if natural.

**Revisionary views** (Derk Pereboom 2014): Free will does not exist; therefore, traditional retributive punishment (punishment as deserved for past wrongs) is unjustified. But consequentialist grounds for practices of moral responsibility remain: we can quarantine dangerous agents, incentivize good behavior, and maintain a practice of reactive attitudes that is useful for social coordination — all without presupposing that the agent deserved the treatment in the metaphysically heavy sense.

## Key Arguments and Counterarguments

### The Consequence Argument (van Inwagen)

Let *N(p)* abbreviate "p is true and no one has or ever had any choice about whether p is true."

**P1.** *N(laws)* — no one has a choice about what the laws of nature are.
**P2.** *N(past)* — no one has a choice about what the remote past was like.
**P3.** If determinism is true, then every fact about the present and future follows from the laws plus the past.
**P4.** The Transfer Principle: *N(p)*, *N(p → q)* ⊢ *N(q)*.
**C.** *N(every present and future fact)* — no one has a choice about anything they do.

**Compatibilist responses**: (i) Deny the Transfer Principle: it fails for certain cases (Lewis); even if we have no choice about the laws, and no choice about the past, we can have a choice about what we do in a sense not captured by the principle. (ii) Deny P2: what the past was like is partly constituted by what we are now doing; if we act otherwise, the past was different (the "backtracking" response). (iii) Develop an alternative account of "having a choice" that is compatible with the constraints the argument imposes.

### Frankfurt Cases (Frankfurt 1969)

**Setup**: Jones is about to vote for candidate A. An evil neuroscientist Black has implanted a device that will ensure Jones votes for A if Jones shows any sign of voting otherwise. Jones votes for A on his own, without the device being triggered.

**Conclusion**: Jones is morally responsible for voting for A, even though he could not have done otherwise (Black's device would have intervened). Therefore, moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise.

**Objections**: (i) *Flicker of freedom* (van Inwagen, Wierenga): there is a moment of freedom — the sign that the device monitors — even in Frankfurt cases; responsibility depends on this flicker. (ii) *Dilemma for Frankfurt cases* (Pereboom, Kane): Either the prior sign that triggers the device is determined, in which case the case begs the question against incompatibilism by already assuming determinism; or it is not determined, in which case the prior sign could have been absent and Jones could have acted differently.

### The Manipulation Argument (Pereboom 2001)

**P1.** A person directly manipulated into acting by a neuroscientist is not morally responsible.
**P2.** There is no relevant difference between a directly manipulated person and a person raised with certain neural dispositions that determine action.
**P3.** There is no relevant difference between a person raised with certain neural dispositions and a person whose neural dispositions are the result of genetics and environment.
**C.** If the neural dispositions result from deterministic processes, no one in a deterministic world is morally responsible.

**Compatibilist responses**: (i) There *is* a relevant difference: the manipulated agent's action does not trace back to their own reasons-responsiveness mechanisms; the normally raised agent's does. (ii) Fischer and Ravizza: reasons-responsiveness is the criterion of responsibility; manipulation bypasses this criterion, but normal upbringing does not.

## Key Papers and Books

1. **van Inwagen, Peter, *An Essay on Free Will* (1983)** — The definitive formulation of the incompatibilist case; develops the Consequence Argument; defines the free will problem for contemporary analytic philosophy.

2. **Frankfurt, Harry, "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" (1969)** — Introduces Frankfurt cases; the founding challenge to the Principle of Alternate Possibilities.

3. **Strawson, P. F., "Freedom and Resentment" (1962)** — The reactive attitudes response to hard determinism; one of the most influential papers in twentieth-century philosophy.

4. **Fischer, John Martin and Ravizza, Mark, *Responsibility and Control* (1998)** — Develops the most systematic compatibilist account using reasons-responsiveness; the standard reference for compatibilist moral responsibility theory.

5. **Kane, Robert, *The Significance of Free Will* (1996)** — The most thorough defense of libertarianism; develops the event-causal indeterministic model using self-forming actions at quantum decision points.

6. **Pereboom, Derk, *Living Without Free Will* (2001)** — The definitive defense of hard incompatibilism; develops the manipulation argument; argues for a pragmatic moral responsibility without desert.

7. **O'Connor, Timothy, *Persons and Causes* (2000)** — Systematic defense of agent causation libertarianism; the best contemporary treatment of this position.

8. **Wolf, Susan, *Freedom Within Reason* (1990)** — Develops the "Reason View" — freedom requires the ability to act in accordance with reason, not merely the ability to do otherwise; interesting intermediate position.

## Live Debates and Open Questions

1. **Manipulation vs. natural causation**: The manipulation argument depends on the claim that there is no relevant difference between being manipulated and being naturally caused. Compatibilists have developed increasingly sophisticated accounts of what the relevant difference might be (history of reasons-responsiveness, mesh with one's own evaluative commitments). Whether any of these succeed is an open question.

2. **Indeterminism and luck**: Even if quantum indeterminacy introduces genuine underdetermination into neural processes, it is unclear how this helps with freedom. An undetermined choice seems to be a matter of luck — the opposite of a controlled, self-determined action. Libertarians have developed responses (Kane's self-forming actions, event-causal probabilism), but the "luck problem" remains.

3. **Moral responsibility without basic desert**: Pereboom's revisionary view requires showing that moral responsibility practices can be maintained without presupposing basic desert. Whether this is coherent — whether resentment and indignation can be rationally maintained without the belief that the target deserved them — is deeply contested.

4. **Neuroscience and free will**: Libet's experiments (showing that neural readiness potentials precede conscious decisions) have been taken to show that "decisions" are post-hoc rationalizations of already-initiated physical processes. The philosophical consensus is that Libet's results are methodologically problematic and do not establish determinism or show that conscious will is causally inert — but the debate continues.

5. **The relationship between free will and moral psychology**: Knobe, Nahmias, and others have developed experimental philosophy of free will, testing intuitions about responsibility in cases that vary across dimensions. This empirical work informs — but does not resolve — the normative and metaphysical questions.

## Connections to Other Domains

**Free Will and Causation/Laws (Domain 04)**: Causal determinism is the threat that generates the free will problem; what determinism consists in depends on one's theory of laws and causation. Libertarians who appeal to agent causation are positing a new kind of causation; their position requires a non-standard account of causation.

**Free Will and Philosophy of Mind (Domain 07)**: The causal exclusion argument threatens to eliminate mental causation entirely; if mental states have no causal powers, agents cannot act for reasons, and the conditions for free will are undermined. The connection between intentional action and mental causation is direct.

**Free Will and Identity/Persistence (Domain 03)**: Parfit's reductionism about persons has direct consequences for moral responsibility: if there is no deep fact about personal identity over time, the basis for holding someone responsible for distant past actions becomes uncertain. Frankfurt cases also implicate identity (which of Jones's "wills" is his real will?).

**Free Will and Metametaphysics (Domain 09)**: Whether the free will problem is a genuine metaphysical dispute or a verbal dispute about what "freedom" means is itself a metametaphysical question. Compatibilists often argue (à la Hirsch) that incompatibilists are using "freedom" in a sense that no one actually cares about.

**Free Will and Philosophy of Religion (Domain 13)**: Divine foreknowledge and free will interact: if God infallibly knows in advance what I will do, can I do otherwise? The open theism debate (Sanders, Hasker) is the theological version of the free will debate; Molinism (God knows counterfactuals of creaturely freedom) is an attempted middle ground.
