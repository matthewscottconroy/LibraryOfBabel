# Free Will and Agency

Imagine that the laws of physics are deterministic: every event is fixed by prior events together with the laws of nature, tracing an unbroken causal chain back to the beginning of the universe. In that case, your decision to read this sentence was fixed billions of years before you were born, determined by the state of matter at the Big Bang. You could not have done otherwise — not because you lacked the strength or the opportunity, but because the laws that govern every particle in your brain left no room for any outcome other than the one that occurred. If this picture is correct, is there any sense in which you are genuinely responsible for what you do? Is there any meaningful sense in which you are the *author* of your actions rather than merely the conduit through which prior causes produce later effects?

This is the free will problem, and it matters enormously. Our entire moral and legal practice — the practice of holding people responsible, of praising and blaming, of punishment and reward — rests on the assumption that people can, at least sometimes, be the genuine authors of their actions. If determinism is true and incompatible with that kind of authorship, then much of what we do to each other is systematically unjustified. The question is not merely academic. It bears on how we treat criminals, how we think about moral desert, whether guilt and pride make sense, and ultimately on how we conceive of ourselves as agents in the world.

The problem is sharpened by a famous argument: the Consequence Argument. If determinism is true, our actions are consequences of the laws of nature and events that occurred before we were born. We have no control over the laws of nature, and no control over events before our birth. Therefore, we have no control over the consequences of these things — including all of our actions. Free will, on this argument, requires that determinism be false. Call this *incompatibilism*: free will and determinism cannot both be true.

Incompatibilism divides into two camps. **Hard incompatibilists** accept the argument and deny that we have free will — either because they accept determinism and conclude that free will is illusory, or because they think the indeterminism of quantum mechanics does not help (random causation is no more compatible with control than determined causation). **Libertarians** — in the metaphysical, not the political, sense — accept incompatibilism too but deny that determinism is true of human agency. They posit a special kind of agent causation, or appeal to indeterminism in neural processes, to secure the openness of the future that genuine free will seems to require.

**Compatibilists** reject the incompatibilist argument entirely. They hold that free will, properly understood, is perfectly consistent with determinism — indeed, that what we care about when we care about free will is precisely what determinism leaves intact. The ability to act on our reasons, the absence of external compulsion, the capacity to respond to incentives and argument — these are what matter for moral responsibility, and they do not require that the future be open in the incompatibilist's sense. Compatibilism is not a counsel of despair but a reconception of what freedom is.

This unit examines the debate across four chapters. We begin by examining determinism itself and the precise structure of the problem. The subsequent chapters develop compatibilism — including its response to Frankfurt cases and manipulation arguments — libertarianism in its various forms, and hard incompatibilism with its revisionary consequences for moral practice. The neuroscience of decision-making adds empirical texture to what might otherwise seem a purely conceptual debate.

Few philosophical problems touch us as closely. The question of whether we are free is the question of whether we are genuinely agents or merely elaborate machines — and the answer shapes everything else we think about ourselves.
