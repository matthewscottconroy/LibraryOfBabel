# Determinism and the Problem of Free Will

Determinism is the thesis that the history of the world is fixed: given the complete state of the universe at any moment and the laws of nature, every subsequent state is uniquely determined. The future flows from the past with logical necessity; there is only one way the world can go from any given point. This is not a metaphor. It is a claim about the causal structure of reality that was taken to be established by Newtonian mechanics and has been challenged but not unambiguously refuted by quantum mechanics.

If determinism is true, then every decision you make was fixed by the state of the universe before you were born. Your "choice" to read this sentence, your "decision" to accept or reject the argument you are about to encounter — these were determined by causes that operated long before you existed. In what sense, then, are they your choices? In what sense are you responsible for actions that were, in the relevant sense, not up to you?

This is the problem of free will, and it is generated by what appears to be an incompatibility between determinism and the kind of freedom that moral responsibility seems to require. If your actions were determined by prior causes over which you had no control, and you had no control over those causes, then — by what seems like a straightforward transitivity of control — you have no control over your actions. And without control over your actions, you are not responsible for them.

The Consequence Argument, formulated with precision by Peter van Inwagen, gives this reasoning formal shape: our actions are consequences of the laws of nature and events that predate our existence; we have no control over either; therefore, we have no control over our actions. This argument is valid; the question is whether its premises are true, and whether "control" means the same thing in all its occurrences.

This chapter develops determinism carefully, distinguishes it from fatalism and from quantum indeterminism, and sets up the landscape of positions — compatibilism, libertarianism, and hard incompatibilism — that subsequent chapters explore. The reader who understands what determinism claims and why it seems to threaten free will is prepared to evaluate the sophisticated responses that follow.
