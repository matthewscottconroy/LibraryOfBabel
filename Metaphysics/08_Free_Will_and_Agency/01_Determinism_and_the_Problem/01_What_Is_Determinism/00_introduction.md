# What Is Determinism?

Determinism is the thesis that every event is necessitated by prior events together with the laws of nature. Given the complete state of the universe at any time and the laws of nature, there is only one physically possible future. Every human decision, every action, every thought is the inevitable consequence of the state of the world a moment before, which was itself the inevitable consequence of the state of the world before that, tracing back through the entire causal history of the universe to the Big Bang — or whatever the first state was.

This is not a trivially obvious claim, and it has not always been assumed to be true. Newtonian mechanics was widely interpreted as supporting determinism: given the positions and momenta of all particles at one time, the equations of motion uniquely determine the positions and momenta at every other time. But quantum mechanics, on standard interpretations, is indeterministic: the outcome of a quantum measurement is not determined by any prior state, but only probabilistically constrained. Whether the universe is fundamentally deterministic or indeterministic is an open empirical question.

For the problem of free will, the question is not merely whether determinism is true, but what its implications would be if it were. The incompatibilist holds that determinism is incompatible with free will: if your action is the inevitable result of prior causes, you could not have done otherwise, and if you could not have done otherwise, you are not free. The compatibilist holds that determinism is compatible with free will: freedom is not about being uncaused but about the right kind of causal history — actions caused by one's own desires, values, and deliberation are free even if those desires and values were themselves determined.

This section examines the content of the determinism thesis, its empirical status, and the preliminary question of what it would mean for free will if determinism were true.
