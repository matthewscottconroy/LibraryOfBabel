# Kinds of Determinism

Determinism is not a single thesis but a family of related claims, and conflating them generates unnecessary confusion. The word "determined" is used freely in philosophy, theology, law, and popular science — often to mean quite different things. Before we can assess whether determinism threatens free will, we need to know which determinism we have in mind.

## Causal Determinism and Its Neighbors

*Causal determinism* — the thesis that every event is necessitated by prior events together with the laws of nature — is the version most relevant to contemporary free will debates. Its classical statement is Laplace's: a sufficiently powerful intellect, knowing the complete state of the universe at one moment and all the laws governing it, could calculate every subsequent state exactly. Notice that this is stronger than simply saying that everything has a cause. Causal determinism says that prior causes, together with laws, *necessitate* their effects: given those causes and those laws, the effect could not fail to occur. Universal causation is a necessary condition for causal determinism but not a sufficient one.

*Logical determinism* starts from a quite different source — the logic of tensed propositions. Aristotle's discussion of the sea battle in *De Interpretatione* is the locus classicus: if it is already true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then the battle is in some sense settled. Is it inevitable? Most philosophers deny that logical determinism carries the same implications for free will as causal determinism. The truth of a proposition about the future does not by itself imply that any causal force has made the future inevitable — propositions can be true without there being a causal mechanism that makes them so. Aristotle's own proposed solution, denying bivalence for future contingent statements, has remained controversial, but the majority view is that logical and causal determinism are distinct claims with distinct implications.

*Theological determinism* — divine foreordination — holds that God has foreordained all events, including all human actions. The parallel with secular causal determinism is close: if God foreordained that Jones would sin, Jones was going to sin regardless of anything Jones does. But then how can Jones be responsible? Defenders of theological compatibilism — Aquinas, Molina, many Reformed theologians — argue that divine foreordination is compatible with the kind of freedom required for moral responsibility, just as causal determinism may be compatible with freedom on secular accounts. Hard theological determinists, by contrast, accept that foreordination and libertarian free will are incompatible and embrace the former. Notice also a further distinction within theological frameworks: *foreknowledge* (God knows what will happen) is sometimes distinguished from *foreordination* (God has caused it to happen). Classical theism typically collapses this distinction, but it matters for evaluating how the theological problem maps onto the secular one.

*Psychological determinism* is a more local thesis: all human choices and actions are causally necessitated by prior psychological states — desires, beliefs, character, and history. This may be true even if strict physical determinism is false, since quantum indeterminism might average out at the level of neural processes, leaving psychological regularities effectively deterministic. Conversely, psychological determinism might be false even in a physically deterministic world, if the psychological level admits of multiple realizations. For free will purposes, psychological determinism is typically the immediately relevant version, because the question is not whether quantum particles behave deterministically but whether human deliberation and choice are governed by prior psychological states.

*Biological determinism* holds that behavior is necessitated by genetic factors. This is a narrower empirical claim about the relative weight of genetic versus environmental influences. Contemporary behavioral genetics shows that genetic factors explain substantial variance in behavioral traits, but "genetic determinism" in the strong form — behavior fixed by the genome — is not the consensus view. Even weaker versions of genetic determinism, which merely claim that genes strongly constrain behavioral outcomes, do not generate the free will problem in its standard form. The stronger thesis would have to be that genes necessitate decisions, which is not what the science shows.

## Why the Distinctions Matter

For free will debates, the most important form is causal determinism as it applies to the psychological processes underlying choice. But the taxonomy matters for evaluating arguments. Van Inwagen's Consequence Argument targets causal determinism specifically. Galen Strawson's basic argument does not require determinism at all — it is directed at the more general condition that an agent cannot be ultimately responsible for a character she did not choose. Pereboom's four-case argument is constructed to show that whether or not determinism holds, free will in the relevant sense is unavailable. Understanding which claim each argument targets is essential for assessing the dialectic.

Across these variants, the general structure of the incompatibilist position takes a recognizable form:

- P1: Free will requires that the agent be the ultimate source of her actions.
- P2: If [causal / psychological / theological] determinism is true, the agent is not the ultimate source — prior conditions determine what the agent does.
- C: Therefore, if [the relevant form of] determinism is true, the agent lacks free will.

Compatibilists typically challenge P2 on behalf of causal and psychological determinism: being determined by one's own reasons and character is precisely what makes an action genuinely the agent's own. It is not the absence of causal determination that constitutes freedom, on this view, but the right *kind* of determination — determination running through the agent's rational capacities rather than around them. Incompatibilists reply that unless there was something genuinely open — unless the prior conditions left room for the agent to have done otherwise — the agent is not the ultimate source in any sense that matters. That exchange is the beating heart of the contemporary free will debate, and getting clear about which determinism is in play is the first step toward engaging it honestly.
