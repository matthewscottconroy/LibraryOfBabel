# Determinism and Science

The relationship between determinism and science has been considerably more complicated than the popular story suggests. A certain picture took hold during the Enlightenment and persisted well into the twentieth century: science reveals a clockwork universe, and so science supports determinism. That picture is wrong — or at least it is much harder to defend than it appears. Any serious engagement with the free will debate has to reckon with what the sciences actually say, rather than with a simplified image of what they are supposed to say.

## The Classical Inheritance

Newtonian mechanics really is paradigmatically deterministic. Given the positions and momenta of all particles at a time, Newton's laws uniquely determine all future and past states. This was the basis for Laplace's demon and for the widespread nineteenth-century assumption that science ratifies a deterministic worldview. T. H. Huxley's argument in "On the Hypothesis That Animals Are Automata" (1874) is representative: once we accept that human beings are complex biological machines, consciousness appears to be an epiphenomenon — a shadow cast by neural processes that does no causal work. The "will" we experience as free is not a cause but a byproduct. This is classical scientific hard determinism in its starkest form.

The image of the clockwork universe was philosophically seductive. Once initial conditions and laws are fixed, everything that follows is settled — including deliberation. But quantum mechanics, which replaced classical mechanics as the fundamental physical theory early in the twentieth century, undermined this picture fundamentally.

## Quantum Mechanics and Genuine Indeterminism

On the standard Copenhagen interpretation, quantum processes are genuinely indeterministic. When a quantum system is measured, the outcome is a probabilistic result that cannot in principle be predicted with certainty even from a complete specification of the prior state. The Heisenberg uncertainty principle sets fundamental limits on the precision with which conjugate variables — position and momentum — can be simultaneously specified, and these limits are not merely epistemic but reflect the structure of reality itself. Two identically prepared quantum systems can, when measured, yield different outcomes. The mathematics of quantum mechanics gives probabilities, not certainties.

This appears to falsify strict causal determinism at the fundamental physical level. But it would be a mistake to read this as a simple victory for the libertarian. Several complications arise immediately.

First, *hidden variable theories* attempt to restore determinism at a deeper level. David Bohm's pilot wave interpretation posits that quantum particles have definite positions at all times, guided by a wave function, with apparent indeterminism resulting from ignorance of the underlying definite state. Bell's theorem shows that local hidden variable theories cannot reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics, but non-local hidden variable theories remain viable. Whether determinism can be recovered at the cost of non-locality is an open question.

Second, one can accept quantum indeterminism while arguing that it is irrelevant to human decision-making. Neural processes and macroscopic objects are effectively deterministic because quantum indeterminism averages out at larger scales — the law of large numbers produces stable macro-level regularities even in a quantum-indeterministic world. On this view, quantum mechanics falsifies strict physical determinism but leaves psychological determinism largely intact for all practical purposes. The indeterminism occurs at scales far below those relevant to deliberation.

Third, the many-worlds interpretation (Everett) holds that quantum mechanics is actually deterministic in a larger sense: at each apparent "measurement," the universe branches into multiple branches, each realizing a different outcome. The appearance of indeterminism is the result of our being located in one branch among many. The universal wavefunction evolves deterministically. Whether this restores cosmic determinism or merely relocates the problem is contested.

## Indeterminism and the Free Will Problem

Even granting genuine quantum indeterminism, a fundamental challenge remains: random quantum events do not obviously provide the kind of agent-controlled openness that free will requires. Consider van Inwagen's point directly: if my decision to raise my hand results from a random quantum fluctuation in my brain, this does not make the decision *mine* in any robust sense — it makes it the result of physical chance, which seems no better than physical necessity. Indeterminism introduces randomness, not control.

This is the *luck objection* applied at the level of physics: indeterminism introduces an element of chance into the causal story, but the element of chance is not supplied by the agent. Libertarians who invoke quantum mechanics must explain how genuinely indeterministic neural processes can constitute the kind of self-determination that free will requires — not merely how they create a gap in the deterministic chain. Robert Kane's attempt to answer this challenge, discussed later, is the most sustained effort available.

## Chaos Theory and Effective Unpredictability

Chaos theory adds a further complication that is worth noting. Even in classical mechanics, sensitive dependence on initial conditions means that small perturbations produce large divergences over time. Chaotic systems — weather patterns, certain fluid flows, multi-body gravitational systems — are technically deterministic but practically unpredictable beyond short time horizons. Lorenz's butterfly effect is the standard illustration: a butterfly's wing-beat can, in principle, alter whether a hurricane forms weeks later.

Chaotic systems raise a question about what determinism means for free will in practice. If the future is deterministic but unpredictable in principle (because quantum indeterminism affects initial conditions, and chaotic dynamics amplify this), then the difference between a deterministic and an indeterministic world may matter very little for everyday deliberation. We deliberate under uncertainty in either case. Whether this observation supports or challenges compatibilism depends on whether what matters for freedom is metaphysical openness or practical uncertainty — a distinction that runs through the entire debate.

## Neuroscience and Its Limits

Contemporary neuroscience adds a further dimension without settling the underlying question. Brain processes are complex, involving trillions of synaptic connections and electrical signals. Whether these processes are fundamentally deterministic, effectively deterministic, or genuinely indeterministic at a level relevant to decision-making is not settled empirically. The Penrose-Hameroff hypothesis that quantum indeterminism in neural microtubules plays a role in consciousness remains highly controversial and is not mainstream in either neuroscience or physics.

What neuroscience does establish is that human decisions have neural correlates — brain states that precede and predict conscious awareness of a decision. This is the finding at the center of Libet's experiments, discussed in a later chapter. It is not the same as showing that decisions are determined, but it complicates naive pictures of deliberate agency. The philosophical question is how to understand the relationship between neural determinants of behavior and the concept of agency that moral responsibility requires.

The upshot is this. The relationship between science and determinism is not a simple alignment. Neither classical physics nor quantum mechanics straightforwardly settles the philosophical debate about free will. The philosophical questions — about the right analysis of "could have done otherwise," about what kind of causal history is sufficient for genuine agency, about the conditions for moral responsibility — must be addressed through philosophical argument, constrained by but not reducible to the empirical sciences. Science tells us what kind of physical processes we are. What it cannot tell us, without philosophical interpretation, is whether those processes are the sort that make us genuinely free.
