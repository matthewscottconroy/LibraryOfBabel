# The Basic Incompatibilist Argument

Imagine you trace any action you have ever performed back through its causal history: back through the neural events that immediately preceded it, back through the events that shaped your character and desires, back through the circumstances of your childhood, back before your birth to the state of the world at a time when you did not exist. If causal determinism is true, that chain is unbroken. The state of the world before you existed, together with the laws of physics, necessitated everything that followed — including what you did this morning. You had no control over the state of the world before your birth. You had no control over the laws of physics. And it seems to follow that you had no control over what you did this morning.

This is the incompatibilist intuition. Its strength comes precisely from its simplicity: it seems to require nothing beyond the definition of determinism and a few plausible principles about what control requires.

## The Argument and Its Premises

We can put the argument in explicit form:

- P1: If determinism is true, every action is necessitated by prior events and laws of nature.
- P2: The prior events and laws of nature that necessitate an agent's actions are not within the agent's control.
- P3: If an action is necessitated by factors entirely outside the agent's control, the agent has no control over the action.
- P4: An agent acts freely only if the agent has control over the action.
- C: Therefore, if determinism is true, no agent acts freely.

Each premise commands substantial initial plausibility. P1 is definitional. P2 seems undeniable: no one controls events that occurred before her birth, and no one controls the laws of physics. P4 articulates a minimal condition for freedom — the connection between freedom and control is not itself controversial. The weight of the argument rests on P3, which captures a transfer principle: if you have no power over X, and X necessitates Y, then you have no power over Y either. We can call this the *transfer of powerlessness*.

The argument is further reinforced by the *Principle of Alternate Possibilities* (PAP): an agent is morally responsible for an action only if the agent could have done otherwise. The intuitive basis for PAP is deep. If a gunman presses a weapon to my head and I hand over my wallet, I am not blameworthy for the transfer — I had no other option. When compulsion is total, responsibility is extinguished. Combined with the claim that determinism eliminates alternate possibilities, we get a parallel argument:

- P1: Determinism eliminates alternate possibilities (given determinism, the agent could not have done other than she did).
- P2: Moral responsibility requires alternate possibilities (PAP).
- C: Therefore, determinism eliminates moral responsibility.

Compatibilists have challenged both arguments in different ways. Against the first, they deny that control requires what P3 assumes; they argue that control consists in the right kind of causal connection between the agent's reasons and her action, not in the absence of prior necessity. Against the PAP-based argument, they challenge the interpretation of "could have done otherwise" — arguing that it should be read conditionally, as "would have done otherwise if the agent had chosen to" — or they attack PAP directly through Frankfurt cases, discussed later. Frankfurt cases attempt to show that an agent can be morally responsible even when she could not have done otherwise, which, if successful, pulls the rug out from under the second argument entirely.

## The Historical Context

The incompatibilist intuition is ancient. The Stoics were determinists who struggled to reconcile fate with the distinction between what is "up to us" and what is not. Chrysippus's compatibilist cylinder analogy was an attempt to hold both commitments at once; his critics, including the Epicureans, held that the attempt was incoherent and that genuine freedom required escape from the deterministic chain altogether. Epicurus invoked the *clinamen* — a random swerve in atomic motion — as a physical basis for that escape. This move, and the objection that randomness is not the same as freedom, has been replayed in every generation since.

In the early modern period, the incompatibilist intuition receives its most rigorous philosophical statement in Kant. Kant held that if the will is part of the natural causal order, it is determined and not free; genuine freedom requires that the will be a noumenal capacity standing outside empirical causation altogether. For Kant, freedom and causal determination are incompatible not merely as a matter of contingent physics but in principle. Whatever else one thinks of Kant's solution — and we will have occasion to examine its difficulties — his diagnosis of the problem has defined the terrain for all subsequent discussion.

## What the Argument Requires

The crucial move is P3 — the transfer principle. Van Inwagen later formalized this as the *Beta* principle: if agent S has no control over proposition P, and P entails Q, then S has no control over Q. The principle underlies his Consequence Argument and captures the intuition that powerlessness propagates across entailment relations. I have no control over the laws of physics. The laws plus the initial state of the universe entail what I will do tomorrow. So I have no control over what I will do tomorrow.

Compatibilists challenge the transfer principle, arguing that it is ambiguous between a reading that is true but does not apply to the free will case and a reading that would do the required work but is not clearly true. David Lewis pressed this objection directly, and the resulting debate about the interpretation of "can" and "could have done otherwise" has been among the most technically productive in analytic philosophy.

We can identify four main strategies for resisting the basic incompatibilist argument. First, reject P3: deny that powerlessness always transfers across entailment. Second, reject P4: hold that freedom does not require control in the sense the argument assumes, but only the right kind of causal history running through the agent's own reasons and character. Third, attack PAP directly through Frankfurt cases, severing the connection between alternate possibilities and moral responsibility. Fourth, accept the argument's conclusion about ordinary causal determination but locate freedom at a different level — as the Stoics located it in rational assent, or as Kant located it in the noumenal will.

Each of these strategies generates a distinct position in the free will debate, and each has been developed with considerable sophistication. What is important to recognize at the outset is that no response is free: each involves giving up something that initially seemed plausible. The incompatibilist argument is not a puzzle that can be dissolved by a clever observation. It is a serious problem, and the positions arrayed against it represent genuine intellectual costs carefully weighed.
