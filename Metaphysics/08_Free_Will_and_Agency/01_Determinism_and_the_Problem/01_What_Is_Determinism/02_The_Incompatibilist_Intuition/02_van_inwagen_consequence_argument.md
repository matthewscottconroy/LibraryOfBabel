# Van Inwagen's Consequence Argument

Before Peter van Inwagen's *An Essay on Free Will* (1983), the incompatibilist position was often stated impressionistically: if everything is determined by prior causes, how can we be genuinely free? Van Inwagen's achievement was to give that intuition rigorous form — to transform what had been a philosophical sensibility into a precise argument whose individual premises could be separately examined and challenged. The result, the Consequence Argument, is the most influential formulation of incompatibilism in the contemporary literature.

The core idea is simple. Determinism says that the consequences of the laws of nature and the past are everything that will ever happen. The past and the laws are not up to us. Therefore the consequences of the past and the laws — which is to say, everything that will ever happen — are not up to us either. We need to make this precise.

## The Notation and Principles

Van Inwagen introduces the symbol N(p) to mean: *p is true, and no one has, or ever had, any choice about whether p is true*. This captures non-accidental necessity from the agent's standpoint — the proposition is true, and no one is in any position to render it false.

He articulates two inference rules governing N:

- **(Alpha)**: If p is a necessary truth, then N(p). Whatever is necessarily true is not something anyone has any choice about.
- **(Beta)**: If N(p) and N(p → q), then N(q). If no one has a choice about p, and no one has a choice about the connection between p and q, then no one has a choice about q either.

With these in place, the argument can be stated precisely.

## The Argument

Let:
- P0 = the complete physical state of the world at some time in the remote past (before any human beings existed).
- L = the conjunction of all laws of nature.
- P = any proposition describing an action A performed by an agent S at some later time T.

If determinism is true, then necessarily: (P0 & L) → P.

- Premise 1: N(P0). No one has, or ever had, any choice about the state of the world before any humans existed.
- Premise 2: N(L). No one has, or ever had, any choice about the laws of nature.
- Premise 3: Determinism is true — that is, necessarily, (P0 & L) → P.
- Premise 4: N((P0 & L) → P). The entailment holds necessarily given determinism, so no one has a choice about it.
- Premise 5: By applying Beta to Premises 1 and 2: N(P0 & L). No one has a choice about the conjunction of the past state and the laws.
- Premise 6: By applying Beta to Premises 5 and 4: N(P). No one has a choice about P — about S's performing A at T.
- Conclusion: If determinism is true, no one has any choice about any of their actions.

The virtue of this formulation is its precision. It forces everyone in the debate to be specific about which step they are rejecting and why.

## Why the Argument Is Significant

The Consequence Argument makes no assumptions about the metaphysics of freedom beyond what is built into the N operator. It does not assume libertarian agent causation, does not require a soul, and does not depend on any particular scientific picture of the world. It is a purely conceptual argument: given determinism and given plausible principles about the transfer of "having no choice," the conclusion follows that we have no choice about anything we do.

Notice also what the argument achieves for the dialectic. It forces the compatibilist to identify exactly what kind of "control" or "choice" is relevant for freedom and to locate where that kind of control fits within the argument. If the compatibilist says "I have a choice about my action in some sense," she must explain which premise denies this — and that explanation will constitute her positive account of what freedom consists in.

## Objections to the Beta Principle

Most compatibilist responses focus on Beta. Critics argue that Beta is either false in some formulations or ambiguous between readings.

*Lewis's objection*: David Lewis distinguishes two senses of "can render false":

- **Strong sense**: S can render P false compatibly with the actual laws of nature and the actual remote past.
- **Weak sense**: There is a possible world in which S renders P false and the actual laws hold — though the past may differ from the actual past.

Van Inwagen writes Beta assuming the strong sense, and on the strong sense it is plausible. But Lewis argues that compatibilism requires only the weak sense: the agent can do otherwise in the sense that there is a possible world with the same laws in which her past is different and she acts differently. The agent's freedom depends on her abilities and capacities, not on whether the actual past is fixed.

Van Inwagen responds that the weak sense is too weak to ground moral responsibility. The weak sense says the agent can do otherwise only in a world where the determining past is different — which means the agent has no power to make the past different in the actual world. Freedom must be grounded in something about the *actual* world, not merely in the existence of possible worlds.

*Widderker's objection*: David Widderker argues that Beta has counterexamples. Even if no one has a choice about P, and no one has a choice about (P → Q), it does not follow that no one has a choice about Q if the agent's own action is what makes P entail Q. The transfer principle can fail when the agent's agency is built into the structure of the entailment itself.

*The McKay-Johnson objection*: Thomas McKay and David Johnson argue that combining multiple applications of Beta can generate conclusions that don't follow from the individual applications — there are agglomerative failures of the principle. Van Inwagen has proposed *Beta-2*, a refined version designed to avoid these objections, but the revised principle faces its own counterexamples.

## The Dialectical Situation

Van Inwagen has consistently maintained that no satisfactory reply to the Consequence Argument is available to the compatibilist. In his "Free Will Remains a Mystery" (2000), he acknowledged that he cannot fully identify what is wrong with compatibilist responses, yet maintains the conviction that if his choices were necessitated by factors outside his control reaching back before his birth, he would not be free. The argument thus operates on two levels: the formal demonstration and the underlying intuition of powerlessness.

The contemporary situation is roughly this: the Consequence Argument is widely regarded as a genuine challenge to compatibilism, but equally widely regarded as a challenge that sophisticated compatibilism can meet — at the cost of revising some intuitive notions of "could have done otherwise." Compatibilists have increasingly moved away from trying to answer the argument directly and toward claiming that the kind of control the argument shows to be absent — what Fischer and Ravizza call *regulative control* — is not the kind of control that matters for freedom and responsibility. What matters, they argue, is *guidance control*: acting from one's own reasons-responsive mechanism. If that distinction can be sustained, the Consequence Argument may show something true but irrelevant. Whether the distinction is sustainable without begging the question against the incompatibilist remains the central open question.
