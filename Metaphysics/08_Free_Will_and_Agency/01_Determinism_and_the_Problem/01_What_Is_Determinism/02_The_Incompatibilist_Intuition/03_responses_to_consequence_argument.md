# Responses to the Consequence Argument

Van Inwagen's Consequence Argument has generated one of the richest debates in analytic philosophy. The richness comes from the argument's precision: because each step is explicit, each can be separately challenged, and each challenge generates a distinct philosophical position. Understanding the responses requires tracking which premise or principle is being targeted and whether the resulting account can handle problems elsewhere.

## The Conditional Analysis Response

The historically oldest compatibilist response reads "could have done otherwise" as a conditional: "would have done otherwise, had the agent willed or chosen differently." On this reading, determinism does not eliminate alternate possibilities in the morally relevant sense, because the conditional can be true even in a deterministic world. An agent acted freely if she would have done otherwise had her desires been different — even if, given her actual desires, she was determined to do what she did.

Van Inwagen's reply raises the question one level. Could the agent have *chosen* otherwise? If choice is itself determined, the conditional analysis must be applied again: "would have chosen otherwise, had she had different second-order desires." But this regress raises the question of whether any level of the analysis captures genuine freedom. Moreover, the conditional can be satisfied even in cases of apparent unfreedom: a hypnotized agent might satisfy the conditional while intuitively not being free, because the hypnosis fixes both her choice and her counterfactual choices. The conditional analysis tracks something real — the connection between will and action — but cannot carry the full weight of freedom. Its failure drove compatibilism toward the more sophisticated accounts that followed.

## The Ambiguity of "Can"

A related response argues that "can" is used in different senses across the premises of the Consequence Argument, and that the argument equivocates. Consider:

- *Ability reading*: S has the physical and psychological capacity to do X, independently of external obstacles.
- *Possibility reading*: Doing X is a causally open outcome at the actual world — there is a possible continuation of history from the actual state in which S does X.

On the ability reading, determinism is compatible with freedom: a determined agent may still have all the capacities required for various actions. On the possibility reading, determinism eliminates alternate possibilities in a strong sense. The incompatibilist needs the possibility reading for the argument to succeed, but the ability reading may be the one that is actually relevant for moral responsibility. Van Inwagen acknowledges the distinction but insists that what matters for responsibility is the possibility reading: if it was not causally possible for the agent to do otherwise given the actual past and laws, the action was not "up to her" in any sense that matters.

## Lewis's Challenge to Beta

David Lewis's response targets the Beta principle directly, pressing the distinction between two senses of "can render false" discussed in the preceding file. Lewis argues that compatibilism requires only the weak sense — there is a possible world with the same laws in which the agent's past is different and she acts differently. Van Inwagen replies that the weak sense is too weak: freedom must be grounded in something about the actual world, not in possible worlds with different pasts. The dispute over how to read "can" has generated an extensive literature without decisive resolution.

## Widderker, McKay, and Johnson on Transfer

David Widderker and others argue that Beta has counterexamples independent of the ability/possibility distinction. Consider: no one has a choice about the fact that the sun will rise tomorrow. No one has a choice about the entailment from "the sun will rise tomorrow" to "if Jones stays indoors, the sun will rise tomorrow." Does it follow that Jones has no choice about whether to stay indoors? Obviously not — Jones has a clear choice about staying indoors, even though the resulting conditional is trivially entailed by something no one controls. The point is that the structure of entailment can generate trivially true propositions whose component parts nevertheless involve the agent's genuine choices. The transfer fails when the agent's agency is built into the entailed proposition.

McKay and Johnson press a different concern: when multiple applications of Beta are chained together, the principle can fail for agglomerative reasons even if each individual application looks valid. Van Inwagen proposed Beta-2 to avoid this, but the revision faces its own counterexamples, and the resulting debate has clarified but not resolved the underlying issue.

## The Sourcehood Response

A different class of responses does not challenge the Consequence Argument directly but argues that it targets the wrong notion of freedom. On *sourcehood* compatibilism, what matters for free will and moral responsibility is not whether the agent had alternate possibilities but whether the action springs from the agent's own reasons, values, and character in the right way.

John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza distinguish *regulative control* — the ability to do otherwise — from *guidance control* — acting from a reasons-responsive mechanism that is genuinely the agent's own. The Consequence Argument, if sound, shows that determinism undermines regulative control. Fischer and Ravizza concede this. But they argue that moral responsibility requires only guidance control, not regulative control. The argument therefore establishes something true but irrelevant.

This response moves the dialectic significantly. Rather than contesting van Inwagen's premises, it contests the relevance of his conclusion. The incompatibilist must then argue that regulative control is required for moral responsibility — a claim that Frankfurt cases put under direct pressure, as we will see.

## The Incompatibilist Who Accepts the Argument

Some philosophers accept the Consequence Argument and diverge on what to do with it. Libertarians — van Inwagen himself, Robert Kane, Timothy O'Connor — hold that the argument succeeds, incompatibilism is true, and free will therefore requires indeterminism. Their project is to develop positive accounts of how indeterministic causation can ground genuine freedom rather than mere randomness. Hard incompatibilists — Derk Pereboom, Galen Strawson — accept the argument and go further: no plausible account of how indeterminism grounds freedom is available, so free will of the kind that grounds basic desert moral responsibility does not exist. The Consequence Argument thus functions as a hinge point: compatibilists must reject it; libertarians must accept it and add something; hard incompatibilists must accept it and deny that anything adequate can be added.

## Assessment

The contemporary consensus is that the Consequence Argument represents a genuine challenge to compatibilism — one that simple conditional analyses cannot meet. Most sophisticated compatibilists have moved toward reasons-responsiveness and sourcehood accounts that try to show why determinism, even if it eliminates regulative control, does not undermine the kind of control that matters for freedom and responsibility. Whether this move ultimately succeeds depends on whether moral responsibility genuinely requires only sourcehood, or whether the absence of alternate possibilities generates a distinct problem that sourcehood accounts cannot solve. That question has not been settled, and the debate over it constitutes one of the most active research programs in contemporary analytic philosophy.
