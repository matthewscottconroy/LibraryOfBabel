# What Is Moral Responsibility?

When someone wrongs you deliberately — not accidentally, not under compulsion, but with clear eyes and apparent choice — your response is different from how you would respond if a storm damaged your property. You feel resentment, not mere displeasure. You hold the person *accountable*. You think they should answer for what they did, perhaps apologize, perhaps be punished. And when someone goes out of their way to help you, you feel genuine gratitude — not the gratitude you might feel toward good weather, but something directed specifically at them as the author of their kindness. This distinction — between reacting to a person as the author of an action and reacting to an event as a mere occurrence — marks the concept of moral responsibility. An agent is morally responsible for an action when she is an appropriate target of these responses: when praise, blame, gratitude, or resentment can be directed at her as the source.

## Kinds and Conditions

We should distinguish moral responsibility from nearby concepts. *Causal responsibility* is too broad: a tree that falls on someone is causally responsible for the injury, but not morally so. *Role responsibility* concerns duties attached to positions — a surgeon is responsible for patient safety in the sense of being obligated to secure it — which is a prospective notion rather than a retrospective one. Moral responsibility in the sense relevant to the free will debate is retrospective: it is the kind of answerability for *past* actions that makes praise, blame, punishment, and reward appropriate reactions.

A further distinction within moral responsibility, due to Gary Watson, matters enormously for what follows. *Attributability* is the relation that holds when an action can be attributed to an agent as an expression of her agency — when it flows from her evaluative commitments in a way that reveals who she is. *Accountability* is the stronger relation that grounds holding the agent to a standard, calling her to answer, and responding with reactive attitudes in their full force. We can attribute an action to an agent — recognize it as hers — without fully holding her accountable in the accountability sense: a child who lies can be recognized as the author of the lie without being subjected to adult moral sanctions.

The conditions that theorists have proposed for moral responsibility have clustered around several themes:

1. **The alternate possibilities condition**: the agent could have done otherwise (the Principle of Alternate Possibilities, PAP). Frankfurt cases, discussed later, put this condition under direct pressure.

2. **The epistemic condition**: the agent knew, or was in a position to know, what she was doing and its moral significance. Ignorance can excuse — but not all ignorance does. Negligent ignorance, ignorance one should have avoided, may leave responsibility intact.

3. **The freedom from compulsion condition**: the agent was not acting under compulsion or coercion that overwhelmed her rational agency. This is the oldest condition, present already in Aristotle's analysis of voluntary action.

4. **The reasons-responsiveness condition**: the agent was capable of recognizing and responding to reasons for action. This is the signature condition of contemporary accounts: an agent who cannot process reasons at all cannot be held responsible for acting in ignorance of them.

5. **The sourcehood condition**: the action must spring from the agent as its source — from her values, character, and reasons in the right way. This is the focus of source compatibilism and of manipulation arguments.

## Basic Desert vs. Attributability

Two quite different notions of what moral responsibility amounts to are frequently conflated, and the distinction is crucial for evaluating the stakes of the free will debate.

*Basic desert* moral responsibility holds that agents can deserve praise and blame in a deep sense that grounds retributive responses: the wrongdoer deserves to suffer for her wrongdoing, independently of any beneficial consequences that suffering might produce. This is the retributive conception that underwrites much ordinary moral practice and criminal justice. It is also the conception that hard incompatibilists like Derk Pereboom argue must be abandoned if we lack ultimate sourcehood.

*Attributability* responsibility is more modest: actions are attributable to agents as expressions of their will and character, which grounds reactive attitudes and practices of holding responsible, without necessarily implying that the agent deserves to suffer or flourish in a retributive sense. Pereboom himself accepts this weaker notion — he thinks actions remain attributable to agents, and that blame as a forward-looking practice remains appropriate, even if basic desert is unavailable.

Susan Wolf draws a related distinction. She argues that full moral responsibility — the kind that grounds both praise and blame — requires not just reasons-responsiveness but what she calls the ability to act in accordance with reason: the ability to recognize what is genuinely good and to respond to it. Her "Reason View" holds that freedom requires rational access to normative truth, not merely the psychological capacity to track reasons-as-perceived.

## The Aristotelian Foundation

Aristotle's analysis of voluntary action in the *Nicomachean Ethics* (Book III, chapters 1-5) has remained foundational. His conditions are two: an action is voluntary — and the agent responsible for it — when the source (*archê*) of the action is in the agent, and when the agent acts with knowledge of what she is doing and its relevant features.

External compulsion (*bia*) excuses when an external cause moves the agent against her will. Ignorance excuses when the agent does not know relevant facts — provided that ignorance is not itself culpable. Aristotle does not explicitly invoke "could have done otherwise," but his conditions imply something in that neighborhood: an agent who is the genuine source of her action, acting knowingly, could (in the relevant sense) have deliberated differently, since deliberation is itself responsive to reasons and character.

One feature of Aristotle's account is particularly important and often underappreciated: he holds that agents are responsible for their characters, not only their individual actions. If a person has developed vicious habits through her own choices, she is responsible for her character even if she can no longer easily act against it. The responsibility is retrospective — her character was formed through voluntary choices even if current action flows from it with something like necessity. This point will resurface when we consider manipulation arguments and the role of causal history in grounding responsibility.

## Contemporary Directions

Contemporary accounts have largely concentrated on the reasons-responsiveness condition and on what Fischer and Ravizza call "guidance control" — acting from a mechanism that is both the agent's own and responsive to reasons. The motivation is clear: reasons-responsiveness captures the connection between responsibility and rational agency that most theorists find compelling. We hold agents responsible for what they do precisely because they are the kinds of beings that can recognize and respond to reasons. When that capacity is absent or severely impaired — as in cases of severe mental illness, extreme compulsion, or manipulation that bypasses rational agency — responsibility is diminished or eliminated.

Whether free will in the sense required for moral responsibility demands the ability to have done otherwise (the alternate possibilities condition) or instead requires some form of authorship and self-determination (the sourcehood condition) is the central dispute dividing the contemporary literature. Frankfurt cases are designed precisely to drive a wedge between these two conditions, showing that the alternate possibilities condition can be false while responsibility remains. Whether that wedge succeeds — and whether sourcehood, without alternate possibilities, is genuinely sufficient — is the question we will be tracking through the rest of this module.
