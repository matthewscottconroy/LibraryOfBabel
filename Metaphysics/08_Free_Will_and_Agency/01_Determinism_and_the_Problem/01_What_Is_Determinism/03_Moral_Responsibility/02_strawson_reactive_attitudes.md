# Strawson and Reactive Attitudes

P. F. Strawson's 1962 paper "Freedom and Resentment" transformed the free will debate, and understanding why requires appreciating what kind of shift it represented. Debates up to Strawson had largely focused on a metaphysical question: given that determinism may be true, is there any kind of freedom that survives? Strawson changed the question. He asked instead: what would we actually have to give up if we accepted the incompatibilist's conclusion? And is there any compelling reason to think we should give it up?

The answer to the first question, Strawson argues, is that we would have to give up the reactive attitudes. The answer to the second is: no.

## Reactive Attitudes and Their Logic

Reactive attitudes are the responses we have to other persons insofar as we take them to be moral agents — members of the moral circle. Resentment is the response to injury or slight inflicted by another agent acting with ill will or disregard for one's interests. Gratitude is the response to benefit conferred by another agent acting with genuine concern. Indignation is the third-person analog of resentment — what we feel when we see someone else treated unjustly. Love involves commitment to another's interests and wellbeing as a person. Moral approbation and disapprobation are the more reflective evaluative responses to another's character.

What these attitudes have in common is that they are responses not to mere physical events but to the *will* of another agent — to the quality of care and regard that agent shows toward persons. Being stepped on carelessly provokes a different response than being stepped on deliberately, even if the physical effect is identical. The difference is entirely in what the action reveals about the other's will and regard. This is what Strawson means by saying that reactive attitudes are responses to the quality of will manifested in another's actions.

Strawson contrasts the reactive stance with what he calls the *objective stance*: treating someone as an object of management rather than a participant in moral relationships. When we take the objective stance toward a person — when we try to understand their behavior in clinical, third-personal terms, as we might understand the behavior of a machine or a patient — we suspend the reactive attitudes. We do not feel resentment toward the person; we ask what psychological or neurological factors explain their behavior, and what intervention is appropriate.

The objective stance is appropriate in specific circumstances: when someone is severely mentally ill, when someone is too young to be held responsible, when someone acts under compulsion that fully nullifies their agency. Crucially, these are *particular* features of individual situations. We adopt the objective stance when we have specific reasons to think a particular person's capacity for moral agency is compromised. We do not adopt it wholesale, toward everyone, on the basis of a general theoretical argument about causal necessity.

## Why Determinism Is Irrelevant

Strawson's key claim is that the truth or falsity of determinism is irrelevant to the justification of the reactive attitudes. He puts the argument this way:

- P1: The reactive attitudes are constitutive of our interpersonal moral practices — not optional additions that could easily be set aside.
- P2: The conditions under which it is appropriate to suspend the reactive attitudes — and adopt the objective stance — are specific features of an individual's situation: severe mental illness, compulsion, immaturity.
- P3: The truth of determinism is a general fact about all events, not a specific feature of any particular individual's situation.
- C: Therefore, the truth of determinism does not, by itself, provide grounds for suspending the reactive attitudes toward anyone.

There is also a reductio at work here. The fully skeptical conclusion — suspending all reactive attitudes toward everyone because determinism might be true — is practically absurd. We cannot coherently adopt the objective stance toward all persons simultaneously; the reactive attitudes are too deeply embedded in what it means to live as a person among other persons. The very relationships of love, friendship, and moral community that constitute human life are structured by these attitudes. Their wholesale abandonment would not be a philosophical achievement but a form of dissociation.

## Compatibilist Reception

Compatibilists found Strawson's approach liberating. If free will is analyzed in terms of the conditions under which reactive attitudes are appropriate, then the question is not about metaphysical causal necessity but about psychology and moral relationships. The agent who acts deliberately, understanding what she does, without compulsion or mental illness, is an appropriate target of reactive attitudes — and this appropriateness does not depend on whether her action was causally determined.

Fischer and Ravizza's reasons-responsiveness account can be read as making Strawson's insight precise. The conditions under which reactive attitudes are appropriate are the conditions under which the agent is reasons-responsive — a psychological condition that is compatible with determinism. Strawson gestured at this; Fischer and Ravizza articulate it.

## Incompatibilist Replies

Incompatibilists argue that Strawson misidentifies the question. Even if reactive attitudes are central to our lives, they may *presuppose* a metaphysical freedom that determinism undermines. If an agent's action was causally determined by factors entirely outside her control, the resentment we feel toward her is, in some sense, misdirected — she is no more genuinely responsible than a complicated machine that causes harm.

Susan Wolf presses this objection: Strawson's account makes the appropriateness of reactive attitudes independent of deep metaphysical facts about agency, but it is precisely those deep facts that matter. The justified resentment I feel when wronged presupposes that the person who wronged me could actually have done otherwise — not merely that she was reasons-responsive, but that she genuinely had an open alternative before her.

Galen Strawson (P. F. Strawson's son) challenges the analysis from the other direction. Even granting that reactive attitudes are central to moral life, the basic argument he develops shows that no one can be ultimately responsible in the deep sense that seems to be presupposed by those attitudes when they are held in their retributive form. The question is not whether reactive attitudes are important but whether they can be justified at the level of basic desert — and his answer is no.

## Legacy

Strawson's paper established what has become the dominant framework for compatibilist philosophy of moral responsibility. The relevant question, on this framework, is not about the metaphysics of causal necessity but about the conditions under which reactive attitudes are appropriate — and those conditions are psychological and relational rather than metaphysical. The paper also made vivid a point that has shaped all subsequent work: the free will problem cannot be isolated from questions about the nature and justification of our practices of holding responsible. It is not a puzzle about causation and necessity in isolation; it is a question about what kind of beings we are and what moral relationships among such beings can coherently look like. That expansion of the problem is Strawson's most lasting contribution.
