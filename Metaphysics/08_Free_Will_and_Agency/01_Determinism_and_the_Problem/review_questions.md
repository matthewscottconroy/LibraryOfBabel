# Review Questions — Determinism and the Problem

1. Causal determinism is often confused with fatalism, yet the chapter insists they are distinct. What exactly is the difference, and why does it matter for evaluating whether determinism threatens the kind of control agents actually exercise when they deliberate and choose?

2. Van Inwagen's Consequence Argument introduces the Beta principle — the transfer of powerlessness across entailment. David Lewis challenges Beta by distinguishing a strong and a weak sense of "can render false." Reconstruct that challenge: which sense does Lewis claim is relevant for freedom, and why does van Inwagen think the weak sense is too weak to ground moral responsibility?

3. The chapter distinguishes several kinds of determinism — causal, logical, theological, psychological, and biological. Why is psychological determinism often the more pressing version for the free will debate, even in a world where quantum mechanics has undermined strict physical determinism?

4. P. F. Strawson argues that the truth or falsity of determinism is irrelevant to the justification of reactive attitudes, while incompatibilists insist that reactive attitudes presuppose a metaphysical freedom determinism destroys. Who has the stronger position, and what does the disagreement reveal about what the reactive attitudes are actually tracking?

5. Aristotle holds that agents are responsible not only for individual actions but for their characters, even when character makes it practically difficult to act otherwise. How does this retrospective extension of responsibility bear on contemporary debates about manipulation and the sourcehood of action? Does it support compatibilism, incompatibilism, or neither?

6. Hume's argument in the chapter claims necessity is actually *required* for moral responsibility, not merely compatible with it. Spell out his reasoning carefully. Is this a genuine insight about responsibility, or does it simply redefine what is at issue in a way that avoids rather than answers the incompatibilist challenge?

7. The chapter notes that quantum mechanics undermines strict physical determinism but that this does not straightforwardly help the libertarian. Explain the luck objection as it applies to quantum indeterminism: why is random quantum fluctuation no better than deterministic necessity from the standpoint of grounding free agency?

8. Gary Watson's distinction between *attributability* and *accountability*, and the related concept of basic desert introduced in the moral responsibility section, reappear throughout the module. Why is that distinction so important for assessing what is really at stake in the free will debate — and which positions in subsequent chapters make the most use of it?
