# Compatibilism

Compatibilism holds that the freedom required for moral responsibility is perfectly compatible with determinism — that the correct analysis of free will shows it to be a matter of the right kind of internal causation, not of the indeterminism of prior causes. If this is right, the apparent incompatibility between determinism and free will dissolves: we can be determined and free, determined and responsible.

The classical compatibilist analysis, associated with Hume and Mill, identifies free will with the absence of external compulsion: you act freely when you act as you will, when your action flows from your own desires and intentions without external interference. The kleptomaniac steals compulsively, not by choice; the citizen cowed by a threat acts under duress. The ordinary person who deliberates and acts on her considered judgment acts freely, even if her deliberation was determined by prior causes. Determinism does not eliminate freedom; it is coercion that eliminates freedom, and coercion is not determinism.

Contemporary compatibilism has developed this classical picture in sophisticated directions. Harry Frankfurt's work on hierarchical desires distinguishes first-order desires (desires for things) from second-order desires (desires about one's desires) and volitions (second-order desires that one's first-order desires be effective). The person who acts freely, on Frankfurt's view, acts on a first-order desire that she endorses at the second-order level — a desire she identifies with, not one that she experiences as alien. Frankfurt cases — thought experiments involving neuroscientists who ensure a particular choice will be made but never need to intervene because the agent makes that choice anyway — challenge the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (that moral responsibility requires the ability to have done otherwise).

Manipulation arguments press back against compatibilism: if an agent was designed from birth by a neuroscientist to make exactly the choices she makes, she seems unfree even if she satisfies all the compatibilist conditions. The agent is deterministically caused to act as she does from the outside; her freedom seems illusory. This chapter develops compatibilism and these challenges with care, preparing the reader for the libertarian and hard incompatibilist responses that follow.
