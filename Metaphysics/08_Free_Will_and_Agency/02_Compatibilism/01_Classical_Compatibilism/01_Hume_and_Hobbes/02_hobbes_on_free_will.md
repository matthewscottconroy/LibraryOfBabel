# Hobbes on Free Will

Thomas Hobbes's treatment of free will appears most fully in his extended debate with Bishop John Bramhall, published as *Questions Concerning Liberty, Necessity, and Chance* (1654), and in *Leviathan* (1651). Hobbes is a thoroughgoing materialist: everything that exists is matter in motion, and thinking, willing, and desiring are motions of the brain and nervous system. There is no immaterial soul, no faculty of will that could stand outside the causal order. Given this starting point, the free will problem looks rather different than it does from within a dualist framework.

## Freedom as the Absence of External Impediment

From his materialist commitments, Hobbes concludes that asking whether the *will* is free is a category mistake. Freedom is a property of *agents* — persons in circumstances — not of mental events considered in the abstract. In *Leviathan* (Chapter 21), he defines a "FREE-MAN" as one "that in those things, which by his strength and wit he is able to do, is not hindered to do what he has a will to do." Notice what this definition contains: ability and absence of external obstruction. A prisoner lacks freedom because she is physically restrained. A person under coercion lacks freedom because a threat overrides her will. But a person who acts from her own desires and capacities, without external hindrance, is free — regardless of what caused those desires.

For Hobbes, the will is the last appetite in deliberation. When we deliberate, we run through a sequence of alternating appetites and aversions, each causing a kind of mental movement toward or away from the envisioned action. The deliberation terminates when the sequence ends — when one desire or aversion wins out and becomes the "last appetite," which is the will. The will is, on this account, the immediate causal antecedent of action. To speak of the "freedom of the will" is, for Hobbes, a confusion: desires are neither free nor unfree — they simply occur. What is free or unfree is the action that follows from the will, and this depends on whether external impediments prevent the action that the will tends toward.

## The Argument from Deliberation

Hobbes makes a distinctive argument: deliberation actually *presupposes* a kind of determinism rather than opposing it. The word "deliberation," he suggests, derives from "liberate" — to free from irresolution. When we deliberate, we are working through to a settled outcome. The process is causally governed by the balance of competing desires and reasons: the strongest desire wins. To suppose that genuinely free deliberation requires that its outcome be causally undetermined would be to suppose that the outcome is settled by something other than the deliberative process itself — which would make it not the result of deliberation at all, but of chance.

This argument anticipates a central criticism of libertarianism: if indeterminism means that the outcome of deliberation is not fixed by the deliberative process, then what settles it? The libertarian must say that the agent herself settles it — but this requires postulating something over and above the causal process of deliberation. The Hobbesian line, by contrast, is that what makes deliberation rational and meaningful is precisely its causal structure: deliberation tracks reasons, and the outcome it produces is the agent's own because the deliberative process was the agent's own.

## Responsibility and Causal History

Hobbes connects responsibility straightforwardly to the causal history of action. An action is the agent's own when it proceeds from the agent's will; the agent is responsible for actions that are her own:

- P1: An action is the agent's own when it proceeds from the agent's own will (desires, motivations).
- P2: An agent is responsible for an action when the action is the agent's own.
- P3: The causal determination of the agent's will by prior states does not make the will not the agent's own.
- C: Therefore, causal determination of action is compatible with moral responsibility.

Bramhall's objection anticipates incompatibilism: if the will is determined by prior causes — including the agent's own character and history, which were themselves determined — then ultimately the action traces back to factors beyond the agent's control. The agent is not the ultimate source; God or Nature is. This anticipates Galen Strawson's basic argument: ultimate responsibility is impossible if character is formed by factors outside one's control.

Hobbes's reply is characteristically blunt: "ultimate" responsibility in Bramhall's sense is a confused notion. Responsibility requires proximate causation by the agent's own will; it does not require that the agent be an uncaused cause of everything in her causal history. The demand for ultimate responsibility sets an impossible standard that no coherent conception of agency could satisfy. We hold agents responsible for what flows from their wills — that is all responsibility requires or could intelligibly require.

## The Political Dimension

An aspect of Hobbes's treatment that is sometimes overlooked is its political context. In *Leviathan*, the discussion of liberty appears in the context of civil liberty — the freedom of subjects to act as the law does not prohibit. Freedom is the absence of legal constraint, just as free will is the absence of physical constraint. The parallel is not accidental. Hobbes wants to ground moral and political responsibility in a single coherent notion of liberty — absence of impediment — without appealing to metaphysical freedom that could be invoked to justify resistance to sovereign authority.

This political dimension illuminates Hobbes's insistence that freedom requires only the absence of external impediment. A political philosophy that grounds freedom in libertarian free will faces a potentially destabilizing problem: if citizens are not ultimately responsible for their actions because those actions trace back to unchosen character and circumstances, the basis for legal and political accountability is undermined. Hobbes's compatibilist account preserves accountability while remaining consistent with a thoroughly naturalistic picture of human motivation.

## Legacy

Hobbes's compatibilism — freedom as the absence of external impediment — was enormously influential. Subsequent compatibilists extended and refined his account, but the basic insight survived: freedom is about the right kind of constraint — its absence — rather than the absence of causal determination. The contemporary debate about which kinds of internal states count as "unfreedom-generating" — addiction, phobia, compulsion, manipulation — is a development of precisely the question Hobbes raised: what kinds of internal factors are analogous to external impediments, and what kinds are simply expressions of the agent's own will? Frankfurt cases and manipulation arguments are, in a sense, attempts to draw that line with greater precision than Hobbes's account provided. Whether they succeed in drawing it is the question that occupies the rest of the compatibilism literature.
