# The Conditional Analysis of Freedom

Suppose you are sitting in your office, reading a philosophy paper, and someone asks whether you could have been doing something else. The natural answer is: of course — if you had wanted to take a walk, you could have taken a walk. The ability seems obvious, even though your being here rather than out walking is the result of your character, habits, and prior states that caused you to sit down with the paper. The conditional analysis of freedom tries to capture this intuition precisely: you could have done otherwise in the sense that you *would have* done otherwise if you had chosen to. The prior determination of your will does not prevent this conditional from being true.

This analysis was advocated by Hume, Mill, and Moore in various forms, and became the standard compatibilist move through the early twentieth century. G. E. Moore's formulation in *Ethics* (1912) is representative: "I could have done otherwise" means "I would have done otherwise if I had chosen otherwise." The freedom relevant to responsibility is a matter of the conditional relationship between volition and action, not a matter of whether alternative volitions were causally open given the actual past.

## The Formal Statement

Let CA be the conditional analysis:

- Agent S could have done A at time T if and only if: if S had chosen to do A at T, S would have done A at T.

Or for alternate possibilities:

- S could have done otherwise than X at T if and only if: if S had chosen to do otherwise than X at T, S would have done otherwise than X at T.

The analysis makes "could have done otherwise" compatible with determinism because the conditional can be true even if, given the actual prior state and laws, S was determined to make the choice S actually made. The counterfactual "if S had chosen otherwise" ranges over possible worlds where S's choice is different — not necessarily worlds where the actual past is preserved.

## What the Analysis Captures

The conditional analysis gets several cases right. Consider why a physically constrained agent is unfree: the conditional is false for the imprisoned agent — even if she had chosen to leave, she could not have left (the bars prevent it). Consider why a compelled agent is unfree: even if she had chosen not to act under compulsion, the compulsive force would have overridden her choice. In both cases, the conditional is false, and intuitively the agent was not free. For a normal agent acting on her desires, by contrast, the conditional is true — if she had wanted to do otherwise, she could have — and intuitively the agent was free.

The analysis also supports a plausible account of why causal determination does not itself undermine freedom: the fact that your desire to read the paper was caused by prior states does not make the conditional false. Given a different desire, you would have done something different. The causal determination runs *through* the conditional in a way that leaves it intact.

## The Principal Objections

Despite these successes, the conditional analysis faces two devastating objections that have driven subsequent compatibilism in different directions.

*Lehrer's case*: Suppose an agent would do A regardless of whether she chose to — she is hypnotized so that she will do A no matter what she chooses. In this case, the conditional "if she had chosen to do A, she would have done A" is trivially true, since she will do A regardless of her choice. But intuitively she is not free — the hypnosis is precisely what makes her unfree. The conditional analysis incorrectly classifies her as free. This shows that satisfying the conditional is not *sufficient* for freedom.

*The iterative regress*: Even if the agent would have acted otherwise had she chosen otherwise, could she have *chosen* otherwise? If the agent's choice is itself determined by prior states, the conditional analysis applied one level up says: S could have chosen otherwise if she would have chosen otherwise if she had willed to choose otherwise. This regress generates circularity and never locates any categorical freedom in the agent's psychology. C. D. Broad noted that at some point the conditional analysis must give way to a categorical ability — the agent must be able, in some non-conditional sense, to make different choices. But then the compatibilist has conceded the point.

Frankfurt cases present a third problem: they appear to show that an agent can be morally responsible even when the conditional is false. In a Frankfurt case, Black stands by with a device that will cause Jones to vote for A regardless of what Jones independently chooses. If Jones had chosen to vote for B, Black's device would have intervened — so it is not true that if Jones had chosen to vote for B, Jones would have voted for B. The conditional is false. Yet many philosophers judge that Jones is still responsible for his vote, since he voted for his own reasons without any intervention. This suggests that the conditional is not *necessary* for responsibility — and the conditional analysis loses its primary motivation.

## From Conditional Analysis to Reasons-Responsiveness

The failure of the simple conditional analysis pointed toward something important: freedom cannot be analyzed purely in terms of what the agent would do given a different choice, considered one action at a time. It requires something about the agent's broader dispositional profile — her capacity to track reasons across a range of circumstances. An agent who satisfies the conditional in normal cases but whose decision-making mechanism is structurally unresponsive to reasons is not free in the relevant sense, even when the conditional happens to be true.

This insight is realized in Fischer and Ravizza's reasons-responsiveness account. The mechanism from which the agent acts is reasons-responsive if, across a suitably wide range of possible circumstances, it would produce different actions in response to different reasons. This is not a simple conditional about what the agent would do given a different single choice — it is a claim about the agent's general sensitivity to reasons across a range. The failure modes of the conditional analysis are avoided: the hypnotized agent's mechanism is not reasons-responsive in the relevant way; and Frankfurt cases are accommodated because what matters is the agent's broader reasons-responsiveness, not any particular counterfactual about what she would have done.

The lesson of the conditional analysis debate, then, is that freedom cannot be captured by a single counterfactual. It requires a richer account of what it means for an action to be genuinely the agent's own — an account that will need to be both dispositional and historical. Contemporary compatibilism is, in large part, the project of developing that account.
