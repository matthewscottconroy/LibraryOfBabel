# Reasons-Responsive Compatibilism

The failures of the conditional analysis left compatibilists with a clear diagnosis: freedom cannot be captured by asking what the agent would have done given a different single choice, considered in isolation. The question needs to be widened. What matters is not any particular counterfactual but the agent's broader pattern of responsiveness to reasons — whether, across a range of circumstances, she tracks reasons appropriately. This dispositional shift is the core of reasons-responsive compatibilism, developed most fully by John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza in *Responsibility and Control: A Theory of Moral Responsibility* (1998).

## The Dispositional Turn

Consider why an addict who *could* act differently given a different choice might still not be free. The addictive craving that drives her choice is not sensitive to the reasons that bear on her choice — the reasons against stealing, say, that she herself acknowledges. Her mechanism is, in a sense, deaf to those reasons even when she can articulate them perfectly well. The conditional analysis might be satisfied (if she chose not to steal, she wouldn't steal), yet something has gone wrong. What has gone wrong is that the mechanism producing the choice is not the kind of mechanism that responds to reasons as reasons. This is precisely what the conditional analysis cannot capture and what reasons-responsiveness is designed to.

Fischer and Ravizza specify two conditions for acting freely in the relevant sense.

## Mechanism Ownership

The mechanism producing the action must be the agent's *own*. Fischer and Ravizza develop a detailed account of what ownership requires: the mechanism must have been acquired through a process of "taking responsibility" — a process in which the agent comes to recognize the mechanism as hers, understands that she is an appropriate target of reactive attitudes based on its operation, and endorses it as part of her practical identity. This is a historical condition: it concerns not just the current-time structure of the mechanism but how it was formed and appropriated.

This condition handles manipulation cases directly. A mechanism directly implanted by neuroscientists is not the agent's own, even if it satisfies all conditions of reasons-responsiveness, because the agent never went through the process of taking responsibility for it. The historical requirement distinguishes ordinary causal formation of character — which can produce a mechanism the agent genuinely owns — from manipulation, which cannot. Whether the distinction can be drawn without invoking incompatibilist assumptions is a contested question we will return to in the manipulation argument files.

## Reasons-Responsiveness: Receptivity and Reactivity

The mechanism must also be "moderately reasons-responsive." Fischer and Ravizza distinguish two dimensions:

- *Receptivity*: the mechanism must be able to recognize reasons. An agent whose mechanism cannot pick up reasons at all — who is entirely unresponsive to any reason for action — lacks the epistemic sensitivity required for responsible action.
- *Reactivity*: the mechanism must be responsive to recognized reasons in the sense that there are possible circumstances in which recognizing a sufficient reason would lead to a different action.

The account requires *strong receptivity* but only *weak reactivity*. An agent's mechanism is moderately reasons-responsive if across a suitably wide range of circumstances, the agent would recognize sufficient reasons and respond to them appropriately, and in at least one possible circumstance where there is a sufficient reason to act otherwise, the agent would act otherwise.

Why only weak reactivity? Because requiring that the agent respond to every reason in every circumstance would set an unrealistically demanding standard that not even paradigmatically responsible agents could meet. All that is required is that the mechanism is not entirely inflexible — that there is some possible world in which a sufficient reason leads to a different outcome.

## Guidance Control versus Regulative Control

Fischer and Ravizza draw a distinction that has become central to the contemporary debate:

- *Regulative control*: the ability to do otherwise — to take a different path. This is the control that van Inwagen's Consequence Argument shows to be absent in a deterministic world.
- *Guidance control*: acting from a mechanism that is reasons-responsive and the agent's own. This is the control that is sufficient for moral responsibility.

The central thesis: moral responsibility requires guidance control, not regulative control. This allows Fischer and Ravizza to accept the Consequence Argument's conclusion — that determinism eliminates regulative control — while maintaining that determinism is compatible with moral responsibility, because moral responsibility requires only guidance control.

This move is importantly different from trying to refute the Consequence Argument. Rather than arguing that regulative control is preserved under determinism, Fischer and Ravizza argue that regulative control is the wrong thing to look for in the first place. What matters for responsibility is whether the action flows from the agent's own reasons-responsive mechanism — and this is compatible with determinism.

## Frankfurt Cases Accommodated

The reasons-responsiveness account handles Frankfurt cases naturally. In the standard case, Jones votes for A while Black's device would have intervened had Jones been about to vote for B. Jones cannot do otherwise. But Jones acts from his own reasons-responsive mechanism: had there been a sufficiently compelling reason to vote for B — say, decisive evidence of A's corruption — Jones's mechanism would have been receptive to that reason and reactive in the appropriate way (in a world where Black's device is absent). Jones has guidance control even though he lacks regulative control. The Frankfurt case thus supports the account: it shows that the absence of regulative control — alternate possibilities — is compatible with genuine responsibility-grounding guidance control.

## Objections

*The mechanism individuation problem*: Which mechanism produced a given action? Mechanisms can be described at different levels of specificity, and the description matters. If we describe the mechanism narrowly — the specific neural configuration at the moment of action — it may not be reasons-responsive. If we describe it broadly — the agent's general deliberative system — it may be. Fischer and Ravizza need a principled account of which level of description is relevant.

*The tracing problem*: Some agents have present mechanisms that are not reasons-responsive as a result of past choices — an addict who chose to experiment with drugs and thereby created an addictive mechanism. Fischer and Ravizza argue for "tracing": responsibility can be traced back to earlier choices that led to the formation of the present mechanism, even if the present mechanism is not itself reasons-responsive. This is plausible but adds complexity, raising questions about how far back the tracing can go and what earlier choices must look like to sustain it.

*Is moderate reasons-responsiveness sufficient?*: Some philosophers worry that an agent might act from a mechanism that is reasons-responsive across a wide range while being, in the actual circumstances, entirely driven by compulsive forces. Fischer and Ravizza hold that responsibility attaches as long as the mechanism is moderately reasons-responsive in general — the actual circumstances need not display that responsiveness. Critics find this counterintuitive.

## Significance

Fischer and Ravizza's account has become the most developed and influential contemporary compatibilist account of moral responsibility. Its central insights — that responsibility requires an appropriate mechanism rather than alternate possibilities, and that the mechanism must be both the agent's own and reasons-responsive — have become reference points for all subsequent work. The account is not without vulnerabilities, particularly around manipulation and mechanism individuation, but it represents the most sophisticated statement of the compatibilist position, and engaging with it is unavoidable for anyone who wants to understand the contemporary debate.
