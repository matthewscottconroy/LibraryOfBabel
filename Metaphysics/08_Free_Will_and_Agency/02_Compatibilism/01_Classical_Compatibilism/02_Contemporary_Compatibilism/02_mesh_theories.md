# Mesh Theories of Free Will

Consider the phenomenon of addiction from the inside. The unwilling addict is moved by a desire for drugs that she despises. She wants the drug; she also wants not to want it. Her first-order desire and her higher-order attitude toward that desire are in conflict. The experience she reports is exactly one of unfreedom: she is doing what part of her wants but not what she, at a deeper level, endorses. Now consider, by contrast, the person who pursues a vocation she loves — who wants to write music, and is glad that she wants to write music, and whose working life is an expression of who she most deeply is. She acts from desires she endorses. Harry Frankfurt's hierarchical theory of free will, developed in his 1971 paper "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person," is an attempt to theorize this difference: freedom consists in a certain coherence or "mesh" between levels of desire.

## The Hierarchy of Desire

Frankfurt begins by distinguishing *wantons* from *persons*. A wanton is an agent who acts on first-order desires — whatever desire is strongest at the moment — without caring about which desire wins. A simple animal, on Frankfurt's picture, is a wanton: it acts on its strongest drive without any higher-order concern about which drives govern it. A person, by contrast, has desires about her desires — she has views about which first-order desires she wants to be moved by.

The taxonomy Frankfurt introduces:

- *First-order desires*: desires to do or not do various things. "I want coffee." "I want to quit smoking."
- *Second-order desires*: desires about one's first-order desires. "I wish I didn't want coffee so much." "I want to want to quit smoking."
- *Second-order volitions*: a special subset of second-order desires — desires that a particular first-order desire be one's *will* (the desire that actually moves one to action). "I want my desire to quit smoking to be the desire that moves me."

Second-order volitions are the key innovation. A second-order *desire* is relatively weak: I might wish I didn't want coffee without caring, at this moment, which desire actually governs my action. A second-order *volition* involves a stronger commitment: I want this particular desire to be my motivating will. Personhood, for Frankfurt, requires this capacity for second-order volition; freedom of will requires that the mesh condition be satisfied.

## The Mesh Condition

An agent has free will when her will — the first-order desire that actually moves her to action — *meshes* with her second-order volitions. She does what she wants to want to do. Her motivating desire is one she endorses at the higher level.

The *unwilling addict* illustrates unfreedom: she is moved by a desire for drugs that she does not want to be her will. The conflict between first-order desire and second-order volition is precisely what makes her feel enslaved by her addiction — not that she is doing something other than what she desires, but that the desire that moves her is not the desire she endorses. She is alienated from her own motivational life.

Frankfurt's formal account:

- S acts freely in doing X if and only if:
  1. S does X by virtue of a first-order desire D.
  2. S has a second-order volition V that D be S's will.
  3. D is in fact S's will — it is what actually moves S.
  4. The mesh condition holds: the desire that moves S is the desire S wants to be moved by.

Unfreedom occurs when the agent is moved by a desire she doesn't endorse, or when she is a wanton with respect to the relevant desire (no higher-order volitions at all), or when her higher-order volitions are frustrated by her actual motivational state.

The theory has several advantages. It captures addiction cases directly: the phenomenology of being controlled by a desire one despises maps exactly onto the failure of the mesh condition. It explains why physical coercion produces unfreedom: the coerced agent acts against her will — against her second-order volitions about how to act. And it draws a principled distinction between the unfree addict and the "willing addict" who endorses her addiction — who, if she has second-order volitions that her drug desire be her will, acts freely by Frankfurt's lights, however morally troubling this may seem.

## The Regress Problem

An immediate objection: if free will requires that first-order desire mesh with second-order volition, what ensures that the second-order volition is itself free? The second-order volition must mesh with a third-order volition, and so on — generating an infinite regress.

Frankfurt's response is to invoke the concept of *identification* or *wholehearted endorsement*: at some level, the agent simply identifies with a desire — she settles the question of what she wills without further higher-order endorsement being required. The regress terminates in identification, not in an infinite hierarchy.

This response faces pressure. What makes identification itself free, rather than just another psychological state that could be manipulated or compelled? Frankfurt's later work addresses this through the concept of *caring* — identification involves a kind of constitutive commitment to one's own practical identity that is not reducible to further desires. Whether this move succeeds in terminating the regress without circularity is contested.

## The Manipulation Objection

Suppose a neuroscientist manipulates an agent into having second-order volitions that mesh with her first-order desires. The agent now satisfies Frankfurt's conditions: she does what she wants to want to do. But the mesh was engineered. Intuitively, she is not free — the neuroscientist has manufactured the appearance of freedom. Frankfurt's account, focused as it is on the synchronic structure of desire rather than its history, seems unable to explain why.

Frankfurt acknowledges that the history of how a mesh was achieved matters. But his original account provides no principled way to distinguish a mesh that arose authentically from one that was manufactured. This gap has motivated the turn toward historical and sourcehood accounts: Fischer and Ravizza's mechanism ownership condition, Watson's account of the agent's "deep self," and Mele's conditions on authentic character formation all represent attempts to add the historical dimension that Frankfurt's original theory lacked.

## Susan Wolf's Extension

Susan Wolf's *Freedom Within Reason* (1990) extends and criticizes the Frankfurt framework. Wolf argues that freedom requires not merely mesh between first-order and higher-order desires but responsiveness to *normative* reasons — what is genuinely good. An agent who identifies with a desire to do evil, whose second-order volitions endorse her first-order evil desires, is not free in the fullest sense because she lacks access to genuine reasons. Her higher-order volitions align with her first-order desires, but both are oriented away from the good.

Wolf's asymmetry thesis: we hold agents fully responsible for bad actions only when they could have recognized and responded to the genuine reasons against those actions. An agent whose psychological constitution makes moral insight impossible is less than fully blameworthy. This modification pushes compatibilism in a substantive direction: freedom requires not just the right structure of desires but genuine rational access to normative truth. The freedom that grounds full responsibility is not just motivational integration but rational self-governance in a normatively loaded sense.
