# Source Compatibilism

Frankfurt cases, if they succeed, establish something striking: an agent can be morally responsible for an action even when she could not have done otherwise. If that is right, the Principle of Alternate Possibilities is false, and the standard incompatibilist argument from determinism to the absence of responsibility loses a crucial premise. But Frankfurt cases also suggest something positive, not merely negative. Jones voted for A for his own reasons, from his own deliberative process, without any intervention by Black's device. The action was his — it sprang from his own agency. What grounds responsibility, the Frankfurt case implies, is not alternate possibilities but something about the source of the action in the agent's own agency. This positive suggestion is the foundation of source compatibilism.

## The Core Claim

Source compatibilism holds that what matters for free will and moral responsibility is not whether the agent had alternate possibilities but whether the action springs from the agent's own values, character, and reasons in the right way. The relevant concept is authorship or self-determination — a kind of ownership of action that is compatible with causal determination. If determinism is true, then whether an action is free depends not on the modal question (could she have done otherwise?) but on the historical and structural question (did this action express her own agency?).

## Watson's Valuational System Account

Gary Watson, in his influential 1975 paper "Free Agency," distinguishes two systems within the agent's psychology:

- *The valuational system*: the agent's values, commitments, and judgments about what is worth pursuing — what the agent cares about and endorses as good.
- *The motivational system*: the agent's desires, cravings, impulses, and drives — what actually moves the agent to action.

For Watson, free action is action in which these two systems coincide: the agent acts on what she values. Unfree action involves the motivational system driving behavior that the valuational system rejects. The addict who steals acts on a craving she doesn't value and wishes she didn't have. The person who acts from wholeheartedly endorsed commitments acts freely — her motivating desires are precisely the ones that her evaluative perspective endorses.

Watson's account is explicitly a form of source compatibilism: what matters is the kind of source from which the action springs, not whether alternate possibilities were available. A Frankfurt case in which Jones votes from his own valuational commitments is a case of free action — the source is right — regardless of what Black's device would have done. Watson later refined the account to acknowledge that the source can be morally problematic while still being genuinely the agent's own, which motivated his distinction between *aretaic* evaluation (assessing character) and *accountability* (holding agents to standards of right conduct).

## Wolf's Reason View

Susan Wolf's *Freedom Within Reason* (1990) develops a source compatibilist position with a distinctive normative dimension. Wolf argues that freedom requires not just that action spring from the agent's own desires and values but that the agent be capable of acting in accordance with *reason* — of recognizing what is genuinely good and acting accordingly.

Wolf formulates an asymmetry:

- P1: An agent is praiseworthy for an action if the action springs from the agent's own character and values.
- P2: An agent is blameworthy for an action only if the agent had the ability to act in accordance with reason — to recognize and respond to genuine reasons.
- C: Full moral responsibility (both praise and blame) requires both sourcehood and reasons-responsiveness in the normatively substantive sense.

This asymmetry has counterintuitive implications that Wolf embraces: an agent can be fully praiseworthy for a good action without alternate possibilities (if the action springs from deep values and there was no way she could have done otherwise), but cannot be fully blameworthy for a bad action if she lacked the capacity to recognize good reasons. The saintly person who could not help but act well is fully praiseworthy; the person with a severe moral deficit who could not recognize the reasons against her harmful action is less than fully blameworthy.

## Fischer and Ravizza's Guidance Control

Fischer and Ravizza's reasons-responsiveness account, discussed in the preceding file, is itself a form of source compatibilism when understood through the lens of mechanism ownership. The requirement that the mechanism producing action be the agent's own — acquired through taking responsibility — is a sourcehood condition: what makes an action free is not the absence of determination but the presence of the right kind of source in the agent's own appropriated agency.

Fischer distinguishes *regulative control* (the ability to do otherwise) from *guidance control* (acting from one's own reasons-responsive mechanism). Source compatibilism, on this development, is the view that guidance control is sufficient for moral responsibility. The sourcehood element — "one's own reasons-responsive mechanism" — captures what Frankfurt cases establish: what matters for responsibility is not what the agent could have done but what the agent actually did and why.

## The Deep Self View

A cluster of positions sometimes called the "deep self" view, associated with Frankfurt, Wolf, and R. J. Wallace, holds that responsible action is action that expresses the agent's "deep self" — her constitutive values, commitments, and evaluative identity. Superficial desires — passing whims, addictive cravings, compulsive impulses — do not express the deep self. The deep self consists in the hierarchically endorsed, reflectively integrated set of values and commitments that define who the agent fundamentally is.

Actions that flow from the deep self are responsible because they are genuinely the agent's own. Actions driven by alienated desires — desires the agent does not endorse and wishes she did not have — are less than fully responsible because they do not express who she is. The agent is identified not with everything that happens in her psychology but with what she endorses as hers.

## The Manipulation Challenge

The most powerful objection to source compatibilism is the manipulation argument, developed at length in subsequent files. Even if an agent's action springs from her own values, character, and deep self, if those values and that character were formed through manipulation, the action does not seem genuinely free. A person whose deep self was manufactured by a neuroscientist — programmed from birth to have specific values — seems to be acting from a manufactured deep self rather than a genuine one. If the source of an action can be artificially created in a way that undermines responsibility, then the mere fact that the action springs from the agent's current character is not sufficient.

Source compatibilists have responded in several ways. Fischer and Ravizza add a historical condition: the mechanism must have been formed through an appropriate process of taking responsibility, which direct manipulation cannot satisfy. Wolf distinguishes processes that *bypass* rational capacities (manipulation) from processes that *shape* them through genuine rational engagement (education, experience). Mele requires that values be formed in ways that allow the agent to critically reflect on and revise them.

Whether any of these responses adequately handles the strongest forms of the manipulation argument — and whether the distinctions they invoke can be stated without presupposing incompatibilist conclusions — is the central open question in contemporary compatibilism.
