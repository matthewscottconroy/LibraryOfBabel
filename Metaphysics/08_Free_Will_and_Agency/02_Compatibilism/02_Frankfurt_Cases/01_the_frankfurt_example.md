# The Frankfurt Example

Before 1969, the Principle of Alternate Possibilities (PAP) was widely regarded as something close to a conceptual truth about moral responsibility: an agent is morally responsible for an action only if she could have done otherwise. The intuitive basis for this principle is deep. When a gunman forces me at gunpoint to hand over my wallet, I am not blameworthy for complying — I had no other option. When addiction or compulsion drives an agent to act in ways she cannot resist, we reduce her responsibility accordingly. The verdict "she couldn't have helped it" is, in ordinary moral thinking, an excuse. PAP systematizes this pattern.

Harry Frankfurt's 1969 paper "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" introduced a thought experiment designed to show that PAP is false. It remains the most discussed case in the contemporary free will literature.

## The Original Example

Frankfurt's case deploys a *counterfactual intervener*: an agent standing ready to ensure a specific outcome but who, as it happens, never needs to act. The case runs as follows:

Black wants Jones to vote for candidate A. Black has connected a device to Jones's brain that monitors his deliberation. If Jones shows any sign of being about to vote for B — perhaps a specific neural signature appears — Black's device will activate and stimulate Jones's brain to ensure he votes for A. As it turns out, Jones deliberates entirely on his own, decides to vote for A for his own reasons, and votes for A. Black's device never activates.

Now consider two questions. First: is Jones morally responsible for voting for A? Intuitively, yes — he voted for A for his own reasons, after his own deliberation, without any external compulsion. Second: could Jones have done otherwise? Apparently not — had he been about to vote for B, Black would have intervened and forced him to vote for A.

If both intuitive answers are correct, PAP is false. Jones is morally responsible for voting for A, but he could not have done otherwise.

## The Structure of Frankfurt Cases

Frankfurt cases share a general structure that makes the result reproducible across many different settings:

1. An agent S performs action A for her own reasons.
2. A counterfactual intervener I would have ensured S did A if S had been about to do otherwise.
3. I never activates because S does A on her own.
4. Intuition: S is responsible for A.
5. Consequence: S could not have done otherwise (I would have intervened).
6. Conclusion: PAP is false.

The counterfactual intervener is the structural key. It ensures the same outcome regardless of what the agent "tries" to do, while leaving the actual causal history untouched — the agent acts from her own reasons without any intervention in fact occurring.

Frankfurt's argument against PAP stated more formally:

- P1: Jones is morally responsible for voting for A. (He voted for his own reasons, without intervention.)
- P2: Jones could not have done otherwise. (Had he been about to vote otherwise, Black would have intervened.)
- P3: If PAP were true, any agent who is morally responsible for an action could have done otherwise.
- C: Therefore, PAP is false.

## Why the Case Is Compelling

The Frankfurt case is compelling because it appears to isolate what actually grounds responsibility — the actual causal history of the action, specifically that it flows from the agent's own deliberation and reasons — while simultaneously blocking alternate possibilities through the counterfactual intervener. These two features come apart. What makes Jones responsible is entirely independent of what makes alternate possibilities unavailable. The counterfactual intervener contributes nothing to Jones's actual deliberation; it is merely present in the background, like a safety net that is never deployed.

If PAP were genuinely a conceptual condition on responsibility, the case should be incoherent — Jones could not be responsible without alternate possibilities. The case's coherence, and the stability of the intuition that Jones is responsible, suggests that PAP is not a genuine conceptual condition. The simplest explanation is that what grounds responsibility is not the availability of alternatives but the character of the actual causal sequence.

## Implications for Incompatibilism

The standard incompatibilist argument that runs through PAP takes this form:

- P1: Determinism eliminates alternate possibilities.
- P2: Moral responsibility requires alternate possibilities (PAP).
- C: Determinism eliminates moral responsibility.

If Frankfurt cases show that PAP is false, P2 is false, and this argument fails. The route from determinism to the absence of responsibility through alternate possibilities is blocked.

This does not vindicate compatibilism across the board. There are other routes to incompatibilism — the Consequence Argument runs through powerlessness, not alternate possibilities; Galen Strawson's basic argument runs through ultimate sourcehood; Pereboom's four-case argument is designed to work whether or not determinism holds and whether or not PAP is true. But Frankfurt cases substantially weaken one major line of incompatibilist argument, and that matters.

Incompatibilists who accept Frankfurt's challenge have largely shifted their ground — away from alternate possibilities and toward sourcehood and ultimate responsibility. Pereboom's response to Frankfurt cases is not to defend PAP but to argue that even if PAP is false, free will in the sense required for basic desert moral responsibility is still unavailable because determinism (and the available forms of indeterminism) undermines ultimate sourcehood. The Frankfurt debate thus reshapes the terrain of the overall debate without resolving it.
