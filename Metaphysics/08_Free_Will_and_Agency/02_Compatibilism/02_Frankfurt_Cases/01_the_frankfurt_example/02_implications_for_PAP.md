# Implications of Frankfurt Cases for PAP

If Frankfurt's cases succeed, they have substantial implications for the Principle of Alternate Possibilities and for the shape of the overall free will debate. But "if they succeed" is doing considerable work. The cases have generated the most technically sophisticated responses in analytic philosophy of action, and the question of whether they actually eliminate all morally relevant alternate possibilities — rather than just most of them — remains genuinely contested.

## The Standard Incompatibilist Argument and What Frankfurt Challenges

The Principle of Alternate Possibilities states: an agent is morally responsible for an action only if the agent could have done otherwise. This seems obvious: if you had no choice, how can you be responsible? It is built into our ordinary practices of excusing on grounds of compulsion and coercion. Aristotle's analysis of voluntary action builds in something like PAP: involuntary actions, those resulting from compulsion or ignorance, do not ground full responsibility.

The standard incompatibilist argument runs:

- P1: Determinism eliminates alternate possibilities.
- P2: Moral responsibility requires alternate possibilities (PAP).
- C: Determinism eliminates moral responsibility.

Frankfurt cases appear to show that P2 is false. Jones votes for A for his own reasons while Black stands ready to ensure that outcome regardless. The structure: responsibility-grounding conditions satisfied, alternate possibilities eliminated. If our intuitions are reliable here, PAP is false and the argument collapses.

## Incompatibilist Responses

Incompatibilists have developed several lines of reply, not all of which involve defending PAP directly.

*The flicker of freedom response*: The most immediate reply is to deny that Frankfurt cases actually eliminate all alternate possibilities. Even in the described case, there is a thin but genuine alternate possibility — a "flicker" — at the moment the triggering sign would or would not appear. For Black's device to fire, there must be some prior indicator that Jones is about to deviate: a neural signature, a preliminary proto-decision. At the moment that sign occurs or fails to occur, something happens that is, arguably, within Jones's control. If Jones could have failed to produce the sign, then there is an alternate possibility at that earlier moment. Robert Kane, Carl Ginet, and others press this response.

Frankfurt's rejoinder: the flicker is too thin to bear the weight PAP demands. If Jones's only alternate possibility is to produce a neural sign that triggers Black's device, resulting in the same outcome (voting for A), this does not constitute the kind of alternate possibility that grounds moral responsibility. The flicker is an artifact of the case's structure, not a genuine exercise of freedom.

*The dilemma objection*: A more systematic response, pressed by David Widerker and Robert Kane, identifies a structural dilemma for Frankfurt cases. The prior sign is either a deterministic indicator of upcoming deviance or not.

- If deterministic: the case builds determinism into its structure. Using a deterministic case to argue against PAP without assuming determinism is dialectically questionable — it may show only that PAP is false given determinism, which is precisely the contested issue.
- If not deterministic: there is a possible world in which Jones produces the sign but then votes for A anyway (a false positive). In that world, Black's device might not intervene, and Jones does have a genuine alternate possibility.

Fischer's response to the dilemma: the case can be specified with a reliable but non-deterministic indicator, and this is enough to block the flicker response without smuggling in determinism. Probabilistic cases in the middle can eliminate all morally significant alternate possibilities without requiring either full determinism or full indeterminism at the sign moment.

*The non-PAP incompatibilist response*: Some incompatibilists concede that Frankfurt cases show PAP is false but argue that this does not save compatibilism. The relevant condition for responsibility, on this view, is not alternate possibilities but *ultimate sourcehood*: the action must trace back to the agent as its ultimate source, without tracing back to factors outside the agent's control. Determinism undermines ultimate sourcehood regardless of alternate possibilities. This is Pereboom's strategy: Frankfurt cases may defeat PAP, but they do not defeat sourcehood-based incompatibilism. His four-case argument, discussed in the manipulation argument files, is designed to establish this result without relying on PAP at all.

*Modified principles*: Some philosophers try to modify PAP rather than abandon it, seeking a version that survives Frankfurt cases. Candidates include:

- *PAP-Robust*: An agent is morally responsible only if she had a robust alternate possibility — one that is meaningfully different, accessible to the agent, and not merely a flicker.
- *PAP-Reasons*: An agent is morally responsible only if she could have responded differently to sufficient reasons.
- *PAP-Moral*: An agent is morally responsible only if she could have done something morally significant, not just something trivially different.

These modifications try to retain the spirit of PAP while making it immune to the flicker response. Whether any such modification can survive the dilemma objection while still grounding the incompatibilist argument is a central open question.

## The Compatibilist Takeaway

For compatibilists, Frankfurt cases serve two related purposes. First, they undermine the standard incompatibilist argument from alternate possibilities, forcing incompatibilists either to defend PAP against Frankfurt cases or to shift to a different basis for incompatibilism. Second, they suggest the shape of the correct account of responsibility: what matters is the actual causal history of the action — whether it flows from the agent's own reasons, values, and deliberation — not counterfactual facts about what the agent might have done in circumstances that did not in fact obtain.

This is the foundation of source compatibilism: responsibility is grounded in the actual sequence, not in alternate possibilities. If the action flows from the agent's own agency — from her reasons, values, and deliberation — she is responsible, regardless of what a counterfactual intervener might have done. The Frankfurt debate has not definitively established this conclusion, but it has shifted the burden of argument significantly in compatibilism's direction. Incompatibilists who continue to press their case must now do so primarily on sourcehood grounds rather than alternate possibilities grounds — which is a harder, though not impossible, position to maintain.
