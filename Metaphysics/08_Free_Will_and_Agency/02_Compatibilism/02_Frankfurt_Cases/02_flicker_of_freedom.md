# The Flicker of Freedom

Frankfurt cases require a structural feature that defenders of PAP have been quick to exploit: the counterfactual intervener must have some way of knowing whether the agent is about to deviate. Black monitors Jones and would intervene if he saw the right sign — a specific neural pattern, a preliminary "proto-decision," some prior indicator of impending deviance. The "flicker of freedom" response, defended by Robert Kane, Carl Ginet, and others, argues that this structural requirement creates a residual alternate possibility that Frankfurt cases cannot eliminate. The flicker is thin, but it is genuine — and PAP requires only that some alternate possibility exist, not a robust or practically significant one.

## The Structural Point

At some moment before the action, Jones either produces the indicator sign or does not. Consider the two scenarios:

- *The sign appears*: Jones produces the neural pattern indicating he is about to vote for B. Black intervenes. Jones votes for A, but as a direct result of Black's intervention.
- *The sign does not appear*: Jones shows no sign of impending deviance. Black does not intervene. Jones votes for A on his own deliberation.

Frankfurt describes only the second scenario — the one where Black never activates. The question the flicker response raises is: at the moment Jones is not producing the sign, could he have produced it? If so, then Jones had, at that earlier moment, a genuine alternate possibility: the possibility of initiating a different causal sequence — one that would have led to Black's intervention and perhaps a different subjective experience, even if the final outcome (voting for A) would have been the same.

## The Significance of the Flicker

Kane argues that even a thin alternate possibility is sufficient to satisfy PAP in the relevant sense. PAP does not require that the alternate possibility be practically significant or that it lead to a dramatically different outcome. It requires only that the agent's deliberation was genuinely open at some point — that there was a moment at which something could have gone differently as a result of the agent's own agency. If there is such a moment, the action is not entirely inevitable, and the agent is not entirely powerless. The flicker preserves this minimal openness.

Ginet concurs: even if the same outcome (voting for A) would result from either branch of the deliberation, the agent faces a situation that is not one of complete powerlessness. Something happened — or could have happened — at the sign moment that was up to her. Responsibility can attach to what happens at that moment, even if the final large-scale outcome was the same on both paths.

## Frankfurt's Response

Frankfurt responds that the flicker is morally insignificant — that it does not constitute the kind of alternate possibility that PAP is meant to capture and that grounds moral responsibility.

His argument:

- P1: PAP is motivated by the intuition that an agent who could not have done otherwise is completely powerless and therefore not responsible.
- P2: A "flicker" — a thin alternate possibility the agent could not meaningfully exploit to produce a different moral outcome — does not give the agent meaningful power over the morally relevant result.
- P3: If Jones's only alternate possibility leads, through Black's device, to the same outcome (voting for A), then Jones is effectively powerless over the outcome that matters.
- C: The flicker does not satisfy the relevant sense of "could have done otherwise" that PAP requires to ground responsibility.

If Jones's only alternate path leads to the same destination through Black's intervention, the alternate possibility seems to offer nothing of moral significance. The robustness of the alternate possibility seems to matter.

## The Robustness Debate

The flicker exchange generated a focused debate about what kind of alternate possibility PAP requires. If PAP is revised to require only that *some* alternate possibility exist (regardless of its significance), the flicker response succeeds. If PAP is revised to require a *robust* alternate possibility — one that is meaningfully different, accessible to the agent, and potentially leading to a different moral outcome — the flicker response fails.

A robust alternate possibility is roughly one that:
1. Is genuinely different in morally significant ways.
2. Is accessible to the agent — not merely counterfactually possible but something the agent could actually exploit.
3. Involves the agent doing something meaningfully different, not just initiating a process that leads to the same outcome anyway.

PAP-Robust: an agent is morally responsible for an action only if she had a *robust* alternate possibility available. On this principle, the flicker of freedom is not enough. But then the question is whether PAP-Robust is still the relevant principle for moral responsibility — whether the intuitions that support PAP support this stronger version of it.

## The Dilemma Objection as Independent Challenge

Kane also presses the dilemma objection independently of the flicker response: the prior sign is either deterministically linked to upcoming deviance or not.

- If deterministic: the case smuggles in determinism, making it illegitimate to use against PAP without assuming determinism.
- If not deterministic: there is a possible world where Jones produces the sign but then votes for A on his own anyway (a false positive). In that world, Black may not intervene, and Jones does have a genuine alternate possibility at the moment of the vote itself.

This dilemma is a powerful independent challenge to Frankfurt cases, operating alongside rather than through the flicker response. Even if Frankfurt cases succeed in eliminating certain kinds of alternate possibilities (different voting outcomes), probabilistic cases may fail to eliminate all alternate possibilities — including the possibility of producing the sign without then voting otherwise.

## Assessment

The consensus in the literature is that Frankfurt cases, when properly constructed with probabilistic triggers, do eliminate all *morally significant* alternate possibilities, and that the residual flicker is not robust enough to ground moral responsibility in the way PAP intends. But the flicker response has been productive precisely in forcing this refinement. It generated the crucial distinction between robust and thin alternate possibilities, focused attention on the structural requirement for prior signs, and motivated the dilemma objection.

The deeper lesson may be that the debate about the flicker is a proxy for a more fundamental question: what are the conditions for moral responsibility, and does the absence of alternate possibilities — of any kind — threaten those conditions? The flicker debate clarifies the terrain. Whether it settles anything depends on which version of PAP one accepts and whether that version can survive both Frankfurt cases and the dilemma objection.
