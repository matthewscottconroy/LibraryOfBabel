# Responses and Refinements to Frankfurt Cases

Frankfurt cases have been modified and refined through three decades of debate, and the trajectory of those modifications illuminates both the power of Frankfurt's original insight and the persistence of the objections it faces. The exchange has also significantly changed the shape of the incompatibilist position: where incompatibilists once relied heavily on the alternate possibilities route, they now press primarily through sourcehood and ultimate responsibility. Understanding how this happened requires tracing the argument through its most important developments.

## The Dilemma Objection

The most systematic challenge to Frankfurt cases is David Widerker and Robert Kane's dilemma objection. The argument turns on the structural requirement that Frankfurt cases include a prior sign enabling the counterfactual intervener to predict and prevent deviance.

The dilemma:

- *Horn 1 — Deterministic sign*: The sign deterministically tracks upcoming deviance. If Jones's neural pattern of type N deterministically guarantees that Jones is about to vote for B, then determinism is built into the case. But this is precisely what is at issue in the free will debate. Using a deterministic case to argue against PAP without assuming determinism is dialectically problematic — the case may show only that PAP is false *given determinism*, not in general.

- *Horn 2 — Non-deterministic sign*: The sign reliably but not deterministically predicts upcoming deviance. Jones might produce the sign without actually voting for B — a false positive. In such a case, Black's device might activate unnecessarily. More importantly, there is a possible world in which Jones produces the sign and then votes for A on his own. That world contains a genuine alternate possibility: Jones produces the sign and votes for A rather than not producing the sign and voting for A. So alternate possibilities are not fully eliminated.

Widerker argues that on either horn, the Frankfurt case either assumes what is at issue (Horn 1) or fails to eliminate all alternate possibilities (Horn 2).

## Fischer's Refinement: Blockage Cases

Fischer's response to the dilemma constructs Frankfurt cases that avoid both horns by abandoning the prior sign requirement entirely. His strategy involves "blockage" cases: alternate possibilities are blocked not by a prior sign triggering intervention but by a prior state that makes a different outcome causally impossible, without determining what the agent actually does.

The structure: Jones's brain is in state S. State S is such that, given the laws of nature, Jones cannot vote for B — if he begins to form the intention to vote for B, neurological processes will abort that intention. But S is not what causes Jones to vote for A. Jones votes for A through his normal deliberative processes, and S would only activate if Jones began to go the other direction. S is a background blockage: always present, preventing voting for B without causing voting for A.

In this version, there is no prior sign whose status creates the dilemma. The blockage state doesn't fire based on monitoring Jones's deliberation; it simply makes voting for B physically impossible. There is therefore no earlier moment at which Jones might have "produced a sign" and thereby accessed an alternate possibility through the sign mechanism.

Fischer argues this version avoids both horns: the blockage is compatible with Jones's deliberation being indeterministic (S just blocks one outcome regardless of how deliberation proceeds), and there is no prior sign generating a flicker. Jones is still intuitively responsible because he voted for his own reasons without any intervention.

Critics respond that blockage cases are conceptually puzzling: it is hard to understand how a state can make an outcome impossible without influencing the causal process producing the actual outcome. If S makes voting for B physically impossible but never activates, what does it mean for S to be present? The critics' intuition is that anything that makes voting for B impossible must bear some causal relationship to what Jones actually does.

## Pereboom's Four-Case Argument

Derk Pereboom's four-case argument is arguably the most important development in this literature after Frankfurt's original paper. Rather than defending or attacking PAP directly, Pereboom argues that the real conditions for moral responsibility go beyond anything Frankfurt cases address.

Pereboom describes four scenarios in which an agent named Plum kills White:

**Case 1**: Neuroscientists directly manipulate Plum's brain at the time of action, producing the desire to kill White and the decision to act on it. Almost everyone judges that Plum is not responsible — he is essentially a puppet.

**Case 2**: Scientists programmed Plum at the beginning of his life with egoistic values and a disposition to reason in self-interested ways. Plum now reasons and decides normally, but the reasoning process runs along lines the scientists designed. Most people still judge that Plum is not fully responsible.

**Case 3**: Plum's character was not directly programmed by scientists but was shaped by upbringing — parents, teachers, peers, social environment — in ways that produce the same result. Intuitions here are more divided, but many people feel Plum's responsibility is at least reduced.

**Case 4**: Plum is an ordinary person in a deterministic world. His character was shaped by genetics and environment in the normal course of development; he reasons and decides normally. He kills White.

Pereboom's argument: there is no morally relevant difference between Case 1 and Case 4. In each case, Plum's action ultimately traces back to factors outside his control. If Plum is not responsible in Case 1 (direct manipulation), and there is no relevant difference between Case 1 and Case 4, then Plum is not responsible in Case 4 either.

The significance for the Frankfurt debate: even if Frankfurt cases show that PAP is false and alternate possibilities are not required for responsibility, Pereboom's argument shows that compatibilism is still threatened. The relevant condition for basic desert responsibility is not alternate possibilities but *ultimate sourcehood* — the action tracing back to the agent as its ultimate source, not to factors outside the agent's control. Determinism undermines this sourcehood regardless of alternate possibilities.

## Compatibilist Responses to Pereboom

Compatibilists have pressed several responses to the four-case argument.

Fischer and Ravizza argue that there is a morally relevant difference between Cases 1-3 and Case 4. In Cases 1-3, the mechanism producing Plum's deliberation is not Plum's own — it was implanted or designed by external agents who did not engage Plum's rational capacities in forming it. In Case 4, the mechanism is Plum's own, having been formed through normal developmental processes that engaged rather than bypassed his developing rational agency. The historical condition for mechanism ownership distinguishes them.

Wolf and others distinguish processes that *bypass* rational agency (direct manipulation) from processes that *shape* it through genuine rational engagement (education, experience). Manipulation bypasses; normal development shapes. This distinction grounds a principled difference in responsibility.

Some compatibilists accept that Cases 1-3 generate reduced responsibility and argue that Case 4 differs sufficiently to yield full responsibility. Normal developmental shaping, they argue, is relevantly different from manipulation in kind, not just degree.

Finally, some compatibilists bite the bullet: if the mechanism is reasons-responsive in all four cases, and reasons-responsiveness is sufficient for responsibility, then the cases are not counterexamples — they reveal that our intuitions about Cases 1-3 are unreliable rather than that our theory is wrong.

## The Legacy of the Frankfurt Debate

The net effect of the Frankfurt debate has been to redirect incompatibilism away from the alternate possibilities route and toward the sourcehood route. Van Inwagen's Consequence Argument, which does not rely on PAP, becomes the central incompatibilist tool for the modal side of the debate. Pereboom's four-case argument provides the complementary route through sourcehood and ultimate responsibility.

Even those who find Frankfurt cases inconclusive on the question of PAP recognize that they have clarified the debate in important ways: they have separated two conditions that were previously conflated (sourcehood and alternate possibilities), and they have shown that much of the intuitive force behind PAP derives from sourcehood considerations — the sense that a responsible agent must be the author of her action — rather than from the bare availability of alternatives. The Frankfurt literature has made that distinction precise, which is a genuine contribution to understanding what free will and responsibility require.
