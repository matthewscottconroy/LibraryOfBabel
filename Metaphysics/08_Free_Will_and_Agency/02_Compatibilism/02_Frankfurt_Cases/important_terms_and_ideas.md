# Important Terms and Ideas: Frankfurt Cases

**Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP)**
The principle that a person is morally responsible for an action only if they could have done otherwise. Frankfurt cases are designed to refute this principle by presenting cases of apparent responsibility without alternative possibilities.

**Frankfurt Cases**
Thought experiments involving a neuroscientist (Black) who has implanted a device in an agent's (Jones's) brain that would intervene to ensure Jones votes Democrat if Jones showed any sign of intending to vote Republican. Jones votes Democrat on his own; Black's device never activates. Jones had no alternative possibility but seems responsible.

**The Flicker of Freedom**
The objection that Frankfurt cases fail because there is always some alternative possibility available to the agent—a "flicker": perhaps the agent could have begun to decide otherwise before the intervener had time to act, even if not completed the alternative action. This preserves PAP.

**The Dilemma Defense**
Widerker's objection: Frankfurt cases must use either (a) a deterministic prior sign (which assumes determinism, begging the question against incompatibilists) or (b) a non-deterministic sign (which allows a flicker of freedom). Either way, Frankfurt cases fail to establish PAP's falsity against the incompatibilist.

**Semi-Compatibilism**
Fischer's view: moral responsibility requires "guidance control" (acting from a reasons-responsive mechanism in the right way) but not "regulative control" (the ability to do otherwise). Semi-compatibilism accepts that PAP is false and argues that this is enough to vindicate compatibilism about responsibility.

**Ultimate Responsibility**
Kane's libertarian concept: genuine responsibility requires that agents be the "ultimate" source of their actions and character—that at some point their choices were not determined by factors entirely outside their control. This requires indeterminism at character-forming moments.
