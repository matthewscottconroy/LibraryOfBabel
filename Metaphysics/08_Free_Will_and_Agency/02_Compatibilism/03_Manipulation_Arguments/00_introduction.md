# Manipulation Arguments Against Compatibilism

Compatibilism holds that actions caused by the agent's own desires, values, and deliberation are free and responsible, even if those desires and values were themselves causally determined. The manipulation argument — associated with Pereboom, Mele, and others — challenges this by constructing cases where the desires and values are instilled by a manipulator, rather than arising "naturally" through the agent's history of development and experience, and asking whether the resulting actions are free.

Pereboom's four-case argument moves from clearly unfree cases to the "determined agent" case, arguing that if we accept that the manipulated agents are unfree, we should accept that the determined agent is unfree too. In Case 1, a team of neuroscientists directly manipulates Jones's brain to cause him to act in a certain way: intuitively, Jones is not free. In Case 2, the neuroscientists manipulate Jones while he is not reflectively aware of the manipulation. In Case 3, the manipulation occurs at birth, shaping Jones's character. In Case 4, Jones acts freely in the ordinary compatibilist sense — his action flows from his character and values — but those character and values were fixed by prior causes over which he had no control. If Case 4 is relevantly similar to Cases 1-3, and if Case 1 is clearly unfree, then Case 4 — the determined agent — is also unfree.

Compatibilists respond by identifying what they take to be relevant differences between manipulation and determination by prior causes. The historical condition response holds that what matters is the etiology of the desires: desires formed through the right kind of history — gradual development, reflection, experience — are free-will conferring even if determined, while desires instilled by manipulation are not. The mesh theory holds that what matters is the right fit between the agent's higher-order values (what the agent values having as motivations) and her first-order desires (what she is motivated by); manipulation typically produces a mismatch.

This section examines manipulation arguments, the compatibilist responses, and the question of whether any compatibilist account can distinguish determined agents from manipulated ones in a principled way.
