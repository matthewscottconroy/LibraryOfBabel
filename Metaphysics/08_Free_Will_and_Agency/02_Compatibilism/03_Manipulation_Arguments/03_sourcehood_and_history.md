# Sourcehood and History

The manipulation argument has pushed compatibilists to add a diachronic dimension to accounts that were previously focused on the synchronic structure of the agent's psychology. Classical compatibilists — Hume, Hobbes, the conditional analysts — asked whether the agent's present-time psychology has the right structure: do her desires bear the right relationship to her actions? Contemporary source compatibilists have come to recognize that this is insufficient. Manipulation cases show that the right present-time structure can be produced by processes that undermine rather than constitute genuine freedom. History matters.

## The Turn Toward History

Why do manipulation cases generate the intuition that the manufactured agent is not responsible, even when she satisfies standard compatibilist conditions? The most natural explanation is this: an agent whose character was directly implanted by neuroscientists did not earn her character through her own deliberation and experience. She was built to have certain values rather than having grown into them. This suggests that genuine self-determination requires not just the right present-time psychology but a history of appropriate self-formation — a history in which the agent's own rational capacities were engaged in producing who she now is.

The general sourcehood claim for responsible action:

- P1: For an action to be genuinely free, it must spring from the agent as its source — attributable to her as an expression of her own agency.
- P2: For an action to spring from the agent as its source, the agent's psychology must be genuinely her own.
- P3: For the agent's psychology to be genuinely her own, it must have been formed through a process in which her rational capacities were appropriately engaged — not bypassed, overridden, or short-circuited.
- C: Free action requires an appropriate history of formation of the agent's psychology.

The challenge is to fill in P3: what processes count as "appropriately engaging" rational capacities, and which count as bypassing them?

## Varieties of Sourcehood Accounts

Fischer and Ravizza require that the mechanism be the agent's own, which involves a process of taking responsibility: the agent must come to see herself as the source of her actions, acquire evidence that her mechanism is reasons-responsive, and be embedded in practices of holding responsible. This is a weak historical condition — it does not require that the agent have consciously chosen her character, only that she has appropriated it through engaged self-understanding. Manipulation cases fail this condition because the manufactured agent never went through this process.

Alfred Mele (*Autonomous Agents*, 1995) is more demanding. He requires that an agent's values were not subject to "compelled acquisition": the values were not implanted against her settled prior values, were not installed without rational engagement, and the agent retains the ability to reflect on and revise them through further deliberation. An agent whose values were formed through socialization and self-reflection can revise them through further reflection — this revisability marks them as authentically hers. An agent whose values were directly implanted may lack this revisability if the implantation went deep enough to prevent critical reflection on the implanted values.

Gary Watson's later work distinguishes aretaic evaluation from accountability and acknowledges the importance of history: the "deep self" that grounds responsible action must be genuinely the agent's own, which requires an appropriate history. The manufactured deep self — however sophisticated in its current-time structure — is not the agent's own.

## What Counts as Appropriate Formation

The central challenge for all historical accounts is specifying what makes a history "appropriate" without begging the question in favor of incompatibilism.

Several candidate criteria have been proposed. An appropriate formation process engages rather than bypasses the agent's capacity to recognize and respond to reasons: education, experience, reflection, and social interaction are appropriate; direct neural manipulation is not. An appropriate history produces values that the agent can critically reflect on and revise: indoctrination and manipulation may install values at a level that is resistant to critical reflection, which is part of what makes them freedom-undermining. An appropriate history does not involve systematic deception: character formation through honest engagement with the world is appropriate; character formation through systematic deception exploits rather than engages rational agency. And an appropriate history is one in which the agent had opportunities at various stages to contribute to her own formation through her own rational choices — she was a partner in the process, not merely its object.

## The Regress Threat

Historical accounts face a potential regress. For the agent's current psychology to be her own, it must have been formed through an appropriate process. But whether that process was appropriate depends on whether the psychology the agent brought to it was genuinely her own — which depends on its prior history. The regress seems to require that there be some point at which the agent's psychology was "originally" her own, before any external influence.

But this original ownership is impossible. No one forms her character in isolation from external influence. The very first dispositions a person acquires are products of genetics and early environment — factors entirely outside her control.

Historical compatibilists respond by rejecting the regress interpretation. The historical condition does not require that the causal history trace all the way back to some original uncaused character. It requires that the formation process at each stage engaged rather than bypassed the rational capacities the agent had at that stage. Early childhood formation, when rational capacities are undeveloped, is subject to a less demanding standard; adult formation, when rational capacities are mature, is subject to a more demanding one. The point is not that there must be an uncaused beginning but that the process of formation must have engaged the agent's developing capacities at each stage.

## The Connection to Hard Incompatibilism

Historical sourcehood accounts are designed to preserve compatibilism. But critics — particularly Pereboom — argue that no causal history can provide ultimate sourcehood. Even an appropriate causal history — one that engaged rational capacities throughout — traces back to factors the agent did not choose: genes, early environment, lucky circumstances. The chain of appropriate formation must begin somewhere, and that beginning was not up to the agent.

This is the hard incompatibilist challenge: not that any specific causal history is freedom-undermining, but that all causal histories ultimately trace to unchosen factors. The historical compatibilist must explain why this ultimate tracing does not undermine freedom. The standard response is that "ultimate" sourcehood is not required — only "proximate" sourcehood — but this faces the challenge of explaining what makes proximate sourcehood sufficient when ultimate sourcehood is unavailable.

The debate between historical compatibilism and hard incompatibilism thus hinges on which level of sourcehood moral responsibility requires. The compatibilist says proximate sourcehood — the right kind of causal history leading to the present — is sufficient. The hard incompatibilist says ultimate sourcehood — the agent being the uncaused originator of her own character — is required, and this is impossible. That exchange defines one of the central fault lines in the contemporary debate.
