# What Is Agent Causation?

The Consequence Argument, if it succeeds, establishes that in a deterministic world no one has any choice about anything they do. The libertarian accepts this and draws the obvious conclusion: if free will exists — and the libertarian insists it does — the world must not be deterministic, at least not all the way down to the level of human agency. But saying that determinism is false is not enough. Quantum randomness doesn't help directly, as we have seen: a random quantum fluctuation in a neuron is not the same as a free choice. What the libertarian needs is a positive account of what kind of causation could ground genuine freedom. Agent causation is the boldest answer to that demand.

Agent causation is the view that agents — persons, selves — can be irreducible causes of their own actions in a sense that cannot be fully analyzed in terms of prior events causing other events. On this view, causation is not only *event causation* (one event causing another), but includes a distinctive kind in which an agent, as a persisting substance, directly brings about events. The agent cause is not reducible to any prior event in the agent's history; the cause is the agent herself.

## The Motivation

The motivation comes from the structure of the problem we are trying to solve. If every action is caused by prior mental events (beliefs, desires, intentions), and those prior mental events were themselves caused by earlier events, then the action is part of a causal chain extending back before the agent's birth. The agent is, in that case, not the ultimate source of her actions — she is the most recent link in a chain whose origin she did not choose. Agent causation is supposed to break this chain: when an agent acts, the cause is not an event in the agent's history but the agent as a substance — a persisting entity that initiates causal sequences without being determined by prior events to initiate them.

This is the agent-causal solution to the Consequence Argument: if the agent is an irreducible cause of her actions, then the past and the laws do not by themselves determine what the agent does. The agent's own causing intervenes, and this causing does not trace back to prior events in the relevant way.

Agent causation theorists draw a sharp distinction between two kinds of causation. *Event causation* (Richard Taylor calls it *transeunt causation*): one event causes another, as a billiard ball's motion causes another ball to move. Causes are states or events; effects are states or events; the causal relation holds between distinct events connected by regularities. *Agent causation* (or *immanent causation*): an agent as a persisting substance causes an event — her action. The causal relation holds between a substance and an event, not between two events.

Richard Taylor, in *Action and Purpose* (1966), argued that our experience of deliberate action reveals agent causation phenomenologically: when I deliberately raise my hand, I experience myself as the initiator of the movement — not merely as the location at which a causal process happens to culminate. Whether this phenomenology is veridical is a separate question, but it suggests that a distinctive category of causation is at work.

## The Roles Agent Causation Plays

Agent causation serves three roles in the libertarian theory. It provides the *indeterminism* required: because agent causation is not event causation, the agent's causing is not determined by prior events. The agent could have done otherwise, categorically, because her causing in this instance was not necessitated by anything prior. It provides the *control* required for free will: the agent is an active initiator, not a passive conduit. The action is hers in a deep sense — she is the cause of it, not merely the most recent link in a chain. And it is compatible with *reasons-explanation*: even though the agent's causing is not determined by prior events, it is not random. The agent acts for reasons; her reasons rationalize the action even if they do not necessitate it. The agent's causing is a rational response to reasons, not an arbitrary indeterministic occurrence.

## Substance Metaphysics and Its Discontents

Agent causation requires that agents be substances, not mere bundles of events or processes. On event-causal accounts of persons, a human being is best understood as a complex ongoing process — a stream of events connected by causal and psychological relations. On agent-causal accounts, persons must be substances capable of originating causal sequences — something over and above the events and processes that constitute their mental and physical lives.

This commitment to a robust kind of substance metaphysics is one source of widespread resistance. Contemporary naturalism tends toward event-causal and process accounts of persons; postulating an additional ontological category — substance causation — sits uneasily with it. Timothy O'Connor (*Persons and Causes*, 2000) has done the most to develop a philosophically rigorous, naturalistically respectable account of agent causation. He argues that agent causation is a form of *emergent causation* — it arises when physical systems reach the appropriate level of complexity. The agent is not a Cartesian soul; she is a complex physical system whose organization gives rise to genuine agent-causal powers. Whether this naturalistic version is coherent or merely renames the problem is contested.

## Historical Roots

Agent causation has roots in Aristotle's notion of substances as efficient causes, in Thomas Reid's distinction between "efficient causes" (agents who originate changes) and "natural causes" (physical events that transmit changes), and in Kant's account of noumenal freedom. Kant's rational will, considered as a noumenal entity, causes events in the phenomenal world without being caused by prior phenomenal events — this is agent causation at the transcendental level, even if Kant does not use that term. Agent causation theorists often see themselves as developing this Kantian insight into the metaphysics of agency while finding it a more naturalistic home than Kant's two-worlds framework provided.
