# Chisholm on Agent Causation

Roderick Chisholm's articulation of agent causation, developed in "Human Freedom and the Self" (1964) and related papers, remains the most explicit and rigorous statement of the libertarian positive account. Drawing on Thomas Reid's distinction between "efficient causes" (agents who originate changes) and "natural causes" (events that transmit changes), Chisholm argues that a complete account of human action must include irreducible agent causation — that the chain of event-to-event causation cannot be the whole story.

## The Central Distinction

Chisholm's central claim is that when a person acts freely, she is a "mover" — she sets something in motion — in a sense that cannot be analyzed in terms of prior events. He distinguishes:

- *Immanent causation*: causation by an agent as a substance. The agent, not any event in the agent, is the cause.
- *Transeunt causation*: event causing event. One state of affairs brings about another in accordance with causal laws.

In free action, the agent is an immanent cause of her action; this causing is not itself caused by prior events. There is no event that is the agent's causing — the agent simply causes, without being caused to cause. The argument:

- P1: Free action requires that the agent be the genuine source of her action — that the action be "up to her."
- P2: If the agent's action (or the mental event that immediately precedes it) is caused by prior events, those prior events — not the agent — are the genuine source.
- P3: Event causation traces through events, not through the agent as a substance.
- C: Free action requires agent (immanent) causation, not merely event (transeunt) causation.

## The Argument from Moral Responsibility

Chisholm's most influential argument for the reality of agent causation starts from the existence of moral responsibility:

- P1: Some agents are sometimes genuinely morally responsible for their actions.
- P2: If all causation is event causation, no agent is genuinely morally responsible for any action — because the action traces to prior events outside the agent's control (the Consequence Argument).
- P3: Therefore, not all causation is event causation — agent causation must be real.

This is an argument from the *existence* of moral responsibility to the *existence* of agent causation. Its force depends on whether we accept P1, and on whether P2 is established by the Consequence Argument. Compatibilists challenge P2: event causation of the right kind (through reasons-responsive mechanisms that are the agent's own) is sufficient for the kind of responsibility that actually exists. Hard incompatibilists challenge P1: if the argument shows that agent causation is required for genuine responsibility, and if agent causation is incoherent, then genuine responsibility in the deep sense does not exist. Both challenges are serious.

## The Formal Structure of Agent Causation

Chisholm acknowledges the strangeness of the agent-causal claim and offers a formal characterization:

- Event E is immanently caused by agent A if and only if: (i) A causes E, (ii) A's causing E is not itself caused by any prior event or state, (iii) A could have refrained from causing E.

Condition (ii) is the one that distinguishes agent causation from event causation — the agent causes without being caused to cause. Chisholm connects this to basic action: A does something if and only if A causes something directly or indirectly. The basic actions — those not caused by causing other things — are immanently caused by the agent. All other actions trace through a chain of caused causings to some basic action that the agent immanently causes.

## The Problem of Rationality

A pressing objection: if the agent immanently causes her action without being caused to do so by any prior mental state, how do her reasons relate to the action? Reasons appear in the causal story as prior mental states, but if the agent's causing is not determined by those reasons, the connection between reasons and action seems severed. Reasons would be at most background conditions for the agent's uncaused causing.

Chisholm's response: reasons function as *motivating reasons* — the considerations in light of which the agent acts — without deterministically causing the action. The agent responds to reasons rather than being moved by them as a billiard ball is moved by a cue. Rational agency is precisely the capacity to be responsive to reasons without being determined by them.

This response draws on a Kantian notion: practical reason responds to reasons as reasons, not merely as causal forces. But critics press further: if the agent responds to reasons without being determined by them, then in any given case, the agent might have responded differently to the same reasons. What explains why she responded as she did rather than differently? Agent causation theorists must say: nothing explains this — the agent simply chose. But this seems to make the specific choice arbitrary, a matter of brute chance even if it is agent-causal chance rather than quantum randomness.

## Comparison with Quantum Indeterminism

Chisholm acknowledges a superficial resemblance between agent causation and quantum indeterminism: in both cases, events occur that are not determined by prior events. But he insists on a crucial difference: quantum indeterminism produces *random* events governed by probability laws, while agent causation produces *chosen* events produced by the agent's rational will. The two are categorically different kinds of indetermination.

This distinction is supposed to draw the line between freedom and mere chance. But critics argue the distinction is less clear than it appears: what makes an event "chosen" by the agent rather than "random" if neither is determined by prior causes? If the agent's choosing is not itself determined, what distinguishes it from a random occurrence? This is the luck objection, which receives sustained discussion in later files.

## Chisholm's Significance

Chisholm's articulation of agent causation matters for three reasons. First, it is the clearest statement of the incompatibilist's positive alternative: not merely the rejection of determinism but a positive account of what freedom consists in. Second, it reveals the deep metaphysical commitments of libertarianism: agent causation requires a robust conception of the agent as a substance with genuine causal powers not reducible to events or processes. Third, it generates the clearest statement of the central challenges for libertarianism: the intelligibility problem (what exactly is agent causation?), the integration problem (how does it interact with physical processes?), and the luck objection (is agent causation just random chance under a different name?). Subsequent libertarians — Timothy O'Connor, Randolph Clarke, Robert Kane — have all grappled with these challenges, either by refining the agent-causal account or by developing alternative positions that avoid its most problematic features.
