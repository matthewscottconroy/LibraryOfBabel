# Event Causal Libertarianism

Agent causation has a clear motivation — it provides both the indeterminism and the control that libertarianism requires — but it pays a heavy metaphysical price. It requires postulating a distinctive kind of causation, substance causation, that sits uneasily with naturalism, creates the integration problem, and faces the charge of intelligibility. Event causal libertarianism is the attempt to secure libertarian freedom without that price: free actions are caused by mental events — beliefs, desires, intentions — in the same way that all actions are caused by mental events. The difference is that this causation is *indeterministic*. The agent's reasons cause the action, but they do not determine it. There is real probability in the causal connection between reasons and action.

## The Desiderata and Their Tension

Event causal libertarianism is motivated by two requirements that appear to pull against each other:

1. *Rational connection*: Free actions must be causally connected to the agent's reasons. Without a causal connection, actions would be random or unintelligible. A free action must be explicable in terms of the agent's beliefs, desires, and values.

2. *Genuine openness*: Free actions must be genuinely undetermined — there must be real alternate possibilities. Without indeterminism, the action is the inevitable outcome of prior causes, and the Consequence Argument applies.

Deterministic event causation satisfies the first requirement but not the second. Agent causation satisfies both but introduces mysterious metaphysics. Event causal libertarianism satisfies both by holding that reasons cause actions probabilistically: the agent's reasons make the action highly probable without determining it. The rational connection is preserved; genuine openness is secured; and we need postulate only the same kind of causation that operates throughout the natural world, with the modification that it is probabilistic rather than necessitating.

## The Framework

On the event causal libertarian view:

- Free actions are caused by mental events — beliefs, desires, intentions, deliberations — which are themselves realized in neural events.
- This causation is *indeterministic*: the mental events raise the probability of the action without making it certain.
- The indeterminism is genuine, not merely epistemic: it is not a matter of ignorance about further determining causes but of real, objective probability.
- Two agents identical in all relevant respects — same character, same reasons, same deliberation — might produce different actions.

The framework preserves the event-causal structure of action explanation: reasons cause actions through mental events connected by probabilistic causal laws. It differs from deterministic event causation only in the modal character of the causal connection — probabilistic rather than necessitating.

## The Physical Basis

Where does the indeterminism come from? Event causal libertarians typically appeal to quantum indeterminacy, with or without chaotic amplification. If neural processes involve genuine quantum indeterminacy — if there is real probability in the behavior of relevant neural events — and if this indeterminacy propagates to the macro level of decision-making, then the causation from reasons to action is genuinely probabilistic. The agent's reasons make the action highly probable without determining it; the quantum gap provides real openness.

Chaotic amplification provides a complementary mechanism: even without direct quantum effects at the neural level relevant to deliberation, sensitive dependence on initial conditions could amplify tiny quantum fluctuations in neural states into macroscopic differences in deliberative outcomes. A tiny quantum indeterminacy could tip a decision one way rather than another.

Whether these physical mechanisms are plausible remains empirically contested. Most cognitive scientists are skeptical that quantum effects play a significant role at the macro level of deliberation, but the question is not definitively settled.

## Advantages over Agent Causation

Event causal libertarianism has several advantages. First, *naturalistic plausibility*: the account postulates only ordinary mental events as causes. The metaphysics is the same as for deterministic event causation — the only difference is the probabilistic character of the causal laws. Second, *integration with science*: because the causes are mental events realized in neural events, the account is compatible with neuroscience. The question is not whether there are mysterious non-physical causes but whether neural events are deterministic or probabilistic in their causal role — an empirical question. Third, *explanatory structure*: the explanation of free action has the same form as the explanation of non-free action. Reasons cause actions through mental events. The indeterminism adds openness without changing the basic pattern.

The main disadvantage: event causal libertarianism faces the luck objection in acute form. If causation from reasons to action is probabilistic, the specific action produced seems to be a matter of luck — not fully attributable to the agent. The gap between what the reasons made probable and what actually happened seems arbitrary. This is the objection Kane addresses most directly, through his concept of self-forming actions.

## The Central Argument

The core argument for event causal libertarianism as the correct libertarian account:

- P1: Free will requires that free actions be caused by the agent's reasons (the rational causation condition).
- P2: Free will requires that free actions be genuinely undetermined — that alternative possibilities be real (the openness condition).
- P3: Agent causation is metaphysically problematic and empirically implausible.
- P4: The only alternative to agent causation that satisfies both P1 and P2 is probabilistic event causation.
- C: Therefore, if libertarian free will is possible, it must be grounded in probabilistic event causation.

The argument is conditional: it identifies event causal libertarianism as the best available libertarian option, not as the correct account of free will full stop. Whether it succeeds depends on whether it can handle the luck objection — which is the topic of the following files.

## Randolph Clarke's Integrated Account

Randolph Clarke (*Libertarian Accounts of Free Will*, 2003) develops a hybrid view combining elements of agent causation and event causal libertarianism. On his view, agents are causally efficacious not merely in virtue of their mental states (events) but also in virtue of their persisting dispositional properties as substances. Agent causation is real but is not the primitive, ungrounded form Chisholm described — it is integrated with event causation and operates through the agent's stable dispositional states. This is designed to secure genuine agent causation while avoiding the intelligibility problems of the pure agent-causal view. Whether the integrated account genuinely secures agent causation or merely renames event causation — and whether it avoids the luck objection — is contested.
