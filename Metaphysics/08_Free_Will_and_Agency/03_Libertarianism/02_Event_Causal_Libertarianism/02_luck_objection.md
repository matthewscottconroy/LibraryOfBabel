# The Luck Objection to Libertarianism

Suppose two agents are molecule-for-molecule identical — same character, same reasons, same deliberation up to the moment of decision. Agent causation or probabilistic event causation, as the libertarian maintains, means they could produce different decisions. One decides to resist the temptation; the other gives in. The difference is not due to any feature of their character, their reasoning, or their values. It is due to an indeterministic physical process in their brains — a quantum fluctuation, or the way indeterministic agent causation happened to fall. In what meaningful sense is either decision more the agent's own decision than a coin flip?

This is the luck objection to libertarianism. It is the most powerful challenge the position faces, and no libertarian account has fully answered it to general satisfaction.

## Two Forms of the Objection

*Present-time luck*: At the moment of decision, if causation from reasons to decision is indeterministic, whether the agent decides one way or another is partly a matter of luck. Consider the two scenarios directly:

- Scenario A: Agent S, with reasons R, in situation C, decides to do X.
- Scenario B: Agent S*, with the same reasons R, in the same situation C, decides to do Y.

If event causal libertarianism is true, S and S* could be entirely identical up to the moment of decision. The difference between their decisions is not due to any feature of the agents — the agents are identical. It is due to which way the probabilistic process went. But if the difference between the two decisions is entirely due to chance rather than to anything about the agents, in what sense are the decisions genuinely theirs?

*Retrospective luck* (Mele): Even after the decision is made, we can ask whether the agent was in control of it. If the agent could have decided otherwise given the same prior states, whether she decided as she did was partly a matter of luck. Alfred Mele argues that this makes indeterministic causation no better than deterministic causation for grounding freedom: in both cases, the decision is an outcome of prior processes; in neither case is the agent the ultimate originator. Indeterminism introduces randomness without introducing control.

Thomas Nagel's taxonomy of moral luck is relevant here. Nagel identifies constitutive luck (luck in who one is — character, temperament), circumstantial luck (luck in the situations one faces), resultant luck (luck in how things turn out), and causal luck (luck in how one is determined by antecedent circumstances). The luck objection to libertarianism involves a fifth kind: *deliberative luck* — luck in which way an indeterministic deliberative process goes. If freedom requires indeterminism, it seems to introduce deliberative luck into the very core of free decision-making, undermining the agent's control precisely where control is most needed.

## The Compatibilist Articulation

Compatibilists press the objection to show that indeterminism makes things worse, not better. The argument:

- P1: An action is free when the agent is in control — when the action is appropriately connected to the agent's reasons, character, and deliberation.
- P2: Deterministic causation by reasons-responsive mechanisms provides this connection: the agent's reasons determine the action through an appropriate mechanism.
- P3: Indeterministic causation introduces a gap between reasons and action: the reasons do not determine the action but only make it probable.
- P4: Whatever fills this gap — the indeterministic quantum event — is not the agent but chance.
- C: Indeterministic causation replaces the agent's control with chance rather than enhancing it.

The implication is that compatibilist determinism — action caused by reasons-responsive mechanisms — provides more genuine control than indeterministic causation. The libertarian's demand for indeterminism undermines rather than secures the very thing the libertarian wants to ground.

## The Luck Objection Applied to Agent Causation

The luck objection applies to agent causation in a parallel form. Even if the agent is causally indeterminate between two actions — if both are within her agent-causal power — what makes her cause one rather than the other? If nothing explains this, the specific action seems arbitrary. Agent causation theorists must say: the agent chose. But if the choosing is not itself determined by anything prior to the choosing, the specific choice seems to be a matter of brute chance even if it is agent-causal chance rather than quantum randomness.

Chisholm's response — that agent causation is rational causing, which distinguishes it from mere randomness — faces the challenge that the distinction is asserted rather than explained. If two agents with identical reasons and agent-causal powers might cause different actions, what is the substantive difference between their situations and a situation of pure randomness?

## Kane's Response: Self-Forming Actions

Robert Kane's response is the most sustained and philosophically developed attempt to answer the luck objection. Kane introduces the concept of *self-forming actions* (SFAs) — actions in which the agent's motivational structure is genuinely divided, with two sets of reasons pulling in different directions simultaneously.

In SFAs:
1. The agent is genuinely torn between competing values or reasons — both are active and operative in her deliberation.
2. The deliberative process is indeterministic at the neural level — competing neural networks sustaining each option are in tension, and the outcome is probabilistic.
3. Both possible outcomes — deciding one way or the other — are motivated by the agent's actual reasons and values.
4. Whichever way the decision goes, the agent endorses it as an expression of her reasons.

Kane's key move: in SFAs, both outcomes express the agent's will and values. If the businessperson decides to stop and help the victim of an assault, this expresses her commitment to civic duty and compassion. If she drives on to protect her business interests, this expresses her self-interested motivation. Neither outcome is alien to her; both are genuinely hers. The indeterminism does not import randomness from outside the agent — it reflects the genuine division within the agent's motivational structure.

The luck objection's assumption — that if both outcomes are possible, one of them must be arbitrary — is challenged: when both outcomes are motivated by the agent's actual reasons, neither is arbitrary. The agent is the author of whichever outcome occurs because both possible outcomes are expressions of her authentic reasons.

## Replies to Kane

Critics find Kane's response insufficient in several respects.

*The residual luck problem*: Even if both outcomes are motivated by the agent's reasons, the fact that *this* outcome rather than *that* one occurred seems to be a matter of luck — of which way the neural indeterminism went. The agent's reasons motivated both options; nothing about the agent distinguishes which was actualized. The gap between motivation and actualization is still filled by chance.

*The endorsement problem*: Kane says the agent endorses the outcome whichever way it goes. But endorsement after the fact does not retroactively eliminate the luck involved in which outcome occurred. If I flip a coin and then endorse the result, the result is still chance, not agency.

*The dual voluntary action problem*: Kane holds that both possible outcomes of an SFA would have been voluntary. But can an agent simultaneously be choosing both outcomes voluntarily? Choices are typically exclusive — you choose one option and reject the others. An agent who would have endorsed either outcome seems not to be making a genuine choice but rather accepting whatever happens.

## Assessment

The luck objection remains the most serious unresolved challenge to libertarianism. The situation:

- Agent causation says the agent causes the action without being determined to do so. The luck objection: the specific action is then arbitrary.
- Event causal libertarianism says reasons cause the action probabilistically. The luck objection: chance, not the agent, fills the gap between reasons and action.
- Kane's self-forming actions say both outcomes express the agent's reasons. The luck objection: but one rather than the other occurred due to neural randomness, not due to the agent.

The deepest available response is to challenge the framing: the luck objection assumes that control requires determination — that the agent fully controls the action only if her prior states determine it. But this assumption is exactly what libertarianism rejects. If control can come in degrees, and if indeterministic action can be more controlled than the luck objection allows because it is rationally caused and endorsed, then the objection points to a genuine tension without definitively refuting libertarianism. The debate continues, and the luck objection is its center of gravity.
