# Non-Causal Libertarianism

Event-causal and agent-causal libertarianism both agree that free actions must be caused — either by events (the agent's reasons and values) or by the agent as a substance. Non-causal libertarianism takes a more radical position: free actions need not have any cause at all. Some free actions are "causally undetermined" in the strongest sense — they are not caused by any prior state, event, or substance; they are simply the agent's direct, uncaused choices.

The motivation for non-causal libertarianism comes from the difficulty that any causal story — even agent causation — seems to face. If the action is caused by the agent, then we can ask what in the agent caused it — what state, property, or decision. If the answer is some state or property of the agent, we have event causation and all its problems. If the answer is "nothing — the agent just chose," then we have non-causal libertarianism under the agent-causal label.

Non-causal libertarianism faces the objection that uncaused actions are mere random events — no more under the agent's control than actions caused by prior events. If nothing caused the choice, then it is arbitrary — it could have been otherwise for no reason. And if it is arbitrary, the agent is not responsible for it. Non-causal libertarians respond by denying that all rational actions must be caused: perhaps the agent's character, values, and deliberation can make an action rational — the right action, for the right reasons — without the rationality being a causal relationship between the values and the action.

Steward's account of "agency" as a genuinely distinctive causal category — neither reducible to event causation nor identical to substance causation — provides one variant of this approach. Agents, on Steward's view, are genuine settlers of matters — they determine what happens through their activity in ways that cannot be reduced to prior deterministic or stochastic event causation.

This section examines non-causal libertarianism, its motivations, and the central difficulty of distinguishing it from mere randomness.
