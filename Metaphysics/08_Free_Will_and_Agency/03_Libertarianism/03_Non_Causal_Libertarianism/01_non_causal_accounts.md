# Non-Causal Libertarianism

Event causal accounts — whether deterministic or indeterministic — make free actions depend on prior mental states. Even on Kane's account, the action is caused by prior desires, beliefs, and deliberative processes, with indeterminism in the causal connection. This means the action is not a fresh beginning; it is a consequence (however probabilistic) of what came before. Non-causal libertarianism takes a more radical position: genuinely free actions are not caused by prior mental states at all, or are caused only in a thin, non-constitutive sense. The agent does not produce the action by producing mental events that then cause the action; the agent simply acts. The acting is basic, uncaused, and irreducible.

Carl Ginet and Hugh McCann are the principal defenders of this position.

## The Motivation

Non-causal libertarianism has a coherent motivation. If all causation must eventually trace through the agent's prior states, and if those states were themselves caused by prior states, the regress of causation undermines ultimate agency no matter how sophisticated the causal account becomes. Event causal libertarianism blocks this regress by inserting genuine indeterminism at some point in the causal chain, but the indeterminism is still *within* the causal chain — it does not break it, it merely makes it probabilistic. Non-causal libertarianism breaks the chain at the action itself, not within the agent's motivational history. The action begins the chain; nothing before it determines or probabilistically causes it.

This is an extreme position — in some ways the most direct expression of the incompatibilist intuition that genuine freedom requires the agent to be a truly fresh beginning.

## Ginet's Simple Indeterminism

Carl Ginet's "simple indeterminism" (*On Action*, 1990) is the most developed non-causal libertarian account. Ginet holds that the basic units of free action — he calls them *acts* — are volitions or intentional bodily movements that are not caused by prior states. The act occurs; it is not caused to occur. But the agent is not a passive bystander: the act is hers because it has a special phenomenal character, the "actish phenomenal quality," that distinguishes acts from mere happenings.

Ginet's key claims:

1. *Basicness*: Some acts are basic — not caused by any prior mental state, including prior intentions. They simply begin; the agent does them without doing something else that causes them.

2. *The actish phenomenal quality*: What makes an act an act rather than a mere event is its phenomenal character — the agent experiences it as something she is doing, not as something happening to her. This experience is not itself a cause of the act; it is the act's constitutive quality.

3. *Rationalization without causation*: Reasons rationalize acts without causing them. The agent acts for reasons — the reasons are the ones *for which* she acts — but this "acting for" relation is not a causal relation. It is a relation of rational intelligibility: the reasons justify the act and are the considerations in light of which the act is performed, without being causes.

Ginet's account can be stated formally:

- An agent A performs a free act X at time T if and only if:
  1. A does X at T. (The act occurs.)
  2. A's doing X has the actish phenomenal quality. (The experience of doing, not of something happening.)
  3. A does X for reasons R. (The act is rationalized by the agent's reasons.)
  4. A's doing X is not caused by any prior event. (No prior causal determination.)

The non-causation condition is what makes the account non-causal. The act simply begins, as a fresh exercise of agency.

## McCann: The Will and the Act of Willing

Hugh McCann (*The Works of Agency*, 1998) develops a non-causal account in which acts of will are basic — not caused by anything prior, not even by prior intentions or desires. There is a difference, for McCann, between *intending* to do something (a prior mental state) and *actually willing* or *deciding* to do it (the act of will itself). The act of will is not caused by the prior intention; it is a fresh exercise of agency. When an agent decides to act, the decision is not produced by her prior desire to decide — the decision is simply the will's own doing.

McCann connects this to the structure of practical reasoning. Practical reasoning terminates in a decision that is not determined by the reasoning — the will's choosing is the basic unit of agency. This preserves the role of reasoning in action without making reasoning a cause that determines the action.

## The Challenge: How Do Reasons Guide Without Causing?

The most pressing objection to non-causal accounts is the challenge of explaining how reasons guide action if they play no causal role. If my reasons for acting are causally inert, how do they explain the action rather than merely accompanying it?

Ginet's answer: reasons rationalize in a non-causal sense. The reason is the consideration that justifies the action and that the agent takes to justify it. The rationalization relation is one of rational intelligibility — the action makes sense in light of the reasons — not a causal relation.

Critics argue that this non-causal rationalization is mysterious. What distinguishes acting for reason R from acting for no reason, if R has no causal influence? If I perform act X and there happens to be a reason R that justifies X, what makes R the reason *for which* I acted rather than a coincidence? The standard answer — the reason caused the action — is precisely what Ginet rejects. Ginet's response: the agent's awareness of R as a justifying reason is constitutive of acting for R. The act is done with an awareness of R as justifying it; this awareness is part of the act's phenomenal character, not a separate causal state.

## Advantages and Disadvantages

Non-causal libertarianism avoids the luck objection in its standard form. Since acts are not caused by prior states, there is no probabilistic gap between prior states and action that chance could fill. The action is just what the agent does, uncaused. This is, in a sense, the most direct vindication of the incompatibilist intuition that genuine freedom requires the agent to be a fresh beginning.

The account also captures certain features of the phenomenology of deliberate action: we sometimes experience deciding as something that simply happens in us — a settling of a question that does not feel like the outcome of a causal mechanism.

Against these advantages, the account faces a distinctive version of the luck objection: if the act is not caused by any prior state, what determines which act occurs? Without a causal answer, the specific act seems arbitrary in a way that undermines rather than grounds agency. The non-causal account seems to disconnect action from the agent's psychological history, which means that in an important sense the action is not the agent's — it is an uncaused event that happens in her.

Whether the actish phenomenal quality can bear the weight Ginet places on it — constituting the act as genuinely the agent's own rather than just an event with a distinctive feel — is the central unresolved question for non-causal libertarianism.

## Position in the Broader Debate

Non-causal libertarianism marks the most radical form of the incompatibilist commitment to genuine origination. Most philosophers who accept libertarianism prefer event causal accounts (Kane, Clarke) or agent causal accounts (O'Connor, Taylor). But the non-causal approach serves as an important reference point: it represents what libertarianism must look like if the demand for genuine origination is pushed to its logical conclusion and the residual causal structures of event causal and agent causal accounts are rejected as insufficient. Whether that logical conclusion is philosophically viable, or whether it collapses into a position indistinguishable from mere chance, is the question that defines the outer boundary of the libertarian program.
