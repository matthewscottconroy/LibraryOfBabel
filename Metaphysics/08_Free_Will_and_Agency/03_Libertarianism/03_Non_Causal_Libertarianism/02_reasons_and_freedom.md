# Reasons and Freedom

There is a dilemma at the heart of libertarianism that no version of the view has fully dissolved. Free actions are supposed to be the agent's own — expressive of her rational nature, performed for reasons, intelligibly connected to her values and deliberation. But libertarianism also requires that free actions be undetermined by prior causes. The tension: if reasons determine actions, indeterminism is compromised; if reasons do not determine actions, the actions seem arbitrary. This is the "Mind Argument" as van Inwagen formulates it, and it cuts to the heart of what any libertarian account must explain.

## The Dilemma

Put starkly, the dilemma runs:

- If determinism is true, actions are determined by prior states — including reasons. The agent could not have done otherwise. Freedom is undermined.
- If indeterminism is true, actions are not fully determined by prior states. But undetermined events are random. Random actions are not the agent's free choices.
- Either determinism or indeterminism is true.
- Therefore, free will is impossible.

Hume anticipated this. The alternatives to causal necessity are either determination (no libertarian freedom) or randomness (no responsibility). The argument suggests that the libertarian is trapped between two conditions, neither of which is freedom.

The challenge for libertarianism is to articulate a third possibility: actions that are neither fully determined by prior states nor random, but genuinely free choices made by agents for reasons. This requires an account of how reasons relate to free actions that is distinct from both determination and mere accompaniment.

## Four Models of the Reasons-Action Relationship

We can distinguish four main models, each reflecting a different theoretical position:

*Determinist causation by reasons* (compatibilist): reasons as mental events determine actions. The reason is the cause; the action is the effect; the connection is deterministic. This preserves rational connection — the action is what it is because the reasons are what they are — but eliminates alternate possibilities in the categorical sense. Compatibilists accept this and argue that deterministic causation by reasons is precisely what makes an action free: the action is the agent's own because it is caused by her own reasons.

*Probabilistic causation by reasons* (event causal libertarianism): reasons probabilistically cause actions. This preserves a rational connection (reasons cause the action) and genuine openness (the action was not inevitable given the reasons). The objection is the luck objection: the gap between what the reasons make probable and what actually occurs is filled by chance, not by the agent.

*Non-causal rationalization by reasons* (non-causal libertarianism): reasons rationalize actions without causing them. The reason is the consideration in light of which the agent acts; it justifies and makes intelligible the action without being a cause. The advantage is that this avoids the probabilistic gap that luck can fill. The disadvantage is that the rationalization relation — acting in light of reasons without being caused by them — remains philosophically underdeveloped and counterintuitive to many.

*Agent-causal responsiveness to reasons*: the agent as substance causes the action in response to reasons. The agent's causing is not determined by the reasons (which would collapse to model 1) and not probabilistically caused by them (which would collapse to model 2). The agent exercises her causal power in response to reasons. This is Chisholm's model. The objection: the relation of "responding to reasons" without being caused by them is as mysterious as the non-causal rationalization of model 3.

## Threading the Needle: Kane's Account

Kane's account of self-forming actions is the most sophisticated attempt to navigate between the two horns. His key move: in SFAs, both possible outcomes are motivated by the agent's actual reasons. The indeterminism does not introduce unmotivated chance into decision-making; it operates within the space of motivated options. Both possible decisions express the agent's authentic reasons and values.

The argument:

- P1: A free action must be one for which the agent has reasons.
- P2: A free action must be genuinely undetermined (libertarianism).
- P3: In self-forming actions, both possible outcomes are motivated by the agent's reasons.
- P4: Therefore, whichever outcome occurs, it is one for which the agent has reasons.
- C: In self-forming actions, indeterminism does not make the action unmotivated — the agent has reasons for whichever outcome occurs.

The randomness horn of the dilemma is avoided because both possible outcomes are motivated by reasons. The determination horn is avoided because neither outcome is determined by the reasons. The result is intended to be genuine freedom: indeterministic action that is genuinely the agent's own because it is motivated by her actual reasons.

## The Ultimacy Condition

The relationship between reasons and freedom is further complicated by the ultimacy condition Kane invokes. For an agent to be ultimately responsible for an action, the action must trace back to the agent as its ultimate source. But the reasons for which the agent acts are themselves products of prior factors — the agent's character, shaped by genetics, upbringing, and experience. If the agent acts for reasons R, and R was formed by factors outside her control, is the action ultimately hers?

Kane's answer involves the regress of SFAs across a lifetime. Through SFAs, the agent shapes her own character over time. The reasons that guide later SFAs are partly products of earlier SFAs. The agent is not the absolute originator of all her reasons — no one is — but she is a genuine co-author of the reasons that guide her, through the history of self-forming choices. This does not fully satisfy the ultimacy condition: even the earliest SFAs occur with reasons that were not chosen. But Kane argues that "ultimate" responsibility does not require that the agent be the absolute originator of all her reasons — only that she be a genuine contributor to the formation of her reasons through her own choices over time.

## Wolf's Normative Dimension

Susan Wolf argues that the relationship between reasons and freedom has a normative dimension that purely psychological accounts miss. Freedom is not just sensitivity to reasons but sensitivity to *good* reasons — to what is genuinely worth pursuing. Her "Reason View" holds that freedom is the ability to act in accordance with reason, where this means not just tracking reasons as the agent perceives them but having genuine rational access to what is normatively correct.

- P1: Full moral responsibility requires that the agent was able to recognize and respond to the reasons that applied to her situation.
- P2: Recognizing and responding to reasons requires not just the psychological capacity to track reasons but access to the normative facts that constitute reasons.
- C: Full moral responsibility requires both psychological reasons-responsiveness and normative access to what is genuinely good.

This has direct implications for the reasons-freedom relationship: freedom is not merely about being the source of action (sourcehood) or having alternate possibilities (PAP), but about having genuine rational access to normative truth. An agent who acts badly despite having this access is fully blameworthy; an agent who lacks this access — due to severe moral deprivation, neurological disorder, or effective indoctrination — is less than fully blameworthy.

## The Core of the Problem

The relationship between reasons and freedom is not a solvable puzzle so much as the central difficulty of libertarianism. Libertarianism needs reasons to genuinely guide free action — otherwise freedom collapses into randomness. But it needs freedom to be undetermined by reasons — otherwise freedom collapses into determinism. Any account must navigate between these requirements, and every attempt to navigate between them faces objections from both directions. That is not a reason to abandon the inquiry, but it is a reason to hold whatever answer one favors with appropriate tentativeness.
