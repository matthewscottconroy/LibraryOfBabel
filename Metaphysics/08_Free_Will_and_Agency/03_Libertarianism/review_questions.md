# Review Questions — Libertarianism

1. Agent causation is introduced to break the chain of event causation that the Consequence Argument traces back to factors outside the agent's control. But the chapter identifies three distinct problems: the intelligibility problem, the integration problem, and the luck objection. Which of these poses the deepest challenge, and why is O'Connor's emergentist response to the intelligibility problem insufficient on its own to answer the other two?

2. Chisholm argues from the existence of moral responsibility to the existence of agent causation: if responsibility is real, substance causation must be real. Both compatibilists and hard incompatibilists challenge one of the two premises this argument requires. What exactly does each challenger deny, and whose challenge is more damaging to Chisholm's overall position?

3. Event causal libertarianism tries to secure libertarian freedom without the metaphysical cost of agent causation by invoking probabilistic causation by reasons. Explain the central tension this account must navigate: why does indeterministic causation by reasons satisfy the openness condition but create a difficulty for the rational-connection condition? Does the luck objection show that event causal libertarianism fails on both counts?

4. Kane's concept of self-forming actions (SFAs) is his answer to the luck objection. The response depends on the idea that in an SFA both possible outcomes are motivated by the agent's actual reasons, so neither outcome is alien to the agent. Critics press the residual luck problem and the endorsement problem. Which criticism cuts deeper against Kane, and does his account of "prospective endorsement" successfully distinguish SFAs from mere chance events?

5. Non-causal libertarianism (Ginet) holds that basic acts are not caused by prior mental states and that reasons rationalize without causing. The chapter notes this avoids the luck objection in one form but incurs a distinctive version of it. What is that version, and can Ginet's actish phenomenal quality provide what the luck objection demands — an explanation of how the act is the agent's own rather than simply a causally unmoored event with a distinctive feel?

6. The chapter frames a dilemma at the heart of libertarianism: if reasons determine actions, indeterminism is lost; if reasons do not determine actions, actions are random. Van Inwagen calls this the Mind Argument. Kane's SFA account, agent causation, and non-causal rationalization each offer a different escape route. Compare two of these escapes: which one makes the most progress on the dilemma without simply relabeling the problem?

7. The chapter's discussion of quantum mechanics concludes that quantum indeterminism may be a necessary but not sufficient condition for libertarian free will. Why is this the right assessment? What philosophical work remains to be done even after granting genuine physical indeterminism — and which libertarian variant (agent causal, event causal, or non-causal) is best positioned to do that work?

8. Libertarianism is motivated by the idea that genuine moral responsibility requires ultimate self-authorship. But Galen Strawson's basic argument — discussed in the following chapter — threatens to show that no causal history, deterministic or indeterministic, can provide ultimate self-authorship, because every history traces to an unchosen starting character. If that argument succeeds, what exactly is left of the libertarian project, and is Kane's response via the regress of SFAs across a lifetime adequate to meet it?
