# Hard Incompatibilism

Hard incompatibilism accepts the Consequence Argument and concludes that we do not have free will. Whether or not determinism is true — and even if quantum indeterminism is real — the kind of freedom required for basic moral desert does not exist. We praise and blame, reward and punish, hold people responsible — but if hard incompatibilism is correct, these practices rest on a fundamental mistake about the causal origins of action.

Hard determinism, the traditional form, pairs this conclusion with the claim that the world is determined. Quantum mechanics appears to undermine classical determinism, but hard incompatibilists often argue that indeterminism doesn't help: if the relevant events are random rather than determined, the agent is still not in control of them, and responsibility seems equally undermined. The threat to free will comes not from determinism specifically but from the fact that all events — whether determined or random — are ultimately traceable to factors beyond the agent's control.

The practical implications are genuinely troubling. If hard incompatibilism is correct, the retributive theory of punishment — the view that wrongdoers deserve to suffer — is unjustified. We should not punish criminals because they deserve it; at most, we should incapacitate them or rehabilitate them or deter others — purely consequentialist grounds. Similarly, reactive attitudes like moral anger, resentment, and indignation — the attitudes that structure our interpersonal relationships and moral practices — are based on a picture of persons as genuinely responsible agents that cannot be sustained.

Revisionary approaches within the hard incompatibilist tradition attempt to preserve something morally important from the wreckage of free will. Derk Pereboom argues that while basic desert is illusory, a robust practice of holding people responsible can be preserved on forward-looking grounds. Bruce Waller argues that eliminating desert-based responsibility opens space for more compassionate and more effective responses to wrongdoing.

The relationship between free will and neuroscience adds empirical texture: Libet's experiments suggest that neural activity precedes conscious intention in simple voluntary movements, though the interpretation of these experiments is hotly contested. This chapter examines hard incompatibilism in its main forms, its implications for moral practice, and the neuroscientific evidence that bears on it.
