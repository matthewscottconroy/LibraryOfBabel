# Hard Determinism

Hard determinism holds that (1) determinism is true, (2) free will requires the ability to do otherwise, and (3) determinism eliminates the ability to do otherwise — and therefore that free will does not exist. This is the position that takes both the threat of determinism and the importance of genuine alternative possibilities most seriously, accepting the incompatibilist's claim that there is no room for free will in a determined world, and accepting the empirical claim (or assuming it for argument's sake) that the world is in fact determined.

The hard determinist's picture of the world is, in a sense, the most straightforward: the universe unfolds according to natural laws, and human beings are parts of the universe. Our deliberations, our choices, our sense of authorship over our actions — all of these are themselves events in the causal order, determined by prior events. The experience of choosing freely is a phenomenological artifact of our ignorance of the causal forces that determine our actions; if we could see the complete causal structure, we would see that each "choice" had to be as it was.

The ethical implications of hard determinism are radical. If no one could have done otherwise, then no one deserves praise or blame, reward or punishment, in the retributive sense. The practice of holding people responsible — of praising and blaming them, of rewarding virtue and punishing vice — would be, on the hard determinist's view, based on a fiction. This does not mean that we should abandon all practices of response to human behavior — we might still modify behavior through incentives and deterrents, and we might still express attitudes that are predictable responses to others' actions. But the metaphysical foundation of retributive responsibility is undermined.

Hard determinism is often confused with fatalism — the view that outcomes are inevitable regardless of what one does. Determinism and fatalism are different: determinism says that what you do is determined by prior causes; fatalism says that outcomes are fixed regardless of what you do. This section examines hard determinism, its arguments, and its moral implications.
