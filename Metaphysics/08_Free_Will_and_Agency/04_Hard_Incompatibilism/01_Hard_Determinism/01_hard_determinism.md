# Hard Determinism

We have examined two main responses to the incompatibilist argument: compatibilism denies that determinism threatens the kind of freedom that matters; libertarianism accepts the incompatibility but maintains that freedom exists because determinism is false. There is a third response: accept the incompatibilist argument, accept that the world is (effectively) determined, and draw the skeptical conclusion. This is hard determinism, and in its contemporary form, hard incompatibilism — the view that whether or not determinism is strictly true, free will in the sense required for genuine moral responsibility does not exist.

## The Historical Version

Hard determinism was associated in the nineteenth and early twentieth century with scientific materialism. The success of Newtonian mechanics seemed to support universal determinism. T. H. Huxley argued in "On the Hypothesis That Animals Are Automata" (1874) that humans are complex biological machines whose behavior is entirely determined by physics. Consciousness is an epiphenomenon — a byproduct of physical processes that does no causal work. The "will" we experience as free is a shadow cast by neural processes, not a genuine cause of behavior.

Baron d'Holbach (*System of Nature*, 1770) stated the position with systematic clarity: all events in the universe, including human decisions, are determined by the prior state of matter and the laws of nature. Human beings are machines; their virtue and vice are as determined as the motion of the planets. The criminal is no more deserving of punishment than a malfunctioning clock is deserving of blame.

The view fell somewhat out of favor after the advent of quantum mechanics, which appears to show that the universe is not deterministically governed at the micro level. This prompted a reformulation.

## Hard Incompatibilism: The Both-Horns Version

The contemporary version, hard incompatibilism, does not depend on determinism being true. The argument:

- P1: If determinism is true, actions are necessitated by prior events outside the agent's control.
- P2: Necessitation by prior events outside the agent's control is incompatible with the kind of freedom required for moral responsibility.
- P3: If indeterminism is true, undetermined actions are random — not controlled by the agent any more than random events generally are.
- P4: Actions that are random are also incompatible with the kind of freedom required for moral responsibility.
- P5: Either determinism or indeterminism is true.
- C: Either way, free will in the relevant sense does not exist.

This is the "both-horns" dilemma: determinism and indeterminism are mutually exhaustive, and neither supports genuine freedom. The libertarian's response to P3 — that Kane's self-forming actions show indeterminism can be non-arbitrary — is the main point of contention. The hard incompatibilist insists that the luck objection cannot be answered: genuine indeterminism is randomness, not freedom.

## Galen Strawson's Basic Argument

Galen Strawson's "basic argument" (developed in "The Impossibility of Moral Responsibility," 1994, and *Freedom and Belief*, 1986) is the most rigorous contemporary formulation. It does not depend on determinism at all. It depends only on the relationship between action, character, and the regress of responsibility.

The basic argument:

- P1: For an agent to be truly morally responsible for an action, she must be truly responsible for the way she is — her character, reasons, and motivational structure — in the respects relevant to the action.
- P2: For the agent to be truly responsible for the way she is, she must have made herself the way she is through prior choices.
- P3: For those prior choices to ground responsibility, the agent must have been truly responsible for the way she was when she made those choices.
- P4: Either this regress continues infinitely (impossible) or it terminates at some point where the agent's character was not the result of her own choices.
- C: Wherever the regress terminates, the agent's character at that point was not the result of her own choices — it was a product of factors outside her control.
- Final conclusion: No one is ever truly morally responsible for their actions.

The argument is powerful because it applies whether or not determinism is true. Even if we grant Kane's libertarianism and allow that some actions are genuinely undetermined, the basic argument still applies: the character from which SFAs spring was not itself fully chosen by the agent.

The argument can be stated more precisely. Let A = the action; let C = the character from which A flows. Responsibility for A requires responsibility for C, since A flows from C. Responsibility for C requires that C was produced by the agent's own prior choices. Let C' = the character from which those choices flowed. Responsibility for those choices requires responsibility for C'. This generates the regress C₀ → C₁ → C₂ → ... which must terminate at some Cₙ that was not the product of the agent's choices. The agent is not responsible for Cₙ; therefore, by regress, she is not responsible for any of the choices or actions that flow from Cₙ.

## Compatibilist Responses

Compatibilists challenge steps P1 and P2. Against P1: responsibility for an action does not require responsibility for the character from which it flows — it requires only that the action flow from the agent's own character *in the right way*. Fischer and Ravizza's guidance control: the agent is responsible if she acts from her own reasons-responsive mechanism, regardless of whether she is ultimately responsible for that mechanism's formation. The mechanism is hers because she has taken responsibility for it through an appropriate process.

Against P2: responsibility for character does not require that character was chosen through prior acts of will. One can be responsible for one's character in a non-voluntarist sense: the character is one's own, and one endorses it, even though one did not "choose" it through a prior discrete act. The demands of the regress argument are too strong — they would require an infinite series of character-forming choices.

Galen Strawson distinguishes between responsibility in a wide sense — the kind that makes someone an appropriate target of reactive attitudes and forward-looking accountability — and responsibility in the narrow sense that grounds retributive desert. He accepts that compatibilist conditions may be sufficient for the wide sense. But he argues that the ordinary concept at work in our retributive practices involves the narrow sense, which the basic argument shows to be unattainable.

## The Hard Determinist Practical Stance

Hard determinism, consistently maintained, demands revision of our practical attitudes toward wrongdoing. If no one is ever truly responsible for their actions, retributive punishment — causing suffering because wrongdoers deserve it — loses its justification. The hard determinist advocates a purely consequentialist approach to wrongdoing: responses to harmful behavior should be justified by their effectiveness at reducing future harm (deterrence, rehabilitation, incapacitation), not by the offender's desert.

Hard determinism also has implications for the reactive attitudes. If no one is ultimately responsible for their character and actions, resentment, indignation, and moral disapprobation may remain appropriate in some modified form — as expressions of one's sense of being harmed and one's values — but not in the retributive form that presupposes that the wrongdoer deserves to suffer. This is Pereboom's position, developed in detail in the following files. The interesting philosophical question is not whether hard incompatibilism has practical consequences — obviously it does — but whether those consequences are as catastrophic as critics allege, or whether, as Pereboom argues, a fulfilling moral life is available without basic desert responsibility.
