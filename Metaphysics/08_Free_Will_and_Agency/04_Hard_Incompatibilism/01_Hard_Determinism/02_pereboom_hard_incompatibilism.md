# Pereboom's Hard Incompatibilism

Derk Pereboom is the most prominent contemporary defender of hard incompatibilism. His position is developed most fully in *Living Without Free Will* (2001) and extended in *Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life* (2014). What distinguishes Pereboom's work is not merely the rigor of his argument for the skeptical conclusion — the argument that free will in the relevant sense does not exist — but the seriousness with which he engages the question of what a well-lived human life looks like once that conclusion is accepted.

## The Central Claim

Hard incompatibilism, for Pereboom, conjoins two theses. First, *incompatibilism*: free will in the sense required for basic desert moral responsibility is incompatible with both causal determinism and the kind of indeterminism that any plausible physical theory provides. Second, *skepticism*: we lack free will in that sense. Neither determinism nor the available forms of indeterminism provide it.

The crucial distinction is between *basic desert* moral responsibility and softer forms. Basic desert holds that wrongdoers deserve to suffer for their wrongdoing in a deep sense that would justify retributive punishment independently of any consequentialist considerations. Pereboom is skeptical of this. He is not skeptical of attributability (the action is attributable to the agent as an expression of her will), answerability (the agent can be called to account), or forward-looking responsibility practices (practices aimed at improving behavior). These forms of responsibility can survive the skeptical challenge. What cannot survive, he argues, is the retributive desert that underwrites punishment for its own sake.

## The Four-Case Argument

Pereboom's most influential argument is the four-case argument, designed to show that even compatibilist conditions are insufficient for basic desert moral responsibility when we trace the causal history of action far enough back.

**Case 1 — Direct Manipulation**: At time T, neuroscientists directly manipulate Plum's brain, producing the desire to kill White and the decision to act on it. The scientists control Plum's deliberative process through direct intervention. Almost everyone judges that Plum is not morally responsible — he is essentially a puppet.

**Case 2 — Indirect Manipulation**: The same neuroscientists designed Plum at the beginning of his life, building in egoistic values and reasoning dispositions that would lead him to kill White at T. Plum reasons and decides on his own, but his reasoning process unfolds exactly as the scientists designed. Most people still judge that Plum is not fully responsible — the design makes the scientists the ultimate cause.

**Case 3 — Social Formation**: Plum's character was not programmed by scientists but was shaped by his upbringing — parents, teachers, peers, social environment — in ways that produce the same result. He reasons on his own, but his character is a product of environmental shaping. Intuitions here are more divided, but many people feel Plum's responsibility is at least reduced.

**Case 4 — Ordinary Deterministic Action**: Plum is an ordinary person in a deterministic world. His character was shaped by genetics and environment in the normal course of development. He reasons and decides normally. He kills White.

Pereboom's argument: there is no morally relevant difference between Case 1 and Case 4. In each case, Plum's action ultimately traces back to factors entirely outside his control. If Plum is not responsible in Case 1, and there is no relevant difference across the cases, he is not responsible in Case 4.

## Why There Is No Relevant Difference

Pereboom anticipates the main compatibilist responses.

The *mechanism ownership* response (Fischer and Ravizza): the mechanism in Cases 1-2 is not Plum's own — it was imposed by external agents — while the mechanism in Case 4 is Plum's own, formed through normal development. Pereboom's reply: this distinction only relocates the problem. Whether a mechanism is "one's own" must ultimately be cashed out in causal terms — the mechanism was formed through a certain causal history. In Case 4, as in Case 3, the causal history traces back to factors outside Plum's control. If the mechanism in Cases 1-2 is not Plum's own because it was caused by scientists, why is the mechanism in Case 4 Plum's own if it was caused by genetics and environment?

The *bypassing/shaping* distinction (Wolf): manipulation bypasses rational agency while normal development shapes it. Pereboom's reply: whether a process bypasses or shapes rational agency does not determine whether the agent is ultimately responsible in the basic desert sense. If Plum's rational agency was shaped (not bypassed) by upbringing into the dispositions that led him to kill White, the shaping still traces to factors outside Plum's control. The bypass/shape distinction matters for attributability but not for desert.

The *degree response*: Cases 1-3 yield reduced responsibility; Case 4 is relevantly different. Pereboom's reply: what would make Case 4 relevantly different is that Plum's action traces to himself as its ultimate source in a way that Cases 1-3 do not. But this requires ultimate sourcehood — which the compatibilist cannot provide.

## The Case Against Libertarianism

Pereboom is equally skeptical of libertarian responses. Even granting Kane's event causal libertarianism, the basic argument (Galen Strawson's) still applies: the character from which SFAs spring traces back to unchosen factors. Moreover, Pereboom argues that event causal indeterminism makes responsibility worse, not better: if Plum's decision to kill White was partly a result of quantum indeterminism in his deliberation, the specific decision — killing White rather than not — was partly a matter of deliberative luck. This introduces an additional dimension of luck that undermines rather than grounds responsibility.

Agent causation, even if metaphysically coherent, does not escape the basic argument: the agent's exercise of causal power in any specific situation is still influenced by prior factors — reasons, character, history — that trace back to unchosen origins.

## Pereboom's Practical View

Having argued that basic desert moral responsibility does not exist, Pereboom develops a detailed account of what can replace it:

*Forward-looking accountability*: We can hold people accountable for their actions not because they deserve to suffer but because accountability has good effects — it helps agents improve behavior, protects victims, maintains social norms.

*Modified reactive attitudes*: The reactive attitudes need not be abandoned but can be reconceived as non-retributive. Resentment at being wronged can be retained as an expression of one's sense of having been harmed, without implying that the wrongdoer deserves to suffer.

*Consequentialist criminal justice*: Criminal justice should eliminate retributive elements. Incapacitation (protecting society from dangerous individuals), rehabilitation (helping offenders change), and deterrence (discouraging future crime) remain legitimate justifications for criminal sanctions. Retribution does not.

*Moral community*: Even without basic desert responsibility, communities can be bound by shared norms, mutual accountability, and reactive attitudes in modified form. Membership in a moral community requires only that members be capable of responding to reasons and engaging in moral dialogue.

## Significance

Pereboom's hard incompatibilism represents the most serious and developed challenge to both compatibilism and libertarianism currently available. His four-case argument has become a standard reference point. His careful attention to the practical implications of the skeptical conclusion has shown that it need not lead to nihilism or despair. And his distinction between basic desert responsibility and other forms of moral responsibility has been widely adopted by philosophers across the spectrum, regardless of whether they accept his skeptical conclusion. Even those who reject hard incompatibilism have found that engaging with Pereboom's arguments clarifies what their own positions require.
