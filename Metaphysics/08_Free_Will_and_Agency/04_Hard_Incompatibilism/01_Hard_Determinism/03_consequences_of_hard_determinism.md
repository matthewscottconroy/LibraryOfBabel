# Consequences of Hard Determinism

If hard incompatibilism is true, the practical consequences are substantial. This is what makes the question more than academic: the free will debate bears directly on how we should organize criminal justice, how we should understand our reactive attitudes, and what sense we should make of our own lives and achievements. Critics of hard incompatibilism often argue that the practical consequences are so catastrophic — that retributive justice, reactive attitudes, and self-respect all require the kind of basic desert that hard incompatibilism denies — that the view must be wrong regardless of the theoretical arguments for it. Pereboom's response is that the pessimist overstates the damage, and that the consequences of accepting hard incompatibilism are more manageable than they appear.

## Retributive Punishment

Retributivism holds that wrongdoers deserve punishment as an end in itself — proportionate suffering is owed to them for their crimes. The Kantian version is stark: the moral law requires that wrongdoers be punished in proportion to their crimes regardless of consequences. Even if civil society were dissolving, Kant held, the last murderer must be executed, because justice demands it.

Hard incompatibilism denies this wholesale. If no one is ultimately responsible for their actions in the sense that grounds desert, no one deserves punishment in the retributive sense. This challenges much of our current criminal justice system, which is deeply informed by retributive intuitions — the language of "getting what you deserve," "paying one's debt to society," and "paying for your crime" all presuppose that criminals deserve to suffer.

The practical implication is a shift from retributive to consequentialist criminal justice. A system consistent with hard incompatibilism would justify punishment only on forward-looking grounds: deterrence (communicating that crime does not pay), rehabilitation (helping offenders become better members of society), incapacitation (protecting society from dangerous individuals), and moral communication (expressing community standards). None of these requires that the offender deserves to suffer — they require only that the relevant sanctions have good consequences.

## The Reactive Attitudes

P. F. Strawson showed that reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, love — are central to moral life. Hard incompatibilism, if fully embraced, might seem to require abandoning them, since they presuppose that others are genuinely responsible for what they do. Pereboom argues that a distinction can be drawn between retributive and non-retributive versions of the reactive attitudes, and that the non-retributive versions can be retained.

Consider resentment. *Retributive resentment* is the feeling that the person who wronged you deserves to suffer for having done so. *Non-retributive resentment* is the feeling that you were wronged, that the other's action expressed insufficient regard for you, that the relationship is damaged — without the further claim that the person deserves to suffer. Hard incompatibilism requires abandoning retributive resentment but need not require abandoning non-retributive resentment. Similarly, gratitude can be retained as warm appreciation of benefit and goodwill without implying that the benefactor deserves special reward in a desert sense.

Whether these non-retributive forms are genuinely the same reactive attitudes as the retributive forms, or pale substitutes, is contested. P. F. Strawson himself seemed to think the reactive attitudes are too deeply embedded in human social life to be revised by philosophical argument. Pereboom thinks they can be reconceived, at least partially, without destroying what is valuable in them.

## Moral Effort and Character

Hard incompatibilism does not imply fatalism — the view that deliberation and effort are pointless because outcomes are fixed regardless. Deliberation is causally efficacious even in a determined world. Deciding to form good habits, to engage in moral reflection, to seek better relationships causally contributes to the formation of better character. The fact that my decision was itself caused by prior states does not make its causal contribution to character change less real.

There are also forward-looking reasons for character development that do not require backward-looking desert. A person of good character tends to live a better life, contribute more to others' wellbeing, and find greater satisfaction in her activities. These consequentialist reasons for moral development do not require that one deserve credit for the character one develops. Integrity and authenticity also provide reasons: even if one's character traces to unchosen factors, it is still one's character — the dispositional structure through which one engages with the world. Improving this character is a matter of authenticity, not merely of earning credit.

## Moral Assessment Without Desert

Hard incompatibilism challenges the idea of *desert* in moral assessment, but it does not eliminate moral assessment. We can still distinguish right from wrong, good character from poor character, actions with good will from actions with malice, behaviors that promote wellbeing from behaviors that cause harm. None of these assessments requires that agents deserve praise or blame in the retributive sense. We can morally assess actions and agents without claiming they deserve to suffer or flourish independently of any good consequences that follow.

This is the "attributability without accountability" move: actions are attributable to agents as expressions of their will and character, and this attribution grounds moral assessment, without implying that the agent deserves to be made to suffer or flourish in a retributive sense.

## Self-Directed Attitudes

Hard incompatibilism has implications for how agents relate to their own past actions. Pereboom argues that *guilt in the retributive sense* — the sense in which one deserves to suffer for past wrongdoing — is not appropriate if hard incompatibilism is true. But *guilt in the forward-looking sense* — a recognition that one acted wrongly, that one's action expressed bad character or values, and a motivation to repair the damage and change behavior — can be appropriate without presupposing desert.

Similarly, *satisfaction* and *self-assessment* — recognizing that one has developed good character or achieved something worthwhile — can be appropriate without presupposing that one deserves special credit. The achievements are genuinely one's own even if their ultimate origin traces to unchosen factors.

## Wellbeing and Life's Meaning

A final concern: does hard incompatibilism undermine the sense that our lives have meaning? If we are not ultimately responsible for what we do — if our actions trace to unchosen factors — can we be genuine authors of our lives?

Pereboom argues that life's meaning does not depend on ultimate responsibility. The things that make life meaningful — love, friendship, intellectual achievement, moral growth, engagement with beauty and nature — are valuable independently of whether we deserve credit for them. A person who develops a genuine friendship, creates meaningful art, or grows in wisdom contributes something real to the world, regardless of whether she ultimately deserves credit for the growth.

Hard incompatibilism may, in fact, liberate us from an excessive preoccupation with desert and credit. If we stop asking "do I deserve this success?" and instead focus on what we contribute and what we genuinely value, we may find that life's meaning is richer than a desert-focused worldview allows.
