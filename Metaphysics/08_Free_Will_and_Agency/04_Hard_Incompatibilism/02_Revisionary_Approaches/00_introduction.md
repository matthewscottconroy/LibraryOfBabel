# Revisionary Approaches to Free Will

Revisionary approaches accept some version of the incompatibilist conclusion — that free will in the traditional sense does not exist or is not what we thought it was — but argue that our practices of holding responsible, of blaming and praising, of making demands on one another, can be revised in ways that are appropriate to what the metaphysics actually permits. Rather than simply accepting hard determinism's nihilistic conclusion, revisionary approaches reconstruct what moral responsibility requires and show how it can be satisfied even without traditional free will.

Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962) — which is not itself a revisionary approach but has generated much of the revisionary tradition — argued that the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, love) are the foundation of our practices of moral responsibility. These attitudes are not dependent on any metaphysical thesis about determinism or alternative possibilities; they are responses to the quality of agents' wills — to what their actions express about their regard for others. Whether determinism is true is irrelevant to whether the reactive attitudes are appropriate: what matters is whether the agent's action expressed good will, ill will, or indifference.

Pereboom's "free will skepticism" accepts that the kind of free will required for basic desert — desert of punishment and reward purely in virtue of one's choices — does not exist, because the causal history of any action undermines the agent's ultimate origination of it. But Pereboom argues that our practices of holding responsible can be reconstructed without basic desert: we can express forward-looking attitudes toward agents, set expectations for future behavior, and communicate the importance of norms, without claiming that agents deserve anything in the backward-looking retributive sense.

Vargas's "revisionism" holds that the concept of free will should be revised in light of what we learn about the metaphysics of agency, keeping what is worth preserving (the practice of holding responsible that is important for human social life) while abandoning what is not (the metaphysically extravagant picture of libertarian free will).

This section examines these revisionary approaches, assessing how much of our practice of moral responsibility they can preserve.
