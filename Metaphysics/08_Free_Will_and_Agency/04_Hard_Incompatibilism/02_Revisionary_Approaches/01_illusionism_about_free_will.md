# Illusionism About Free Will

Suppose we accept, on theoretical grounds, that libertarian free will does not exist — that the folk belief in ultimate self-determination and retributive desert is false. What should we do with this conclusion? One natural answer: accept it, communicate it, and help people revise their beliefs and practices accordingly. But another answer, philosophically more uncomfortable, is to ask whether widespread acceptance of the truth might itself be harmful. What if human moral life depends in ways we cannot easily replace on a belief that happens to be false? This is the question that motivates illusionism about free will.

## The Illusionist Position

Illusionists about free will accept the philosophical arguments against libertarian free will but argue that these arguments, if widely communicated and accepted, could have harmful social and psychological consequences. Saul Smilansky (*Free Will and Illusion*, 2000) is the most prominent defender. Smilansky's position is nuanced: he accepts compatibilist free will as real — agents are genuinely reasons-responsive, act from their own values, and are appropriate targets of reactive attitudes in Strawson's sense. But the folk concept of free will, he argues, is more demanding than this. It involves the idea that agents are ultimately self-determined in a way that makes them deserving of praise or blame independently of any forward-looking justification. This stronger folk concept is an illusion. But it is, Smilansky contends, a foundational illusion — ordinary moral life depends on it.

## The Argument for Illusionism's Practical Value

Smilansky's central argument:

- P1: Human moral life depends on certain beliefs about ourselves — beliefs that we are genuinely capable of choosing otherwise, that we deserve credit or blame, that our choices truly express our authentic selves in a deep sense.
- P2: These beliefs are not fully vindicated by compatibilist free will, and the deep reality (if hard incompatibilism is true) is that we lack the ultimate self-determination these beliefs presuppose.
- P3: If these beliefs are widely abandoned — if people genuinely internalize the hard incompatibilist conclusion — important features of moral life may be damaged: self-respect, moral urgency, the sense that choices matter, the appropriateness of reactive attitudes in their retributive form.
- C: The illusion of libertarian free will has genuine value; undermining it may cause net harm; philosophical caution is therefore warranted.

The conclusion Smilansky draws is not that philosophers should actively promote the illusion but that they should be circumspect about how aggressively they broadcast the skeptical conclusion. There is value in a certain philosophical reticence — in not making the hard incompatibilist conclusion maximally prominent — even if intellectual honesty requires acknowledging it to those who ask directly.

## Critiques of Illusionism

The position has been challenged from several directions. The *epistemic objection* holds that deliberately maintaining a false belief, or encouraging others to maintain one, violates basic epistemic norms. Intellectual honesty requires that we believe what is true and communicate truth to others. Smilansky acknowledges this but argues that the illusion is not one we can simply abandon through philosophical argument — it may be too deeply embedded in human psychology to be dislodged. This gives a pragmatic reason for caution without committing to actively promoting falsehood.

The *compatibilist objection* argues that Smilansky overstates the gap between folk belief and philosophical truth. If compatibilist free will is genuine — if agents are genuinely reasons-responsive, genuinely act from their own values, and genuinely are appropriate targets of reactive attitudes — then the folk concept of free will may be largely accurate. What is illusory may be only the most extreme retributive uses of the concept, not the concept as ordinarily employed. On this view, there is no foundational illusion to preserve — only philosophical confusion to dissolve.

The *optimism objection*, from Pereboom, argues that the hard incompatibilist conclusion, properly understood, does not damage moral life as severely as Smilansky fears. If people can internalize the distinction between basic desert responsibility (which hard incompatibilism denies) and forward-looking accountability (which survives), the practical damage may be minimal. The assumption that moral life essentially depends on the illusion of desert may itself be an illusion.

The *political objection* holds that there is something troubling about the idea that philosophical truths should be withheld from people who cannot handle them. This distinction between philosophers who know the truth and ordinary people who need comfortable fictions is both paternalistic and historically associated with positions that have not aged well. If the truth is hard to bear, the appropriate response is to help people develop the resources to bear it — not to manage them through selective disclosure.

## Dennett's Contrasting View

Daniel Dennett's position is opposed to illusionism from the other side. Dennett argues that compatibilist free will is not a pale substitute for libertarian free will but the genuine article — the only kind worth having (*Elbow Room*, 1984; *Freedom Evolves*, 2003). The libertarian who wants "more" than compatibilism provides is suffering from a philosophical confusion about what freedom requires. The folk belief in free will, properly understood, is largely correct: people really are reasons-responsive, really do act from their own values, and really are appropriate targets of reactive attitudes. There is no illusion to preserve — and no illusion to worry about undermining.

For Dennett, the very idea that we should worry about protecting a false belief reveals an error: it shows that the illusionist has accepted the incompatibilist's framing of the debate, when the right response is to challenge that framing. Compatibilist free will is not a consolation prize for people who can't have the real thing — it is the real thing.

## Experimental Philosophy and Folk Concepts

Recent experimental philosophy has investigated what ordinary people actually believe about free will. The findings are complex: ordinary people seem to have both libertarian and compatibilist intuitions, and their responses depend heavily on how cases are presented. This complexity complicates the illusionist position. If ordinary people don't have a single, coherent folk concept of free will that is straightforwardly libertarian, it is unclear what illusion exactly is at stake — and the claim that moral life depends on a specific libertarian folk concept becomes less compelling.

If folk psychology about free will is inconsistent or context-dependent, ordinary people may be more cognitively flexible than Smilansky assumes — capable of modifying their intuitions in light of argument without the catastrophic collapse of moral life that his position predicts. Whether that cognitive flexibility is available in practice, and whether the philosophical community has an obligation to test it, is a question that illusionism raises without fully answering.
