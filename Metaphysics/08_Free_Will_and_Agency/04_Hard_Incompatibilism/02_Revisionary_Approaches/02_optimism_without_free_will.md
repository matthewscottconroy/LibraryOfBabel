# Optimism Without Free Will

Pereboom's "optimism" about life without free will makes a claim that is easy to misunderstand: abandoning belief in basic desert moral responsibility need not lead to despair, nihilism, or the unraveling of moral practice. This might sound like a consolation offered by someone making the best of a bad situation — the philosophical equivalent of saying "it's not so bad." It is, in fact, the more substantive claim that most of what makes life meaningful and morally important does not depend on free will in the libertarian sense, and that some features of moral life may actually improve once the retributive framework is shed.

## The Argument Against Pessimism

The natural objection to hard incompatibilism is that its practical consequences are catastrophic. Pereboom confronts several specific concerns.

*Concern: self-respect is undermined.* If we cannot take credit for our achievements — because they trace to factors outside our control — we cannot be properly proud of ourselves.

Pereboom's response: self-respect does not require basic desert. We can take genuine satisfaction in our achievements — in the effort invested, the character developed, the relationships built — without claiming to ultimately deserve these things in a retributive sense. A person's achievements are genuinely hers even if their ultimate origin traces to unchosen factors. They express who she is; they are the work of her values and capacities. Pride as appreciation of one's own values and achievements is compatible with hard incompatibilism. What is not compatible is only pride as a claim to deserved special credit independent of any forward-looking justification.

*Concern: interpersonal relationships are undermined.* Love, gratitude, and friendship presuppose that we genuinely choose to love, benefit, and be loyal — and hard incompatibilism appears to undermine this.

Pereboom's response: the value of love and friendship does not depend on their being undetermined choices. What matters in a relationship is the quality of the care and regard each party has for the other — not whether that care is ultimately uncaused. A friendship built on genuine affection, mutual concern, and commitment is valuable regardless of whether those affections trace to unchosen factors. The quality of the relationship is real; the genetic and developmental history of the affections does not make them less genuine.

*Concern: moral effort is pointless.* If our characters are effectively determined, why bother trying to improve?

Pereboom's response: moral effort is causally efficacious regardless of whether it is ultimately self-determined. The decision to cultivate good habits, engage in moral reflection, and seek better relationships causally contributes to the formation of better character. The causal contribution is real whether or not the decision was ultimately self-determined. Moreover, the value of moral growth — the satisfaction of becoming a better person, the improvement in relationships and wellbeing — does not depend on deserving credit for that growth.

## The Modified Reactive Attitudes

Pereboom's most philosophically careful work concerns the reactive attitudes themselves. He distinguishes two forms of each reactive attitude:

*Resentment as a claim to desert*: The wrongdoer deserves to suffer for what they did to me; the resentment is a claim that their suffering would be intrinsically appropriate. This form presupposes basic desert.

*Resentment as a registration of harm*: I was wronged; the other's action expressed insufficient regard for me; the relationship is damaged. This form registers a moral fact without claiming the other deserves to suffer.

Hard incompatibilism requires abandoning the first form but permits the second. Similarly, indignation (third-person resentment), guilt (self-directed recognition of wrongdoing without the claim to deserved self-punishment), and gratitude (warm appreciation of benefit without the claim to deserved reward) can all be retained in non-retributive forms.

The deeper question is whether these non-retributive forms are genuine reactive attitudes or philosophical reconstructions that substitute for the original. Strawson himself seemed to think the reactive attitudes are too embedded in human social life to be revised by argument. Pereboom's position is that they can be reconceived — that the retributive elements are not constitutive of what is most valuable in them, and that their revision is practically possible even if emotionally difficult.

## Consequentialist Criminal Justice

Pereboom's most practically significant recommendation is criminal justice reform. If basic desert responsibility does not exist, retributive punishment — causing suffering because wrongdoers deserve it — is unjustified. But consequentialist criminal sanctions remain fully justified:

- *Incapacitation*: protecting society from dangerous individuals.
- *Rehabilitation*: helping offenders change their behavior and values.
- *Deterrence*: discouraging future crime by demonstrating the costs.

Pereboom employs the quarantine model as an analogy: we quarantine people with dangerous infectious diseases not because they deserve to be quarantined but because quarantine protects the public. The justification is purely forward-looking. Similarly, we can incapacitate dangerous individuals not because they deserve to be imprisoned but because incapacitation protects potential victims.

A system based on these justifications would be both more humane and more effective, Pereboom argues, than our current system. Enormous suffering is imposed in the name of desert, with questionable deterrence and rehabilitation benefits. Evidence-based rehabilitation, appropriate incapacitation, and effective deterrence would constitute genuine moral progress.

## Compassion and Causal Understanding

Hard incompatibilism promotes what Pereboom calls a more compassionate response to wrongdoing. Understanding that harmful behavior results from a causal history — genetic vulnerabilities, traumatic upbringing, social deprivation, mental illness — makes therapeutic concern more natural than retributive anger. We already modulate our reactive attitudes when we learn about the causal history of behavior: a judge who understands that a young offender's criminal behavior is the product of severe abuse and inadequate mental health care may be less inclined to punitive severity. Hard incompatibilism extends this modulation consistently, rather than applying it selectively to sympathetic cases.

This does not mean treating all wrongdoers identically or abandoning assessment of severity of harm. More dangerous individuals still require stronger interventions; more severe harms still warrant stronger responses. The emotional tone shifts — therapeutic concern rather than righteous indignation — without eliminating the functional distinctions.

## Challenges to the Optimist View

The *phenomenological challenge*: Some philosophers argue that the reactive attitudes are not rationally revisable in the way Pereboom proposes. Resentment, gratitude, and indignation are not theoretical commitments that can be revised in light of philosophical argument; they are constitutive features of human emotional and social life. The attempt to purify them of retributive elements may produce something that is no longer the genuine article.

The *practical challenge*: Even if the optimist view is defensible, implementing it is difficult. Criminal justice systems, cultural norms, and ordinary moral practice are deeply shaped by retributive intuitions. Moving toward a fully consequentialist practice would require a large-scale cultural shift whose prospects and side effects are uncertain.

The *self-defeating challenge*: Promoting the hard incompatibilist conclusion widely might undermine social mechanisms that make moral life possible before adequate replacements are in place. The optimist may be right that a fully consequentialist moral practice would be better, but the transition is treacherous.

Pereboom acknowledges these challenges but maintains that the optimist view points in the right direction for moral progress. The history of moral reform — the abolition of slavery, the critique of torture, the reform of criminal justice — has often required revising deeply held intuitions in light of broader moral considerations. Hard incompatibilism may be a further stage in this ongoing development, rather than an endpoint.
