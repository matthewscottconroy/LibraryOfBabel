# Skeptical Theism and Free Will

The relationship between free will and theism is philosophically rich and structurally parallel to the secular debate in ways that are easy to underestimate. Traditional theistic accounts hold that God creates human beings with free will, enabling genuine choice and genuine moral responsibility. The existence of free will is sometimes offered as a theodicy — an explanation of why God permits evil: God respects human free will and therefore permits humans to choose evil, because genuine free will requires the possibility of moral failure. Understanding the interaction between free will and theistic commitments illuminates both debates.

## The Free Will Defense

Alvin Plantinga's free will defense (*God, Freedom, and Evil*, 1974) is the most influential theistic deployment of the free will concept. The argument:

- P1: A world containing creatures with free will who sometimes freely choose good is more valuable than a world containing no free creatures at all.
- P2: Libertarian free will requires the genuine possibility of both choosing good and choosing evil.
- P3: God cannot both create free creatures and ensure that those creatures always choose good — that would be a contradiction.
- C: God's creation of free creatures (a great good) entails the possibility of evil; God's permission of evil is therefore consistent with goodness and omnipotence, given that God desired the greater good of genuine freedom.

Plantinga's crucial claim is P3: even an omnipotent God cannot make it true that free creatures always choose good. To ensure this would be to revoke their freedom. The existence of evil is therefore not evidence against God's omnipotence or goodness — it is evidence of God's decision to create a world with genuine free agents.

Notice that the FWD requires libertarian free will. A world in which creatures are deterministically arranged to choose good is not a world of genuine free will, on the incompatibilist interpretation. Compatibilist free will would not serve the theodicy purpose, because an omnipotent God could — compatibilistically — create creatures who are deterministically disposed to choose good and who are also (compatibilistically) free. Plantinga's defense depends on the libertarian claim that genuine freedom cannot be compatibilist.

## Divine Foreknowledge and the Freedom Problem

A long-standing theological problem concerns the tension between divine foreknowledge and libertarian free will:

- P1: If God is omniscient, God knew from eternity that agent A would perform action X at time T.
- P2: If God knew this from eternity, it was always true that A would perform X at T.
- P3: If it was always true that A would perform X at T, then A could not have done otherwise.
- C: If God is omniscient, no agent ever could have done otherwise — libertarian free will is eliminated.

Several responses have been developed. *Open theism* denies P1: an omniscient God does not foreknow what free creatures will choose, because free choices are genuinely undetermined until they occur. God's omniscience extends to necessary truths and truths about determined events; genuinely free future choices are not yet truths at all. Open theists — Clark Pinnock, John Sanders, Gregory Boyd — argue that this revision of divine omniscience is required for consistency with genuine libertarian freedom.

*Middle knowledge* (Molinism) maintains divine foreknowledge through knowledge of "counterfactuals of creaturely freedom" — God's knowledge of what any possible creature would freely do in any possible circumstance. God, knowing these counterfactuals, can providentially arrange circumstances so that creatures freely choose as God desires, without God determining those choices. The creature still freely chooses; God knows in advance because God knows the relevant counterfactuals.

*The timelessness solution* (Boethius, Aquinas) holds that God is not temporal — God does not foreknow events in the sense of knowing them before they occur. God's eternal awareness of all events is simultaneous with those events from the standpoint of eternity. God's "knowing" that A will do X at T is not threatening to A's freedom for the same reason that my knowing now that you are reading this sentence does not threaten your freedom to do so. This solution has been widely influential in classical theism, though critics dispute whether atemporality is coherent and whether it genuinely dissolves the problem.

## The Manipulation Argument Applied to Divine Creation

The manipulation argument against compatibilism applies with particular force in the theistic context. If God creates human beings with specific characters, values, and dispositions — even if these are ordinary human natures rather than specially engineered personalities — this looks like a form of global creation of character:

- P1: God created human beings with the natures they have, including their cognitive and motivational dispositions.
- P2: Human actions flow from these natures.
- P3: If the creator of one's nature is morally responsible for actions flowing from that nature, then God bears ultimate moral responsibility for human wrongdoing.
- P4: This contradicts the theistic commitment to human moral responsibility.
- C: Either theism is false, or the FWD fails, or the manipulation argument against compatibilism fails.

This creates a dilemma for theists who rely on the FWD. If the FWD requires libertarian free will, and if libertarian free will faces the luck objection and agent causation problems discussed earlier, the theist must either defend libertarianism against those objections or revise the theodicy.

## Theological Determinism and Compatibilist Theism

Some theologians have accepted determinism — including divine determination of all events — while arguing that this is compatible with human freedom in the compatibilist sense. Jonathan Edwards (*Freedom of the Will*, 1754) argued that true freedom consists in doing what one pleases — and that God's determination of human choices through their own natures and desires is fully compatible with this freedom. God determines that humans freely choose, by determining the desires from which they freely act. This is straightforwardly compatibilist theology: divine determinism is compatible with human freedom and responsibility.

Calvinist theology generally holds that God determines all events — including human choices — while maintaining that human beings are genuinely responsible because those choices flow from their own desires and character. Augustine's later writings move in a parallel direction: divine grace moves the will to choose good, but this movement is not a violation of freedom — it is the restoration of genuine freedom. The will moved by grace acts freely because it acts from its own transformed nature.

## Skeptical Theism's Bearing

Skeptical theism proper — the view that we should be skeptical of our ability to judge whether God has sufficient reasons for permitting apparent evils — bears on free will debates indirectly. If we cannot be confident that God lacks sufficient reasons for permitting the apparent evils associated with human freedom (including the evil of misused freedom), then arguments from evil that invoke free will considerations are weakened. But more directly: if the FWD requires libertarian free will, and if libertarian free will faces serious philosophical objections, the theist who relies on the FWD must either defend libertarianism or provide a different theodicy. Skeptical theism provides an escape hatch — even if we cannot explain why God permits specific evils associated with human freedom, we have reason to think God may have reasons we cannot fathom — but this is compatible with the philosophical free will debate continuing on its own terms.

## The Compatibilist Revision of the Free Will Defense

Some theists have proposed revising the FWD to use compatibilist rather than libertarian free will. The revised argument: God creates human beings with reasons-responsive natures that are capable of self-governance. This compatibilist freedom is sufficient for moral responsibility. God's decision to create such beings, and to allow them to act from their own reasons, explains why evil exists — God respects human rational agency even when it goes wrong.

The challenge: if compatibilist free will is compatible with God's determining human choices through their natures, then God could have created human beings with natures reliably oriented toward good — and thus could have prevented evil while preserving compatibilist freedom. J. L. Mackie's objection reasserts itself: an omnipotent God who can determine compatibilistically free choices could have made creatures who always compatibilistically freely choose good. Plantinga's original response — that this would violate genuine freedom — requires libertarianism. The compatibilist revision of the FWD cannot answer Mackie's objection without reintroducing libertarian freedom. This dialectic shows the intimate connection between the philosophical free will debate and theology: the fate of the free will defense in philosophy of religion tracks the fate of libertarianism in philosophy of action.
