# The Libet Experiments

In the 1980s, Benjamin Libet performed a series of experiments that became the most cited — and most misunderstood — empirical data in the free will debate. The misunderstanding is instructive: the way these experiments have been interpreted and over-interpreted reveals something important about how philosophical conclusions can outrun what empirical data actually shows.

## The Experimental Setup and the Key Finding

Libet's protocol was simple. Subjects sat before a clock face whose hand rotated once every 2.56 seconds. They were asked to flex their right wrist whenever they felt the urge to do so, to note the clock position when they first became conscious of the intention to move (the "W" time), and to move whenever they felt like it without planning in advance. EEG recorded brain activity continuously, with particular attention to a signal called the readiness potential (RP) or Bereitschaftspotential — an EEG pattern associated with preparation for voluntary movement.

The striking result: the readiness potential began approximately 550 milliseconds before the wrist flexion. Subjects reported becoming conscious of their intention to move approximately 200 milliseconds before the movement. This leaves a gap of about 350 milliseconds between the onset of neural preparation and conscious awareness of the intention. The timeline:

- RP onset: approximately -550 ms
- Conscious awareness of intention (W): approximately -200 ms
- Actual movement: 0 ms

The popular interpretation drew a dramatic conclusion: by the time we consciously decide to move, the brain has already begun preparing the movement. Conscious decision is a post hoc awareness of a neural process already underway. Therefore consciousness plays no causal role in initiating action. Therefore free will is an illusion.

This inference moves far too fast.

## Libet's Own, More Careful Interpretation

Libet himself drew a more nuanced conclusion. He noted that subjects could also *veto* the movement — consciously inhibit an already-initiated movement up to 200 ms before execution. This "conscious veto" suggests some role for conscious agency, even if the initiation is unconscious. Libet distinguished between the *urge* to move (which might be the result of unconscious neural processes) and the final *decision* to act (which might involve conscious veto). The RP might measure the neural build-up toward an urge, not toward a decision; the decision might be the acceptance or rejection of the urge. Libet himself speculated that conscious agency might consist primarily in the power to veto urges — "free won't" rather than free will — which is a considerably more modest claim than the popular interpretation assumes.

## Methodological Criticisms

Several methodological criticisms substantially complicate the standard interpretation.

The clock-watching task is highly artificial. Asking a subject to note a clock position at the moment of a felt intention involves complex retrospective inference. The reported W time may reflect the moment the subject constructs a narrative about when the intention arose, not the moment of intention itself. There is good reason to think subjects confabulate — constructing an account of when they intended rather than accurately reporting a phenomenological event.

The "intention" or "urge" reported may not be a genuine decision to act but a feeling of growing tendency. The experimental task forces a discrete temporal report onto what may be a continuous process. And the experiment studies the least deliberate kind of action — simple, arbitrary, repetitive wrist flexions — with results that may not generalize at all to complex deliberative choices about significant matters.

## Philosophical Criticisms

Even setting methodology aside, the philosophical inferences are problematic.

Even if the RP precedes conscious awareness of intention by 350 ms, this does not show that consciousness plays no causal role. The conscious awareness at -200 ms might be a late-arriving component in a causal process that includes both unconscious neural preparation and conscious endorsement. Consciousness need not initiate the process to be causally relevant — it might be what allows the process to continue, execute, or be vetoed.

The inference from "consciousness follows neural preparation by 350 ms" to "consciousness plays no causal role" commits a fallacy: temporal order is not sufficient to establish causal irrelevance. If consciousness is itself a neural process, its temporal relationship with other neural processes does not settle the causal question about what constitutes the decision.

The Libet experiment cannot distinguish between two interpretations: (a) consciousness of intention is a post hoc awareness of an already-settled neural process, and (b) consciousness of intention is a constitutive part of the decision process that arrives somewhat late in a complex process involving many simultaneous neural events. The data are consistent with both.

## Subsequent Research

John-Dylan Haynes and colleagues (2008) extended the Libet finding dramatically: brain activity in prefrontal and parietal regions could predict subjects' choices (left or right hand movement) up to 10 seconds before subjects reported becoming aware of their decision. However, the predictive accuracy was approximately 60% — substantially above chance but far from certain. This leaves substantial room for the agent's conscious deliberation to play some role in the final decision. Alfred Mele and others have argued that the neural activity detected in these studies may reflect dispositions or tendencies that may or may not culminate in a decision rather than decisions proper.

## What the Experiments Establish and Do Not Establish

The Libet experiments cannot show that free will is an illusion — this requires philosophical argument about what free will requires, which is not settled by neuroscience. They cannot show that consciousness is causally irrelevant to action — this involves assumptions about causation that the timing data do not establish. And they cannot show that deliberate, complex choices are like simple, arbitrary wrist flexions — the generalization is empirically unsupported.

What they do establish: neural processes associated with voluntary action precede conscious awareness of intention to act by a significant interval; the initiation of some voluntary movements is substantially unconscious; the relationship between conscious experience and neural causation is more complex than naive folk psychology assumes.

For the philosophical positions arrayed in this debate, the Libet experiments function differently. Compatibilists can note that reasons-responsive deliberation may be largely unconscious without this undermining freedom — what matters for freedom is not that consciousness initiates every neural process but that the overall mechanism is reasons-responsive and the agent's own. Hard incompatibilists see the Libet data as supporting their view that "conscious free will" is at best a late-arriving awareness of already-underway causal processes. Libertarians must explain how conscious awareness can play the role in free action that libertarianism requires, given that it arrives late in the process — and Libet's own veto suggestion is the most promising avenue. The data constrain the debate without settling it.
