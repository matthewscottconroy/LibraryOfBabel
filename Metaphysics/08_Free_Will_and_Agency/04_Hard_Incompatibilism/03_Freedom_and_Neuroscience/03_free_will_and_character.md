# Free Will and Character

There is a way of approaching the free will problem that is, in some respects, more Aristotelian than Kantian: instead of focusing on whether any particular action was freely chosen at the moment of choice, ask whether the agent has the kind of character that makes action an expression of genuine self-governance. On this character-based approach, freedom is primarily a property of persons — of agents with stable, well-integrated, reasons-responsive characters — rather than a property of individual actions. A single action can be evaluated for its freedom only against the background of the character from which it flows.

## Aristotle's Character-Based Account

Aristotle's ethics provides the classical framework. In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, virtues and vices are stable character traits (*hexeis*) that dispose a person to act well or badly in characteristic ways. The practically wise person (*phronimos*) acts well not by consulting rules or calculating consequences at each moment but by having the right dispositions — perceiving situations correctly, feeling appropriate emotions, and responding naturally in ways that express virtue. The freedom involved in virtuous action is not the freedom of a fresh undetermined choice; it is the freedom of self-governance, of acting from a well-integrated character that is genuinely one's own.

Crucially, Aristotle holds that we are responsible for our characters, not merely for individual actions. Character is formed through practice: we become courageous by doing courageous acts, just as we become carpenters by doing carpentry. This means character formation involves our own choices and actions, even if those choices are themselves partly determined by prior character. The agent who develops a bad character through repeated bad choices is responsible for that character — even if, at the current stage, she can no longer easily act against it. The responsibility is retrospective, extending back through the history of character formation.

## Watson's Double-Aspect Theory

Gary Watson's "Two Faces of Responsibility" (*Agency and Answerability*, 2004) develops a character-based account that distinguishes two dimensions of moral assessment:

- *Aretaic evaluation*: evaluating agents in terms of virtues and vices — excellence and deficiency of character. This is the Aristotelian dimension: we assess the person as having good or poor character.
- *Deontic evaluation*: evaluating actions in terms of rights and duties — whether the action was right or wrong.

Watson argues that moral responsibility involves both dimensions. The blameworthy agent is not just one who performed a wrong action but one whose action expresses poor character — whose action is an expression of who she is, at the level of values. Conversely, the praiseworthy agent is not just one who performed a right action but one whose action expresses good character.

This double-aspect theory has implications for the free will debate. The aretaic dimension of responsibility is grounded not in alternate possibilities but in the attribution of action to the agent's character. Actions are the agent's own when they express who she is — her stable values and dispositions. This is source compatibilism: what matters for responsibility is the right kind of character-source, compatible with determinism.

## The Neural Basis of Character

Contemporary neuroscience has illuminated the neural basis of character in ways that raise new questions about responsibility. The prefrontal cortex — especially the ventromedial and dorsolateral regions — is involved in planning, self-control, moral judgment, and the suppression of impulsive behavior. Damage to the prefrontal cortex can produce profound character changes: impulsivity, poor social judgment, disinhibition. The case of Phineas Gage, whose personality changed dramatically after an iron rod passed through his prefrontal cortex, established this vividly. Antonio Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis uses the Gage case to argue that emotional processing in the prefrontal cortex is essential for practical reasoning and decision-making.

The amygdala is involved in emotional learning, particularly fear conditioning and the recognition of emotional significance in situations. Damage to the amygdala is associated with deficits in learning from punishment and in recognizing the emotional significance of one's actions. Psychopaths show reduced amygdala activity in response to emotional stimuli — a finding that may help explain their characteristic callousness and failure to learn from the aversive consequences of harmful behavior.

Dopaminergic systems, involved in reward processing and reinforcement learning, are associated with addiction and compulsive behavior when dysregulated. These conditions impair the character traits of self-control and moderation in ways that affect the exercise of agency.

## Character, Neuroscience, and Responsibility

The neuroscientific picture raises the question: if character is neurally implemented, and neurological conditions can alter character without involving the agent's deliberate choices, are agents responsible for the characters these conditions produce?

We can identify several response strategies. The *constitutional responsibility response* holds that even if character is neurally implemented and neurologically influenced, agents can still be responsible for how they respond to their characters. An agent with a short temper due to neurological predisposition can work to develop emotional regulation skills; the failure to do so may be blameworthy even if the predisposition itself is not.

The *tracing response* (Aristotelian in spirit) holds that if a character trait emerged from past choices, responsibility for the current character can be traced back to those choices, even if the current character makes change difficult. An agent who developed impulsivity through habitual self-indulgence is responsible for that impulsivity through the earlier choices that cultivated it.

The *attributability response* holds that even if neurological conditions shape character without the agent's deliberate choice, the resulting character is still the agent's own — it is the stable pattern through which she engages with the world. Actions that express this character are attributable to the agent in the sense required for aretaic evaluation, even if the agent's responsibility for the character's formation is limited. We can assess the person without necessarily claiming she deserves retributive punishment.

The *capacity-based response* focuses on what the agent could do at the time of action: what matters for responsibility is whether the agent had the capacity to recognize and respond to reasons at that moment. Neurological conditions that impair this capacity diminish responsibility; conditions that incline the agent toward certain responses without eliminating the capacity to recognize and respond to reasons do not.

## The Virtue Ethics Approach

A virtue-ethics approach to free will does not require that each action be "freely chosen" in the libertarian sense. What matters is the cultivation of character through practice, and this cultivation — while causally determined — is still meaningfully attributable to the agent in ways that ground responsibility.

The virtuous person acts well effortlessly and reliably — not because each action is a fresh deliberated choice but because her character is such that acting well is natural to her. The courageous person does not deliberate anxiously about whether to act bravely in the face of danger; she perceives the situation clearly and acts appropriately. The freedom involved here is not freedom from determination but the freedom of self-governance — acting from one's own well-integrated character. This is compatible with compatibilism: the agent's actions are determined by her character, but because the character is genuinely hers — developed through her own engagement with the world, her own choices over time, her own reflective endorsement — the determined actions are her own.

## Wolf's Normative Dimension

Susan Wolf argues that character-based responsibility requires not just that actions spring from the agent's character but that the agent's character is oriented toward what is genuinely good. The virtue ethics tradition assumes that the truly virtuous person perceives and responds to genuine values — not merely to what she believes is valuable. This normative dimension is missing from purely psychological accounts of character.

Wolf's Reason View holds that responsible action requires the ability to recognize and act on what is genuinely good — not merely what one believes is good. This condition connects character-based responsibility to normative realism: the character that grounds responsible action must be not just integrated and stable but also oriented toward genuine values. Hard incompatibilism can concede that character formed by appropriate causal processes constitutes a kind of freedom — the freedom of self-governance — while maintaining that such character ultimately traces to unchosen factors, so ultimate desert responsibility remains unavailable. The development of a well-integrated, reasons-responsive character oriented toward the good is, on the virtue ethics picture, the primary task of moral psychology, regardless of how the metaphysical debate about ultimate responsibility is resolved.
