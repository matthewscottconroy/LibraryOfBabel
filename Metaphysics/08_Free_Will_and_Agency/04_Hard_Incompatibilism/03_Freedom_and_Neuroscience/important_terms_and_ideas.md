# Important Terms and Ideas: Freedom and Neuroscience

**Readiness Potential (Bereitschaftspotential)**
A brain electrical signal detected by EEG that begins ~550ms before a voluntary movement. Libet found that subjects' reported awareness of their intention to move came ~350ms after the readiness potential began, suggesting unconscious brain processes initiate movement before conscious willing.

**The Libet Experiment**
Libet's 1983 experiment: subjects watched a clock and reported the position of the hand when they first became aware of the intention to move their wrist. The readiness potential preceded this reported awareness by ~350ms. This was interpreted as suggesting that conscious will does not initiate action.

**W-Judgment**
Libet's term for subjects' reports of when they first became consciously aware of their wish (W) to move. The gap between the readiness potential (RP) and the W-judgment is the empirical core of Libet's challenge to conscious agency.

**Veto Power**
Libet's own compatibilist suggestion: even if unconscious processes initiate movement, the conscious mind may exercise "veto power"—the ability to consciously inhibit the motion once the intention arises. This preserves a role for consciousness in free action.

**Epiphenomenal Consciousness**
The view that conscious experience and conscious intention are causally inert—they accompany but do not cause behavior. Wegner's interpretation of Libet's data suggests that conscious willing is such an epiphenomenon: a post-hoc rationalization rather than a genuine cause.

**Neuroscientific Skepticism about Free Will**
The broader claim that neuroscience shows free will is an illusion: brain processes cause behavior, consciousness is a late-arriving spectator, and the sense of being the author of one's actions is a confabulation. Philosophers like Mele argue this conclusion is not supported by the evidence as interpreted.
