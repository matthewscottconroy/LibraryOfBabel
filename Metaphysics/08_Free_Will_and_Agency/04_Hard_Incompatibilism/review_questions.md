# Review Questions — Hard Incompatibilism

1. Galen Strawson's basic argument does not depend on determinism: it turns instead on a regress from action to character to the impossibility of self-originating one's own character. Fischer and Ravizza respond that responsibility for character does not require voluntarist self-origination — only that the character be genuinely one's own through an appropriate history. Does their historical condition successfully block the regress, or does Pereboom's four-case argument show that no causal history can provide what the basic argument demands?

2. Pereboom distinguishes *basic desert* moral responsibility from attributability and forward-looking accountability, and argues that only the first is threatened by hard incompatibilism. Is this distinction philosophically clean? Could someone coherently maintain that an agent's action is genuinely attributable to her — as an expression of her will and character — while denying that she deserves to suffer for it?

3. The Libet experiments are frequently cited as empirical evidence against free will. Assess the inferential steps from readiness potential data to the conclusion that conscious will is an illusion. What does the chapter identify as the key philosophical and methodological errors in that inference, and what, if anything, do the experiments establish that genuinely bears on the philosophical debate?

4. Smilansky's illusionism and Pereboom's optimism represent two very different stances toward the hard incompatibilist conclusion. Smilansky thinks widespread acceptance of skepticism about basic desert would damage moral life, while Pereboom thinks the damage is overstated and some features of moral life may actually improve. Which assessment is more plausible, and what empirical or philosophical assumptions does each depend on?

5. Pereboom proposes a quarantine model for criminal justice: we incapacitate dangerous individuals for the same reason we quarantine those with dangerous diseases — forward-looking protection, not desert. What are the strongest objections to this model, and does it account for the communicative and expressive dimensions of punishment that retributivists typically emphasize?

6. The chapter on neuroscience and responsibility draws a distinction between neural explanation and excuse, and identifies three conditions — epistemic, capacity, and self-control — that neural abnormalities must impair to reduce responsibility. Evaluate this framework: does it successfully integrate neuroscientific findings with a philosophical account of responsibility, or does it concede too much to the hard incompatibilist by showing that all three conditions have neural bases that can be disrupted by factors the agent did not choose?

7. The free will defense in philosophy of religion — Plantinga's argument that God cannot create free creatures guaranteed to choose good — requires libertarian free will. If the libertarian account fails, as hard incompatibilists argue, what follows for the free will theodicy? Does the theist have resources to reconstruct the defense using compatibilist freedom, or does the collapse of libertarianism undermine the theodicy entirely?

8. Taken as a whole, the module traces a path from determinism through compatibilism and libertarianism to hard incompatibilism, with each position responding to problems generated by its predecessor. Does this trajectory suggest that the hard incompatibilist conclusion is the stable endpoint of the debate — the position all others collapse into when pressed hard enough — or is there a position along the way that can withstand the cumulative force of the arguments? Where would you locate the strongest defensible position, and what is the best argument against it?
