# Domain Survey: Metametaphysics

## Overview

Metametaphysics is the philosophical study of metaphysics itself — an inquiry into the methods, presuppositions, and epistemic status of metaphysical theorizing. While first-order metaphysics asks what exists, what properties are, and how the past relates to the present, metametaphysics asks whether these questions are genuine, what it would mean to answer them, and how we could ever know that an answer is correct. The field occupies a position analogous to metaethics in ethics: just as metaethics asks whether moral claims are objectively true and what moral reasoning consists in, metametaphysics asks whether ontological claims are objectively true and what ontological reasoning consists in.

The central debate in contemporary metametaphysics is between **deflationism** (or "easy ontology") and **heavyweight realism**. Deflationists, following Carnap's distinction between internal and external questions, hold that ontological disputes are not about genuine matters of fact — they are disputes about which linguistic or conceptual framework to adopt, and framework choice is not subject to truth or falsity but only to pragmatic evaluation. When philosophers debate whether numbers exist, they are not arguing about how the world independently is, but about whether to adopt the "number framework." The question asked inside the framework ("is 7 prime?") has a trivially affirmative answer; the question asked outside all frameworks ("do numbers *really* exist?") is not a factual question at all. Carnap, Hirsch, and Thomasson have developed this deflationary tradition.

Heavyweight realists — Lewis, Sider, Schaffer, Fine — reject deflationism. They hold that ontological questions are genuine, that the world has a determinate structure independent of our conceptual schemes, and that the task of metaphysics is to describe that structure accurately. For Lewis, the fundamental ontology includes concrete possible worlds, sets, and perfectly natural properties — this is not a matter of conventional choice but of what the world is actually like. For Sider, the world has genuine "joints" — perfectly natural properties and relations — and the task of ontology is to carve reality at these joints. For Schaffer, the central ontological question is not what exists but what is fundamental — which entities are prior in the grounding hierarchy.

A third force in the metametaphysical debate is the **neo-Aristotelian** revival, centered on the notions of **grounding** and **fundamentality**. Where Quine's flat ontology recognizes only existence (being is univocal), the neo-Aristotelian view holds that reality is stratified: some entities are fundamental (not grounded in anything else), and others exist derivatively (in virtue of other things). This stratification is a real feature of the world, not a feature of our conceptual scheme. Fine, Schaffer, and Rosen have developed this framework; it raises its own metametaphysical questions about the logic and epistemology of grounding.

## Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

### The Deflation-Realism Axis

**Carnapian deflationism** ("Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology," 1950): Ontological questions are either:
- *Internal*: asked within a linguistic framework; answered by the methods of that framework (mathematics, science). "Are there numbers?" asked in arithmetic → trivially yes.
- *External*: asked about whether to adopt a framework; this is a pragmatic question about utility, not a factual question.

There are no "deep" external ontological questions with objective answers. Disputes that seem to be about the ultimate nature of reality are either internal questions (trivially answerable) or confused pseudo-questions.

**Hirsch's quantifier variantism** (2002, 2011): The English quantifier "there is" admits of different "meanings" corresponding to different conceptual schemes. When a mereological universalist says "there is a sum of the Eiffel Tower and my left shoe" and a non-universalist says "there is no such thing," they are not contradicting each other — they are using "there is" with different meanings. Ontological disputes are verbal disputes about how to use quantifiers.

- *Sider's objection*: The natural language quantifier "there is" has a determinate meaning — the one that perfectly carves reality at its joints. There is a fact of the matter about whether the Eiffel-Tower-plus-shoe entity exists; the universalist is right because the most joint-carving ontological description includes unrestricted mereological composition.

**Thomasson's easy ontology** (2015): Ontological questions about ordinary objects (tables, persons, numbers) are answerable easily by attention to conceptual and semantic rules together with uncontested empirical facts. We know the rules for applying "table"; we know there is some wood arranged table-wise; so there is a table. No deep metaphysical investigation is needed. Metaphysical questions that appear to go beyond this are ill-formed.

**Sider's heavyweight realism** (*Writing the Book of the World*, 2011): Reality has genuine, mind-independent structure. Some predicates (the fundamental ones, like the predicates of fundamental physics) "carve reality at its joints" — they pick out genuinely natural properties and relations. Ontological disputes are to be settled by determining which description of reality is most natural — most joint-carving. Metaphysics aims at a "fundamental language" that describes the world in perfectly natural terms.

**Schaffer's priority monism** (2010): The cosmos (the one maximally inclusive whole) is the single fundamental entity; all particulars exist derivatively, as proper parts of the cosmos. What grounds the parts is the whole, not vice versa. This is "ontological priority monism" — not the elimination of parts but the claim that the whole is prior.

**Fine's real metaphysics** (2009): The central question of metaphysics is not "what exists?" (the Quinean question) but "what is real?" in the sense of being part of the fundamental layer of reality. Some entities exist but are not real in this sense — they exist derivatively, in virtue of other things. The task of metaphysics is to determine which entities are genuinely real and how the real determines the rest.

### Grounding

**Grounding** is a purported relation (or family of relations) that holds between facts, propositions, or entities when one "obtains in virtue of" another. The truth of "this rose is red" grounds "this rose is colored"; the existence of water molecules grounds the existence of water; the physical facts ground the mental facts (physicalism); the relational facts ground the intrinsic facts (if relational). Grounding is:
- **Non-causal** (distinct from causation — it is synchronic and conceptual/metaphysical)
- **Explanatory** (it is a species of "in virtue of" explanation)
- **Irreflexive** (nothing grounds itself)
- **Asymmetric** (if A grounds B, B does not ground A)
- **Transitive** (if A grounds B, and B grounds C, then A grounds C)

**Objections to grounding**:
- *Wilson's objection* (2014): Grounding is not a single determinate relation; what philosophers call "grounding" is actually a family of more specific relations (realization, identity, logical entailment, supervenience). Using the single notion "grounding" masks important differences.
- *Sider's parsimony objection*: Positing a primitive grounding relation in addition to the entities is ontologically and ideologically costly.
- *Circular grounding*: some apparent grounding claims seem circular (the form of a thing grounds the matter, but the matter is needed for there to be a form). Bradley's regress threatens to iterate.

**Ontological dependence**: Closely related to grounding — an entity *x* ontologically depends on entity *y* if *x* could not exist without *y*. Rigid vs. non-rigid dependence; generic vs. specific dependence (Fine's taxonomy).

### Methodology in Metaphysics

**Philosophical intuitions**: Much metaphysical argument proceeds by eliciting intuitions (thought experiment responses) and then arguing from them. How much evidential weight do intuitions carry? They may be culturally variable, subject to framing effects, or generated by cognitive processes unresponsive to metaphysical truth.

**Inference to the best explanation**: Ontological hypotheses (there are numbers; there are universals) are justified insofar as they figure in the best explanations of a range of facts. This is a methodological analogue of scientific IBE applied to metaphysics.

**Naturalizing metaphysics** (Ladyman, Ross): Metaphysical claims must be compatible with and constrained by natural science. Speculative metaphysics that outstrips physics is idle. The only ontology is the one implied by our best physical theories. *Objection*: this makes metaphysics parasitic on science and unable to address questions (about consciousness, moral properties, modal metaphysics) that science does not address.

**A priori vs. a posteriori metaphysics**: Some metaphysical truths are known a priori (logical truths, conceptual truths); others are discovered empirically (the necessity of "water is H₂O" is known a posteriori). The boundaries between a priori and a posteriori metaphysics are contested.

## Key Arguments and Counterarguments

### Carnap's Internal/External Distinction

**Carnap**: "Do numbers exist?" has two readings:
- (Internal) "Within arithmetic, are there numbers?" → Yes, trivially.
- (External) "Independent of all frameworks, do numbers exist?" → This is not a factual question; it's a pragmatic question about framework adoption.

**Quine's response**: There is no principled internal/external distinction. The choice of a logical framework is itself a theoretical posit subject to revision in light of evidence. The pragmatic considerations Carnap uses to evaluate frameworks (simplicity, fruitfulness) are the same ones we use to evaluate empirical theories. There is no privileged metaframework from which framework choices are made.

**Hirsch's response to Sider**: Even if Sider is right that there is a most natural quantifier, ordinary ontological disputes are not about that quantifier — they are conducted in natural language, which admits of multiple reasonable interpretations. The universalist's natural-language claim is not more natural than the non-universalist's.

### The Grounding Regress

If every non-fundamental fact is grounded in other facts, and those grounding facts themselves require grounding, is there a well-founded foundation? 

**Foundationalism**: There are fundamental, ungrounded facts; derivative facts are grounded ultimately in the fundamental ones. (Most neo-Aristotelians assume this.)

**Infinitism about grounding**: The grounding chain descends infinitely without terminus. *Problem*: if there is no ground floor, is anything explained by appealing to grounds?

**Coherentism about grounding**: Facts can be mutually grounding, with no ungrounded foundation. *Problem*: this seems to allow circular explanations.

Fine's constraint: grounding must be well-founded (no infinite descending chains) for it to play the explanatory role intended.

### Ontological Realism and Joint-Carving

**Sider's argument for realism about structure**:

**P1.** Some descriptions of the world are more natural than others (compare "all electrons have negative charge" vs. "all grue things are grue").
**P2.** Naturalness is a mind-independent feature of descriptions — it is not we who make "electron" more natural than "grue."
**P3.** The most natural descriptions of reality correspond to its fundamental structure — its "joints."
**C.** Reality has a mind-independent joint-carving structure, and the task of fundamental metaphysics is to describe it.

**Deflationist response**: "More natural" may just mean "more useful for our purposes" — it need not be mind-independent naturalness but only pragmatic superiority.

## Key Papers and Books

1. **Carnap, Rudolf, "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950)** — The founding document of deflationism; the internal/external distinction.

2. **Quine, W. V. O., "On What There Is" (1948)** — The opposing founding document; ontological commitment as unavoidable; collapse of the internal/external distinction.

3. **Sider, Theodore, *Writing the Book of the World* (2011)** — The most developed contemporary defense of heavyweight realism via the notion of structure and joint-carving.

4. **Fine, Kit, "The Question of Ontology" (2009)** — Argues for a real/merely existent distinction; defends a neo-Aristotelian approach to ontology.

5. **Schaffer, Jonathan, "On What Grounds What" (2009)** — Systematic development of priority monism and the grounding framework.

6. **Hirsch, Eli, *Quantifier Variance and Realism* (2011)** — The most developed defense of quantifier variantism; papers collected from 1982–2010.

7. **Thomasson, Amie, *Ontology Made Easy* (2015)** — The most thorough defense of easy ontology; argues that most ontological questions are answerable by attending to conceptual rules.

8. **Bennett, Karen, *Making Things Up* (2017)** — Systematic treatment of grounding and building relations; develops the concept of building relations (composition, realization, constitution, grounding) in a unified framework.

## Live Debates and Open Questions

1. **The logic of grounding**: Is grounding irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive? Counterexamples to each have been proposed. The logic of grounding is still being worked out; different formal systems (Fine's system, Correia's system) give different verdicts.

2. **The ontology of grounding**: Is grounding a relation between facts, propositions, or entities? Is it a single relation or a family of relations? Wilson argues for the latter; most neo-Aristotelians assume the former.

3. **Metaphysical necessity and grounding**: Is the grounding relation itself metaphysically necessary — if A grounds B, must A always ground B? Or can the same ground fail to ground in other worlds? (Fine's "weak necessity" of grounding vs. fully contingent grounding.)

4. **Feminist and social critiques of methodology**: Haslanger, Appiah, and others have argued that the canonical method of appealing to "intuitions" in thought experiments is not theory-neutral; intuitions encode culturally specific assumptions. Does this undermine the evidential value of thought-experiment methodology?

5. **Mathematics and metaontology**: Whether mathematical entities exist (Platonism vs. nominalism) is both a first-order ontological question and a test case for metaontological positions. How the debate about mathematics is resolved affects the plausibility of deflationism, quantifier variantism, and heavyweight realism.

## Connections to Other Domains

**Metametaphysics and Ontology (Domain 01)**: The methodological debates in metametaphysics are immediately relevant to first-order ontology. Whether the Quinean criterion or Fine's "what is real" question is the right starting point for ontology is itself a metaontological question. The deflationism debate applies directly to disputes about the existence of universals, numbers, and possible worlds.

**Metametaphysics and Truth/Reality (Domain 12)**: The metametaphysical debate about realism directly maps onto the debate between metaphysical realism and anti-realism about truth. Deflationism about ontology is structurally similar to deflationism about truth; heavyweight realism in ontology requires something like correspondence truth.

**Metametaphysics and Metaphysics of Science (Domain 10)**: The "naturalized metaphysics" program (Ladyman, Ross) is a metametaphysical position that constrains ontology by science. This directly intersects with the structural realism debate in philosophy of science.

**Metametaphysics and Philosophy of Language**: The Carnap-Quine debate is simultaneously a debate in philosophy of language about the nature of analytic-synthetic distinction and the status of theoretical terms. Sider's "joint-carving" connects metaphysics to philosophy of language via reference magnetism.

**Metametaphysics and Free Will (Domain 08)**: Compatibilists sometimes employ a deflationary metametaphysical strategy: the free will debate, on Hirsch-style analysis, is verbal — the compatibilist and incompatibilist use "free will" differently. This metaontological move is contested but influential.
