# Metametaphysics

There is something unusual about the inquiry you are engaged in. When physicists disagree about the correct interpretation of quantum mechanics, they can in principle settle their dispute by appeal to experiment, prediction, and theory choice guided by empirical adequacy. When historians disagree about the causes of the First World War, they can in principle settle their dispute by consulting archives, assessing evidence, and tracing causal chains through documented events. But when metaphysicians disagree about whether there exist numbers, whether properties are universals or tropes, whether the present moment is ontologically privileged — what method settles these disputes? What counts as evidence? And is there really a fact of the matter, or are metaphysicians engaged in something more like negotiating a useful conceptual scheme?

These questions constitute metametaphysics: not metaphysics itself, but inquiry *into* metaphysics — its nature, methods, and limits. Metametaphysics asks whether metaphysical questions are genuine and substantive, or whether they are merely verbal disputes about how to deploy words like "exists" and "is real." It asks what tools are appropriate for metaphysical investigation, given that the subject matter is too general for any particular empirical science to adjudicate. And it develops positive frameworks — grounding, fundamentality, essence — that promise to give metaphysics not just questions to ask but resources for answering them in a principled way.

The challenge is real and has a distinguished history. Rudolf Carnap, in a famous 1950 paper, argued that external questions about whether numbers or universals *really* exist — questions asked from outside any particular conceptual framework — are pseudo-questions, neither true nor false but merely the expression of a preference for one linguistic framework over another. Questions that have determinate answers, on Carnap's view, are always internal to a framework: "Are there prime numbers greater than 1000?" has a determinate answer relative to the framework of arithmetic. "Are there numbers?" asked from the outside is an invitation to adopt or reject the framework, not a genuine question about what exists.

Contemporary metametaphysics has largely rejected Carnap's dismissal, but not by ignoring the force of his challenge. The first chapter of this unit examines **what metaphysics is** — its characteristic questions and methods, and the range of views about whether those questions are substantive. The deflationary tradition, descended from Carnap and developed by neo-pragmatists, continues to press the charge that metaphysical disputes are at bottom disputes about language. The inflationary tradition insists that some disputes are genuinely substantive and that we have methods for making progress on them.

**Ontological commitment** asks what it means to say that something exists, and what our theories commit us to. The Quinean framework — to exist is to be in the range of a bound variable of our best theory — has been enormously influential, but has also been subjected to powerful challenges. Quantifier variance holds that there is no single, privileged sense of "exists" and that different communities speaking different languages may both be correct when they give apparently conflicting answers to questions about what there is. Neo-Fregean approaches offer a different picture, grounding ontological claims in abstraction principles that can be known a priori.

**Grounding and fundamentality** provides positive tools for a revitalized metaphysics. Even if we are deflationists about some ontological questions, there remain questions about the priority structure of reality — what depends on what, what is more fundamental than what, what would remain if we subtracted everything non-fundamental. Grounding is a relation of non-causal, metaphysical dependence: consciousness may be grounded in brain states, the existence of a heap of sand may be grounded in the existence of its grains, the truth of a mathematical theorem may be grounded in mathematical facts. Understanding grounding illuminates what we mean when we say that one thing is "nothing but" another.

**Essence and real definition** completes the unit by examining the metaphysical notion of essence — what something is in its very nature, what it must be to be that thing at all. Kit Fine's work has shown that essence cannot be identified with necessity: some necessary truths about a thing are not grounded in its essence. This distinction opens new possibilities for metaphysics and reconnects contemporary analysis with an ancient Aristotelian tradition.

Metametaphysics is, in an important sense, the most self-conscious part of philosophy: it turns the tools of inquiry back on inquiry itself.
