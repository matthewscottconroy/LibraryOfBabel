# What Is Metaphysics?

Before practicing metaphysics, it is worth pausing to ask what we are doing when we do it. Physics investigates the fundamental particles and forces; biology investigates life; mathematics investigates abstract structures. What does metaphysics investigate, and what methods does it use? These questions are not merely preliminary — they are themselves philosophical questions that shape everything that follows.

The traditional answer locates metaphysics as the study of being as such — the most general features of reality, the categories that apply to everything that exists, the principles that hold across all domains. This is the Aristotelian conception: metaphysics is "first philosophy," the inquiry into the structure of being before any particular domain is restricted. On this view, metaphysics is not a generalization of the sciences but a different kind of inquiry altogether — one that investigates the presuppositions of inquiry rather than the domain of any particular inquiry.

This conception has been challenged from multiple directions. Logical positivists argued that metaphysical statements are literally meaningless — they are neither empirically verifiable nor analytically true, and therefore they say nothing at all. This dismissal is now widely regarded as too quick (the positivists' own criterion of meaningfulness is not itself empirically verifiable), but it raised a genuine challenge: what makes a metaphysical question substantive rather than merely verbal? If two philosophers differ about whether mereological sums exist, are they disagreeing about the world or about how to use the word "exists"?

Carnap's more sophisticated version of the challenge distinguishes internal questions (asked within a conceptual framework) from external questions (asked from outside any framework). Internal questions have determinate answers; external questions are merely practical proposals about which framework to adopt. On Carnap's view, canonical metaphysical questions are external — they are not genuine factual questions but disguised proposals for conceptual revision.

This chapter examines what metaphysics is and what makes it possible, taking seriously both the traditional conception and the challenges to it. The reader who has worked through it will approach subsequent chapters with a clearer sense of what is at stake and what kind of inquiry is underway.
