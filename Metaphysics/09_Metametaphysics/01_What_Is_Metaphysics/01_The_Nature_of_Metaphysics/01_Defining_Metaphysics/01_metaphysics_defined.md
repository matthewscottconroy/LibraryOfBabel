# Metaphysics Defined

Here is a puzzle we can feel before we try to explain it. Physics tells us about electrons and spacetime. Biology tells us about organisms and evolution. Chemistry tells us about substances and reactions. Each of these disciplines is about something specific. But now ask: what is it for anything at all to exist? What makes a thing a substance rather than a property? What explains the difference between what must be true and what merely happens to be true? These questions are not the questions of any particular science, because they apply equally to whatever any science might discover. The discipline that addresses them — with whatever rigor we can bring to bear — is metaphysics.

The name is historical accident. Andronicus of Rhodes, arranging Aristotle's works in the first century BCE, placed certain texts after (*meta*) the physics (*ta physika*), and the label stuck. Whether he meant to indicate subject matter (inquiry into what lies beyond the physical) or merely position in a reading order is still debated. What matters is that the label came to designate inquiry into being as such — into the most general and fundamental features of reality rather than the features of any particular domain.

We can put the discipline's scope more precisely. Let S be the set of all possible inquiry-domains. Metaphysics is then characterized by three claims:

- P1: For any domain D in S, there are questions about the ontological presuppositions, modal structure, and categorical commitments of D that D itself does not settle by its own methods.
- P2: The discipline that addresses such presuppositional questions with appropriate generality and rigor is metaphysics.
- C: Metaphysics is the framework discipline presupposed by all other inquiry-domains, and hence indispensable to the full interpretation of any of them.

This is not to say that metaphysics is epistemically prior to science — we do not first settle all metaphysical questions and then do science. The relationship is one of mutual constraint. Scientific results set limits on what metaphysical theories can say; metaphysical frameworks determine how scientific results should be interpreted. Whether the quantum state is a feature of individual systems or of the universe as a whole is a question that physics raises but does not by itself answer.

## Levels of Inquiry and the Shape of This Module

It is useful to distinguish three levels at which inquiry operates. *First-order* metaphysical questions ask what exists and what its features are: do properties exist as universals? Are there abstract objects? Do composite objects exist over and above their parts? *Metametaphysical* questions ask about the status, methodology, and legitimacy of first-order inquiry: are these questions substantive, and if so how can they be answered? *Framework-level* questions ask which conceptual apparatus is most apt for the enterprise: should grounding, fundamentality, essence, or ontological commitment be the central concepts?

Traditional divisions of the subject track the first level. *Ontology* — the study of being *qua* being — asks what kinds of things there are and what it means to exist. *Cosmology* in the philosophical sense asks about the overall structure and origin of the universe. *Philosophical theology* asks about God. *Mereology* asks about parts and wholes. *Modal metaphysics* asks about necessity, possibility, and essence. Contemporary work has tended to narrow its focus while deepening its tools, replacing comprehensive systems with careful treatment of specific, precisely formulated problems.

This module operates primarily at the metametaphysical level. When later modules take up time, causation, universals, personal identity, or mathematical objects, they are engaged in first-order inquiry. Here we are asking what kind of enterprise that is — whether its questions have determinate answers, what methods are appropriate to them, and how competing theories should be evaluated.

## How to Demarcate the Subject

Three proposals for distinguishing genuinely metaphysical questions from others have been influential, and each has real merit.

The *generality criterion* holds that metaphysical questions are those that apply across every domain of inquiry: "Are there numbers?" matters to every discipline that uses mathematics, while "Is there dark matter?" concerns a specific empirical domain. The difficulty is that the boundary between general and domain-specific is gradual rather than sharp.

The *a priori criterion* holds that metaphysical questions are those that empirical investigation alone cannot settle. Whether abstract objects exist, what causation consists in, whether personal identity requires psychological continuity — none of these seems answerable by experiment. The objection from naturalists like Ladyman and Ross is that this criterion insulates metaphysics from scientific accountability in ways that threaten its credibility.

The *grounding criterion*, developed by Fine (2009) and Sider (2011), holds that metaphysical questions concern the ground-level structure of reality — what is fundamental and how non-fundamental features are grounded in fundamental ones. This makes metaphysics the study of reality's hierarchical architecture, which is not directly visible to any particular science. Of the three proposals, this is the one that does most work in contemporary discussions, and it will frame much of what follows in this module.

Notice that the concept of grounding appears in both the subject matter and the methodology of metaphysics. First-order metaphysics identifies the grounding structure of specific domains. Metametaphysics asks what grounding itself is and whether the hierarchical picture of reality it presupposes is coherent. The two inquiries are not separate but deeply intertwined.
