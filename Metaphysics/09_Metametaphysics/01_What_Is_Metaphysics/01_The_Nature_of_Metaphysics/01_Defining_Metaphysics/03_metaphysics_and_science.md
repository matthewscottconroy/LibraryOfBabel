# Metaphysics and Science

Consider what happens when physicists debate the ontological status of the wave function. Is it a real physical field pervading space, or a bookkeeping device for predicting measurement outcomes, or something stranger still? No experiment can settle this directly — the competing interpretations of quantum mechanics are observationally equivalent. Yet the question is not merely verbal, and it is not a question physics can answer by its own methods, because the methods of physics presuppose some answer. This is a genuinely metaphysical question that arises within science and cannot be dissolved by appeal to empirical data alone. It illustrates a general pattern: metaphysics and science are neither the same discipline nor wholly separate ones. They stand in a relation of mutual constraint that is the topic of this section.

## The Continuity Thesis

Quine and Armstrong both defend a *continuity thesis*: metaphysics and science differ in degree of generality, not in kind. Science identifies the categories and laws governing specific domains; metaphysics identifies the most general categories applying to all domains. The argument runs:

- P1: Science aims to describe the fundamental structure of reality within a domain.
- P2: Metaphysics asks which features are common to all domains, and how they relate.
- P3: Questions at any level require both conceptual analysis (to understand what is being asked) and empirical input (to determine which answers are correct).
- C: The methods of metaphysics and science are not categorically different; they form a continuous spectrum from the most general to the most domain-specific.

The continuity thesis does not imply that metaphysics reduces to science. Metaphysics retains a kind of primacy over methodology: deciding which scientific entities to take seriously, and how to interpret the ontological commitments of theories, requires philosophical reasoning that science cannot perform on itself. An experiment cannot tell you whether its results commit you to properties as universals or merely to resemblances among particular objects; that question requires philosophical argument.

The opposing autonomy thesis, developed by Fine (2012) and Sider (2011), holds that metaphysics addresses questions about the *ground-level structure* of reality — which features are fundamental, and what grounds what — that no purely scientific investigation can settle, because those questions concern the framework presupposed by any scientific investigation. Physics discovers that electrons have spin 1/2. Metaphysics then asks whether this spin is intrinsic or relational, whether it is fundamental or derivative, and whether the fundamental/derivative distinction is itself a genuine feature of the world. These questions are not empirical in any obvious sense, yet they constrain how we interpret the physics.

We can state the autonomy position formally:

- P1: Science answers questions about what entities and properties there are within the domain of natural phenomena.
- P2: Metaphysics answers questions about the ground-level structure of reality: which features are fundamental, and what grounds what.
- P3: No purely scientific investigation can settle ground-level structural questions, because those questions concern the framework presupposed by any scientific investigation.
- C: Metaphysics is autonomous from science in the respect that it addresses questions science presupposes but cannot itself answer.

The continuity and autonomy theses are not straightforwardly incompatible. We can hold that the methods of metaphysics and science overlap substantially while insisting that certain structural questions about fundamentality and grounding belong specifically to metaphysics. What is not defensible — from either direction — is a picture in which metaphysics and science are hermetically sealed from each other.

## Ladyman and Ross: The Science-First Challenge

James Ladyman and Don Ross (*Every Thing Must Go*, 2007) mount the most systematic recent case against non-naturalistic metaphysics. Their "principle of naturalistic closure" holds that any metaphysical claim must either be implied by well-confirmed scientific theories or be continuously motivated by scientific practice. Claims about haecceities, primitive modal facts, or Lewisian possible worlds fail this test and should be rejected. In their place, Ladyman and Ross propose "rainforest realism": a metaphysics derived directly from fundamental physics, in which the fundamental ontology consists not in individual objects but in *real patterns* — scale-relative, information-theoretic entities recognized by the special sciences.

Several objections arise. The principle of naturalistic closure is not itself a scientific claim — it is a normative metametaphysical principle, and its status is exactly the kind of philosophical question it purports to rule out. Moreover, the appeal to "real patterns" presupposes criteria of ontological commitment — what makes a pattern real? — that require philosophical articulation independent of physics. And the history of science provides a cautionary note: what counts as "fundamental physics" has changed dramatically across centuries, and tethering metaphysics too closely to current physics risks obsolescence rather than rigor.

## Three Case Studies

The interaction between metaphysics and science is most visible at specific flashpoints. *Quantum mechanics* has generated metaphysical debate about holism: Schaffer has appealed to quantum entanglement to argue that composite systems are more fundamental than their parts, supporting a form of priority monism (discussed in section 3). Whether this metaphysical conclusion follows from the physics alone, or requires additional philosophical argument, is disputed. *Spacetime ontology* presents the debate between substantivalism (spacetime is a genuine entity independent of objects) and relationism (spacetime is merely the totality of spatiotemporal relations between objects): general relativity constrains the options but does not settle the dispute. *Laws of nature* illustrate the pattern most cleanly: whether laws are ontologically robust necessitating relations between universals (Armstrong) or supervenient on the categorical facts (Lewis) is a metaphysical question that is genuinely informed by scientific practice without being answerable by it.

The metametaphysical upshot is that neither pure a priorism nor pure scientism captures how metaphysics actually works at its best. The discipline uses conceptual analysis, inference to the best explanation, coherence reasoning, and careful attention to scientific results. The weight given to each depends on the specific question at issue. Neither science nor metaphysics has complete authority over the other; each constrains the other's conclusions. A metaphysics incompatible with quantum mechanics is suspect; deciding whether quantum wave functions are real physical entities requires philosophy of science. That relationship of mutual constraint — not deference and not independence — is the defensible position.
