# Responses to Carnap

Carnap's challenge generated several lines of response that together constitute much of contemporary meta-ontology. What is striking about the best responses is not that they simply reject Carnap's skepticism about metaphysics, but that they accept what is correct in it and then argue that something substantial survives the concession.

The most direct and influential response is Quine's. Quine accepts that there is no god's eye view from which we can evaluate frameworks from the outside; on this much he agrees with Carnap. But he denies that this forces a pragmatist conclusion. Our total theory of the world — science, mathematics, and common sense taken together — already determines which existence claims to accept, and the question of what our best theory commits us to is a genuine theoretical question, not a practical one. The criterion of ontological commitment makes this precise: a theory is committed to the existence of whatever its quantifiers range over in its canonical logical formulation. Whether numbers exist is a question about whether any adequate formulation of our best theories quantifies over them — and that question has a determinate answer.

The key move is this: Quine accepts Carnap's rejection of the "view from nowhere" but denies that this rejection requires Carnap's pragmatist conclusion. Ontology is not a practical question about which framework to adopt; it is a theoretical question about what our best total theory commits us to. There is always a fact of the matter, even if it is revisable. The argument runs:

- P1: There is no standpoint outside all linguistic frameworks from which to evaluate frameworks.
- P2: But there is a standpoint already internal to inquiry — our total theory of the world, including science, mathematics, and common sense.
- P3: From within this total theory, we can ask which entities we are committed to, via the criterion of ontological commitment.
- C: The relevant ontological question is internal to our total theory, not external to all frameworks — and thus substantive, not merely pragmatic.

This response requires that there be a unique best total theory, or at least a theory with determinate commitments at any given time. Quine's holism allows that the theory is always revisable, but it is still a theory with real commitments.

## Sider's Structural Response

Theodore Sider (*Writing the Book of the World*, 2011) develops the most systematic contemporary defense of substantive metaphysics against Carnapian deflationism. His central claim is that there is a notion of *structure* or *joint-carving* that is not framework-relative: some ways of dividing up the world correspond to genuine features of reality, others impose arbitrary divisions. Scientific theories that use fundamental predicates — mass, charge, spin — are better at describing reality than theories that use artificial predicates like "grue" or "bleen." The best explanation of why fundamental predicates are better is that they carve nature at its joints.

Sider extends this structural notion to logical vocabulary, including the existential quantifier itself. Not all ways of deploying "∃x" are equally structural; there is a "natural" or "joint-carving" quantifier whose use corresponds to the genuine structure of existence as a feature of the world:

- P1: Some predicates carve nature at its joints (e.g., "electron," "mass") and some do not (e.g., "grue").
- P2: The best explanation of why joint-carving predicates figure in laws and support reliable inference is that they correspond to genuine features of the world.
- P3: The same distinction applies to logical notions, including the quantifier: some quantifiers are more structural than others.
- P4: The natural quantifier corresponds to the genuine structure of existence as a feature of the world.
- C: There is a unique, joint-carving existential quantifier, and questions asked in it have determinate metaphysical answers.

This directly targets both Carnap's framework-pragmatism and Hirsch's quantifier variance (Section 2). Even granting that "there are chairs" can be made true within some framework, the structural question remains: are chairs among the fundamental constituents of reality, tracked by the joint-carving quantifier? That question is not framework-relative.

## Fine's Neo-Aristotelian Response

Kit Fine (2001, 2009) offers a different path. The key metaontological notion for Fine is not quantification (Quine) or structure (Sider) but *reality*: some things are merely representationally real — they exist relative to how we represent the world — while others are constitutively real — they are part of the fundamental fabric of reality, independent of representation. To say "numbers exist" in the quantificational sense is to say less than to say "numbers are part of reality" in Fine's constitutive sense. Metaphysics, for Fine, asks which entities are genuinely real, not merely posited by a useful framework.

This response relocates the key notion rather than drawing a sharp line between internal and external questions. Even granting — as Carnap insists — that "there are numbers" is trivially true within mathematics, the question of whether numbers are *real* in the constitutive sense remains open and substantive. Fine's "reality" operator is not the same as the existential quantifier. Whether the operator is coherent and non-trivial is a fair question, and Fine's answer is that "reality" is primitive: as fundamental as existence itself, and deliberately irreducible to existence-talk.

## Thomasson's Easy Ontology

Amie Thomasson (*Ordinary Objects*, 2007; *Ontology Made Easy*, 2015) defends a position that is sympathetic to Carnap but subtly distinct. On her "easy ontology," ontological questions are trivially answered by applying conceptual rules:

- P1: Ontological questions are questions about what exists, and "exists" has its meaning fixed by conceptual rules governing its use.
- P2: Conceptual rules governing physical object terms entail, given wood arranged table-wise, that there is a table.
- P3: We know there is wood arranged table-wise.
- C: We trivially know that tables exist.

Thomasson's position differs from Carnap's in that she claims these trivial existence claims are genuinely true, not merely pragmatically adopted. Tables really exist; it is just easy to know this. She is more skeptical about questions of fundamentality and explanatory priority — the harder questions that Sider and Fine take as central. Critics respond that Thomasson's easy ontology dissolves the wrong problems: the interesting metaphysical question is not "do tables exist?" but "what is the relationship between tables and their constituent particles?", and that is not trivially answered by conceptual analysis.

## The Verbal/Substantive Distinction in Practice

A recurring difficulty in all of these debates: even granting a distinction between substantive and verbal disputes, applying it to specific cases is hard. Is the debate between endurantists and perdurantists merely verbal? Hirsch argues yes — both parties describe the same phenomena using "persists" differently. Sider argues no — there is a fact about whether objects have temporal parts, captured only by one party's quantifier use. Their disagreement about this case is itself a substantive metametaphysical disagreement, one that illustrates how difficult it is to apply the verbal/substantive distinction in practice.

This difficulty does not show that the distinction is illusory. It shows that metametaphysics is a substantive discipline: determining which disputes are genuinely substantive requires real philosophical work. That work is what the rest of this module undertakes.
