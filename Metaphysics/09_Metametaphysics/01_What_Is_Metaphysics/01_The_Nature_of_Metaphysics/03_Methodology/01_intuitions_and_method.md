# Intuitions and Metaphysical Method

Here is a puzzle about how metaphysics gets started. When a physicist proposes a theory about quarks, she has experiments to run. When a metaphysician proposes a theory about the persistence of persons, what does she have? The answer, in practice, is that she has cases — carefully constructed thought experiments that seem to have obvious verdicts — and the theory is tested by whether it delivers the right verdicts. The judgment that *of course* this is the same ship after its planks are gradually replaced, or that *surely* personal identity requires more than mere bodily continuity, is what we mean by a metaphysical intuition. The methodological question is what evidential weight these intuitions should carry.

The role of intuitions in metaphysics is roughly analogous to the role of observation in science: they constitute the data that theories must account for, and they can in principle revise those theories or be revised by them. When a metaphysical theory entails something that conflicts sharply with a well-established intuition, this counts against the theory. When a theory coheres with intuitions across a wide range of cases, this counts in its favor. Lewis was explicit about this in *On the Plurality of Worlds*: intuitions are "that which we believe without any particular reason," and they are to be weighed against theoretical virtues when evaluating philosophical theories.

But the evidential status of intuitions is genuinely contested, and several objections are serious. Intuitions may reflect cultural or cognitive biases rather than facts about reality — the intuition that persons are distinct from their bodies may track features of human social cognition, not metaphysical structure. Intuitions conflict: different people have divergent intuitions about personal identity in fission cases, about whether composite objects exist, about the statue and the lump. And for cases very different from the ordinary environments in which our cognitive faculties evolved, our intuitions may be systematically unreliable.

A further worry is the *concept/world ambiguity*: metaphysical intuitions may track conceptual facts — facts about how our concepts work — rather than worldly facts about how reality is. When we intuit that persons persist through moderate bodily change, perhaps we are detecting something about the concept of *person*, about how "person" is used in English, rather than a fact about the metaphysical nature of persons. This connects directly to Carnap's challenge: if metaphysical intuitions are really intuitions about concepts, then metaphysics is really conceptual analysis, not inquiry into the structure of the world.

## The Defeasible Evidence View

The standard account in analytic metaphysics treats intuitions as *defeasible evidence* — evidence that has genuine epistemic weight but can be overridden by stronger considerations. The structure is:

- P1: Metaphysical theories must be evaluated against data.
- P2: The primary data for metaphysics are carefully considered intuitive judgments about cases.
- P3: Intuitions are defeasible but not arbitrary — they can be overridden by consistency considerations, theoretical virtues, or empirical discoveries.
- C: Intuitions constitute the evidential base of metaphysics, to be weighed against other considerations in the manner of reflective equilibrium.

Not all intuitions carry equal weight, and the relevant distinctions are worth drawing. *Pre-theoretic* intuitions are immediate reactions before philosophical training; *reflective* intuitions are judgments reached after careful consideration of alternatives and consequences. Reflective intuitions carry more evidential weight. *Modal* intuitions — judgments about what is necessarily or possibly the case — are among the most important: Kripke's arguments in *Naming and Necessity* depend heavily on the intuition that Hesperus could not fail to be Phosphorus. *Structural* intuitions — judgments about hierarchical dependence, about which facts depend on which — inform grounding theory, though they are typically less secure than modal intuitions because grounding is a more technical notion. *Existential* intuitions — that there are numbers, that composite objects exist — provide starting points for ontological inquiry but are among the most contested, since both Carnapians and quantifier-variance theorists argue they may be merely verbal.

## The Debunking Challenge

The most serious objection to intuition-based metaphysics is the *debunking argument*. If we can explain why people have a certain intuition in terms that do not cite the intuition's truth, then the intuition does not constitute evidence for its content. Applied to personal identity: if human intuitions about personal persistence are explained by evolutionary pressure to track and re-identify conspecifics for purposes of cooperation, punishment, and social cohesion — and this explanation does not require that our intuitions track metaphysical facts about persons — then those intuitions do not provide evidence about the metaphysical nature of persons.

The debunking argument is powerful but has limits. Showing that an intuition has a non-truth-tracking cause does not show that the intuition is false or that its content is not true — a valid inference may still reach the right conclusion even if the path to it was not epistemically ideal. And if debunking arguments undermine metaphysical intuitions on the grounds that they have evolutionary explanations, analogous arguments may equally undermine perceptual beliefs, which are also products of evolutionary pressures. The debunking challenge needs to be applied selectively, and that requires an account of why some intuitions are more vulnerable than others.

The neo-Quinean response (Sider): intuitions can track both conceptual and worldly facts, and the task of metaphysics is to distinguish these. When an intuition remains stable across conceptual revision — when it does not simply evaporate once we realize it might reflect a feature of our concepts rather than of the world — it is more likely to be tracking worldly facts. Fine's response adds a different angle: there is an objective notion of essence — what is true of a thing in virtue of its nature — that is distinct from both conceptual and modal facts. Intuitions about essence can track this objective notion, making metaphysical inquiry irreducible to conceptual analysis.

The role of intuitions in metaphysics surfaces throughout the rest of this textbook. In the personal identity module, conflicting intuitions about fission cases drive the debate between psychological continuity theories and biological theories. In the universals module, the intuition that properties are shared grounds the argument for universals. In the causation module, intuitions about counterfactual dependence inform the debate between Humean and non-Humean views. Metametaphysics asks the foundational question that frames all of these debates: what is the epistemic status of the intuitions being appealed to, and when are they strong enough to bear the theoretical weight placed on them?
