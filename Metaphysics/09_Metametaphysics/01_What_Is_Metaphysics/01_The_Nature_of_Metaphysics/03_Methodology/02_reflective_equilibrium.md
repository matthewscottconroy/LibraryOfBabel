# Reflective Equilibrium in Metaphysics

Intuitions about cases and theoretical principles rarely line up neatly from the start. The intuition that I am the same person I was ten years ago suggests that personal identity is determinate and stable. The intuition that there is something it is like to persist through time suggests that persistence has a phenomenological dimension. But the theoretical principle that no two distinct objects can occupy the same location at the same time creates pressure on accounts of how objects persist through change. Reconciling these is not a matter of picking the intuitions we like and finding a theory to fit them, nor of picking a theory and ignoring inconvenient intuitions. It requires a back-and-forth process of revision and adjustment that Rawls called *reflective equilibrium* — a method originally developed for moral philosophy that has been broadly adopted in analytic metaphysics.

The method works as follows. We begin with a set I of initial intuitive judgments about specific cases, and a set T of candidate theoretical principles. We check for coherence: do the principles in T entail verdicts consistent with the intuitions in I? Where incoherence arises, we decide whether to revise the intuition or the principle, and iterate until a stable, coherent fit is achieved. Lewis offers a useful heuristic for the key decision at each step: the strength of the case for revision depends on the relative firmness of the intuition versus the theoretical principle. A widely shared, reflectively stable intuition has the epistemic status of a datum that must be accommodated; a context-sensitive intuition can be overridden by theoretical considerations.

This method is holistic and fallibilist. No intuition is sacrosanct: if a strong theoretical case can be made against it, the intuition can be revised. But intuitions are not arbitrary either — they are the only data we have for evaluating abstract metaphysical claims, and a theory that conflicts with many strong intuitions is thereby seriously impugned.

*Narrow reflective equilibrium* seeks coherence between intuitions about specific cases and principles at roughly the same level of abstraction. *Wide* reflective equilibrium expands the circle: the background theories considered include formal logic and mathematics (a metaphysical view should not conflict with basic logical and mathematical truths), physical science (a metaphysical view should be compatible with fundamental physics), cognitive science (a metaphysical view should be compatible with what we know about how humans represent the world), and sometimes moral theory where metaphysical views carry ethical implications. Wide reflective equilibrium is methodologically ambitious: it uses more evidence, but it also introduces more opportunities for error and makes equilibrium harder to achieve.

## Case Study: Persistence and Equilibrium

The debate about persistence through change illustrates how reflective equilibrium works in practice. Consider the three main competing positions. *Endurantism* holds that objects persist by being wholly present at each time at which they exist. *Perdurantism* (four-dimensionalism) holds that objects persist by having distinct temporal parts at each time. *Presentism* holds that only the present moment exists, so persistence is not a matter of an object being at multiple times at all.

The relevant intuitions include: (I1) I am the same person I was ten years ago; (I2) there is something it is like to persist through time as an enduring subject; (I3) change over time is real and not merely apparent. The relevant principles include: (T1) no two distinct objects can occupy the same location at the same time; (T2) what exists at a time is exactly what is wholly present at that time; (T3) the temporal dimension is analogous to the spatial dimension.

Perdurantism handles T3 well but conflicts with I2 — the phenomenology of persistence as a continuing subject is hard to accommodate if I am really a series of temporal parts. Endurantism handles I1 and I2 well but faces the problem of temporary intrinsics: if I am wholly present at two times and have different properties at each (being standing at t1, being seated at t2), how can I be one and the same object? The same material object, wholly present at two times with incompatible intrinsic properties, seems to violate T1 unless properties are indexed to times. Presentism avoids some of these problems but faces the truthmaker problem: what makes it true, now, that I was once a child?

Equilibrium requires deciding which intuitions and principles to hold fixed. Lewis's perdurantism bites the bullet on I2, treating the phenomenology of persistence as metaphysically unreliable. Endurantists bite the bullet on the pure form of T1, solving the problem of temporary intrinsics by indexing properties to times or to temporal stages. Which equilibrium is reached depends on the relative initial firmness of I2 and T1 for each theorist — and this is a genuine philosophical disagreement, not merely a verbal one.

## The Truth-Tracking Problem

The deepest objection to reflective equilibrium is epistemological: even if a set of intuitions and principles achieves perfect coherence, there is no guarantee that the resulting system is true. Coherence is a property of sets of beliefs, not a guide to facts about mind-independent reality. Two distinct challenges arise. The *underdetermination problem*: multiple mutually incoherent belief systems might each achieve internal coherence, with nothing in the method to determine which is true. The *systematic error problem*: if our intuitions are systematically biased — by evolution, culture, or the limitations of human cognition — no amount of internal coherence can correct them.

The responses available here are not trivial, but they are genuine. All evidential methods face underdetermination; the question is whether reflective equilibrium is worse than alternatives, and the alternatives are not obviously superior. Wide reflective equilibrium can be partly self-correcting: by including cognitive science, it can identify systematic biases in intuitions and discount them accordingly. And the alternative — abandoning intuitions as evidence altogether — leaves metaphysics without any evidential base, which is arguably worse than having a fallible one.

Every module in this textbook implicitly practices reflective equilibrium. The personal identity module begins with intuitive verdicts about fission cases and seeks principles that systematize them. The mereology module begins with the intuition that wholes are distinct from their parts and tests mereological universalism against it. The metametaphysics module makes this implicit method explicit, asking whether it is epistemically sound — and if not, what should replace it.
