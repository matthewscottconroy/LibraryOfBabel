# Experimental Philosophy

When a metaphysician presents a thought experiment and says "surely everyone would agree that...", she is making an empirical claim — the claim that her intuition is widely shared. Experimental philosophy (x-phi) takes this claim seriously enough to test it. The movement, which emerged prominently in the early 2000s, uses surveys and psychological experiments to investigate philosophical intuitions empirically, asking whether the verdicts philosophers invoke are robust, cross-culturally stable, and genuinely universal. The results have often been unsettling, and they pose a challenge that metaphysics cannot simply ignore.

The case for x-phi can be stated directly:

- P1: Metaphysical theories are evaluated partly by whether they cohere with widely shared intuitions.
- P2: Whether a given intuition is widely shared is an empirical question, not answerable from the armchair.
- P3: Armchair philosophers frequently assume that their own intuitions are widely shared, without empirical verification.
- C: Empirical investigation of intuitions is a necessary check on the assumptions of armchair metaphysics.

This does not conclude that empirical investigation replaces philosophical argument. It concludes that philosophers cannot simply assume universality without evidence — and that where empirical evidence reveals variation, the standard justification for invoking an intuition as evidence is weakened.

The empirical findings are varied and sometimes surprising. The Weinberg, Nichols, and Stich studies (2001) found that intuitions about epistemological thought experiments — including Gettier cases — varied across cultural groups (Western, East Asian, South Asian), suggesting that even apparently obvious intuitions may not be universal. The *Knobe effect* revealed that attributions of intentionality to an agent's side effects depend on the moral valence of those effects: people are more willing to say an agent "intentionally" caused a bad side effect than a good one, even when the agent's attitude is identical in both cases. Nichols and Bruno (2010) found that people's verdicts on personal identity fission cases depend on framing — whether the case is presented in terms of physical or psychological continuity — suggesting that what appears to be a single intuition may be sensitive to framing in ways that undermine its reliability. Survey work on mereological intuitions suggests that people are neither systematic universalists nor systematic nihilists but apply different standards in different contexts.

## Three Stances on X-phi's Significance

These findings have been interpreted in quite different ways, and the disagreement is itself instructive.

The *debunking stance* (Stich, Weinberg) holds that if intuitions vary across populations in ways that track demographic features rather than metaphysical facts, they are unreliable evidence. The philosopher's confidence in her thought experiments is empirically unwarranted, and philosophy should either abandon the appeal to intuitions or radically revise its methodology.

The *calibration stance* (Knobe, Nichols) is more measured: x-phi helps philosophers calibrate their use of intuitions. Not all intuitions are equally reliable; empirical investigation reveals which are robust and which are context-sensitive or framing-dependent. Philosophers should rely on robust, widely-shared, frame-insensitive intuitions and discount fragile ones. On this view, x-phi improves rather than undermines philosophical methodology.

The *concept-mapping stance* (Ludwig, 2007) argues that x-phi does not directly test metaphysical intuitions but maps folk concepts. Finding that intuitions vary across populations shows that the folk concept of "person" or "intentional action" is complex or contested — but this is a finding about concepts, not about metaphysical facts. Philosophical analysis should take account of conceptual complexity without abandoning the project of analyzing the concepts themselves.

## Objections to X-phi

Several objections to x-phi's significance for metaphysics are worth taking seriously.

The *expertise objection* (Kauppinen, 2007; Sosa, 2007): philosophical intuitions are not folk intuitions. They are the careful, considered judgments of trained philosophers who have worked through many cases and their implications, tested their verdicts for consistency, and weighed them against theoretical principles. Folk intuitions studied by x-phi surveys may be less refined and less reliable than expert intuitions. We do not use folk physics to test professional physics; why should folk intuitions test professional philosophy?

The *comprehension objection*: survey subjects may not understand the cases in the way the philosopher intends. A question about a "brain transplant case" might be interpreted as a question about the feasibility of the surgery, or about the psychological continuity of the subject, or about something else entirely. If subjects do not understand the cases correctly, their responses are not evidence about the relevant philosophical question at all.

The *methodology objection*: even if individual intuitions are unreliable, philosophical methodology involves much more than consulting individual intuitions. It involves constructing theories, evaluating them for consistency and explanatory power, and checking them against many intuitions simultaneously. X-phi targets only one element of this complex process and should not be taken as a verdict on the whole.

## The Metametaphysical Stakes

From the perspective of metametaphysics, x-phi raises a foundational challenge. If intuitions are the evidential base of metaphysics and x-phi shows that intuitions are unreliable or culture-relative, then metaphysics lacks a secure evidential foundation. The Carnapian will welcome this: if there is no solid evidence for metaphysical claims, then those claims are not substantive — they are, at best, pragmatic choices between frameworks.

The defender of substantive metaphysics must respond by showing either that the relevant intuitions are not undermined by x-phi results, or that an alternative evidential base exists, or that x-phi has been misapplied. Each response is available and has been pursued, but none is without cost.

The productive upshot — regardless of where one lands on x-phi's significance — is that x-phi forces the metametaphysician to be explicit about which intuitions are being used and why. Rather than appealing vaguely to "common sense" or "what everyone agrees," we are pressed to identify specific intuitions, defend their epistemic status, and explain why they should count as evidence for or against specific theories. This is a genuine methodological improvement, and it is one that careful metaphysicians should welcome.
