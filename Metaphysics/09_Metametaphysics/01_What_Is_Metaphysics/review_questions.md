# Review Questions — What Is Metaphysics?

1. The chapter distinguishes three demarcation criteria for metaphysics: the generality criterion, the a priori criterion, and the grounding criterion. What does each criterion get right, and what objection presses hardest against each? Does combining them produce a more defensible account than any one criterion alone, or does the combination generate new problems?

2. Carnap argues that external existence questions — "do numbers really exist?" — are not meaningful theoretical questions but merely practical choices between linguistic frameworks. The chapter treats this as the most serious deflationary challenge. What exactly makes it more challenging than the blunt verification principle, and what is the strongest thing a defender of substantive metaphysics can concede to Carnap without losing the case for genuine ontological inquiry?

3. The verification principle is self-undermining: it is neither analytic nor empirically verifiable. Yet the chapter also says positivism left a "productive methodological residue" — demands for precision, suspicion of verbal disputes, requirement that claims carry determinate implications. Can both things be true simultaneously? What would it mean for a philosophical critique to be wrong and still beneficial?

4. Quine, Fine, and Thomasson each respond to Carnap's internal/external distinction while conceding something to it. Compare their responses: which concession is largest, which retains the most of what makes metaphysics distinctive, and which response is most vulnerable to a Carnapian counter-reply?

5. The chapter says that metaphysical intuitions are roughly analogous to observations in science — defeasible evidence that theories must accommodate. But the debunking argument claims that if an intuition has a non-truth-tracking evolutionary explanation, it loses evidential force. Does this argument apply equally to perceptual beliefs? If so, what would it take for a debunking argument to be selectively targeted at metaphysical intuitions without undercutting science as well?

6. Reflective equilibrium requires deciding whether to revise an intuition or a theoretical principle when they conflict. The persistence debate illustrates this: perdurantists revise the phenomenological intuition (I2) that there is something it is like to persist as a continuing subject; endurantists revise the principle (T1) that no two distinct objects can occupy the same location with incompatible intrinsic properties. What determines which choice is epistemically better, and is there any non-circular way to make that determination?

7. Experimental philosophy finds that intuitions about thought experiments vary across cultures and are sensitive to framing. The expertise objection replies that philosophical intuitions are refined, trained, and tested for consistency in ways folk intuitions are not. Does this objection fully defuse the x-phi challenge, or does it only push the problem back — now requiring an account of why expert philosophical training produces reliable, rather than systematically biased, intuitions?

8. The chapter says metaphysics operates at the metametaphysical level — asking what kind of enterprise first-order inquiry is — while the rest of the textbook operates at the first-order level. But the grounding criterion makes metaphysics the study of reality's hierarchical architecture, which is itself a first-order claim about what reality is like. Does that mean the grounding criterion is internally unstable, or is there a coherent way to be simultaneously doing first-order and metametaphysical inquiry?
