# Ontological Commitment

To say that something exists is to make an ontological claim. To have a theory that implies the existence of something is to be ontologically committed to that thing. But what exactly does ontological commitment consist in, and how do we determine what a theory is committed to?

Quine's answer is the most influential in contemporary analytic philosophy: the ontological commitments of a theory are the objects that must be in the range of the theory's bound variables for the theory's claims to be true. To be committed to Fs is to quantify over Fs — to say "there exists an x such that x is F." If our best scientific theories quantify over electrons, we are committed to electrons; if they quantify over mathematical objects, we are committed to mathematical objects; if they can be reformulated so as to not quantify over some entity type, we are not committed to those entities.

This criterion is elegant and has the virtue of connecting ontological questions to the logical structure of our theories. But it has been challenged at multiple points. The challenge from quantifier variance, developed by Eli Hirsch, holds that there is no single, privileged sense of the quantifier "there exists" — different linguistic communities can use quantifiers with different ranges without one being correct and the other incorrect. If quantifier variance is coherent, then the Quinean criterion cannot provide a neutral way of determining what exists; the answer will vary with the conventions of the linguistic framework.

Neo-Fregean approaches, associated with Wright and Hale, offer a different picture. They ground ontological claims in abstraction principles — principles like "the number of Fs is the same as the number of Gs if and only if F and G are equinumerous" — and argue that these principles are analytic, knowable a priori, and sufficient to ground genuine ontological claims about numbers. This connects ontological commitment to meaning rather than to the logical structure of empirical theories.

The chapter develops these three approaches and the debates between them, asking what it takes to settle — or dissolve — questions about what there is.
