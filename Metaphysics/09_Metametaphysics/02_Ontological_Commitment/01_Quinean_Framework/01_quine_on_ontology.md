# Quine on Ontology

We want to know what exists. The question is not as simple as it sounds, because asking it naively invites confusion: we seem to need to pick out the things that exist in order to say what there is, and that appears circular. Quine's great contribution was to dissolve this appearance of circularity by grounding ontology in something we actually do: construct and evaluate theories. What exists is what our best theories say exists. More precisely: a theory is committed to the existence of exactly those entities that must be values of its bound variables in its canonical logical formulation for the theory to be true. This is Quine's criterion of ontological commitment, and it is the starting point for contemporary metaontology.

The naturalistic motivation is clear. Philosophy is continuous with science, and ontology is a matter of what we must posit to make our best theories true. There is no privileged standpoint outside our theories from which to evaluate ontological questions; we work from within our total theory of the world and ask what that theory commits us to. The key principle, stated formally:

A theory T is ontologically committed to Fs if and only if, in the canonical first-order logical notation of T, bound variables must take Fs as values for T to be true.

The "canonical" qualification matters. Ordinary language generates misleading surface forms: "there is a lack of oxygen in the room" does not posit *lacks* as entities. "The average family has 2.3 children" does not posit average families. Canonical paraphrase into first-order logic removes these misleading surface forms, revealing what the theory is genuinely committed to. The criterion then applies to the paraphrase, not to the original sentence. "There is an x such that x is a number and x is prime" commits us to numbers; "there is an x such that x is a rabbit" commits us to rabbits.

Quine combines this criterion with holism: our total theory of the world faces the "tribunal of experience" as a whole, not sentence by sentence. Adjustments to one part of the theory can ripple through others. This means ontological commitments are revisable in light of theoretical change, including scientific progress. We should prefer theories with fewer ontological commitments, all else equal — the naturalized version of Ockham's Razor. An entity is "necessary" just in case there is no theoretically adequate formulation of our best theories that avoids quantifying over it.

## The Indispensability Argument

The most important application of Quine's framework is the *indispensability argument* for mathematical Platonism, developed jointly with Hilary Putnam:

- P1: We ought to believe in the existence of all entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
- P2: Mathematical entities (numbers, functions, sets) are indispensable to our best scientific theories — physics, chemistry, and biology all quantify over mathematical objects.
- P3: By the criterion of ontological commitment, our best scientific theories are committed to mathematical entities.
- C: We ought to believe in the existence of mathematical entities.

This argument is significant for two reasons. First, it grounds mathematical existence in the same evidential base as physical existence — our best theories — rather than in mysterious mathematical intuition. Second, it illustrates the Quinean method: ontological questions are settled by asking what our best theories commit us to, not by direct metaphysical inspection.

Hartry Field's nominalism (*Science Without Numbers*, 1980) responds by attempting to show that P2 is false — that physics can be reformulated without quantifying over mathematical objects, using only physical facts and a relational space-time apparatus. Whether Field's project succeeds is one of the most important open questions in the philosophy of mathematics.

## Abstract Objects and the Identity Constraint

Quine's own ontological commitments were complex. He accepted sets (because first-order set theory provides an adequate framework for mathematics) but resisted properties, propositions, and meanings, on the grounds that their identity conditions are unclear. The principle: *no entity without identity*. We can accept an entity into our ontology only if we can specify determinate identity conditions for it.

Sets have clear identity conditions: two sets are identical iff they have the same members. Numbers (reconstructed set-theoretically) inherit this clarity. Properties are more troublesome: is the property of being triangular identical to the property of being trilateral? They apply to exactly the same things, but many philosophers think they are distinct properties. Quine took this difficulty as a reason to resist properties as fundamental entities. The problem connects to contemporary debates about structured propositions and fine-grained versus coarse-grained conceptions of content.

## The Landscape of Alternatives

Quine's framework has generated a rich landscape of alternative metaontological positions, each responding to features of the Quinean approach its proponents find inadequate. *Neo-Fregeanism* (Wright, Hale) accepts a different criterion of ontological commitment — the presence of singular terms in analytic truths, not quantifiers — and argues that abstraction principles like Hume's Principle generate objects without requiring indispensability. *Quantifier variance* (Hirsch) denies that there is a unique "right" reading of the existential quantifier, deflating the significance of Quinean ontological questions. *Grounding-based metaphysics* (Fine, Schaffer) shifts the central question from "what exists?" to "what is fundamental?" — accepting that existence questions may have trivial answers while insisting that grounding questions are substantive.

Each of these positions is examined in subsequent sections of this module. What they share is a recognition that the Quinean framework, for all its power, leaves important questions unanswered — particularly the question of which entities, among those that exist, are fundamental and which are derivative. Quantification alone does not settle questions of grounding and fundamentality, and those questions turn out to be among the most important in contemporary metaphysics.
