# To Be Is to Be a Value of a Variable

"To be is to be the value of a variable." This slogan encapsulates Quine's criterion of ontological commitment in a phrase, and it is worth dwelling on what it actually says. It is a refinement of "to be is to exist" — not a tautology but a methodological instruction: ground ontological questions in logical form rather than in folk ontological intuitions. Instead of asking directly "do numbers exist?", ask whether there is a canonical formulation of mathematics (and of the scientific theories that use mathematics) that does not quantify over numbers. If not — if every adequate formulation includes quantifiers ranging over numbers — then numbers exist.

This shifts the ontological question into a question about the best logical regimentation of our theories, which is tractable in a way that direct metaphysical inspection is not. The slogan also encapsulates Quine's anti-reductive stance: tables, molecules, and quarks exist because our best theories quantify over them, and there is no reason to try to eliminate them in favor of sense data or phenomenal reports.

The appeal to *canonical* notation is essential here. Ordinary language generates what look like quantified claims over entities we do not actually want to posit. Consider:

- "There is a lack of oxygen in the room" — does not posit *lacks* as entities.
- "The average family has 2.3 children" — does not posit average families.
- "There is something odd about his behavior" — the "something" may or may not refer to a property or fact.

Canonical paraphrase reformulates such statements in first-order logic in a way that accurately reflects what we are committed to. "There is a lack of oxygen" becomes "it is not the case that there is sufficient oxygen" — no quantification over lacks. The criterion applies to the paraphrase.

But this raises an immediate question: is there always a unique correct canonical paraphrase? Quine assumed there is a fact of the matter, but critics have argued that paraphrase is underdetermined — multiple canonical forms are compatible with the same ordinary-language claims, with different ontological commitments. This underdetermination threatens the precision that makes the slogan attractive.

## A Worked Example: The Ontology of Properties

Consider "Scarlet is a shade of red." Does this commit us to properties — scarlet, red — as entities? Apply the Quinean method.

*Step 1*: Identify apparent quantification. "Scarlet is a shade of red" makes scarlet a subject, suggesting quantification over colors or properties.

*Step 2*: Attempt canonical paraphrase. A nominalist paraphrase might be "all scarlet things are red things" — quantifying only over individual objects, not properties. Does this paraphrase preserve all the relevant information?

*Step 3*: Assess adequacy. The nominalist paraphrase does not capture the claim that scarlet is a *shade of* red — a relation of determinate to determinable. A fuller paraphrase might require quantification over color-determinates and their ordering relations, which begins to look like quantification over properties again.

*Step 4*: Draw the ontological conclusion. If no adequate paraphrase eliminates quantification over properties (or property-like entities), the sentence commits us to them. If an adequate paraphrase exists, it does not.

This example illustrates why debates about the ontology of properties — universals versus nominalism — are, in the Quinean framework, debates about the possibility of adequate paraphrase. Armstrong's argument for universals depends in part on the claim that predication cannot be adequately paraphrased without commitment to properties.

## The Logic-First Objection

A significant objection to the slogan is that it assumes first-order classical logic is the canonical language. But this assumption is contestable, and different logics generate different ontological commitments for the same pre-theoretic claims.

Boolos (1984) argued that English quantification sometimes takes a genuinely plural form — "some critics admire only each other" — that cannot be adequately captured by first-order quantification over sets or properties. Plural logic quantifies over "pluralities" rather than sets, and plural quantification may not commit us to sets or classes. In free logic, standard assumptions about singular terms are relaxed, allowing for non-referring terms without trivializing ontological commitments. Second-order logic, used in many formal theories, quantifies over properties directly — and Quine was suspicious of it precisely because of its apparent commitment to properties. The choice of canonical logic is therefore not neutral: it builds in ontological assumptions, and the slogan presupposes a contestable choice.

## The Connection to Grounding

The most influential contemporary critique of the slogan is that it focuses on the wrong question. Even granting that the existential quantifier provides the right measure of ontological commitment, quantification does not help with questions about grounding and fundamentality. Two theories might agree on what exists — both quantify over the same entities — while disagreeing about which entities are fundamental and which are derivative.

Fine (2009) argues that the central question of metaphysics is not "what exists?" but "what is real?" — where "real" captures a notion of fundamentality or constitution that goes beyond mere existence. Sider (2011) makes the analogous point using "structure": two theories may be equivalent in their existential commitments while differing in how much structure they attribute to the domain.

This critique does not show that the Quinean slogan is false or that existence questions are unimportant. It shows that existence is not the only important metaontological notion, and that grounding and fundamentality require additional theoretical apparatus beyond what quantification supplies. The slogan tells us what there is. It does not tell us what there fundamentally is — and that, it turns out, is a different and deeper question.
