# Objections to the Quinean Framework

Quine's approach to ontology is enormously influential, but it has attracted persistent objections from several directions. Understanding these objections is not merely a matter of fair play: together they map the limitations of the Quinean framework and motivate the supplementations — grounding, structure, fundamentality — that occupy the rest of this module.

The first and most unsettling objection arises from within Quine's own commitments. Quine himself argued for the *indeterminacy of reference*: there is no fact of the matter about what the terms of a language refer to. "Gavagai" (his famous example) could refer to rabbits, rabbit-stages, or undetached rabbit-parts, and no behavioral evidence can settle which. If the reference of predicates is indeterminate, the same indeterminacy affects the reference of the quantifier — what counts as a "value" of a variable is subject to the same underdetermination. The criterion of ontological commitment then yields indeterminate results:

- P1: Ontological commitment is determined by which entities must be values of bound variables in a canonical formulation of our best theories.
- P2: The reference of the quantifier is subject to indeterminacy just as the reference of predicates is.
- P3: If there is no fact of the matter about what "there is" ranges over, there is no determinate fact about ontological commitment.
- C: Quine's criterion yields indeterminate results.

Quine's response was to distinguish *ontological relativity* — the thesis that ontological claims are relative to a background language — from the *use* of the criterion within a fixed language. Within English (or within a fixed theoretical language), we can ask what our theories commit us to; the indeterminacy arises only when we try to evaluate theories across different linguistic backgrounds. Whether this response adequately defuses the problem, or merely localizes it, is debated.

## The Proxy Function Argument

A more technical problem for the Quinean criterion involves *proxy function arguments*. Suppose we have a theory T committed to entities of type E. Now consider a variant theory T* identical to T except that every occurrence of an entity of type E is replaced by a proxy — say, its unit set. T* is empirically equivalent to T: it makes the same observational predictions. But it is committed to sets rather than concrete objects. Quine's criterion, combined with the requirement that theories face the "tribunal of experience," cannot distinguish T from T* in terms of ontological commitment.

Sider (2009, "Ontological Realism") uses this argument to motivate the claim that the Quinean framework needs supplementation with a notion of structure or joint-carving: the correct theory is not merely the one that is empirically adequate but the one that uses predicates which carve nature at its joints. Two empirically equivalent theories can differ in how well they track the genuine structure of the world, and this difference is what makes one of them correct in a substantive sense.

## The Deflationary Challenge

Thomas Hofweber (2005, 2016) distinguishes two readings of claims like "there are numbers." On the *internal* reading, "there are numbers" is a claim within the domain of discourse of arithmetic — a claim about what the number-quantifier ranges over within mathematics. On the *external* reading, "there are numbers" is a claim about what extra-mathematical entities exist — a claim in the domain of metaphysics. Hofweber argues that ordinary mathematical practice involves only the internal reading, and that the external reading generates pseudo-questions. The sentence "2 is a number" does not commit us to a Platonic entity; it merely says that 2 falls under the number-predicate in arithmetic.

The challenge for Quine: if there are two readings of existential claims, and the internal reading does not carry ontological weight, then the Quinean criterion — which applies to both readings — is not selective enough to settle the metaphysical question. Quine needs to show that there is a single, unambiguous reading of the quantifier that carries ontological weight. The deflationary challenge — closely related to Carnap's framework-relativism — says this cannot be done.

The *paraphrase problem* adds another dimension. Quine's criterion depends on there being a fact of the matter about which formulation of a theory is canonical. But the debate about the ontology of holes (Lewis and Lewis, 1970) illustrates the difficulty. Ordinary discourse seems to quantify over holes: "there are three holes in this cheese." The nominalist wants to paraphrase this as "this cheese is perforated in three places," eliminating quantification over holes. But is this paraphrase adequate? It loses information about the spatial properties of holes, their persistence conditions, and their causal role. And there is no neutral criterion for determining when a paraphrase is "adequate": if adequacy requires capturing all semantic content, many nominalist paraphrases fail; if it requires only preserving observational predictions, too many paraphrases succeed, making ontological commitment indeterminate.

## The Grounding Supplement

The most influential contemporary response to the limitations of the Quinean framework is not to replace it but to supplement it with a notion of grounding or fundamentality. The framework then becomes two-stage:

- *Existence question*: What entities exist? — Answered by the criterion of ontological commitment.
- *Fundamentality question*: Among the entities that exist, which are fundamental? — Answered by the grounding hierarchy.

Fine (2009) argues that this two-stage approach captures what is right about the Quinean framework while adding what is needed to capture metaphysical priority. Sider (2011) adds that even the quantifier can be more or less structural, so the existence question may itself need to be refined by structural considerations.

The supplemented framework is the background for the remainder of this module: Section 3 develops the concept of grounding, and Section 4 develops the concept of essence — together providing the fine-grained metaphysical apparatus that quantification alone cannot supply. The Quinean question "what exists?" is real and important; it just is not the only metaphysical question, and answering it is not sufficient for understanding the structure of reality.
