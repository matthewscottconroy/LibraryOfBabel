# What Is Quantifier Variance?

Suppose a mereological universalist and a mereological nihilist are having an argument. The universalist says: "There is a chair in this room." The nihilist says: "There is no chair — only particles arranged chair-wise." Are they really disagreeing about the world? Eli Hirsch's answer is: no. They are using "exist" in different senses, and both of their claims are true in their respective senses. The chair-talk and the particles-arranged-chair-wise-talk are both true; they are not competing descriptions of the same facts but different languages for describing the same facts. If Hirsch is right, a large swath of traditional ontological debate evaporates into a terminological fog. This is the thesis of *quantifier variance*.

The thesis holds that the existential quantifier ("there is," "there exists") is not univocal — it can mean different things in different linguistic frameworks, and these different meanings are equally legitimate. We can state it precisely. Let E₁ and E₂ be two different meanings of the existential quantifier, such that:

- In language L₁ using E₁, "there are chairs" is true.
- In language L₂ using E₂, "there are no chairs (only particles arranged chair-wise)" is true.

Quantifier variance (QV) then holds:
- QV1: E₁ and E₂ are genuinely different semantic values for the existential quantifier.
- QV2: Both are equally legitimate — neither captures a more fundamental or correct notion of existence.
- QV3: L₁ and L₂ can express all the same facts about the world — they are intertranslatable in a strong sense.

QV implies that the universalist and nihilist are, when each uses the quantifier in her own way, asserting different things rather than disagreeing about the same fact.

## Hirsch's Arguments

Hirsch's key argument is that we can easily construct coherent languages in which the quantifier functions differently. In *Nihilese*, "∃x" ranges only over simple, non-composite objects; complex entities do not fall under it. A Nihilese speaker who says "there is no chair" is not disagreeing with the English speaker who says "there is a chair," because their quantifiers mean different things. In *Universalese*, "∃x" ranges over every possible mereological sum, including the mereological sum of the Eiffel Tower and the number seven. These languages are coherent and have the same expressive power as English — they can communicate everything the English speaker can communicate, just in different vocabulary. Since quantifier meaning is not fixed by logic, it can vary.

Hirsch also deploys a principle of charity. When we interpret a speaker's language, we should, all else equal, interpret her so that her assertions come out true. Applied to the mereological debate: both the universalist and the nihilist sincerely assert what seem like true propositions about the world given their concepts. The best interpretation of each that makes their assertions mostly true assigns different meanings to "exists." Charity thus supports QV.

A related argument draws on Putnam's model-theoretic considerations. If a theory T is true under a model M, then T is also true under a permuted model M* (which reassigns entities by a permutation of the domain), because T's sentences are insensitive to which objects play which roles so long as structural relations are preserved. The quantifier can range over different domains without changing the theory's truth value, provided the structural relations among domain elements are preserved. This suggests that the quantifier has no fixed, unique extension — it can be reinterpreted across different but structurally equivalent domains.

## Implications and Stakes

If QV is correct, the deflationary implications are profound. The dispute between mereological universalists and nihilists is merely verbal; the dispute between Platonists and nominalists about mathematical objects may be merely verbal; the debate about temporal parts may be merely verbal. Metaphysics as a discipline seeking determinate, mind-independent facts about existence is misconceived.

If QV is incorrect — if there is a natural or structural notion of the existential quantifier — then ontological debates can be genuinely substantive. There are correct and incorrect answers to questions about what exists, and metaphysics can in principle settle them.

The debate about QV is thus not a narrow technical dispute about semantics. It is a debate about the legitimacy of metaphysics as a discipline. Notice that the deflationary and inflationary positions are not merely mirror images of each other: the deflationist needs only to show that the quantifier can vary without loss; the inflationist needs to identify a principled, non-conventional basis for selecting one quantifier meaning as privileged. That positive task — showing what makes one quantifier more structural or more natural than another — is the real burden of the inflationary response, and it is where the most interesting contemporary work is being done.
