# Implications of Quantifier Variance for Metaphysical Debates

If quantifier variance is correct, many classical debates in ontology would turn out to be merely verbal — not disagreements about how the world is, but disagreements generated by different uses of "exists." The mereological debate, the Platonism/nominalism debate, the debate about universals: each would be, at least in part, a terminological fog. This section works through what QV analysis actually looks like applied to specific debates, and what its limits are.

Consider the mereological debate first. The universalist holds that any plurality of objects composes a further object; the nihilist holds that only simple, non-composite objects exist. Hirsch's QV analysis: in the universalist's language, "∃x" ranges over all mereological sums; in the nihilist's language, "∃x" ranges only over simples. The universalist says "the chair exists"; the nihilist says "there are particles arranged chair-wise." Both are making true statements — they are translations of the same fact into different quantifier languages. If this analysis is correct, the dispute is merely verbal: neither party is wrong; they are simply using "exists" differently, and the apparent metaphysical disagreement is a linguistic misunderstanding.

The key evaluative question is whether the nihilist can *really* express everything the universalist can express using only simples and arranged-predications. Sider argues that the nihilist's paraphrase language cannot capture causal and modal claims about composites without implicitly quantifying over composites. "The chair caused the foot to be bruised" seems to require the chair as a causal relatum — not merely that particles arranged chair-wise did something. If causal and modal claims resist paraphrase into purely atomic terms, the nihilist's language is less expressive than the universalist's, and the dispute is not merely verbal.

A similar analysis applies to mathematical ontology. Platonists hold that numbers, sets, and functions are abstract objects existing independently of minds; nominalists deny this. QV analysis: the Platonist uses "exists" in a sense that includes abstract objects; the nominalist uses it in a sense restricted to concrete objects. Both can express arithmetical facts — the Platonist uses "there are prime numbers greater than 100" while the nominalist uses a paraphrase that does not quantify over numbers. If this analysis is correct, the debate is merely verbal.

But the stakes here are high. If the debate is merely verbal, then the substantial apparatus developed by neo-logicists, fictionalists, and nominalists to settle the ontological question is wasted effort. Sider's objection cuts here too: the nominalist's paraphrase of mathematical claims cannot avoid quantifying over *something* — if not numbers, then possible worlds (for modal fictionalism), proofs, or story-elements (for fictionalism). Whether these alternatives are preferable to Platonism is not a merely verbal question.

The debate about universals and nominalism exhibits the same structure with a distinctive wrinkle. The universalist (Armstrong) holds that properties are abstract entities — universals — shared by multiple individuals; the nominalist (Lewis) holds that there are no universals, only particular individuals and their resemblances. QV analysis: the universalist's "exists" ranges over universals; the nominalist's does not. Both can express every fact about property attribution and resemblance — the universalist says "redness is a universal instantiated by fire trucks and roses"; the nominalist says "fire trucks and roses resemble each other in a certain respect."

The difficulty for the QV analysis in this case is particularly revealing. The universalist claims that universals are needed to *explain* why fire trucks and roses are both red — what their resemblance *consists in*. The nominalist claims that resemblance is primitive and does not require explanation by universals. This is an explanatory disagreement, not merely a disagreement about quantifier meaning. It is a disagreement about what is explanatorily fundamental — and that is not obviously a verbal matter.

## The Philosophical Stakes

If QV is correct across the board, the conclusion is that many central debates in analytic metaphysics are pseudo-disputes, resolvable by recognizing an equivocation on "exists." Metaphysics as a discipline aiming at substantive truths about what there is would need to be abandoned or radically reconceived. The right approach to ontology would be pragmatic: adopt whichever quantifier language is most useful for your purposes.

If QV is incorrect — if there is a fundamental, non-variable notion of existence — then these debates remain genuine disagreements about facts, and metaphysics can in principle settle them by inquiry into the fundamental structure of reality. The pragmatic stance toward ontology is then inadequate: correctness, not usefulness, is the standard.

The debate about QV is therefore a debate about whether metaphysics is a genuine discipline or a sophisticated form of conceptual engineering. This is the most fundamental question in metaontology, and the responses examined in the next section — Sider's structural quantifier, Fine's reality operator — attempt to show that the inflationary position has the resources to resist the QV challenge.
