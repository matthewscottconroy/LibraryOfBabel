# Responses to Quantifier Variance

Several responses to quantifier variance defend the substantive character of ontological debates, approaching the problem from different angles. What the best responses share is a positive claim: not merely that QV is wrong, but that there is something in the nature of reality — structure, or the distinctive notion of being "really real" — that makes one use of the existential quantifier privileged over others. Without a positive story of this kind, the inflationary position is merely an assertion of disagreement with Hirsch, not a reason to prefer inflationism.

The most developed response is Sider's, developed in *Writing the Book of the World* (2011). Sider argues that logical notions — including the existential quantifier — can be more or less *structural*: they can carve reality at its joints or cut it arbitrarily. The joint-carving quantifier tracks a genuine feature of the world — existence as a real feature — while non-joint-carving quantifiers impose arbitrary categories:

- P1: Some predicates carve nature at its joints (e.g., "electron," "mass") and some do not (e.g., "grue," "schmelctron").
- P2: The best explanation of why joint-carving predicates figure in laws and support reliable inference is that they correspond to genuine features of the world.
- P3: The same distinction applies to logical notions, including the quantifier: some quantifiers are more structural than others.
- P4: The natural quantifier corresponds to the genuine structure of existence as a feature of the world.
- C: There is a unique, joint-carving existential quantifier, and questions asked in it have determinate metaphysical answers.

Sider's response requires that "structure" is itself a genuine, non-conventional feature of the world. He accepts this: structure is a primitive fundamental notion, not analyzable further. Quantifier variance would require that even "structure" is subject to variance — but Sider argues this is implausible because structure is already the most fundamental notion, with nothing more fundamental in terms of which it could vary.

## Fine's Reality Response

Kit Fine (2009) offers a different approach through his distinction between things that merely "exist" and things that are "in reality." Fine argues that even granting quantifier variance, there is a notion of being *really real* — of being part of the fundamental furniture of the world — that is not subject to variance. Hirsch's quantifier variance allows that both the universalist and nihilist can truly assert "chairs exist" (in their respective senses). But it does not show that chairs are part of reality in Fine's constitutive sense — only that both can truly assert "chairs exist." The substantive question is not "do chairs exist?" but "are chairs part of reality?" — and this question is not subject to quantifier variance because Fine's "reality" operator is not the same as "∃x."

This response may look like a verbal maneuver — substituting "reality" for "exists" and declaring the new notion immune to variance. Fine's answer is that "reality" is a primitive notion with distinct theoretical role and that its irreducibility to existence-talk is precisely the point. Whether this primitive notion is genuinely coherent is a fair challenge, and the debate about it remains open.

## Semantic Constraints and Logical Form

Hale and Wright argue from the neo-Fregean perspective that there are principled constraints on legitimate existential quantifiers. Any genuine existential quantifier must satisfy certain logical conditions: existential generalization (from "Fa" we can infer "∃xFx"), instantiation (from "∃xFx" and "∀x(Fx → Gx)" we can conclude "∃xGx"), and correct commutation with negation, conjunction, and other logical operators. Hale and Wright argue that these constraints severely limit the possible meanings of "∃x": any two logically equivalent expressions of existence must have the same meaning. If this is right, quantifier variance collapses — there is only one logical notion of existence, modulo the logical constraints, and it cannot vary across linguistic communities.

This response is technical and depends on a contested view of the relationship between logical form and semantic content. Hirsch can respond that the logical constraints do not uniquely fix a single quantifier meaning — they constrain the shape of quantifier meanings while allowing multiple distinct meanings to satisfy the constraints. The debate here is genuinely difficult.

## The Broader Picture

Locating the debate on a map: Carnap and Hirsch hold that existence questions are not univocal and metaphysically substantive — they are either framework-relative or subject to meaning variance. Quine and Sider hold that there is a genuine, univocal notion of existence — determined by the structure of the world (Sider) or by our total scientific theory (Quine) — and that questions about what falls under it are substantive.

The Carnap-Hirsch position is epistemically modest and consonant with verificationist intuitions: claims beyond all possible evidence are suspect. The Quine-Sider position is epistemically ambitious: there is a fact of the matter about existence that careful philosophical investigation can track. Both positions are internally coherent. The debate between them is, at bottom, a debate about the metaphysics of structure: is there a genuine feature of the world that corresponds to existence in a way that distinguishes one quantifier meaning from all others? Answering this question requires the apparatus of grounding and fundamentality developed in the next section — and so the debate about quantifier variance connects deeply to the broader project of contemporary metaphysics, rather than being a merely semantic sideshow.
