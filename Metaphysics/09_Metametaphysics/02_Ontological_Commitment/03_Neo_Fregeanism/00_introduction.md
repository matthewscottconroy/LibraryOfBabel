# Neo-Fregeanism and Ontological Commitment

Neo-Fregeanism — associated with Crispin Wright and Bob Hale — is a program in the philosophy of mathematics and ontology that attempts to recover a substantial form of Platonism about abstract objects through a logicist strategy inspired by Frege. The central innovation is the use of abstraction principles — biconditionals that introduce reference to abstract objects on the basis of an equivalence relation among more familiar items — to secure ontological commitments without the problematic Fregean comprehension principle that Russell's paradox refuted.

The simplest abstraction principle is Hume's Principle: the number of Fs equals the number of Gs if and only if there is a one-to-one correspondence between the Fs and the Gs. From this principle, together with second-order logic, it is possible to derive the Peano axioms of arithmetic. The neo-Fregean claims that this derivation establishes the existence of natural numbers: numbers are abstract objects introduced by Hume's Principle, and arithmetic is analytic in a sense that Frege's original logicism was supposed to establish but could not, given Russell's paradox.

The metaontological significance of neo-Fregeanism is that it challenges the Quinean picture of ontological commitment. On the Quinean picture, we incur ontological commitments by quantifying over entities in our best theories; the existence of mathematical objects follows from the indispensability of mathematics in science. Neo-Fregeanism offers a different route: we can secure ontological commitment to abstract objects through abstraction principles that are, in some sense, a priori and conceptually guaranteed, without relying on the empirical indispensability of mathematics in physics.

Critics of neo-Fregeanism have pressed the "Bad Company" objection: not all abstraction principles are acceptable — some generate contradictions — and it is not clear how to distinguish the acceptable from the unacceptable principles without already having an ontological theory in the background.
