# Neo-Fregean Abstracta

How do we come to know that numbers exist? The empiricist answer — through observation — seems clearly wrong, since numbers are not the kind of thing one observes. The rationalist answer — through some special faculty of mathematical intuition — seems mysterious. Neo-Fregeanism, developed by Crispin Wright and Bob Hale, offers a third path: we know numbers exist because abstract objects are introduced by a special kind of semantic principle, the abstraction principle, which is knowable a priori and from which the existence of numbers follows directly.

The paradigm is *Hume's Principle* (HP): the number of Fs = the number of Gs if and only if the Fs and the Gs are in one-one correspondence. HP introduces numbers as objects: the left side of HP involves a singular term ("the number of Fs"), and if HP is true and its right side can be established, we are licensed to infer the existence of numbers. Wright argues that HP, understood as an implicit definition of the concept of number, is epistemically innocent — it can be known a priori, and its acceptance does not require prior knowledge of numbers. Once HP is accepted, the existence of numbers follows: since every concept is one-one correlated with itself, the number of self-identical things exists.

The program extends to other abstracta. Directions can be introduced by the principle that the direction of line l = the direction of line m iff l is parallel to m. The general form — an abstraction principle — is:

α(Φ) = α(Ψ) ↔ Φ ~ Ψ

where α is an abstraction operator, Φ and Ψ range over first-level entities (concepts or objects), and ~ is an equivalence relation. The left side introduces a singular term referring to an abstract object; the right side specifies the identity condition for that object. Wright's claim is that abstraction principles are epistemically innocent if they are (1) logically consistent, (2) conservative over independently characterized entities, and (3) provide genuine criteria of identity for the objects they introduce. If these conditions are met, accepting the principle does not require independent evidence for the abstracta — their existence is guaranteed by the coherence of the principle itself.

## The Metaontological Significance

Neo-Fregeanism challenges both the Quinean and Carnapian frameworks in distinctive ways. Against the Quinean: Quine holds that ontological commitment is determined by quantification in canonical theories, and that we are committed to numbers only if our best theories indispensably quantify over them (the indispensability argument). Neo-Fregeanism offers a different route: we are committed to numbers because abstraction principles are conceptually or analytically true, and they introduce numbers as objects. This is a criterion of ontological commitment based on singular terms and implicit definition rather than indispensability.

Against the Carnapian: Carnap holds that "do numbers exist?" is a merely practical question about which linguistic framework to adopt. Neo-Fregeanism holds that the existence of numbers is established by the analytic truth of Hume's Principle — not a pragmatic choice but a substantive claim certified by the logic of the concept of number.

The program connects interestingly to grounding. One might ask: what grounds the existence of the number 2? On the neo-Fregean view, it is grounded in (a) the truth of Hume's Principle as a conceptual truth, and (b) the fact that there exist concepts F and G that are equinumerous. The number 2 is "nothing over and above" the fact that there are pairs of equinumerous concepts — its existence is conceptually derived from this fact. Fine (2002) has raised doubts about whether abstraction principles genuinely ground the existence of abstract objects or merely provide a criterion for recognizing their existence. If HP is merely a principle for recognizing which numbers are identical, and numbers exist independently of HP, then HP does not ground their existence. If HP is constitutive of what numbers are, then it does. The distinction maps onto the difference between constitutive and evidential grounding.

## The Caesar Problem

The Caesar problem, noted by Frege himself, is one of the most persistent objections to neo-Fregeanism. Hume's Principle tells us that the number of Fs equals the number of Gs when the Fs and Gs are equinumerous. But HP does not tell us whether Julius Caesar is or is not identical to some number. It does not rule out "Julius Caesar = 0."

This is a problem because a successful introduction of a new kind of object should fully determine the identity conditions of objects of that kind — including their distinctness from objects of other kinds. If HP does not determine whether Caesar is a number, it has not fully introduced numbers as a determinate kind.

Several responses have been proposed. The *sortal response* (Hale): numbers and persons belong to different sortal categories, and cross-sortal identity claims are undefined or false. "Is Caesar a number?" is a category mistake like "is redness loud?" The *structural response* (Wright): numbers are introduced as having a specific role in the number sequence, and Caesar does not play that role. The Caesar problem illustrates a general challenge for implicit definition: an implicit definition secures the existence of an abstract kind only if it fully determines the identity conditions of members of that kind, including negative identity claims. Achieving this without already presupposing independent knowledge of the entities is difficult — and this difficulty shadows the neo-Fregean program throughout.
