# Hume's Principle

Hume's Principle (HP) states: the number of Fs = the number of Gs if and only if the Fs and the Gs are equinumerous — in one-one correspondence. The name comes from Hume's observation in the *Treatise* that when comparing numbers, what we check for is bijection. Frege used HP as a step in his derivation of arithmetic, and neo-Fregeans have placed it at the center of their program.

HP is an *abstraction principle*: it introduces a new kind of object (numbers) by specifying an identity condition for them (equinumerosity of the corresponding concepts). The right-hand side of HP involves only second-order logic and the notion of equinumerosity; the left-hand side introduces numerical terms as genuine singular terms. HP thus "abstracts" numbers from concepts via the equivalence relation of equinumerosity.

The central claim of neo-Fregeanism is that HP is analytic — or at least knowable a priori — and that from HP alone (together with second-order logic) one can derive the Peano-Dedekind axioms of arithmetic. This result, known as *Frege's Theorem*, is mathematically robust:

1. ∃x(Nx ∧ ¬∃y(Sy = x)) — there is a natural number with no predecessor (zero exists).
2. ∀x(Nx → ∃y(Sy = x ∧ Ny)) — every natural number has a successor.
3. ∀x∀y(Nx ∧ Ny ∧ Sx = Sy → x = y) — the successor function is injective.
4. ∀x(Nx → Sx ≠ 0) — zero is not the successor of any natural number.
5. ∀F(F0 ∧ ∀x(Nx ∧ Fx → FSx) → ∀x(Nx → Fx)) — mathematical induction.

The derivation proceeds by defining 0 as the number of Fs where F is the concept "is not identical to itself" (an empty concept), defining "n precedes m" in terms of concepts and their numerical values, and showing that the natural numbers are the objects reachable from 0 by the ancestral closure of the predecessor relation. If we accept HP as true (or as analytically true), we get all of arithmetic for free — without additional mathematical axioms. HP thus plays the role of a single "definition" of number from which everything else follows.

## The Analyticity Claim

Wright's claim that HP is analytic is the load-bearing philosophical claim, and it is controversial. Several positions have been staked out. The *strong analyticity* view (Wright, early work): HP is true in virtue of meaning — it is part of what we mean by "number" that numbers are equinumerous iff there is a bijection between them. The *implicit definition* view (Wright, later work): HP need not be analytic in the sense of being guaranteed by meanings we already had; rather, it functions as an implicit definition that introduces the concept of number, and accepting it is a priori. The *a priori but not analytic* view (Hale): HP might be knowable without empirical investigation without being analytic in any strong sense. Field's objection: HP is a substantive claim about the existence of abstract objects, and it is not obviously a priori at all. The neo-Fregean assumption that HP is epistemically innocent is question-begging.

The analyticity debate matters because the program's claim to secure a priori knowledge of mathematical existence rests on it. If HP is not a priori, arithmetic is not a priori — and the neo-Fregean alternative to both empiricism and Platonism fails.

## The Bootstrapping Worry and its Responses

A deep worry about HP as an implicit definition: implicit definitions seem to "create" their objects by decree rather than describe independently existing ones. When we define "number" via HP, are we stipulating that numbers exist, or recognizing that they do? If accepting HP is sufficient to guarantee the existence of numbers, then existence is too easy to achieve — we could, by the same method, introduce an infinite variety of abstract objects simply by writing down abstraction principles.

Several responses have been developed. The *content view* (Wright): Accepting HP does not create numbers — it explains what it would take for numbers to exist. If the right-hand side of HP can be satisfied (if there are equinumerous concepts), then numbers must exist — but this is a consequence of what numbers are, not a decree. The *deflationism response* (Schiffer): Mathematical existence is "deflationary" in a way that ordinary existence is not. Saying "numbers exist" is different from saying "electrons exist"; the former is a conceptual matter, the latter is empirical. The bootstrapping worry dissolves if we recognize that mathematical existence is not heavyweight. The *ontological humility response* (Fine): The neo-Fregean should not claim that HP grounds the existence of numbers — that goes too far. Rather, HP provides an account of what it is for numbers to exist; whether they do exist must be settled independently. This more modest reading reduces the bootstrapping worry but weakens the program.

## Connection to Metaontology

The neo-Fregean program is significant for metaontology because it offers a criterion of ontological commitment that is neither Quinean (indispensability in best theories) nor Carnapian (pragmatic framework adoption). It proposes that singular terms in analytic truths carry ontological commitment: if HP is analytic and contains the singular term "the number of Fs," then numbers exist. This criterion implies a distinctive view of the relationship between language and ontology — the structure of our conceptual vocabulary, when governed by analytic or quasi-analytic principles, reveals the structure of reality. This "linguistic route to ontology" has been influential and contested in equal measure, and its fate depends substantially on whether the bad company objection can be answered.
