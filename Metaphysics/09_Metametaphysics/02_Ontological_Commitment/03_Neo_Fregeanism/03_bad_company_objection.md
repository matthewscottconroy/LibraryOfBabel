# The Bad Company Objection

Hume's Principle looks like a model of how to introduce abstract objects without mysterious epistemic apparatus. But consider Frege's Basic Law V (BLV): the extension of concept F = the extension of concept G iff for all x, Fx iff Gx. BLV looks structurally just like HP — it is an abstraction principle with an equivalence relation on the right-hand side. Yet BLV is inconsistent: Russell's Paradox follows from it in any domain with more than one element. If the neo-Fregean program justifies HP by appeal to its structural features as an abstraction principle, why doesn't the same justification apply to BLV?

This is the *bad company objection*. Not all abstraction principles of the form of HP are acceptable: some are inconsistent, some are mutually inconsistent with HP itself, some are "inflationary" in objectionable ways. The neo-Fregean program needs a principled criterion of acceptability that admits HP but excludes the bad companions. Without such a criterion, HP is merely in better-looking company than BLV, not in a different logical category.

The objection can be stated as a reductio:

- P1: The neo-Fregean holds that HP is legitimate because it has the form of an abstraction principle with an equivalence relation on the right-hand side.
- P2: BLV also has the form of an abstraction principle with an equivalence relation on the right-hand side.
- P3: But BLV is inconsistent — it leads to Russell's Paradox.
- P4: Therefore, the structural form of an abstraction principle is not sufficient to guarantee its acceptability.
- C: The neo-Fregean must provide an additional criterion that distinguishes HP from BLV.

The force of the objection is that whatever criterion is proposed risks being either question-begging (it already presupposes that numbers exist in the way HP says, which is what HP is supposed to establish) or insufficient (it leaves open too many other bad companions).

## The Proposed Criteria

The most natural response is the *consistency criterion*: an abstraction principle is acceptable iff it is logically satisfiable. HP is consistent; BLV is not. But Cook's "Nuisance" principle — a consistent abstraction principle — is mutually inconsistent with HP over infinite domains. HP and Nuisance together generate a contradiction. If consistency alone is the criterion, both HP and Nuisance are acceptable individually, but they cannot be jointly accepted. The consistency criterion is necessary but not sufficient.

The *conservativeness criterion* was first used by Field (1980) and developed by Wright and Hale. An abstraction principle is acceptable iff it is conservative — it does not entail new truths about objects not mentioned in the principle. HP is conservative: adding HP to a theory about non-numerical entities does not generate new theorems about those entities. But Weir (2003) argues that even conservative abstraction principles can introduce entities in an "inflationary" way, generating more objects than the base relation can sustain.

Fine (2002) proposes the *stability criterion*: an abstraction principle A is acceptable iff there is a set of acceptable abstraction principles including A that is collectively consistent. HP passes: it can be included in a consistent set including the direction abstraction and others. BLV fails: it cannot be included in any consistent set because it is inconsistent by itself. This criterion is more discriminating than mere consistency, since it requires compatibility with other acceptable principles. The circularity worry — that "acceptable" must be identified independently of stability — Fine addresses by giving a recursive characterization: a principle is stable if included in a maximal consistent set of abstraction principles, where maximality is determined by an ordering of principles by strength.

A different approach focuses on the character of the equivalence relation itself. HP's equivalence relation (equinumerosity) is well-behaved: a genuine logical relation over concepts, definable in second-order logic. BLV's equivalence relation (coextensionality) is also definable in second-order logic, but it generates inconsistency because the domain of concepts has "too many" members relative to the domain of objects. Boolos (1987) suggested that BLV fails because the equivalence class of the concept "being non-self-membered" — at the heart of Russell's Paradox — has no object to correspond to. This points toward a structural feature of acceptable abstraction principles: the equivalence relation must "line up" with the objects it introduces in a way that does not require more objects than the domain can sustain. Making this criterion precise without circularity is an ongoing challenge.

## The Metaontological Significance

The bad company objection is not merely a technical problem. It raises a fundamental metaontological question: what makes an existence-generating principle legitimate? The neo-Fregean wants to say that HP is legitimate because it is analytically or conceptually true. But BLV is just as "analytic-looking" as HP, yet is inconsistent. This suggests that the legitimacy of existence-generating principles cannot be settled by their logical form alone. Some extra-logical criterion — consistency, conservativeness, stability — must be invoked.

But invoking such criteria takes us outside pure conceptual analysis into considerations of mathematical ontology: what structures are possible, what the domain of objects can sustain. This means the neo-Fregean program cannot be purely a priori — it requires substantive assumptions about the mathematical universe that are not themselves conceptually guaranteed. The bad company problem thus reveals that the "linguistic route to ontology" is not self-contained. The legitimacy of abstraction principles depends on features of the ontological domain that cannot be read off from the principles themselves. Defenders of neo-Fregeanism argue this is a limitation that can be managed rather than a fatal problem; critics argue it collapses the neo-Fregean alternative into something that looks more like old-fashioned Platonism than its proponents want to admit.
