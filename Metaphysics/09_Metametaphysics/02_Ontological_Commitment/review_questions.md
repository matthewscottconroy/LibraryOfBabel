# Review Questions — Ontological Commitment

1. Quine's criterion says a theory is committed to whatever its bound variables must range over in canonical logical formulation. Two problems press on this from opposite directions: the indeterminacy of reference threatens to make the criterion yield indeterminate results, while proxy function arguments show that empirically equivalent theories can differ in their commitments. Do these problems reinforce each other, or do they require different responses?

2. The "no entity without identity" constraint drives Quine to accept sets but resist properties, because properties lack determinate identity conditions (is the property of being triangular identical to the property of being trilateral?). Is this constraint itself part of Quine's general naturalism, or is it an independent and potentially contestable requirement? What would happen to the Quinean framework if the constraint were dropped?

3. Hirsch's quantifier variance thesis implies that disputes about mereological composition, mathematical Platonism, and temporal parts may be merely verbal — not disagreements about the world but about how to use "exists." The chapter argues Sider's response is that there is a joint-carving existential quantifier. Assess this debate: what positive evidence is there that one quantifier meaning is more "natural" than others, and what would it mean for that evidence to be persuasive without being question-begging?

4. The neo-Fregean program claims that Hume's Principle — which states that the number of Fs equals the number of Gs iff there is a bijection between them — is analytic or a priori, and that this is sufficient to establish the existence of numbers. Quine's indispensability argument reaches the same conclusion (mathematical objects exist) but by a completely different route (indispensability to best theories). Evaluate which route is stronger. What does each argument concede that the other does not?

5. The bad company objection shows that not every structurally similar abstraction principle is acceptable — Basic Law V is inconsistent. The proposed remedies (consistency, conservativeness, stability) each face objections. Does the bad company problem show that the neo-Fregean "linguistic route to ontology" cannot be purely a priori, or only that we need a more careful account of which abstraction principles are analytic?

6. Quantifier variance appears to make ontological debates "merely verbal" — but the chapter shows that the universalist/nominalist debate about properties resists this deflationary treatment because the two sides disagree about what is *explanatorily fundamental*. How does this case reveal the limits of the QV analysis? Does it suggest that ontological debates always have a residual explanatory component that survives even after the quantifier meaning is fixed?

7. The Quinean supplement provided by grounding separates the existence question ("what entities exist?") from the fundamentality question ("which entities are fundamental?"). If both questions require independent answers, does this mean that Quine's original framework is not just incomplete but positively misleading — because accepting everything as equally real once it passes the ontological commitment test obscures the most important metaphysical distinctions?

8. The chapter traces a dialectic from Carnap through Quine, Sider, Fine, and Thomasson, each responding to the last. If you had to identify a single unresolved tension running through all these positions, what would it be? Consider: is it possible to have both a determinate criterion of ontological commitment and a principled account of which commitments are substantive, without either collapsing into deflationism or helping yourself to primitive notions whose coherence is exactly what is at issue?
