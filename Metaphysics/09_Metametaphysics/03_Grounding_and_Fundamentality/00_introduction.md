# Grounding and Fundamentality

Even if we set aside questions about whether certain entities exist and agree that both mental properties and physical properties are real, there remains a further question: which of these is more fundamental? Which is primary, which is derived? The mental is said to be grounded in the physical: there is no mental difference without a physical difference; mental facts hold in virtue of physical facts; the physical is ontologically prior. But what is this "in virtue of" relation? What is grounding?

Grounding is a relation of metaphysical dependence — not causal dependence (one event producing another) but constitutive dependence (one fact making another fact hold). The truth that Socrates's singleton set exists is grounded in the truth that Socrates exists: not because the latter causes the former, but because the latter is what makes the former obtain. The moral fact that an action is wrong is grounded in natural facts about harm and preference. The biological fact that an organism is a heart is grounded in facts about its evolutionary history and function. These grounds-groundee relations are asymmetric: the ground is more fundamental than what it grounds.

The concept of grounding has become central to contemporary metaphysics because it provides a framework for articulating what we mean when we say that one kind of fact is "nothing but" another kind, or that one level of reality is "built from" another. This is more precise than the vague notions of reduction and supervenience that previously served these roles, and it preserves the intuition that the mental is genuinely distinct from the physical (contra reductionism) while also being derivative from it (contra dualism).

Fundamentality — what is at the most basic level, ungrounded in anything further — is the other side of the grounding relation. Whether there is a fundamental level at all (physicalism's hope), whether the grounding hierarchy is well-founded (whether every chain of grounds terminates), and what it would mean for something to be fundamental are questions that this chapter takes up in detail. Grounding and fundamentality together provide the conceptual framework for understanding what it means to say that reality has layers and structure.
