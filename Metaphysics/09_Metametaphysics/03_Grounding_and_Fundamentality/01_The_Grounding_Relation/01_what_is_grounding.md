# What Is Grounding?

We speak naturally of some facts depending on others: mental facts depend on physical facts; moral facts depend on natural facts; the existence of a set depends on the existence of its members. But what exactly is this dependence? It is not causal — no event brings the mental into being from the physical; the mental is constituted by the physical, not produced by it over time. It is not mere logical entailment — two theories might entail each other while standing in no dependence relation. And it is not simply supervenience — the fact that no mental difference occurs without a physical difference tells us nothing about *why* the mental is as it is, only that it covaries with the physical.

The concept of *grounding* was developed in contemporary analytic metaphysics to capture this explanatory dependence precisely. When A grounds B, A metaphysically explains why B obtains, and B is in some sense less fundamental or dependent on A. The concept has deep roots — Aristotle's substance as that on which other things depend, Leibniz's hierarchy grounded ultimately in God, Kant's conditions of possibility are all implicitly grounding talk — but it became a central explicit topic only in the early 2000s, through work by Kit Fine, Jonathan Schaffer, Gideon Rosen, and others.

Paradigm examples: the properties of a mental event are grounded in the physical properties of the neural event that realizes it; the truth of "there is a table here" is grounded in facts about the arrangement of physical particles; the moral wrongness of an act is grounded in its natural features; mathematical truths are grounded in the axioms of set theory. In each case, the grounding facts are more fundamental, the grounded facts are derivative, and the relation is one of metaphysical explanation rather than causal production.

## Formal Notation and the Structure of Grounding Claims

Following Fine (2012) and Rosen (2010), grounding claims are expressed in several notations:

- *Operational notation*: Γ < A (the facts in Γ ground the fact A), where "<" is the strict grounding operator.
- *Predicate notation*: GRND(A, B) or "A grounds B."
- *Fine's "in virtue of"*: A obtains in virtue of B.
- *Correia's notation*: B ≺ A (B is grounded in A).

Grounding can be *full* or *partial*: A fully grounds B if A is sufficient by itself to ground B; A partially grounds B if A is among the grounds of B but not sufficient alone. It can be *immediate* or *mediate*: A immediately grounds B if there is no intermediate fact C such that A grounds C and C grounds B; A mediately grounds B via C if the chain goes through C. Immediate grounding is directly explanatory; mediate grounding explains through a chain.

Most accounts attribute four formal properties to grounding. It is *irreflexive* (nothing grounds itself), *asymmetric* (if A grounds B, B does not ground A), *transitive* (if A grounds B and B grounds C, then A grounds C), and *necessitating* — though this last is debated: many hold that if A grounds B, then necessarily, if A obtains, B obtains. In any system where grounding is irreflexive, asymmetric, and transitive, grounding generates a strict partial order on facts — a hierarchy with no loops and no reflexive edges. Whether this hierarchy has minimal elements — fundamental facts at the bottom — is the question of foundationalism versus infinitism about grounding, taken up in Section 3.2.

## Distinguishing Grounding from Supervenience

The distinction from supervenience is particularly important. Supervenience is a *modal* claim: the mental supervenes on the physical iff there is no possible world in which two situations are physically identical but mentally different. Grounding is an *explanatory* claim: the mental is grounded in the physical iff facts about physical configuration explain why mental facts hold.

Supervenience without grounding is possible. The mental might supervene on the physical even if neither grounds the other — if both are grounded in a common cause, or if the relationship is merely nomological rather than constitutive. Mathematical truths supervene on everything (since they are necessary), but it is not clear that everything grounds mathematical truths. More importantly: grounding is supposed to *explain* supervenience. When we say the mental supervenes on the physical, we leave open *why* — is it because of identity, reduction, or some weaker explanatory dependence? Grounding answers: the physical grounds the mental, and this grounding relation is what makes the supervenience hold. Rosen (2010) and Schaffer (2009) both use grounding to replace or supplement supervenience as the central notion of dependence in metaphysics, on the grounds that supervenience is too weak and too modal to carry the explanatory load.

## The Hierarchical Picture

Schaffer (2009) argues that grounding's most important feature is that it generates a hierarchical picture of reality. Reality has a layered structure: fundamental physical facts at the bottom; chemical facts grounded in them; biological facts grounded in chemical facts; psychological facts grounded in biological facts; social facts grounded in psychological facts. This hierarchy is presupposed by every first-order metaphysical debate in this textbook. The metaphysics of causation asks whether causal facts are fundamental or grounded in regularities. The metaphysics of modality asks whether modal facts are fundamental or grounded in essences. The philosophy of mind asks whether mental facts are fundamental or grounded in physical facts. Grounding is the conceptual glue holding the hierarchical picture together.

Fine (2012) argues that grounding is a *primitive* concept — it cannot be analyzed in terms of causation, supervenience, or necessity. It is a sui generis explanatory relation at the heart of metaphysics. Whether this primitivism is defensible, or whether grounding can be analyzed in more familiar terms, is a central open question — and one we return to in Section 3.3, where the connection between grounding and essence is examined.
