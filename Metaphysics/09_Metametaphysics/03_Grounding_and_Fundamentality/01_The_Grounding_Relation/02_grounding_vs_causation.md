# Grounding vs. Causation

Both grounding and causation are explanatory relations, and they are easy to conflate. But they differ in ways that matter enormously for metaphysics. Getting the distinction clear is not merely a taxonomic exercise — it shapes how we understand the relationship between mind and body, between moral and natural facts, and between what exists derivatively and what exists fundamentally.

Causation relates events through time: a cause precedes its effect (or, in limiting cases of simultaneous causation, coincides with it), and the connection is mediated by laws of nature. Causation is contingent: under different laws or initial conditions, the same cause might not produce the same effect. Grounding, by contrast, holds between facts synchronically — it is not a temporal relation. When the mental is grounded in the physical, this is not a claim about how a physical event produces a mental event over time; it is a claim about the constitutive relationship between mental and physical properties *at the same time*. Grounding is also (typically) necessitating and non-contingent: if A grounds B, then necessarily, given A, B obtains. This is unlike causation, which can fail to produce its effect under different circumstances.

The key difference is perhaps the "nothing over and above" locution. When we say the mental event is grounded in the physical event, we imply that the mental facts are *constituted* by physical facts — there is no additional "mental stuff" beyond the physical. Effects, by contrast, are not constituted by their causes: the explosion is not "nothing over and above" the spark; it is a distinct event that the spark brought about. Schaffer (2017) characterizes grounding as *constitutive* explanation in contrast to causal explanation:

- *Causal explanation*: Why does this match burn? Because it was struck against the box. The explanation cites a temporally prior event that produced the explanandum.
- *Constitutive explanation*: Why is this act wrong? Because it is an act of gratuitous cruelty. The explanation cites features that are co-present with the explanandum — the wrongness and the cruelty are simultaneous, not sequential. The wrongness does not follow *from* the cruelty; it *consists in* it.

The formal contrast can be summarized:

| Feature | Causation | Grounding |
|---|---|---|
| Relata | Events/states | Facts/propositions |
| Temporal | Diachronic (typically) | Synchronic |
| Modal | Contingent | Necessitating (typically) |
| Direction | From earlier to later | From more to less fundamental |
| "Nothing over and above" | No | Yes |
| Explanatory | Nomological | Constitutive |

## The Causal Exclusion Problem

The grounding/causation distinction is directly relevant to non-reductive physicalism in the philosophy of mind. The causal exclusion problem (Kim, 1989): if mental events are wholly caused by physical events (causal closure of the physical), and if mental events are identical to physical events, how can mental properties play any causal role? Physical properties seem to "exclude" mental properties from doing any causal work — the physical event does all the causing, leaving no room for the mental properties to contribute.

One response invokes the distinction between causation and grounding. The physical event causes the subsequent event (in the causal sense). The mental properties of that event are grounded in its physical properties (in the constitutive sense). These are different relations: causal efficacy belongs to the physical event qua physical, while grounding explains why the event has mental properties as well. The worry about exclusion applies to causal roles; the grounding relation is not in competition with causation.

Bennett (2003) and others have argued this dissolution is too quick: even if we distinguish the relations, we still need an account of how grounded (mental) properties can make a difference to what happens. If the physical fully causes the effect, the mental properties are epiphenomenal — they do not contribute to causation even if they are "grounded in" the physical. The exclusion problem re-emerges as a problem about grounded properties and their causal relevance. The debate is ongoing, but what is clear is that the grounding/causation distinction is a precondition for even *stating* the exclusion problem precisely.

## Fine's Priority Argument

Fine (2012) offers an argument that grounding has a kind of explanatory priority over causation:

- P1: Causation is a relation between events.
- P2: What counts as an event — what individuates events and their properties — depends on the fundamental structure of reality.
- P3: The fundamental structure of reality is described by grounding claims — which facts are fundamental, which derivative.
- C: Causal relations presuppose grounding relations: which causal relations hold depends on which grounding relations hold.

This does not mean grounding is more *real* than causation. It means grounding is more *fundamental*: grounding claims describe the basic structure of reality that makes causal relations possible. If the physical is fundamental and the mental is grounded in the physical, then causal relations involving mental events are possible because the mental is constituted by the physical — and the constitution is the grounding relation.

The grounding/causation distinction bears on every module in this textbook. In the philosophy of time, grounding asks whether temporal facts are fundamental or grounded in something non-temporal. In the metaphysics of causation itself, grounding asks what makes causal facts true — are they fundamental features of the world, or grounded in regularities, or in categorical properties? In ethics, grounding asks why moral facts hold given natural facts. The distinction is not a local technical matter; it is structurally central to how we understand every domain of reality.
