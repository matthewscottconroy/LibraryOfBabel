# What Is Fundamental?

If we ask what the most fundamental constituents of reality are, the question feels as if it has an answer — somewhere at the bottom of the hierarchy, there must be something that grounds everything else but is not itself grounded in anything. Physics points toward quantum fields, spacetime, or structural features of the quantum world. But the concept of fundamentality is not itself a physical concept, and getting it clear is a philosophical task. This section examines what it means for something to be fundamental, distinguishes several conceptions of fundamentality, and connects the concept to the grounding framework already in place.

The link between fundamentality and grounding is the natural starting point. An entity, fact, or property is fundamental iff there is nothing that grounds it:

*Grounding-based fundamentality* (Rosen, Schaffer): F is fundamental ↔ ¬∃G(G < F).

On this conception, fundamentality is a purely relational notion — it is defined in terms of what F is not grounded in. This has the advantage of precision and connection to the grounding framework, but Bennett (2017) argues it is inadequate: truly fundamental entities should have a *positive* feature (being bedrock) rather than merely a negative one (not being grounded). She proposes an *intrinsic fundamentality* view: fundamental entities have a kind of primacy that is not merely a matter of failing to be grounded in something else.

A third approach is Sider's. Properties are fundamental iff they are "structural" — they carve nature at its joints. A property is structural if it appears in the fundamental laws of nature and if nature's actual distribution of the property is not merely a matter of how we choose to describe things. Sider extends this to all logical and metaphysical vocabulary: the quantifier itself can be more or less structural, and the most structural use of the quantifier tracks what there fundamentally is.

## What Does Physics Tell Us?

Physicalism holds that the fundamental entities are those posited by fundamental physics, and this connects metaphysics to empirical science. As our best physical theories change, so might our view of what is fundamental.

Currently, the main candidates include: *quantum fields* (quantum field theory suggests the fundamental entities are not particles but fields, with particles as excitations); *spacetime* (general relativity treats spacetime as a genuine entity with dynamic properties — curvature, expansion — and matter-energy as a feature of this structure); and *structural features* (ontic structural realism, following Ladyman and Ross, suggests that only relational and structural features of the quantum world are fundamental, with no intrinsic properties at the fundamental level).

The metametaphysical upshot is a naturalist constraint: the concept of fundamentality cannot be fixed entirely independently of physics. What physics identifies as its most basic ontology provides the best current candidate for the fundamental entities. But physics does not by itself tell us what fundamentality *is* — only what instantiates it. The philosophical work of understanding the concept remains.

## Relative and Absolute Fundamentality, and Completeness

Fundamentality can be *absolute* or *relative*. Absolute fundamentality: entity F is fundamental simpliciter — at the very bottom of the grounding hierarchy, with nothing more basic. Relative fundamentality: F is more fundamental than G, meaning F is "closer to" the grounding floor than G. Relative fundamentality allows us to say that chemistry is more fundamental than biology, and physics is more fundamental than chemistry, without committing to the existence of an absolute grounding floor. This is useful for those skeptical of foundationalism about grounding — if there might be infinite grounding chains with no bottom, we can still draw meaningful distinctions among levels.

A related concept is *completeness*: a set of facts is complete if it grounds all other facts. The fundamental facts, if they form a grounding floor, constitute the minimal complete set — the smallest set of facts from which all other facts derive. This connects to physicalist supervenience: physicalism holds that the physical facts form a complete set. Grounding physicalism strengthens this: the physical facts not only modally determine all other facts but also ground them — they constitute the metaphysical explanatory base.

The completeness criterion raises an important question: can there be multiple minimal complete sets, or is there a unique fundamental base? If multiple sets of facts are each complete and no proper subset of either is complete, then the fundamental base may not be unique. This would be a form of groundless fundamentality — or multiple alternative "floors" — and it would complicate the neat hierarchical picture that makes grounding attractive.

The concept of fundamentality structures every first-order metaphysical debate in this textbook. Are universals fundamental entities, or grounded in particulars and their resemblances? Are persons fundamental, or grounded in physical and psychological continuity facts? Are mental properties fundamental (dualism) or grounded in physical properties (physicalism)? Are causal relations fundamental, or grounded in laws and initial conditions? These are all questions about where in the grounding hierarchy the relevant entities or facts sit — and answering them requires both a clear conception of fundamentality and detailed argument about the particular domain.
