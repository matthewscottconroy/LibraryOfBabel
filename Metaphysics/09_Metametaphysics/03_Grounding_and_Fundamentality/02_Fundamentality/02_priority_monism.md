# Priority Monism

The intuitive picture in science and in everyday thought is that complex things are made of simpler things, and understanding the simpler things is how we understand the complex. Particles compose molecules, molecules compose cells, cells compose organisms. Explanation flows upward from parts to wholes, from simpler to more complex. Jonathan Schaffer's priority monism directly challenges this picture. On his view, the direction of metaphysical priority runs the other way: the whole is prior to its parts. The cosmos as a whole is the fundamental entity, and all other entities — galaxies, organisms, molecules, particles — are derivative aspects or parts of the fundamental whole.

Priority monism must be distinguished from *existence* monism, which holds that only one thing exists. Schaffer does not deny that tables, people, and planets exist. He denies that they are *fundamental*. They exist as derivative, dependent parts of the one fundamental concrete object — the cosmos — and everything else is a proper part of it.

The motivations for priority monism are partly conceptual, partly empirical. The thesis of causal closure — that all physical events have sufficient physical causes — suggests that the universe as a whole is a causally closed system, with parts deriving their causal powers from their embedding in the whole. More specifically, quantum entanglement suggests that the properties of subsystems are fundamentally dependent on the states of larger systems. In an entangled quantum system, the joint state of the system cannot be fully described by the states of its parts; the joint state carries information that the individual states do not.

## Schaffer's Argument

The argument for priority monism proceeds in two stages.

*Stage 1: The cosmos as a genuine entity*

- P1: Anything that has intrinsic properties is a genuine entity.
- P2: The cosmos has intrinsic properties (total mass-energy, total entropy, spatiotemporal structure).
- C1: The cosmos is a genuine entity.

*Stage 2: The cosmos is fundamental*

- P3: In any grounding hierarchy, there is either a fundamental ground or an infinite regress.
- P4: An infinite regress of parts grounding wholes — each part grounded in its parts — is vicious: it generates an infinite chain of explanation with nothing explanatorily basic.
- P5: If there is a fundamental ground among concrete objects, it is either the cosmos or atomic simples.
- P6: Quantum mechanics provides evidence that the properties of subsystems are dependent on the states of larger systems, not the reverse.
- C2: The cosmos is the fundamental entity.

P4 is disputed by infinitists about grounding. P6 is an empirical premise whose metaphysical implications are contested; different interpretations of quantum mechanics handle entanglement differently, and whether non-separability implies holism about the fundamental ontology depends on which interpretation is correct. Decoherence explains why macroscopic objects behave as if they have definite states independent of their environments, which may restore a form of local independence at the macroscopic level. The quantum motivation for priority monism is suggestive rather than conclusive.

## Priority Pluralism: The Alternative

Priority pluralism (Sider, Lewis) holds that atomic simples are fundamental, and composite entities are grounded in their parts — the familiar bottom-up picture:

- P1: Scientific explanation proceeds from parts to wholes: we explain complex systems by appeal to their constituents.
- P2: The best explanation of why scientific explanation takes this direction is that parts are more fundamental than wholes.
- P3: If parts are more fundamental, the grounding direction runs from parts to wholes.
- C: Priority pluralism is the correct view.

Schaffer responds by distinguishing the *epistemological* direction of explanation (from simpler to more complex, because we have more direct access to parts) from the *metaphysical* direction of grounding (from fundamental to derivative). Science may explain from parts to wholes for epistemological reasons even if the metaphysical priority runs from wholes to parts. This is a real distinction, but pressing it requires an account of why we should accept the metaphysical direction independently of the epistemological direction — and that account must not simply assume what is at issue.

Some philosophers resist both poles, arguing that the grounding relation does not have a simple linear direction. Fine (2012) suggests the hierarchy may be complex — with some facts at the same level of priority, some questions about priority having no determinate answer. Bennett (2017) argues for "building" as the fundamental notion: building relations (composition, constitution, realization, causation) hold between concrete objects and facts, and the direction of building may vary. In some cases, parts build wholes; in other cases, wholes determine parts; genuine mutual dependence may exist.

Priority monism has implications for every first-order debate. If the cosmos is fundamental, then properties of particular objects are grounded in features of the whole — suggesting a kind of holism about properties, including possibly the essential properties of particular electrons. For causation, priority monism implies that local causal claims are grounded in features of the cosmos as a whole — total states of the universe — rather than in local events. For personal identity, persons are derivative entities grounded in the cosmic whole, which makes local facts about brains or psychological continuity insufficient by themselves for personal identity. These implications are typically treated as costs, and they motivate the search for a view that captures the holistic insights of quantum mechanics without requiring full commitment to the cosmos as the unique fundamental entity.
