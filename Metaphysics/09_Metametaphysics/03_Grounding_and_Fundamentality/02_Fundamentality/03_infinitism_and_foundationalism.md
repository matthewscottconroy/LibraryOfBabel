# Infinitism and Foundationalism About Grounding

Here is a natural thought: grounding chains must terminate somewhere. If every fact is grounded in something more fundamental, and that in something more fundamental still, there must eventually be facts that ground everything else without being grounded in anything — fundamental facts, the bedrock of reality. Without this bedrock, the grounding hierarchy provides no ultimate explanation; it merely defers explanation indefinitely downward. This is the *foundationalist* position about grounding, held implicitly or explicitly by Schaffer (2010), Fine (2001), and Rosen (2010).

The argument for foundationalism parallels the epistemological argument for foundational beliefs:

- P1: Grounding is an explanatory relation: A grounds B means A explains why B obtains.
- P2: Explanations must eventually "bottom out" in unexplained explainers — if every explanation requires a further explanation, explanation is never complete.
- P3: If grounding chains do not terminate, the grounding hierarchy provides no ultimate explanation — it defers explanation indefinitely.
- C: Grounding chains must terminate — foundationalism is true.

But P2 is exactly what the *infinitist* challenges. Ross Cameron (2008) argues that the demand for a terminus is incoherent. If grounding chains do terminate in "brute" fundamental facts, those facts are simply unexplained — the "explanation" the grounding hierarchy provides is merely deferred to the fundamental level and stopped there by fiat. An infinite grounding chain is no worse: it defers explanation at every level, but foundationalism does the same — it just defers to the fundamental level and stops. The infinitist sees no principled reason why stopping is better than continuing.

The infinitist response to the vicious regress objection is worth taking seriously. An infinite causal chain — each event caused by an earlier event — is not vicious merely because it is infinite; each causal step is a genuine explanation, and the infinite chain provides infinite explanation. Similarly, an infinite grounding chain may be fine if each grounding step is genuinely explanatory. The question is whether the demand that explanation be "complete" is a real epistemic requirement or a contingent feature of human cognitive limitations.

## Formal Structures

A formal characterization helps here. A grounding hierarchy is *well-founded* iff every grounding chain has a terminus — there is no infinite descending chain A₁ > A₂ > A₃ > ... where each Aᵢ₊₁ grounds Aᵢ. Foundationalism about grounding holds that the hierarchy is well-founded. Well-foundedness is familiar from set theory: the membership relation on the von Neumann universe is well-founded (every set is reachable from the empty set in finitely many steps). Non-well-founded set theories (allowing "hypersets" that are members of themselves) are coherent but non-standard. Dixon (2016) argues by analogy that non-well-founded grounding hierarchies are coherent — there could be infinite descending chains of grounds — and that this does not generate vicious regress, because infinite explanations can be complete in a relevant sense.

Leibniz famously argued that every composite is made of further composites, with no ultimate simples — a form of infinitism about the grounding of composite facts by their parts. If every composite fact (the fact that this table exists) is grounded in facts about its parts, and every part is itself composite, then there is an infinite regress of grounding. This may not be vicious, but it does leave fundamental physics in an odd position: if there are no ultimate simples, what are the laws of physics about?

## Brute Facts and the Regress Problem

A key challenge for foundationalism: fundamental facts are brute — they have no explanation. This seems epistemically unsatisfying. Why should we accept that the laws of physics, or the existence of simples, or the structure of spacetime, are brute facts with no further explanation?

Several responses are available. The *pragmatic response*: every explanatory framework must start somewhere; "why?" questions terminate at some point by necessity. Fundamental facts are the pragmatically necessary stopping points, not arbitrary brutes. The *self-sufficiency response*: fundamental facts can be self-sufficient in a weak sense — they require no external explanation. This is different from saying they explain themselves (which would violate irreflexivity); it means they require no explanation at all. The *coherentist supplement*: fundamental facts can be partially explained by their coherence with other fundamental facts, even if no single one grounds the others — this adds a structural constraint without introducing circular grounding.

The choice between foundationalism and infinitism about grounding has implications for every first-order debate. In the philosophy of mind: if there are fundamental physical facts, the mental is grounded in them; if there are no fundamental facts, the mind-body relation is part of an infinite regress. In mereology: if simples exist, they are the foundation; if composition always continues, there are no simples. In the metaphysics of causation: if causal relations are grounded in laws, and laws are themselves grounded in something further, where does the grounding stop? The choice of position here is not merely technical — it shapes the entire explanatory structure of one's metaphysical system.
