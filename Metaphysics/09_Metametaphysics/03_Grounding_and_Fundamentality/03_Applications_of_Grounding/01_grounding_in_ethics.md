# Grounding in Ethics

Consider the claim that torture is wrong. If we ask why it is wrong, a natural answer invokes natural facts: torture causes severe suffering, it degrades persons, it serves no compensating benefit. These natural facts seem to explain the moral fact. But what is the structure of this explanation? It is not causal — the natural facts do not cause the wrongness to come into existence over time. It is not identity — wrongness is not the same property as suffering-causing. The relation seems to be grounding: the moral fact obtains in virtue of the natural facts, and those natural facts are why the moral fact holds.

The application of grounding to ethics clarifies a debate that has often been conducted in less precise terms. The *supervenience* of the moral on the natural is widely accepted: any two situations exactly alike in all natural respects must be exactly alike in all moral respects. A moral difference requires a natural difference. But supervenience is a modal claim about covariation — it does not explain *why* moral facts supervene on natural ones. Grounding goes deeper: the claim that moral facts are grounded in natural facts explains the supervenience by providing the metaphysical explanation of why the moral is as it is.

Using grounding notation (following Fine and Rosen), let MF be a moral fact and NF be a natural fact. The grounding claim is NF < MF — NF grounds MF. Three readings of this claim track different metaethical positions. *Strong grounding*: NF alone fully grounds MF — the wrongness of torture is fully explained by its causing suffering. *Partial grounding*: NF is among the grounds of MF, but additional facts (about human dignity, rational agency, or moral conventions) are also required. *Mediate grounding*: NF grounds MF via intermediate facts — perhaps NF grounds the fact that torture is bad for persons, and that welfare fact grounds the moral wrongness. The strength of the grounding claim affects the metaethical implications significantly.

## Non-Naturalism and Brute Grounding

Non-naturalists like Russ Shafer-Landau (*Moral Realism*, 2003) accept that moral facts supervene on natural facts — and so accept a grounding relation — but deny that this grounding is explained by identity or reduction. The moral is grounded in the natural, but this grounding is itself brute: there is no further explanation of why wrongness is grounded in harm-causing. Moral facts are real and objective, not natural (not identical to any natural property), but constitutively dependent on natural facts.

This position uses the grounding framework to articulate a "non-naturalist but not non-realist" view: moral properties are real (they make genuine claims about the world) while being irreducible (they are not identical to natural properties). The grounding relation connects them to the natural without eliminating their distinctness.

Dreier's objection: if the grounding of the moral in the natural is brute — if there is no explanation of why harm-causing grounds wrongness — then the connection seems arbitrary. Why does harm-causing ground wrongness rather than rightness? The non-naturalist owes an account. Appealing to moral intuitions risks circularity; appealing to further natural facts seems to concede naturalism. The non-naturalist's response is that the grounding connection between harm and wrongness is itself a fundamental feature of moral reality, analogous to the grounding connection between physical and mental facts in non-reductive physicalism. Not all metaphysical connections require reductive explanation; some are primitively connecting features of the world.

## Particularism and Variable Grounding

Moral particularism (Dancy) holds that moral reasons do not have fixed valences: a feature that grounds wrongness in one context might ground rightness in another. Telling a lie is typically wrong, but lying to protect an innocent person from harm might be right. The same natural feature (lying) grounds different moral facts in different contexts.

This challenges the standard grounding picture, which holds that NF < MF uniformly and necessarily. Particularism requires a context-sensitive account:

(NF in context C₁) < MF₁ (lying is wrong in this context)
(NF in context C₂) < MF₂ (lying is right in this context)

The natural fact is the same; the grounded moral fact differs because of context-specific features. This is a form of non-monotonic grounding: the moral significance of a natural feature depends on what else is present. Generalists (Ross, Scanlon) deny particularism and hold that moral features have fixed prima facie significance — lying is always prima facie wrong, though this can be overridden by other considerations. In grounding terms: the lying fact always partially grounds a wrongness fact, but this partial grounding can be outweighed by other partial groundings running in the opposite direction.

## Grounding and Moral Ontology

The application of grounding to ethics has consequences for moral ontology. If moral facts are grounded in natural facts, then moral properties are "nothing over and above" natural properties in the constitutive sense grounding captures — real but derivative, not additional metaphysical items over and above the natural. This is a form of *non-reductive moral realism*: moral facts are objective and mind-independent, but constituted by natural facts rather than additional items in the fundamental inventory of the world.

This structure parallels non-reductive physicalism about mental properties, discussed in the philosophy of mind module. Just as mental properties are real but grounded in physical properties, moral properties are real but grounded in natural properties. Whether the parallel is exact — whether moral grounding requires distinctive features absent from the mental/physical case, particularly in connection with the normativity of moral facts — is an important open question that connects the grounding framework to debates in both metaethics and the philosophy of mind.
