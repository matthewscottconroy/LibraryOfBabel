# Review Questions — Grounding and Fundamentality

1. The chapter argues that grounding is needed because supervenience is too weak and too modal to carry the explanatory load: knowing that the mental supervenes on the physical leaves open *why* the supervenience holds, whereas grounding claims to answer that "why" question. But Fine argues that grounding is itself primitive and cannot be further analyzed. Does appealing to a primitive relation solve the explanatory problem, or does it merely rename it? What would a genuine explanation of why the mental depends on the physical look like, and could grounding provide it?

2. Grounding is characterized as synchronic, necessitating, constitutive, and carrying the "nothing over and above" implication — unlike causation, which is diachronic, contingent, and productive. The chapter uses this distinction to dissolve the causal exclusion problem: physical events cause subsequent events while mental properties are grounded in physical properties, so the two relations do not compete. Assess this dissolution: does it succeed, or does it create a new problem about whether grounded mental properties can be causally relevant at all?

3. Transitivity is one of the four formal properties attributed to grounding, but the Trump objection challenges it. The objection shows that transitivity can generate the wrong grounding verdict when intermediate grounds involve level-specific features (the type "royal flush" rather than individual card facts). Fine responds by relativizing grounding to levels. Is this a satisfying reply, or does it change the subject — replacing grounding as a single unified relation with a family of level-relative relations whose relationships to each other are unexplained?

4. Priority monism holds that the whole cosmos is the fundamental entity, with all parts being derivative. Priority pluralism holds that simples are fundamental, with wholes being derivative. Schaffer motivates monism partly from quantum entanglement, and Sider defends pluralism partly from the direction of scientific explanation. The chapter notes that epistemological and metaphysical priority directions can come apart. What additional evidence — beyond the direction of scientific explanation and the holism of entanglement — could in principle determine which view is correct?

5. The debate between foundationalism and infinitism about grounding parallels the epistemological debate between foundationalism and coherentism about justification. The key issue in both cases is whether an infinite chain provides genuine explanation (or justification) or only defers it. What are the strongest considerations on each side, and does the parallel to epistemology help clarify the metaphysical debate or distort it?

6. The application of grounding to ethics shows that moral particularism requires context-sensitive or non-monotonic grounding: the same natural fact can ground different moral facts in different contexts. But the standard formal account of grounding — irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive, necessitating — seems to require that grounding relations hold necessarily and uniformly. Does moral particularism reveal a genuine tension in the formal grounding framework, or can the framework accommodate it by building contextual factors into the specification of the grounding base?

7. Fine's account of grounding-via-essence holds that A grounds B only if it is part of the essence of some entity that A's obtaining necessitates B's obtaining. This works smoothly for sets ({Socrates} is grounded in Socrates because containment is constitutive of {Socrates}'s essence) but faces challenges for mental states, since pain's essence — typically its functional role — does not include being realized by C-fiber firing specifically. Does this asymmetry suggest that there are two fundamentally different kinds of grounding (essence-explained and not), or that Fine's essentialist account needs revision to handle multiple realizability?

8. The concept of fundamentality is used throughout the textbook to frame debates about universals, persons, causation, and mathematical objects. If you had to state a single criterion that separates fundamental from derivative entities — drawing on all three accounts examined in the chapter (grounding-based, intrinsic, and structural) — what would it be, and which of the textbook's prior first-order debates does the choice of criterion settle or fail to settle?
