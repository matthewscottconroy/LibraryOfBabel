# Essence and Real Definition

What does it mean to give a real definition — not a nominal definition that specifies how a word is used, but a definition that specifies what a thing genuinely is? What water *is* is H₂O — not just a name for it, not a description of its superficial properties, but what it is by its very nature. What a triangle *is* is a three-sided closed plane figure — not a contingent feature of triangles, but what makes something a triangle in the first place. Real definitions specify essences: the properties that constitute what a thing is, that it must have in order to be what it is.

Kit Fine's work in the 1990s transformed the philosophical discussion of essence by distinguishing it from modal necessity. It is necessary that Socrates is a member of the singleton set {Socrates} — there is no possible world where Socrates exists but is not a member of {Socrates}. But it is not part of Socrates's essence to be a member of that set. That fact is about the set, not about Socrates; it tells us something about the set (that it essentially contains Socrates) rather than about Socrates. Essence must be understood in terms of real definition — what a thing is *by its very nature* — not in terms of modal necessity.

This distinction has substantial consequences. If essence cannot be defined modally, then our grip on what things essentially are cannot come simply from reflection on what is necessary. We need a separate account of how we come to know essences — one that is not simply a corollary of modal epistemology. And the applications of essence in metaphysics — to the definition of natural kinds, to the individuation of properties, to the grounding of laws of nature — must be rethought in terms of real definition rather than modal structure.

This chapter develops Fine's account of essence, its relationship to modality and to identity, and its metaontological significance. The questions here connect to virtually every other chapter in this textbook, because the notion of essence underlies most of the substantive ontological commitments that metaphysics makes.
