# Real Definition

There are two very different things you might mean by defining something. You might specify how a word is used — give its meaning, its role in a conceptual scheme. Or you might specify what the thing itself is — its nature, the features that make it the kind of thing it is independently of how we talk about it. Aristotle called the second a *real definition*, and it is his concept that Kit Fine revived, with considerable precision, in contemporary analytic metaphysics.

The contrast is vivid in cases where the two come apart. A nominal definition of "gold" might be: the yellow, shiny, valuable metal used in jewelry. This captures how we ordinarily use "gold," what features we associate with it. A real definition specifies what gold essentially is: the element with atomic number 79. The real definition is not a report on word use; it is a substantive discovery about the nature of a substance, arrived at not by conceptual analysis but by chemistry. Similarly for water: the nominal definition is "the clear, tasteless, odorless liquid that fills rivers and oceans"; the real definition is H₂O.

Fine's specific contribution is to insist that real definition is irreducible to modal notions. A real definition specifies what is true *in virtue of an entity's nature* — and this is more fundamental than necessity. Fine distinguishes between facts that are true in virtue of an entity's essence and facts that are merely necessarily true. "Socrates is such that 2+2=4" is necessarily true of Socrates — in every possible world where Socrates exists, 2+2=4. But it is not part of Socrates's essence that 2+2=4; that is part of the nature of the numbers, not of Socrates. More generally: for any object x and any necessary truth p, "x is such that p" will be necessarily true of x — but this cannot be essential to x for every necessary truth, on pain of making every entity essentially related to every necessary fact.

The formal operator for essence uses a subscript: □ₓ φ means "it is part of the essence of x that φ." Then:
- □_{Socrates} (Socrates is human): It is part of Socrates's essence that he is human.
- ¬□_{Socrates} (2+2=4): It is not part of Socrates's essence that 2+2=4.
- □_{{Socrates}} ({Socrates} contains Socrates): It is part of the essence of {Socrates} that it contains Socrates.

The key implication: □φ (necessary truth) does not entail □ₓ φ for every entity x. But □ₓ φ does entail □(x exists → φ): if φ is part of x's essence, then necessarily, if x exists, φ holds.

## Real Definition and Metaphysical Explanation

Fine's central claim is that real definitions are not merely descriptive but explanatory: they explain why the things defined have the properties and relations they do. The real definition of {Socrates} — that it essentially contains Socrates as its sole member — explains why {Socrates} depends on Socrates and not vice versa, why {Socrates} exists iff Socrates does, and why this particular singleton is determined by exactly this individual. This explanatory role connects real definition to grounding: real definitions are the fundamental explanatory grounds of grounding claims. If we want to know why A grounds B, we look to the real definitions of the entities involved and find that the nature of the grounded entity B requires A.

Fine's neo-Aristotelian program recovers this insight across different domains. For abstract mathematical objects, real definitions are given by abstraction principles or set-theoretic membership conditions. For physical objects, real definitions of natural kinds are given by their microstructural properties — gold is essentially element 79. For sets, real definitions are given by membership conditions — a set is essentially the set of its members. For persons, real definitions are given by origin conditions or psychological/biological constitution, depending on one's theory of personal identity.

Each module in this textbook is, on the neo-Aristotelian picture, concerned with the real definition of a specific domain: the metaphysics of time asks for the real definition of temporal facts; the metaphysics of causation asks for the real definition of causation; personal identity asks for the real definition of persons. Making real definition central does not dissolve these debates but gives them a common form.

## Two Significant Challenges

The *naturalist challenge* holds that real definitions are supposed to specify the essential features of things independently of how we describe them, but our only way of specifying features is through descriptions. This suggests that all definitions are ultimately nominal: they specify how concepts or words apply, not how things are "in themselves." Fine's response: the essential operator □ₓ is not a linguistic notion — it is a metaphysical operator that specifies what is true in virtue of an entity's nature. The nature of an entity is a real feature of that entity, not a feature of our descriptions of it. Whether this response is ultimately satisfactory depends on whether primitive essences are intelligible — a question that connects to the debates about essence and modality examined in the next section.

The *vagueness challenge* holds that for many entities — persons, artifacts, social institutions — there are no sharp essential properties. Where does a person end and their environment begin? What is the essential constitution of a table, down to which atoms? If there are no sharp essences, there are no real definitions — only vague, context-sensitive characterizations. Fine's response: vagueness in how we apply essence-talk does not show that there are no essences. Vagueness in "tall" does not show there is no property of height; similarly, vagueness in which properties are essential to persons does not show that persons have no essences. It shows that determining their essences is difficult, not that there is nothing to determine.
