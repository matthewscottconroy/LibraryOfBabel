# Essence vs. Necessity

For most of the twentieth century, it was widely assumed that essential properties and necessary properties are the same thing. Property P is essential to object x iff necessarily, if x exists, then x has P. Socrates is essentially human iff it is impossible for Socrates to exist without being human. This modal analysis of essence was the background assumption of Kripke's and Putnam's work on natural kind terms and rigid designation, and it seemed like the natural way to cash out Aristotelian essentialism in the terms of possible worlds semantics.

Kit Fine's 1994 paper "Essence and Modality" argued that this identification is a mistake — that essence is a more fundamental concept than necessity, and that they come apart in a decisive way. The argument is elegant and has been broadly accepted as establishing the inadequacy of the modal analysis.

Fine's counterexample: Consider the property of being such that 2+2=4. This property is such that necessarily, if Socrates exists, he has it — since 2+2=4 is a necessary truth, "Socrates is such that 2+2=4" is necessarily true of Socrates. By the modal analysis, this property should be essential to Socrates. But it is not part of Socrates's essence that 2+2=4. That is part of the essence of the numbers, not of Socrates. We can put the argument as a reductio:

- P1: The modal analysis holds that P is essential to x iff necessarily (x exists → Px).
- P2: For any necessary truth p and any object x, "x is such that p" satisfies this condition: necessarily (x exists → x is such that p).
- P3: Therefore, by the modal analysis, every object has every necessary truth as part of its essence.
- P4: But this is absurd: it is not part of Socrates's essence that 2+2=4, or that water is H₂O, or that nothing is both red and not red.
- C: The modal analysis of essence is false.

This argument is now widely accepted as showing that the modal analysis is inadequate. The challenge for post-Finean metaphysics is to give an account of essence that does not reduce to necessity.

## The Essential vs. the Merely Necessary

Fine distinguishes two categories of necessary truths about an entity. *Essential* (in virtue of x's nature): "Socrates is human," "{Socrates} contains Socrates," "Water is H₂O." These hold because of the nature of the entity in question. *Merely necessary* (not in virtue of x's nature): "Socrates is such that 2+2=4," "Socrates is such that nothing is both red and not red." These hold necessarily but not because of Socrates's nature — they hold because of the natures of numbers, logic, or other entities entirely.

The distinction maps onto the subscript notation. □_{Socrates} φ means "it is part of Socrates's essence that φ." Being human satisfies this; being such that 2+2=4 does not. A precise characterization: φ is part of x's essence iff φ's truth is explained by what x is — by the nature of x itself. If φ's truth is explained by the nature of some other entity y (the numbers, logic, water), then φ is not part of x's essence even if φ is necessarily true of x.

## The Priority of Essence over Modality

Fine's positive claim goes beyond just rejecting the modal analysis: essence has explanatory priority over modality. Modal facts about what is necessary are explained by what things essentially are. The necessities "Socrates is necessarily human" and "water is necessarily H₂O" are explained by the essences of Socrates and water respectively:

- P1: Every necessity must have an explanation — "why is it necessary that p?" is a legitimate question.
- P2: The explanation of "why is it necessary that water is H₂O?" is: because it is part of the essence of water to be H₂O.
- P3: This explanation is not itself modal (it doesn't say "because it is necessary that...") — it appeals to essence.
- C: Essence explains necessity and hence is explanatorily prior to it.

This argument is disputed by those who think necessity is itself primitive and that essence should be defined in modal terms. The debate connects to the broader question between modal and non-modal approaches to metaphysics. Lewis's counterpart theory analyzes de re necessity in terms of similarity relations across possible worlds — which is context-sensitive and does not invoke essence as primitive. Fine's objection to this: if de re modal claims are context-relative (because the counterpart relation is context-sensitive), there are no absolute facts about what Socrates necessarily has — and this conflicts with the intuition that essentialism is a substantive, non-contextual thesis.

## Objections and Replies

The *primitivism objection*: Fine's account invokes essence as a primitive, unanalyzable notion, which gives us no deeper understanding of why some properties are essential and others are not. Fine's reply: primitivism about essence is no worse than primitivism about necessity, widely accepted. We understand modality by appeal to possible worlds (Lewis) or primitive modal operators (Plantinga), and these are themselves primitives. Essence is a well-understood notion in the Aristotelian tradition, and its theoretical role is determinate and productive.

Plantinga's worlds-based alternative: P is essential to x iff x has P in every possible world in which x exists. Fine's counterexample does not obviously arise if we restrict to "well-behaved predicates" — perhaps the restriction excludes the property of being such that 2+2=4. Fine's reply: the restriction is ad hoc unless grounded in an independent account of what makes a predicate well-behaved; and in every possible world where Socrates exists, 2+2=4 holds — so even the restricted version of the worlds-based account faces the problem.

Sider's deflationary alternative: Essence is not primitive — it is a useful heuristic for locating which properties figure in fundamental laws or the fundamental structure of the world. P is essential to x iff P is a structural feature of x. Fine's reply: structurality does not capture all the cases that essence covers. It is part of Socrates's essence that he is human, but "human" may not be a structural notion in physics. Fine's essence is more fine-grained than Sider's structure — it operates at the level of individual entities and their natures, not at the level of physical theory.

Fine's essence/necessity distinction has applications across the whole textbook. In the metaphysics of persistence: whether it is essential to an object to persist through time, or merely contingent, depends on the nature of the object — organisms essentially persist through biological processes; artifacts might persist only contingently. In the metaphysics of causation: causal relations might be necessary given the laws of nature without being essential to the causes and effects involved. In personal identity: whether psychological continuity is essential to persons (a Lockean view) or merely a necessary condition depends on whether it figures in the real definition of persons or merely in what is modally required for their persistence.
