# Essence and Identity

What makes an entity *this* entity rather than some other? And what makes it the same entity it was yesterday, last year, a hundred years ago? These are questions about identity — about what individuates an entity and what sustains its identity through time. They are, at root, questions about essence. The essential features of a thing are precisely the features whose loss would mean the destruction of the thing rather than its persistence in a changed state. To know what makes Socrates Socrates, and what must be preserved for Socrates to persist, is to know something about Socrates's essence.

*Haecceitism* holds that each particular has a primitive "thisness" — a *haecceity* — that distinguishes it from all other particulars, even qualitatively identical ones. Socrates is essentially this very individual, not merely an individual of certain kinds. Haecceitism allows for trans-world identity: the same individual can appear in multiple possible worlds, distinguished by its haecceity rather than by its qualitative profile. Formally, let H_s be the haecceity of Socrates:

- □_{Socrates} H_s: It is part of Socrates's essence that he has haecceity H_s.
- ∀x(x ≠ Socrates → ¬H_s(x)): No other individual has Socrates's haecceity.
- □∀x(H_s(x) → x = Socrates): Necessarily, anything with Socrates's haecceity is Socrates.

The metaphysical cost of haecceitism is that haecceities are primitive, qualitatively ungrounded properties — the identity of individuals is not fully explained by their qualitative profile. Ockham's razor suggests eliminating haecceities if possible.

*Anti-haecceitism* and *origin essentialism* (Kripke) offer an alternative. The essential properties of an individual include its origin — the causal-historical process that produced it. Socrates is essentially the individual produced by certain gametes and born to certain parents. No other individual could have had exactly the same origin; had different gametes been involved, the resulting individual would not have been Socrates. The argument proceeds:

- P1: We can coherently ask whether a given individual could have had different properties.
- P2: The answer depends on what that individual essentially is.
- P3: For organisms, the origin (the gametes from which they developed) is the most fundamental individuating feature.
- P4: Therefore, origin is essential to organisms.
- C: Origin essentialism is true for biological individuals.

P3 is contestable, but origin essentialism has become the default view about biological individuals. Its advantage over haecceitism is parsimony: individual identity is grounded in causal-historical origin, a qualitative (though highly specific) feature of the world, rather than in primitive individuating properties.

## The Ship of Theseus

The ship of Theseus thought experiment is the canonical illustration of how essence bears on persistence through change. If every plank of Theseus's ship is gradually replaced with a new plank, is the resulting ship the same ship? A variant: if the old planks are collected and used to build a second ship, which is the original Ship of Theseus?

The essentialist analysis makes the stakes clear: the answer depends on which properties are essential to *this ship*.

*Material essentialism*: A ship essentially consists of the particular material components that originally constituted it. Gradual replacement destroys the ship and creates a succession of qualitatively similar successors. The "restored" ship from old planks is the original; the gradually replaced ship is not.

*Functional-continuity essentialism*: A ship essentially fulfills a certain functional role — transporting people, belonging to Theseus's fleet. Functional-causal continuity determines persistence. The gradually replaced ship, which continuously functions as Theseus's ship, is the original; the reconstructed ship from old planks is a copy.

*No essentialist fact*: For artifacts, there may be no determinate essence — identity conditions for artifacts are vague or conventional. "Is this the same ship?" does not have a determinate answer; it depends on which identity-criteria we adopt.

Fine's framework suggests: the answer depends on the constitutive essence of *this particular ship*. If its constitutive essence involves its material components (as for some natural objects), material continuity is required. If its constitutive essence involves its role in Theseus's fleet (as for an artifact), functional continuity is sufficient. For artifacts, unlike natural kinds, the constitutive essence may itself be indeterminate.

## Essence and Personal Identity

Personal identity is the deepest application of essence to questions of persistence. What makes me the same person I was ten years ago?

*Psychological continuity essentialism* (Locke, Parfit): What is essential to a person is psychological continuity — overlapping chains of memories, intentions, beliefs, and personality traits. A person persists iff there is sufficient psychological continuity between temporal stages. This makes persons essentially psychological entities; purely physical continuity without psychological continuity does not preserve personal identity.

*Biological continuity essentialism* (Olson): What is essential to a person is biological continuity — being the same biological organism. A person persists iff the biological organism persists. This makes persons essentially biological entities; psychological continuity is not essential.

*Narrative essentialism*: What is essential to a person is the continuity of a life narrative — a coherent, ongoing story in which the person is the protagonist. This is more holistic than either psychological or biological accounts.

Fine's essence operator clarifies the debate without resolving it: we need to know what is part of the essence of *this person* — psychological profile, biological constitution, or narrative self-understanding? The answer determines which continuity relation is required for personal identity. Parfit's radical conclusion — that personal identity may not matter, and what matters is only psychological connectedness regardless of strict numerical identity — goes beyond the essentialist framework in a distinctive way: it suggests that the right response to uncertainty about personal identity is to focus on what we care about rather than what is metaphysically essential. Whether this pragmatic turn is acceptable or represents an evasion of the metaphysical question is itself an important issue in the personal identity debate.
