# Essence and Modality

The relationship between essence and modality runs in both directions, and understanding both directions is essential for contemporary metaphysics. Essence constrains what is possible: if it is part of the essence of gold to be element 79, then no possible world contains gold that is not element 79. Conversely, Kripke showed that some necessary truths — "water is H₂O," "Hesperus is Phosphorus," "gold is element 79" — are not knowable a priori but are discovered through empirical science. This combination of metaphysical necessity with a posteriori discovery depends on a tight connection between the essences of things and the necessity of truths about them.

Fine's position, as we have seen, makes essence prior to modality: the essences of things explain why the modal facts are as they are, not the other way around. But even those who resist this priority claim must acknowledge the intimate relationship. The modal profile of an entity — what it can and cannot be — is determined by its essence. Understanding the essences of natural kinds and individuals is what allows us to understand which modal truths are necessary and which merely contingent.

## Kripke's Contribution: A Posteriori Necessities

Before Kripke, it was widely assumed that all necessary truths are knowable a priori. Kant's synthetic a priori was the exception, but even Kant restricted necessary a posteriori knowledge to appearances rather than things in themselves. The logical positivists held that all necessary truths are analytic and hence knowable a priori.

Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1970/1980) showed that there are necessary a posteriori truths: truths that hold in every possible world but are discovered empirically rather than through conceptual analysis. "Water is H₂O" is necessary — in every possible world, water is H₂O — but it was discovered through chemistry. "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is necessary — they are the same planet — but this was an empirical discovery. "Gold is element 79" is necessary — but established through scientific investigation.

The explanation: these necessities arise from the essences of natural kinds and individuals. Water is essentially H₂O; that is what water is. The necessity is metaphysical (grounded in essence), not logical (grounded in logical form) or epistemic (knowable a priori). Kripke's distinction between metaphysical necessity, logical necessity, and epistemic necessity is now standard in analytic philosophy. It allows us to say: a truth can be necessary without being analytic, and it can be a posteriori without being contingent.

## The Essentialist Account of Modality

If essence explains necessity, we have an essentialist account of the space of possible worlds:

□p (p is necessary) iff p is explained by the essences of the entities involved.

Applied systematically: Logical necessities ("either p or not-p") are grounded in the essence of logical constants. Mathematical necessities ("2+2=4") are grounded in the essences of numbers and arithmetic operations. Natural kind necessities ("water is H₂O") are grounded in the essence of water as a natural kind. De re necessities about individuals ("Socrates is essentially human") are grounded in the essence of Socrates.

On this picture, a possible world is a way things could be while respecting the essential features of the entities that exist. Impossible worlds are ways things cannot be because they violate essences. The essentialist account grounds modality in reality — in the natures of actual entities — rather than in a plurality of concrete worlds (Lewis) or in primitive modal operators (Plantinga).

## De Re vs. De Dicto Modality

Fine's essence operator is explicitly *de re*: □ₓ φ expresses what is part of x's essence — a claim about the object x (the *res*), not about any proposition. The de re necessity of φ for x is grounded in x's essence, and this distinguishes Fine's approach from de dicto necessity, which is grounded in the meanings of terms rather than the natures of objects.

The distinction matters for debates about essentialism. *De dicto essentialism* is relatively uncontroversial: there are necessary de dicto truths (all bachelors are unmarried). But these are grounded in meanings, not in the essences of objects. *De re essentialism* claims that particular objects have essential properties they could not lack while remaining the same object. This is the kind of essentialism defended by Kripke and Fine, and it was the target of Quine's skepticism.

Quine's objection: "is it necessarily or contingently true that this is a prime number?" depends on whether we describe the number as "nine" (not necessarily prime) or as "the number of planets" (contingently — and not prime). There is no fact of the matter about which properties an object necessarily has, independent of how it is described. Kripke's response: rigid designation fixes the reference of a name to an object independently of description. When we use "Socrates," we refer to a particular individual, and we can then ask which of his properties he necessarily has — and this question has a determinate answer given by his essence. Rigid designation is what makes de re essentialism coherent.

Lewis's counterpart theory offers a different response to de re modality that does not invoke essence as primitive. On Lewis's modal realism, de re necessity — what properties Socrates necessarily has — is analyzed via counterpart relations: all of Socrates's counterparts in other possible worlds are human. But the counterpart relation is context-sensitive, which means de re modal claims are not absolute but context-relative. Fine's objection: if essentialism is context-relative, it is not the substantive, non-contextual thesis it appears to be. Lewis bites this bullet and accepts a kind of pragmatic essentialism. For Fine, this is unacceptable — it makes essential properties less fundamental than modal facts, reversing the order of explanation that his essentialist program requires.
