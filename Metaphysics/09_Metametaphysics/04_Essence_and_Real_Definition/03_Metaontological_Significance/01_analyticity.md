# Analyticity

"All bachelors are unmarried." This sentence seems to be true in a way that is entirely different from "snow is white." The first is true because of what the words mean; the second requires us to actually look at snow. Kant marked this distinction as analytic versus synthetic: analytic judgments are those where the predicate is "contained in" the subject, while synthetic judgments add something beyond what the subject-concept contains. The logical positivists built their whole program on this distinction, using analyticity to explain how logical and mathematical truths can be known a priori without empirical investigation.

Quine's famous attack in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) challenged the distinction at its foundations. The argument proceeds through several stages. Define analyticity via synonymy: a sentence is analytic iff it becomes a logical truth when synonyms are substituted for synonyms. "All bachelors are unmarried" becomes "all unmarried men are unmarried" — a logical truth — when we substitute the synonym "unmarried man" for "bachelor." But to explain synonymy, we appeal to interchangeability salva veritate — substitutability in all contexts without change of truth value. This requires "analytically" available contexts — modal contexts in which truth value is preserved. So synonymy presupposes analyticity, and analyticity presupposes synonymy. The definitions are circular. Moreover, Quine argues, our beliefs face the "tribunal of experience" as a corporate body; any belief — including logical and mathematical beliefs — can in principle be revised in light of recalcitrant experience. There is no sharp boundary between beliefs that are immune to revision (analytic) and those subject to empirical revision (synthetic).

These are powerful arguments, but they have not put the distinction entirely to rest. Grice and Strawson (1956) responded that even if the distinction is not precisely definable, it tracks a real difference that competent speakers recognize. That "all bachelors are unmarried" is analytic while "some bachelors are happy" is synthetic is something speakers intuitively grasp, and this recognition is evidence that the distinction is real even if not precisely articulable.

## Analyticity, Necessity, and the Kripkean Distinctions

Kripke's work freed analyticity from its traditional association with necessity. Analyticity is about truth in virtue of meaning; necessity is about truth in all possible worlds. These come apart: "Bachelors are unmarried" is analytic and necessary; "Water is H₂O" is necessary but not analytic (it was discovered through chemistry, not by conceptual analysis); "The actual sky is blue" is contingent but knowable a priori (it is analytically true that whatever is actually the case, is the case). Kripke's distinctions allow analyticity to retain a clear domain — conceptual or linguistic truths — without claiming to cover all necessary truths. This is an important clarification: once we distinguish analyticity from necessity, the Quinean attack on analyticity does not automatically undermine the necessary a posteriori truths that Kripke identified.

Boghossian (1997) distinguishes two forms of analyticity. *Metaphysical analyticity*: a sentence is true in virtue of its meaning — meaning determines truth value. *Epistemic analyticity*: understanding a sentence is sufficient for justification in believing it. Even if the positivists conflated these, epistemic analyticity may be defensible and important for understanding a priori knowledge. We can accept that understanding "all bachelors are unmarried" is sufficient for knowing it, without accepting that all necessary truths are "true in virtue of meaning" in a deep metaphysical sense.

## The Status of Metaphysical Truths

The metametaphysical question is sharp: are metaphysical truths analytic? Three views have been influential.

The *conceptual-analyst view* holds that many metaphysical claims are analytic — attempts to uncover the content of concepts. Personal identity analyzed in terms of psychological continuity (Locke's analysis), causation analyzed in terms of counterfactual dependence (Lewis's analysis) — these are attempts to specify what our concepts require. On this view, metaphysics is a conceptual discipline.

The *Quinean view* holds that no metaphysical claims are analytic, because the analytic/synthetic distinction is untenable. All claims — including metaphysical ones — are synthetic and empirically revisable in principle. Metaphysical claims are simply the most general synthetic claims, subject to revision in light of theory change.

The *neo-Aristotelian view* (Fine) holds that metaphysical truths are neither analytic (they are not truths of meaning) nor contingent synthetic truths (they concern the essential structure of reality). They are truths about the essences of things — a category that cuts across the analytic/synthetic distinction. What is true in virtue of gold's essence (that gold is element 79) is not analytic — it was not knowable a priori before chemistry — but is necessary — gold could not be otherwise. The neo-Aristotelian view implies that the analytic/synthetic distinction, even if restored in some form, does not carve at the right joints for metaphysics. The relevant distinction for metaphysics is between essentialist truths (grounded in the nature of things) and non-essentialist truths (not so grounded).

## Analyticity and Easy Ontology

Thomasson's easy ontology (discussed in Section 2) makes significant use of analyticity. Ontological questions like "do tables exist?" are answered by applying conceptual rules: given wood arranged table-wise (an empirical fact), the conceptual rule for "table" entails that there are tables. This entailment is analytic — it holds in virtue of the meaning of "table." Many metaphysical existence claims are then analytic consequences of ordinary conceptual rules plus empirical facts.

Sider and Fine object that analyticity of this kind does not settle the genuinely deep questions. Whether tables are fundamental or derivative, whether they are identical to or grounded in their particles, whether the concept of "table" carves nature at its joints — these are not answered by applying conceptual rules. The deep questions of metaphysics are not about analyticity at all; they are about the structure of reality.

The position defended throughout this module is that metaphysics is primarily a synthetic, substantive discipline, but one that uses conceptual analysis as a tool for clarifying what questions are being asked and what evidence would bear on answering them. Analyticity is a component of good philosophical methodology — it helps us avoid merely verbal disputes and clarify the content of our claims — but it is not the whole of metaphysics. The genuinely deep questions about fundamentality, grounding, and essence are not answerable by semantic analysis alone.
