# Conceptual Truths in Metaphysics

What would it take for a metaphysical dispute to be merely conceptual rather than substantive? The question is not abstract: it bears directly on whether debates about mereology, personal identity, causation, and modality are genuine disagreements about the world or differences in how we use words. We have already seen Hirsch's quantifier variance thesis apply this skepticism to ontological debates. The same pressure can be applied more broadly: perhaps much of what looks like metaphysics is really the study of our concepts — the ways we carve up and categorize reality — rather than the study of reality itself.

A conceptual truth holds in virtue of our concepts — the ways we categorize the world. "All bachelors are unmarried" is a classic case: its truth is guaranteed by how we use "bachelor," not by how the world is. In contemporary metaphysics, there is debate about how many and which metaphysical claims share this character. If a metaphysical dispute is merely conceptual — if the parties simply use their terms differently — then it is not a substantive disagreement about the world, and resolving it requires only conceptual clarification, not metaphysical investigation.

Against the reduction of metaphysics to conceptual analysis: many metaphysical truths are not analytic or conceptual. "Water is H₂O" is a metaphysical claim about the essence of water, but it was discovered empirically. "The universe began with a Big Bang" is a cosmological claim that is not conceptually guaranteed. The metaphysical questions about grounding, fundamentality, and essence that have occupied this module are not answerable by analyzing concepts. They require investigation of the world, possibly continuous with physics and the other sciences.

## Two-Dimensional Semantics

Two-dimensional semantics (Kaplan, Chalmers, Jackson) provides a framework for distinguishing conceptual from substantive dimensions of metaphysical claims. The *primary intension* of an expression is a function from possible worlds (as contexts of use) to extensions: the primary intension of "water" maps each possible world to the dominant watery substance in that world — H₂O on Earth, XYZ on Twin Earth. The *secondary intension* is a function from possible worlds (as circumstances of evaluation), holding the reference fixed: the secondary intension of "water" maps every possible world to the actual watery substance, H₂O.

Chalmers uses this to separate conceptual from metaphysical truths. A *conceptual* truth holds with respect to all possible worlds in the primary intension — "all bachelors are unmarried" is true in every world considered as a context. A *metaphysical* truth holds with respect to all possible worlds in the secondary intension — "water is H₂O" is metaphysically necessary. Not all metaphysical truths are conceptual truths: "water is H₂O" is metaphysically necessary but not conceptually true (its primary intension is not necessarily true). And some conceptual truths are not metaphysical truths: "bachelors are unmarried" might be conceptually necessary while being revisable if our concept of bachelor changed.

This framework offers a way to apply Chalmers's criteria for verbal disputes. A dispute is merely verbal iff the parties use a key term with different meanings, and would agree on all relevant facts if the semantic difference were made explicit. A dispute is substantive if the parties share the meanings of their terms and genuinely disagree about the facts. Chalmers suggests mereological universalism versus nihilism may be verbal on these grounds; other disputes — about consciousness, modal facts, God — may be substantive. The challenge is to determine which category a given dispute falls into, and this is itself not a merely conceptual matter.

## Jackson's Conceptual Analysis Program

Frank Jackson (*From Metaphysics to Ethics*, 1998) defends conceptual analysis as central to metaphysics. On Jackson's view, metaphysics identifies the "location" of complex facts within the overall logical space of possibilities — determining which possible worlds have which moral facts, how moral facts are related to physical facts across worlds, and so on. This location project is carried out partly through conceptual analysis: we analyze moral concepts to determine what conditions must hold for moral claims to be true. If the analysis reveals that moral facts are nothing over and above physical facts, non-reductive moral realism is false. If the analysis reveals that moral facts are sui generis, it is true.

Sider's objection: the location project assumes that the conceptual structure of our terms determines the metaphysical structure of the corresponding facts. But this fails if our concepts are imprecise or false to reality. The right metaphysical question is not "what does our concept of X require?" but "what is the nature of X?" — and the answer may not correspond to any analysis of our concept of X. The conceptual analysis program is useful for clarifying what questions we are asking, but it cannot settle what the answers are.

## Conceptual Engineering

Conceptual engineering (Cappelen, Plunkett, Haslanger) is the project of revising, improving, or constructing concepts for theoretical and practical purposes. Rather than asking what our concept of X actually means, it asks what our concept of X should be. Applied to metaphysics: we might decide that "do tables exist?" is not the right question, and engineer a concept of existence that is more theoretically useful — asking instead whether tables are metaphysically fundamental, causally efficacious, or scientifically indispensable. These questions are more precise and more productive.

The conceptual engineering perspective supports the deflationary Carnapian/Hirschian position: rather than discovering the "correct" meaning of "exists," we decide which concept of existence is most useful for our theoretical purposes. Critics respond that conceptual engineering cannot be the whole story in metaphysics, because we want to know about the world, not just about which concepts are useful. Even if we engineer a maximally precise concept of existence, we still want to know whether the entities falling under it are real — and this is a question about the world, not about our concepts.

The question of what is "merely conceptual" arises in every module of this textbook: whether "person" has determinate identity conditions (personal identity), whether causation is analyzed by our concept of cause (causation), whether necessity is a conceptual or a metaphysical notion (modality). Metametaphysics asks these questions about the discipline as a whole. Getting it right — identifying which metaphysical claims are merely conceptual and which are substantive — is essential for understanding what progress in metaphysics looks like and how metaphysical debates can be resolved.
