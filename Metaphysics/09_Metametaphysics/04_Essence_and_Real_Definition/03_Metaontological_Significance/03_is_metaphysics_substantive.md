# Is Metaphysics Substantive?

We have, by the end of this module, accumulated a formidable amount of evidence bearing on a single question: does metaphysics address genuine, determinate questions about the structure of reality — questions with answers that are independent of our conventions, interests, and linguistic choices? Or are metaphysical debates, at bottom, disputes about how to use words, or practical choices about which framework to adopt, or unanswerable pseudo-problems that transcend all possible evidence?

The deflationary case is not trivially dismissible. Consider: many metaphysical disputes have continued for centuries without resolution, while scientific disputes are typically resolved by evidence. Endurantism and perdurantism are compatible with every observed fact about objects and time; Platonism and nominalism are compatible with all mathematical practice. If theories are empirically equivalent, what could ground the claim that one is correct? The Carnapian position — that external existence questions are not meaningful theoretical questions but practical choices between frameworks — draws precisely this moral: when the frameworks are empirically equivalent, there is no fact of the matter to settle.

The inflationary response — defended by Sider, Fine, and the neo-Quinean tradition — insists that there is a fact of the matter, even when it is not decidable by empirical evidence alone. Reality has a structure, and some ways of describing it carve it at its joints while others do not. This structural notion is not framework-relative; it is a feature of how the world is. Metaphysics, as the discipline that identifies the structural features of reality, is substantive:

*The joint-carving argument* (Sider): Some predicates carve nature at its joints (mass, charge, spin) and some do not (grue, bleen). The best explanation of why joint-carving predicates figure in laws and support reliable inference is that they correspond to genuine features of the world. The same distinction applies to the quantifier: there is a natural, joint-carving use of the existential quantifier, and questions asked in it have determinate metaphysical answers.

*The essentialist argument* (Fine): Things have natures — essential properties — that are not determined by our conventions or conceptual choices. The essence of gold (being element 79) is not a matter of how we use "gold"; it is a feature of what gold is. Metaphysics, which aims to identify the essences of things, is a substantive discipline.

*The success of science*: Science successfully describes the fundamental structure of reality. Science is continuous with metaphysics; if science is substantive, metaphysics should be too. The Quinean: metaphysics is the most general part of our empirical theoretical framework, and it is substantive for the same reasons science is.

## The Meta-Level Problem

There is a second-order problem that the deflationist must face. The question "is metaphysics substantive?" is itself a metaphysical (or at least philosophical) question. If the deflationary claim is substantive, the deflationist is doing substantive metaphysics in arguing for deflationism. If the deflationary claim is merely a practical recommendation about which framework to adopt, it is not an argument for anything at all — it is just a preference. Either way, deflationism cannot entirely escape substantive commitments.

Sider presses this point: the deflationist must say something substantive in arguing for deflationism. "The existential quantifier is subject to variance" is either a substantive metaphysical claim (in which case the deflationist is doing substantive metaphysics) or a merely pragmatic recommendation (in which case it provides no reason to accept deflationism). The inflationary position has the advantage of consistency: it claims that metaphysical questions are substantive, and it asks substantive metaphysical questions.

## Metaontological Pluralism

A sophisticated intermediate position is metaontological pluralism: metaphysical questions come in different kinds, and their substantiveness varies.

*Trivially substantive questions* (Thomasson): "Do tables exist?" has a determinate answer — yes — but arriving at it requires only applying conceptual rules to empirical facts. This substantiveness is "easy."

*Genuinely substantive questions* (Sider, Fine): "Are tables fundamental?" "What grounds the fact that there are tables?" "Do tables have haecceities?" These require genuine metaphysical investigation using the apparatus of grounding, essence, and fundamentality.

*Merely verbal questions* (Hirsch): "Do mereological sums exist?" may be merely verbal — the answer depends on which quantifier-meaning one adopts, and this is a pragmatic rather than a metaphysical choice.

The pluralist view suggests that different metaphysical questions have different characters, and the task of metametaphysics is to sort questions into the appropriate categories. This sorting task is itself substantive — and it is what this module has been doing throughout.

## The Metametaphysics Module as a Model

The debates examined here — about grounding, essence, ontological commitment, the scope of the Quinean framework — are not merely verbal disputes. They concern the fundamental structure of philosophical inquiry and the methods by which first-order metaphysical questions should be approached. The arguments of Carnap, Hirsch, and Ayer have been treated seriously throughout, not dismissed, because they raise genuine methodological concerns that any substantive metaphysics must address: what distinguishes a substantive dispute from a merely verbal one? what role does evidence play? what makes one account better than another when both are empirically adequate?

The neo-Quinean and neo-Aristotelian responses — Quine, Fine, Sider, Schaffer — provide the substantive accounts of structure, essence, and grounding that answer these concerns. Metaphysics is a substantive discipline: it addresses genuine questions about the structure of reality, answers them using appropriate methods, and can make progress. The deflationary challenges were instructive because they forced the discipline to be explicit about what makes it substantive — and that explicitness is itself one of the genuine achievements of contemporary metametaphysics.

Each first-order module in this textbook presupposes a metametaphysical framework: it takes for granted that its questions are substantive, that intuitions provide evidence, and that the grounding/essence framework is appropriate for articulating the issues. This module makes those presuppositions explicit and subjects them to critical scrutiny — which is, in the end, the deepest form of philosophical investigation.
