# Review Questions — Essence and Real Definition

1. Fine's counterexample to the modal analysis of essence is that "Socrates is such that 2+2=4" is necessarily true of Socrates but is not part of his essence. The argument concludes that essence cannot be analyzed as necessity. Plantinga's response is to restrict the modal analysis to "well-behaved predicates" — excluding gerrymandered properties like "is such that 2+2=4." Fine replies that this restriction is ad hoc unless grounded in an independent account of predicate well-behavedness. Assess this exchange: does Fine's counterexample decisively establish that essence is more fundamental than necessity, or does it only show that a naive modal analysis fails while leaving a refined modal account open?

2. The constitutive/consequential distinction tracks a difference between core essential features (being human for Socrates) and their logical consequences (being a mammal for Socrates). The chapter suggests that ordinary intuitions about what is "essential" track constitutive rather than consequential essence. If this is right, what methodological implications follow for how philosophers should use thought experiments about essences — in personal identity, say, or mereology — given that our intuitions may be sensitive to a distinction that the relevant theories often ignore?

3. Origin essentialism holds that Socrates is essentially the individual who developed from particular gametes, and this grounds individual identity without appeal to primitive haecceities. Material essentialism about artifacts holds that a ship essentially consists of its original material components. But the Ship of Theseus thought experiment produces sharp conflicting intuitions, and Fine suggests that for artifacts, there may be no determinate constitutive essence. Does this suggest that essence admits of degrees of determinacy — some entities having precise essences and others only vague or conventional ones — and if so, what becomes of the neo-Aristotelian project of giving real definitions across all domains?

4. The chapter argues that essence explains grounding asymmetry: {Socrates} depends on Socrates because containment of Socrates is constitutive of {Socrates}'s essence, while being contained in {Socrates} is not constitutive of Socrates's essence. This is a clean result for mathematical objects. But for mental states, pain's essence is its functional role, not its physical realization — which means the grounding of pain in C-fiber firing cannot be explained by the essence of pain. What does this limitation reveal about the scope of Fine's neo-Aristotelian program, and how might the program be extended or revised to cover cases of multiple realizability?

5. Kripke established that natural kind necessities like "water is H₂O" are necessary a posteriori — known through chemistry, not conceptual analysis. Fine says essence explains such necessities: water is necessarily H₂O because H₂O is part of water's essence. Lewis's counterpart theory analyzes the same necessities in terms of the counterpart relation across possible worlds, without invoking primitive essence. Fine objects that Lewis's analysis makes de re modal claims context-relative and therefore not absolute. Assess this objection: does the context-sensitivity of counterpart relations genuinely undermine de re essentialism, or is it a feature Lewis should welcome rather than avoid?

6. The chapter examines three debates about natural kinds — chemical kinds (essentialism works cleanly), biological species (homeostatic property clusters fit better than essences), and social kinds (constructed essence on Haslanger's account). What does the variation across these cases reveal about the relationship between essence and natural science? Does it suggest that essentialism is a local tool suited to some domains but not a universal framework for all of metaphysics, or that the concept of essence is flexible enough to accommodate the variation?

7. Quine's challenge to analyticity — that the analytic/synthetic distinction cannot be sharply drawn because our beliefs face the tribunal of experience as a corporate body — threatens the neo-Fregean program (which relies on Hume's Principle being analytic) and also Thomasson's easy ontology (which relies on conceptual rules analytically entailing existence claims). The neo-Aristotelian response is that the relevant distinction for metaphysics is not analytic/synthetic but essentialist/non-essentialist — between what is true in virtue of an entity's nature and what is not. Does this response successfully bypass Quine's objection, or does it face an analogous problem: how do we know which claims are true in virtue of natures versus merely contingently true?

8. The final section asks whether metaphysics is substantive and answers yes, but identifies a self-referential threat: the claim that metaphysical questions are substantive is itself either a substantive metaphysical claim (in which case the deflationist is committed to doing substantive metaphysics to argue against it) or merely a pragmatic preference (in which case it provides no real argument). Looking back across the entire module — from the nature of metaphysics, through ontological commitment and grounding, to essence — does the body of substantive work done in those chapters itself constitute the best argument that metaphysics is a genuine discipline? Or does the discipline still owe an answer to the deflationist that is not simply a demonstration of productivity?
