# Domain Survey: Metaphysics of Science

## Overview

The metaphysics of science investigates the ontological commitments, fundamental categories, and explanatory structures of scientific theories. It occupies the intersection of metaphysics and philosophy of science, asking: what does our best science tell us about what exists? What is the relation between the objects and properties posited at different levels of scientific description — fundamental physics and chemistry, chemistry and biology, biology and psychology? Are higher-level sciences autonomous with their own genuine ontology, or do they reduce to physics? And what is the structure of scientific explanation — is it causal, nomological, or based on unification?

**Scientific realism** — the view that the entities posited by our best scientific theories genuinely exist, and that scientific progress consists in approximating truth about the mind-independent structure of reality — is one of the central positions in this domain. Its opponents include **van Fraassen's constructive empiricism** (we should believe only that our theories are empirically adequate, not that they are true about unobservable entities), **Kuhnian anti-realism** (scientific theories are incommensurable; "truth" is paradigm-relative), and **social constructivism** (scientific facts are socially constructed by the scientific community).

The **reduction** debate asks whether the special sciences (biology, chemistry, psychology) reduce to physics — whether their laws, kinds, and explanations are derivable from or eliminable in favor of physics. Nagel's model of reduction requires that the reducing and reduced theories be logically connected by bridge laws; this model has been challenged on grounds of multiple realizability (Putnam) and by the autonomy of higher-level explanations (Fodor). Contemporary discussion focuses on **emergence** — whether higher-level properties can arise from lower-level ones in ways that are not reducible, and whether emergence involves anything more than supervenience.

**Structural realism**, developed by Worrall (1989) and elaborated by Ladyman and Ross (2007), proposes a middle ground: scientific revolutions eliminate posited objects but often preserve mathematical structure; we should be realists about structural relations but agnostic about the nature of the individual objects that instantiate them. **Ontic structural realism** (Ladyman) goes further — there are no individuals at the fundamental level; only structures exist.

## Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

### Scientific Realism and Its Rivals

**Scientific realism** (Putnam, Boyd, Psillos): Our best scientific theories are approximately true; the entities they posit (electrons, genes, quarks) exist; the success of science is best explained by assuming it is tracking reality. The "no miracles argument": if our theories were not approximately true, the predictive success of science would be a miracle.

**Constructive empiricism** (van Fraassen, *The Scientific Image*, 1980): The aim of science is empirical adequacy, not truth. We should accept theories as empirically adequate (saving the phenomena) but remain agnostic about the existence of unobservables. Belief in unobservables goes beyond what the evidence warrants.

**Structural realism** (Worrall 1989): There is continuity across theory change at the level of mathematical structure. Fresnel's equations for light were approximately correct even though Fresnel's ontology (the ether) was replaced by Maxwell's field theory. What was preserved was the structural content, not the object posits. Conclusion: be realists about structure, agnostics about objects.

**Ontic structural realism** (Ladyman and Ross 2007): Not just epistemic agnosticism about objects, but the positive ontological claim that there are no individuals at the fundamental level — only structures (patterns of relations). Individual objects are emergent or conventional groupings of fundamental structural patterns.

**Scientific anti-realism / instrumentalism**: Scientific theories are not true or false descriptions of reality but instruments for prediction. Truth is not an appropriate category for scientific theories.

### Reduction and Emergence

**Nagelian reduction**: A higher-level theory *T2* reduces to a lower-level theory *T1* if all the laws of *T2* are derivable from *T1* together with bridge laws connecting the predicates of *T2* to predicates of *T1*.
- *Problem*: bridge laws seem to be contingent biconditionals (pain ↔ C-fiber firing), not identities or necessary connections; this makes the reduction incomplete.

**Kim's reductionism** (*Mind in a Physical World*, 1998): Reduction is the only way to preserve mental causation; non-reductive physicalism is unstable because of the causal exclusion argument. Either mental kinds are reducible to physical kinds, or they are causally inert.

**Fodor's multiple realizability argument** (1974): The special sciences are autonomous because their kinds are multiply realizable in lower-level kinds. There is no single physical description that covers every instance of "pain" or "gene"; hence there is no Nagelian bridge law, and no reduction.

**New wave reductionism** (Churchland 1985, Bickle): Reduction is not Nagelian derivation but rather the correction and displacement of higher-level theories by lower-level ones. The reduced theory is not preserved but shown to be approximately true at best, false at worst.

**Weak emergence** (Bedau 1997): Higher-level properties are weakly emergent from lower-level properties if they are in principle derivable from lower-level facts but only by a very complex, non-analytic path (e.g., simulation). This is ontologically compatible with reductionism.

**Strong emergence**: Higher-level properties are strongly emergent if they are not derivable even in principle from lower-level facts — there are genuine causal powers at the higher level not predictable from lower-level descriptions. Strong emergence requires downward causation and is incompatible with standard physicalism.
- Examples proposed: Bedau resists strong emergence; Hasker, Moreland, and some philosophers of biology defend it for consciousness.

**Levels of organization**: Biology in particular seems to have levels — molecular, cellular, organism, population, ecosystem — with genuine causal mechanisms at each level. Machamer, Darden, and Craver argue that levels are defined by mechanisms, not by aggregation of parts; this supports a moderate autonomy of higher-level explanations.

### Natural Kinds

**Essentialism about natural kinds** (Kripke, Putnam, Ellis): Natural kinds have essential properties — necessary microstructural constitutions. Water is necessarily H₂O; gold is necessarily element 79. The kind "water" is defined not by its surface properties (clear, odorless, drinkable) but by its underlying molecular structure.

**Homeostatic property cluster (HPC) theory** (Boyd 1991): Natural kinds are clusters of properties that tend to co-occur because they are causally sustained (by homeostatic mechanisms). There is no single essential property; membership is a matter of cluster membership, not essence. This accommodates biological species (which do not have microstructural essences) without abandoning natural kinds.

**Social construction of kinds** (Hacking 1999): Some kinds — especially human kinds (mental illness classifications, social categories) — are constructed through practices of classification and their feedback effects on those classified. This is not anti-realism but a different model of how kinds come to be real.

**Eliminativism about natural kinds**: There are no natural kinds corresponding to many scientific categories; scientific classification reflects useful organizational choices, not the joints of nature.

## Key Arguments and Counterarguments

### The No Miracles Argument (Putnam)

**P1.** Our best scientific theories achieve extraordinary predictive success (novel predictions, engineering applications).
**P2.** If our best theories were not approximately true, this success would be miraculous — inexplicable coincidence.
**P3.** Inference to the best explanation: the best explanation of scientific success is approximate truth.
**C.** Our best scientific theories are approximately true; the entities they posit exist.

**Pessimistic meta-induction** (Laudan): The history of science is full of successful theories that are now known to be false and whose posited entities are now thought not to exist (caloric, phlogiston, the ether, the luminiferous medium). By induction: our current successful theories are probably also false. The no-miracles argument proves too much if it is applied naively to past theories.

**Structural realist response** (Worrall): The no-miracles argument is vindicated if we restrict realism to the *structural* content of theories; the structural content is what gets preserved through theory change (Fresnel's equations → Maxwell's equations), even when the object posits are abandoned.

### The Causal Exclusion Argument and Special Sciences

**Kim's argument**: Non-reductive physicalism — the view that mental properties supervene on physical properties without being identical to them — is unstable because of the causal exclusion argument. Either mental properties reduce to physical ones (reductionism) or they are causally inert (epiphenomenalism). The multiple realizability argument may show that mental types ≠ physical types, but this comes at the cost of mental causation.

**Fodor's autonomy response**: The special sciences have their own genuine natural kinds; the fact that these kinds are multiply realizable means they cannot be reduced. But they are causally efficacious — the property of being "in pain" causally explains avoidance behavior, even if there is no single physical property that grounds it. *Kim's rejoinder*: there is no single higher-level causal power — only the lower-level physical realizer causes anything.

### The Argument from Quantum Non-Individuality

**P1.** Quantum particles (bosons, fermions) violate the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles — two electrons in the same state are genuinely distinct but qualitatively indistinguishable.
**P2.** If they are indistinguishable in all their intrinsic properties and relations, they cannot be individual objects (bare particulars or bundles of properties).
**C.** The fundamental entities of quantum mechanics are not individuals; only structural/relational features are real.

**Response** (Saunders): Weak discernibility — fermions can be weakly discerned by irreflexive relations (being opposite spin from the other) without having any intrinsic distinguishing properties. This preserves individuality. *Ladyman and Ross response*: weak discernibility is only relational, which itself supports structural rather than object-based ontology.

## Key Papers and Books

1. **van Fraassen, Bas, *The Scientific Image* (1980)** — Definitive statement of constructive empiricism; the standard anti-realist reference.

2. **Worrall, John, "Structural Realism: The Best of Both Worlds?" (1989)** — Introduces structural realism as a middle position preserving realism about structure across theory change.

3. **Ladyman, James and Ross, Don, *Every Thing Must Go* (2007)** — Radical ontic structural realism; argues that analytic metaphysics is idle; only science can inform ontology.

4. **Fodor, Jerry, "Special Sciences, or the Disunity of Science as a Working Hypothesis" (1974)** — Defends the autonomy of special sciences; multiple realizability argument against reduction.

5. **Boyd, Richard, "Realism, Anti-Foundationalism, and the Enthusiasm for Natural Kinds" (1991)** — Develops the homeostatic property cluster theory of natural kinds.

6. **Kim, Jaegwon, *Mind in a Physical World* (1998)** — Develops the causal exclusion argument; argues for reductionism or epiphenomenalism.

7. **Putnam, Hilary, "What is Mathematical Truth?" (1975)** and "Explanation and Reference" (1973) — The no-miracles argument and the causal theory of reference applied to natural kinds.

8. **Machamer, Peter, Darden, Lindley, and Craver, Carl, "Thinking about Mechanisms" (2000)** — The influential mechanisms-based account of scientific explanation.

## Live Debates and Open Questions

1. **Quantum gravity and spacetime ontology**: Both loop quantum gravity and string theory suggest that the spacetime manifold of general relativity is not fundamental. The ontology of quantum gravity — discrete loops, strings, causal sets — has direct metaphysical implications for the nature of space and time.

2. **The reality of higher-level causation**: The ongoing debate between Kim's reductionism and Fodor's autonomy of special sciences has not been resolved. The introduction of interventionist frameworks (Woodward) provides new tools, but the question of whether higher-level properties have genuine causal powers distinct from their realizers remains open.

3. **Species ontology**: Do biological species exist as natural kinds, individuals, or social constructs? Kitcher, Hull, and Dupré have argued for different positions; the debate connects to the broader question of whether natural kind essentialism applies in biology.

4. **The unity of science**: Is there a unified science to which all sciences reduce, or is the world genuinely disunified (Dupré's "disorder of nature")? The unification question has both metaphysical and methodological dimensions.

5. **Laws in the special sciences**: Do biology, economics, and psychology have genuine laws, or only useful generalizations? The status of ceteris paribus laws — laws that hold "other things being equal" — is central to the autonomy debate.

## Connections to Other Domains

**Metaphysics of Science and Ontology (Domain 01)**: Scientific realism is an ontological position about the existence of theoretical entities. The indispensability argument (Quine-Putnam) is an argument from the practice of science to the existence of mathematical and scientific posits. The debate about natural kinds directly engages the universals debate.

**Metaphysics of Science and Causation/Laws (Domain 04)**: The relationship between laws and natural necessity is central to both domains. Scientific laws are paradigm cases for the debate between the Best Systems Account and the DTA account. Mechanisms-based explanation competes with nomic explanation as the fundamental form of scientific understanding.

**Metaphysics of Science and Universals/Particulars (Domain 02)**: Natural kinds are the primary case for universals that have scientific import. Armstrong's universals are explicitly those picked out by mature natural science; the HPC theory of kinds is a non-essentialist realism that attempts to preserve the naturalness of scientific kinds.

**Metaphysics of Science and Modality (Domain 05)**: Essential natures of natural kinds (Kripke, Putnam) generate a posteriori necessities — the metaphysical necessity of "water is H₂O." Whether scientific kinds have essential natures (and thus ground modal facts about their instances) is directly relevant to the modal debate.

**Metaphysics of Science and Metametaphysics (Domain 09)**: Ladyman and Ross's "scientific metaphysics" is a metametaphysical position: only science can inform ontology; armchair metaphysics is idle. This is the most aggressive naturalization of metaphysics and directly challenges the methodology of most first-order metaphysics.
