# Metaphysics of Science

Science works. No reasonable person can survey the history of physics, chemistry, biology, and medicine without concluding that scientific inquiry has given us genuine knowledge of the natural world — knowledge that was not available before, knowledge that has predictive and technological power that places it in a different category from mere opinion or myth. The electron, the gene, the atom, the cell: these concepts track something real. Or do they? When we say that science has discovered what is really there, we are committed to a philosophical claim — a claim about the relationship between scientific theories and the world — that is more contestable than it appears.

Scientific realism holds that mature, successful scientific theories are at least approximately true descriptions of what exists, including the unobservable entities they posit. Electrons are not merely convenient fictions; they exist independently of our theories, and when we describe their charge and mass we are describing features of something real. The anti-realist disagrees: perhaps our theories are empirically adequate — perhaps they correctly predict all observable phenomena — without being true of the unobservable domain. Perhaps we have no reason to believe in electrons over and above their predictive usefulness.

The debate about scientific realism is the entry point of this unit, but it is not the whole of it. Metaphysics of science encompasses a range of foundational questions that bear on what our best sciences are telling us about the structure of the world. What are natural kinds — the categories that biology, chemistry, and physics use to classify the world? Are they discovered or constructed? Are species, elements, and natural laws genuinely there in nature, or are they artifacts of our conceptual schemes? What does it mean for one science to reduce to another, or for higher-level phenomena to emerge from lower-level constituents in ways that are not simply predictable from the underlying physics? And what does quantum mechanics — the most successful physical theory ever devised — tell us about the nature of reality, given that no consensus interpretation has emerged after a century of effort?

These questions define the four chapters of this unit. We begin with the **scientific realism debate** — its history, its central arguments, and the positions that have emerged. The no-miracles argument for realism contends that the predictive success of science would be miraculous if theoretical entities did not exist; the pessimistic meta-induction against realism observes that past successful theories have repeatedly been shown to be false, so why should current theories be different? Structural realism offers a compromise: we can be realist about the mathematical structure of our theories without committing to specific claims about the underlying ontology.

**Natural kinds** examines the categories that scientific taxonomy employs. The essentialist tradition holds that natural kinds have real essences — intrinsic features that make things the kind they are, and that science progressively discovers. The historicist and pluralist traditions challenge this picture, arguing that natural kinds, especially in biology and the social sciences, may be more context-dependent, interest-relative, and multiply realizable than essentialism allows. The case of psychiatric and social kinds — whether depression or schizophrenia or race are natural kinds — raises particularly sharp questions about the relationship between science and social reality.

**Reduction and emergence** addresses the structure of scientific explanation across levels. Can psychology be reduced to neuroscience? Can neuroscience be reduced to physics? Reduction, if successful, shows that the phenomena at one level are "nothing but" phenomena at a lower level, redescribed. But many philosophers have argued that genuine emergence occurs — that higher-level phenomena have properties and causal powers that cannot be derived from or predicted by lower-level physics alone. Strong emergence is philosophically significant but contentious; weak emergence is less contested but raises the question of whether it amounts to anything more than complexity.

**Philosophy of physics** takes us to the foundations of our deepest scientific theories. Quantum mechanics presents the measurement problem: what happens during measurement? Is the wave function all there is? Does it always evolve unitarily, giving rise to many-worlds? Or does it collapse? Thermodynamics raises the puzzle of why entropy increases in one temporal direction if the underlying microphysics is time-reversal invariant. These are not merely technical questions internal to physics; they are metaphysical questions about the constitution and temporal structure of the physical world.

The metaphysics of science is where the most rigorous empirical inquiry meets the most fundamental philosophical questions. It is, accordingly, where precision matters most.
