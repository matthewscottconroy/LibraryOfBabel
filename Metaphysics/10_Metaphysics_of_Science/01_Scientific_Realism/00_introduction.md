# Scientific Realism

The electron was postulated in the late nineteenth century as a theoretical entity — something invoked to explain observable phenomena but not directly observable itself. By the mid-twentieth century, physicists were manipulating electrons routinely, using them to produce effects in ways that would not be possible if the electron theory were merely a useful fiction. Ian Hacking made this point forcefully: if you can spray electrons, they are real. We should believe in the existence of the unobservable entities posited by our best mature scientific theories, because the success of those theories — in prediction, manipulation, and the detection of new phenomena — would be miraculous if the entities they posit did not exist.

This is the no-miracles argument for scientific realism, and it is powerful. The anti-realist response is the pessimistic meta-induction: history shows that our best scientific theories have repeatedly been shown to be false, their central theoretical entities replaced by different entities in successor theories. Caloric, phlogiston, the luminiferous ether — all were once central theoretical posits of successful theories, all have been abandoned. Why should we trust our current theories any more than our predecessors trusted theirs?

The debate between scientific realism and anti-realism is not merely about the unobservable. It bears on what science is for, what constitutes scientific progress, and how we should understand the relationship between theory and experiment. Structural realism — associated with Worrall — offers a compromise: we should be realists about the mathematical structure that successful theories preserve, not about the specific nature of the entities they describe. This explains the partial retention of structure across theory change while accommodating the changes in the interpreted ontology.

This chapter develops the realism debate carefully, examining the major arguments on each side and the positions that attempt to navigate between full-blooded realism and dismissive anti-realism. The questions raised here bear on every topic in the metaphysics of science that follows.
