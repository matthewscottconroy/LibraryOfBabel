# The Scientific Realism Debate

Is science in the business of discovering how the world really is, or of constructing useful instruments for predicting and controlling experience? This is the central question of the scientific realism debate, and it is not merely an academic puzzle. How we answer it shapes our understanding of what scientific progress consists in, what scientific explanation achieves, and how confident we should be in the theoretical posits of our best scientific theories — entities like electrons, quarks, dark matter, and spacetime.

The scientific realist holds that our best scientific theories are approximately true descriptions of the world, including the unobservable aspects of the world that the theories describe. Electrons, genes, and fields are real; they exist and have the properties our theories ascribe to them. When science makes progress — when it produces theories that are more empirically accurate, more explanatory, more unified — it is making progress toward truth, discovering more and more about the actual structure of the world.

The anti-realist response comes in many forms. The instrumentalist holds that scientific theories are not true or false descriptions of unobservable reality but merely useful tools — instruments for organizing and predicting experience. The question "Are electrons real?" is as confused, on this view, as asking "Is the game of chess real?" — a useful framework, but not a description of anything independent of our practices. Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism is a more sophisticated anti-realism: theories can be empirically adequate (true of what is observable) without being true of the unobservable; and the aim of science should be understood as empirical adequacy rather than truth.

The "pessimistic meta-induction" — the observation that every successful scientific theory in history has eventually been replaced by a different and incompatible theory, suggesting that our current best theories will eventually be replaced too — is the anti-realist's most powerful challenge. The realist must explain why the approximate truth of current theories can be expected to survive the revolutions of future science.

This section examines the scientific realism debate, the arguments on each side, and the sophisticated positions that have been developed in the contemporary literature.
