# The No-Miracles Argument

Maxwell's electromagnetic theory predicted the existence of radio waves before any were produced experimentally. Dirac's relativistic quantum mechanics, developed for other purposes entirely, predicted the existence of antimatter — and Anderson subsequently found the positron. Newtonian mechanics predicted the return of Halley's comet to within days. In each case, a theory constructed to explain one class of phenomena succeeded in predicting quite different phenomena, in domains the theory's creators had not envisioned. We need to ask: what is the best explanation of this pattern?

Hilary Putnam's no-miracles argument (NMA) is the primary positive argument for scientific realism. In his memorable formulation from *Mathematics, Matter and Method* (1975), realism is "the only philosophy that doesn't make the success of science a miracle." The force of the argument derives from the empirical explanandum — it is not merely that science makes true predictions, but that it does so in new and unexpected domains, on the basis of theoretical posits that were not constructed with those domains in mind. The NMA is an inference to the best explanation (IBE):

- **P1**: Mature scientific theories T₁, T₂, … exhibit systematic, novel predictive success S — they correctly predict phenomena in new domains, beyond the evidence used to construct them.
- **P2**: The best explanation of S is that T₁, T₂, … are approximately true and their theoretical terms refer.
- **P3**: We are justified in accepting the best explanation of an explanandum when that explanation is sufficiently superior to alternatives.
- **C**: Therefore, we are justified in believing that T₁, T₂, … are approximately true and that their theoretical terms refer.

## Novel Prediction and the Electron

The history of the electron illustrates the argument's force with particular clarity. Thomson postulated the electron in 1897 to explain cathode ray behavior. Subsequently, the same entity entered independent theoretical frameworks — Bohr's atomic model, Millikan's oil-drop experiment, the quantum mechanics of Heisenberg and Schrödinger — and was used to predict phenomena across entirely disparate domains: atomic spectra, the photoelectric effect, semiconductor behavior, superconductivity. The convergence of independent experimental manipulations on a single set of properties provides strong evidence for reference. Ian Hacking's *Representing and Intervening* (1983) presses this point: "if you can spray them, they are real." When we can routinely manipulate an entity to produce predictable effects, its existence is no longer seriously in doubt. The electron is not merely a convenient theoretical posit; it is a causal factor we intervene upon.

We might ask: what does the anti-realist say here? The most natural response is that theories are merely instruments for prediction, and success in new domains shows that the instrument is robust — not that it tracks hidden reality. But this response, as we will see, struggles to explain why the theoretical apparatus is robust in precisely the domains where the posited entities would be expected to operate.

## The Role of IBE and Its Discontents

The NMA depends essentially on IBE as an epistemically legitimate mode of inference. Peter Lipton's *Inference to the Best Explanation* (2004) provides the most systematic defense: we are justified in accepting the hypothesis that, if true, would provide the most explanatory account of the explanandum, provided that explanation is sufficiently better than rivals.

Van Fraassen challenges IBE on grounds of pragmatic virtue versus truth-tracking. The "best explanation" may be the most useful conceptual tool without being more likely to be true. The anti-realist can grant that theoretical posits are fruitful predictive tools without conceding their literal truth. This is the crux of the disagreement: the NMA works only if being a good explanation is evidence of approximate truth, and that is precisely what the constructive empiricist denies.

Several further objections press harder. The **pessimistic meta-induction** (Laudan, discussed in the next section) raises a historical challenge: many past successful theories posited entities we now believe do not exist, so success does not seem to have tracked truth. The **base rate objection** questions whether approximate truth is the most probable explanation even granting the explanandum. The **Duhemian objection** notes that successful prediction requires the conjunction of core theory plus auxiliary hypotheses — empirical success does not localize confirmation to theoretical posits in particular. And the **structural realist restriction** (Worrall) argues that the NMA supports only knowledge of the mathematical relations encoded in the theory: the electromagnetic field equations are preserved across theory change; the ether is not, so the NMA supports the equations, not the ether.

## Selective Realism as Response

The dominant contemporary realist strategy is *selective realism*: not all components of a successful theory deserve equal credence. Psillos, in *Scientific Realism: How Science Tracks Truth*, argues that the components of a theory responsible for its empirical success are the ones we should be realists about. An entity earns realist commitment when it plays an indispensable explanatory role in generating the theory's novel predictions. Theoretical elements that are idle with respect to empirical success — the ether's mechanical properties beyond the electromagnetic field equations — do not earn such commitment.

This selective strategy attempts to honor the no-miracles intuition while accommodating the historical lessons of theory change: we should be realists about the causally active parts of theories, not their total theoretical content. Whether this strategy succeeds is the central question in the continuing debate.
