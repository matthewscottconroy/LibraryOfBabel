# The Pessimistic Meta-Induction

The history of science contains a remarkable embarrassment for the scientific realist. Phlogiston theory was once not merely a fringe hypothesis but a well-developed framework that successfully explained combustion, calcination, and the properties of metals. Caloric fluid explained heat transfer with quantitative precision. The luminiferous ether — the medium through which electromagnetic waves were supposed to propagate — was postulated by theories that made successful novel predictions. None of these posited entities survived. The phlogiston, caloric fluid, and ether do not exist. Yet the theories that relied on them worked. If scientific success tracked truth, we should not expect this pattern. But the pattern is precisely what we find.

This is Larry Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction (PMI), developed in his 1981 paper "A Confutation of Convergent Realism." The argument is a straightforward inductive generalization over the historical record:

- **P1**: Theory T₁ was empirically successful at time t₁, yet its central theoretical posits U₁ are now believed to be non-referring (the entities do not exist).
- **P2**: This pattern of success-without-reference holds for a long list of theories: T₁, T₂, …, Tₙ — phlogiston theory, caloric theory, ether theories, crystalline spheres, vital force, and more.
- **P3**: By induction over this historical record, we have reason to believe that currently successful theories T* will also prove to have non-referring central posits.
- **C**: Therefore, we should not believe that the central posits of current theories refer, and the no-miracles argument is undermined.

## Assessing the Historical Evidence

The force of the PMI depends on how we read the historical record, and this is where the realist's response begins. Not all failures of reference are alike.

Consider phlogiston. It was not a slightly misdescribed version of oxygen; it carried fundamentally wrong causal stories. Phlogiston was released during combustion rather than combined with oxygen; the direction of the causal arrow was reversed. Compare this with caloric fluid. Caloric had some properties — it was conserved, it flowed from hot to cold — that map onto genuine features of heat transfer, even if the substance ontology was wrong. One might argue for partial reference rather than total failure. Consider also the ether's mechanical properties (rigidity, density): these were never the basis of Maxwell's equations, which describe field propagation in vacuum. The ether's additional mechanical properties were idle with respect to the theory's predictive success from the beginning.

This variability matters. The realist does not need to show that every element of past successful theories referred; only that the elements that actually drove the novel predictions referred, or approximately so.

## The Selective Realism Response

The dominant realist reply is the divide-et-impera strategy associated with Psillos and Kitcher: the PMI targets a straw man. The realist need not be committed to all posits of a successful theory, only those directly responsible for its empirical success. The question is whether the posits driving the novel predictions were retained in successor theories.

Electrons survive from classical to quantum electrodynamics; phlogiston did not survive because it was not the locus of any distinctively successful prediction about combustion products. The conserved quantity structure of caloric — heat flows from high to low temperature, is conserved in calorimetric mixing — is preserved in the transition to kinetic theory. What was abandoned was the substance model; what was preserved was the relational and quantitative structure. Kinetic energy plus the statistical properties of molecular motion do the explanatory work that caloric did, and do it better.

Worrall's structural realism (discussed in the subsequent section) offers a more radical version of this response: what is preserved across theory change is the mathematical structure. Fresnel's equations for the reflection and refraction of polarized light are entirely preserved in Maxwell's electromagnetic theory. The ether ontology was abandoned; the equations were not. The realist should be committed to the mathematical structure, not to the underlying posits.

## Stanford's Deeper Challenge

Kyle Stanford's *Exceeding Our Grasp* (2006) poses a more radical problem that does not simply recycle the Laudanite historical record. Even if selective realism can handle past theory change, Stanford argues that we are systematically unable to conceive of the alternatives to current theories that future science might adopt. We are cognitively limited in such a way that we cannot imagine what successor theories will look like — just as past scientists could not imagine quantum mechanics or general relativity. The "problem of unconceived alternatives" is not a historical induction but an epistemic humility argument: the very cognitive capacities that make us good at constructing theories also make us blind to the full range of alternatives that evidence is compatible with.

The realist faces this challenge without a fully satisfying response. Historical track record is limited evidence about future cognitive limitations. Whether this constitutes a decisive objection or a counsel of appropriate modesty depends on how one weighs the argument's general epistemic structure against the particular successes of the sciences.

## Significance

The PMI remains the most powerful objection to scientific realism. The debate it has generated has produced increasingly refined accounts of what the realist is committed to — not naive commitment to all theoretical posits, but a selective, structurally-informed, and historically sensitive commitment to the causally active elements of our best scientific theories. Whether any version of this selective realism can fully neutralize the PMI while preserving a robust enough realist commitment is still open. The pessimist and the realist agree on the historical facts; they disagree about what those facts establish, and about whether the entities driving scientific success today have better grounds for trust than phlogiston ever did.
