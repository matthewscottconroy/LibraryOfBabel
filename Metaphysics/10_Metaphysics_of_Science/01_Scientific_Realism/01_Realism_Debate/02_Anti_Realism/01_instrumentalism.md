# Instrumentalism

Newtonian mechanics was the crowning achievement of the scientific revolution — two centuries of successful prediction, applied to everything from artillery trajectories to planetary orbits. Then it was revealed to be false for objects moving at velocities approaching the speed of light and for strong gravitational fields. One lesson to draw from this is that science converges on truth and the Newtonian approximation was just that — an approximation. Another lesson is more deflationary: perhaps theories should never be evaluated for truth at all, only for predictive power. On this second reading, Einstein did not show that Newton was wrong; he showed that a more powerful instrument was available.

This deflationary lesson, developed into a philosophical position, is instrumentalism. Instrumentalism holds that scientific theories are instruments for prediction and control rather than true or false descriptions of reality. Asking whether electrons "really exist" is, on this view, a misguided question: the electron concept is a theoretical tool that helps us predict observable phenomena, and its "truth" consists only in its predictive utility.

The position has a distinguished philosophical lineage. Ernst Mach argued that scientific theories are economical summaries of sensory experience, to be judged solely by their capacity to compress and organize experiential data. Pierre Duhem, in *The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory* (1906), held that physical theories are classifications of experimental laws rather than explanations of phenomena — they organize observational knowledge without revealing hidden reality. John Dewey's pragmatism generalized the point: all concepts are instruments for managing experience.

## The Structure of the Instrumentalist Commitment

Instrumentalism involves three interlocking commitments. First, theoretical terms are in principle eliminable: any genuinely cognitive content expressible using theoretical vocabulary can, in principle, be re-expressed using only observational terms. Talk of electrons is shorthand for talk of pointer readings, cloud chamber tracks, and current flows. Craig's theorem in mathematical logic supports the technical claim — for any first-order theory with both theoretical and observational vocabulary, there exists a recursively axiomatizable theory in purely observational vocabulary with the same observational theorems. But as Hempel noted, the Craig re-axiomatization is practically useless: the observational core of an interesting theory is typically infinite and non-systematizable without the theoretical vocabulary. Theoretical terms do real cognitive and organizational work.

Second, predictive success is the only epistemic standard. A theory is acceptable when and because it generates accurate predictions across a wide domain. Correspondence to hidden reality is not an additional requirement. Third, the instrumentalist is agnostic about unobservable existence — neither asserting nor denying that electrons, quarks, and fields exist. These are simply not the right questions to ask about theoretical posits.

Formally: let T be a scientific theory with theoretical vocabulary V_T and observational vocabulary V_O. Instrumentalism maintains that for every empirically testable claim of T, there exists an observationally equivalent claim expressible in V_O alone; that the cognitive content of T just is this set of observationally equivalent claims; and that theoretical terms contribute no additional cognitive content — they are heuristic tools, not referential terms.

## Systematic Success as the Central Problem

Contemporary instrumentalism is less common than it once was, partly because it struggles to explain the specific character of scientific success. If theories are mere instruments, why do they work so systematically across entirely new domains? The electron theory of the 1890s was not designed to predict the behavior of semiconductors in transistors — but it does. This cross-domain success looks more like tracking real structure than like a fortunate choice of predictive instrument.

Richard Boyd's explanatory coherence argument presses this point: if theories are instruments, the fact that we can apply the same theoretical vocabulary across cosmology, chemistry, and biology — and get novel predictions right — is not explained. Realism, which says the theoretical vocabulary tracks real kinds and causal structures, explains this success naturally. The instrumentalist has to say the convergence is a fortunate coincidence; the realist says it is what we would expect if the theories were approximately true.

## The Relationship to Constructive Empiricism

A softer anti-realism, associated with van Fraassen's constructive empiricism, departs from traditional instrumentalism in an important way. Traditional instrumentalism says "electrons exist" is not truth-apt, or is merely shorthand for observational conditionals. Van Fraassen says "electrons exist" is truth-apt — the sentence has a determinate truth value — but we have no justification for believing it true. Constructive empiricism preserves the meaningfulness of theoretical claims while suspending belief in them.

The distinction matters: constructive empiricism is a more philosophically careful position, because it does not require the difficult claim that theoretical sentences lack cognitive content. It simply restricts rational epistemic commitment to the observable domain. Whether this restriction can be maintained in the face of the systematic success problem is the central question it faces — a question taken up in the next file.

Instrumentalism nevertheless retains appeal for those skeptical of inference to the best explanation and for those who take seriously the underdetermination of theory by evidence. If any finite body of observational evidence is compatible with infinitely many inequivalent theories, there is never a uniquely correct theory to be realist about. The instrumentalist argues that we are always choosing among functionally equivalent instruments — and utility, not truth, is the appropriate criterion.
