# Constructive Empiricism

Suppose we accept everything the scientific realist says about how well quantum electrodynamics predicts experimental results. Accept that the predictions are extraordinarily accurate, that the theory has succeeded in novel domains, that it is the most precisely confirmed theory in the history of science. What, exactly, does all this establish? The realist's answer is: that the theory is approximately true, including its claims about electrons and photons and quantum fields. But consider a more cautious answer — that the theory accurately describes the observable phenomena — and ask whether this more cautious answer is consistent with everything we actually know. If it is, then perhaps the cautious answer is all we are entitled to.

This cautious answer is Bas van Fraassen's constructive empiricism, developed in *The Scientific Image* (1980) and elaborated in *Laws and Symmetry* (1989) and *The Empirical Stance* (2002). Constructive empiricism holds that the aim of science is empirical adequacy — accurately representing observable phenomena — not truth about unobservable reality. We can accept a theory as empirically adequate while remaining agnostic about whether its posits exist.

## The Central Framework

Van Fraassen's key distinction is between accepting a theory and believing it. To accept a theory is to use it, to apply it in reasoning and prediction, and to commit to taking its observable consequences seriously. To believe it is to hold it true. Van Fraassen maintains that the rational scientist accepts theories but need not believe them beyond the claim of empirical adequacy.

**Empirical adequacy**: A theory T is empirically adequate iff it "saves the phenomena" — the observable consequences of T are true. T correctly describes the observable world, including observable entities, events, and regularities.

This is formalized model-theoretically. Let T be a theory with a set of models M(T). Let W be the observable world — the collection of actual observable events and regularities. Constructive empiricism maintains:

- **Empirical adequacy**: ∃m ∈ M(T) such that W is isomorphic to the empirical substructure of m.
- **Agnosticism about unobservables**: Neither affirm nor deny that the non-observable parts of m represent reality.

The observable/unobservable distinction bears considerable weight here. Observables are entities and processes that can, in principle, be directly detected by the unaided senses under suitable conditions. Van Fraassen acknowledges that the line is vague and context-sensitive but insists it is not arbitrary. Cells are observable: a suitably positioned human could observe them with the naked eye at the right scale, or with a light microscope that merely extends natural perception. Electrons are not observable: no configuration of physical circumstances would enable a human to see an electron directly. Electron tracks in bubble chambers are observable; electrons are not. The philosophical import is straightforward — our epistemic commitments should be proportioned to what falls within the scope of actual or possible observational access.

## Pragmatic Virtue and the Challenge to IBE

A key feature of constructive empiricism is its epistemic humility: van Fraassen does not say that electrons do not exist; he says we cannot know whether they do. The rational epistemic attitude toward theoretical claims about unobservables is suspension of judgment — the "agnostic" stance. This is explicitly non-dogmatic: if evidence could establish the existence of an unobservable by making it observable, constructive empiricism would permit belief. The issue is not impossibility but the absence of warrant.

This generates a direct challenge to the no-miracles argument. Van Fraassen argues that the features leading us to prefer one theory over another — simplicity, unifying power, explanatory depth — are pragmatic virtues rather than truth-indicators. A beautiful, simple theory that unifies many phenomena may be empirically adequate without being literally true of the unobservable world. IBE selects theories on grounds of pragmatic virtue; this is not evidence that those theories are true of domains beyond observation.

Van Fraassen reinforces this with a selection argument that echoes evolutionary reasoning: we observe only successful theories because unsuccessful ones are not accepted and do not persist in the scientific community. The success of current theories is not surprising — we observe them precisely because they are successful. This does not require the theories to be true; it requires only that they have survived a selection process. The appearance of the NMA explanandum is itself an artifact of theory selection.

## The Primary Objections

The vagueness of the observable/unobservable distinction presses hard here. With electron microscopes, X-ray crystallography, and radio telescopes, we "observe" things technically beyond unaided-sense perception. Is Jupiter's moon Io observable? It can be seen with a small telescope. Is the DNA double helix observable? It can be imaged by atomic force microscopy. Van Fraassen holds that extension of sense perception is different in kind from theoretical inference; critics dispute this. If what counts as "observation" is not sharply drawn, the distinction between realism and constructive empiricism blurs.

Churchland presses the symmetry problem: the same arguments that give us epistemic access to tables and trees — they affect us causally, their postulation explains regularities in experience — apply with equal force to electrons. If any detection process that produces reliable evidence counts as observation, the distinction collapses. And the argument from scientific practice cuts against van Fraassen's account: when physicists say they have "discovered" a new quark, or biologists say they have identified the molecular structure of a protein, they are not merely claiming empirical adequacy. Van Fraassen's account seems to misrepresent the epistemic attitudes of working scientists.

Even granting the distinction, the challenge returns: why does a theory developed using fictional theoretical posits manage to generate accurate predictions about new observable phenomena? If the posits are not approximately tracking real structure, their predictive success in new domains is precisely what Putnam called a miracle. Constructive empiricism dissolves the NMA's explanandum by denying that we should seek a deeper explanation of success — but this may be less a solution than a refusal to engage the puzzle.

Despite these objections, constructive empiricism remains the most philosophically sophisticated and internally consistent anti-realist position. Van Fraassen's articulation of the empirical adequacy standard, and his careful attention to the distinction between pragmatic and epistemic virtue, have sharpened the realism debate considerably — whatever one ultimately thinks about the position's tenability.
