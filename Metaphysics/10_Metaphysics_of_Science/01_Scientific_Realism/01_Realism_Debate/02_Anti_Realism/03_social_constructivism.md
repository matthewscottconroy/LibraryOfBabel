# Social Constructivism

When the structure of DNA was determined in 1953, the discovery emerged from a specific institutional context: competitive pressure between Cambridge and Linus Pauling's group, access restrictions on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray data, and the social dynamics of a particular laboratory at a particular moment in history. Social constructivists note all this and draw a striking conclusion: not just that the discovery happened to occur in a social context, but that what counts as the discovery, and even what the facts are, is constituted by social processes. Remove the social context and you do not get the same facts.

Social constructivism about science holds that scientific knowledge is not simply discovered from nature but is constructed through social processes — negotiations, conventions, power relations, and cultural values among scientists. The social constructivist typically challenges the view that scientific theories are constrained by nature alone; social factors play a constitutive role in determining what counts as scientific fact.

The tradition developed in the sociology of scientific knowledge (SSK) in the 1970s and 1980s, associated with the Edinburgh School (David Bloor, Barry Barnes) and the Bath School (Harry Collins, Trevor Pinch). Bloor's "strong programme," articulated in *Knowledge and Social Imagery* (1976), proposed four principles: causality (explain scientific beliefs by their causes), impartiality (the same causes explain true and false beliefs alike), symmetry (the same causes explain successful and unsuccessful beliefs alike), and reflexivity (the programme applies to itself). The symmetry principle is philosophically significant. Traditional accounts explain successful beliefs by their truth and unsuccessful beliefs by social factors — prejudice, ideology, self-interest. The strong programme insists this is asymmetric without justification: social causes operate equally in producing both kinds of beliefs, and the truth of a belief is not what causes its acceptance.

Latour and Woolgar's *Laboratory Life* (1979) documented the social processes by which scientific facts are "constructed" in a laboratory setting — through negotiation, inscription devices, controversy, and stabilization through consensus. Their ethnographic study of the Salk Institute biology laboratory was presented as evidence that scientific reality is a social artifact, not an independently existing structure that investigators uncover.

## Varieties and Their Fates

Several distinct positions travel under the constructivist label. Strong constructivism holds that scientific facts are constituted by social processes; the natural world does not constrain scientific theories in any way that could not in principle have gone differently given different social arrangements. Moderate constructivism holds that social factors influence which questions get asked, how evidence is interpreted, and which theories are accepted — but that the natural world imposes real constraints that social factors cannot override. Scientific knowledge is socially mediated but not socially constituted. Constructivism about categories accepts that the natural world exists independently while holding that the categories we use to describe it are socially constructed — categories like "depression" or "race" are social constructs that may not carve nature at its joints, even though underlying biological processes are real.

Most philosophers of science can accept moderate constructivism and constructivism about categories. Strong constructivism is far more contentious, and for good reason.

The self-undermining objection is powerful: if all knowledge claims are socially constructed rather than genuinely truth-apt, then the claim that all knowledge is socially constructed is itself merely socially constructed. It has no more authority than any competing view. Bloor attempted to handle this through "reflexivity" — applying the strong programme to itself — but critics argue this merely underscores the view's inability to make substantive claims about the actual nature of scientific knowledge.

A second objection is equally compelling. Technologies based on scientific theories work the same way regardless of the cultural context in which they were developed. Quantum mechanics, developed in the specific social context of early twentieth-century European physics, produces transistors, lasers, MRI machines, and GPS satellites that function equally well in any society. If scientific claims were merely socially constructed artifacts of European academic culture, there is no principled explanation of why they should work reliably in entirely different cultural settings.

## What Social Context Cannot Explain Away

The conflict between realism and strong social constructivism erupted most publicly in the "Science Wars" of the 1990s, typified by Alan Sokal's deliberate hoax article published in *Social Text* (1996), designed to demonstrate the lack of intellectual standards in some parts of science studies. The episode provoked a broader conversation about the relationship between scientific authority, social context, and epistemic standards.

The most balanced assessment is that the sociological and philosophical perspectives need not conflict if they target different questions. Sociology asks how beliefs come to be accepted; philosophy asks whether beliefs are justified. The process of scientific discovery is deeply social — hypotheses are generated in contexts, interpreted by communities, validated by institutions — but the epistemic warrant for a scientific claim may nevertheless derive from its relationship to evidence rather than from its social provenance. These are distinct.

Helen Longino's "contextual empiricism" is one developed version of a middle path: social values inevitably shape background assumptions in science, but this does not undermine the epistemic authority of science as long as the processes of criticism and peer review are sufficiently pluralistic and open. The legitimate insights of SSK — that scientific communities are social organizations, that cognitive norms are partly socially transmitted, that scientists have interests that can distort research — do not require the radical epistemological conclusions of strong constructivism. They are best preserved within a framework that also takes seriously the truth-seeking character of scientific inquiry and the real constraints imposed by the natural world.
