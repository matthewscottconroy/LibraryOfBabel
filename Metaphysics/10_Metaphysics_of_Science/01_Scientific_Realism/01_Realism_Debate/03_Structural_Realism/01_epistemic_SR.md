# Epistemic Structural Realism

Fresnel's nineteenth-century optics described light as a wave propagating through a mechanical ether. His equations for the reflection and refraction of polarized light at a surface were confirmed with great accuracy. Then Maxwell's electromagnetic theory arrived, and the ether was eliminated entirely — light is an electromagnetic wave in a field that requires no medium. The equations, however, were not eliminated. Fresnel's formulas survived intact as limiting cases of Maxwell's theory. The substance changed; the mathematical structure remained.

This observation drives John Worrall's epistemic structural realism (ESR), introduced in his 1989 paper "Structural Realism: The Best of Both Worlds?" ESR holds that science gives us knowledge of the *structure* of the world but not of the intrinsic nature of the entities underlying that structure. We know the mathematical and relational properties of physical entities — the equations that describe how they behave and how they relate to each other — but we do not know what those entities are "intrinsically."

## The Worrall Position and Its Historical Precedents

Worrall develops ESR as a middle path between scientific realism and constructive empiricism. The realist is right that science makes genuine epistemic progress — that scientific theories reveal something about the deep structure of reality, not merely its observable surface. The anti-realist is right that the pessimistic meta-induction gives reason to doubt claims about the intrinsic nature of unobservable entities. ESR satisfies both desiderata: we get genuine knowledge, but it is structural knowledge. The structure was right; the underlying nature was not.

Worrall notes that this view has antecedents in Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis* (1902). Poincaré argued that the relations between things — the mathematical relations captured in scientific equations — are the genuine content of scientific knowledge, while claims about the nature of the things themselves are conventional or empty. This point was developed by Bertrand Russell in *The Analysis of Matter* (1927): physics gives us knowledge of the structural properties of the physical world (its abstract relational organization), not of the intrinsic qualities of the entities that instantiate those structures. Physics tells us the form of the world, not its content.

The formal characterization follows naturally. Let T be a physical theory with a mathematical structure S(T) — a set of mathematical objects, relations, and functions — and an intended physical interpretation I mapping elements of S(T) onto physical entities and properties. ESR maintains that scientific knowledge from T consists in knowledge that the world has a structure isomorphic (or homomorphic) to S(T), that this knowledge does not extend to the intrinsic nature of the physical entities instantiating the structure, and that across theory change from T to T', when S(T) is embedded in S(T') as a limiting case, structural knowledge is preserved and accumulated. In particle physics, the physical content of the Standard Model is what is invariant under the gauge symmetry group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1). This invariant structure is what ESR says we genuinely know.

We can see the pattern in multiple transitions. Classical mechanics describes particles with definite positions and momenta in phase space; quantum mechanics replaces this with state vectors in Hilbert space. The algebraic structure of Poisson brackets (classical) corresponds to commutator brackets (quantum), preserving structural relations while changing the nature of the entities. Maxwell's equations are structurally invariant under Lorentz transformations; special relativity reconceives space and time but preserves those equations exactly.

## Two Objections

The most prominent challenge is the "hidden nature" or "no relations without relata" objection, developed by Psillos and others. If structural relations hold between entities, those entities must have some intrinsic nature in virtue of which they can stand in relations. A relation is a polyadic entity that applies to things — and the things it applies to must be something. Structure cannot float free of substance.

If ESR accepts that there are entities underlying the structure but denies that we can know their intrinsic nature, ESR resembles Kantian noumena: things in themselves that are causally operative but epistemically inaccessible. This is metaphysically unsatisfying. We posit a hidden realm that does no explanatory work beyond serving as the substrate for the known structure. The uncomfortable position motivates the move to ontic structural realism: if we cannot know the intrinsic nature, perhaps there is none to know.

A further objection comes from M. H. A. Newman's 1928 argument against Russell's structuralism, adapted for ESR by Psillos. Newman proved that any collection of objects can be given any structural property whatsoever, as long as the right number of objects is present. If ESR's knowledge claim is purely that the world has some structure isomorphic to the theory's mathematical structure, this claim is nearly trivially satisfied by any world of the right cardinality. The claim becomes substantive only if we specify which relations the mathematical structure represents — but specifying which relations requires going beyond purely structural claims to claims about the intrinsic character of those relations. Worrall and Zahar have attempted to respond by restricting the claim to "physically significant" or "causally active" relations, but this response may presuppose enough about the nature of entities to undermine the purely structural ambitions of ESR.

These two objections together — the hidden relata problem and the Newman problem — define the principal challenges that ontic structural realism is designed to address. Whether it succeeds in doing so, and at what metaphysical cost, is the subject of the next file.
