# Ontic Structural Realism

Suppose two electrons are placed in the same quantum state. Classical mechanics would say there are two distinct particles — distinguishable in principle by their different positions. Quantum mechanics disagrees. The wave function for two identical particles is symmetrized (for bosons) or antisymmetrized (for fermions) in such a way that there is simply no fact about which particle is which. They are not distinct individuals who happen to be qualitatively alike; the quantum formalism offers no basis for that distinction. What we have, it seems, are relations — a combined quantum state with certain symmetry properties — without genuinely distinct relata.

Ontic structural realism (OSR), developed most fully by James Ladyman and Don Ross in *Every Thing Must Go* (2007) and defended in different forms by Steven French and Décio Krause, draws the radical conclusion from this kind of case: the structural features of science are not merely what we can know but what there is. There are no individuals with intrinsic natures underlying the structure. There is only structure. Physical reality is, at its most fundamental level, purely relational.

## Motivation from Quantum Mechanics

The statistical behavior of quantum particles is the primary driver. Bosons, like photons, satisfy Bose-Einstein statistics: when two bosons can occupy a state, the probability distribution over combined states differs from what classical probability theory predicts, as if the particles are not genuinely distinct. Fermions, like electrons, satisfy Fermi-Dirac statistics and the Pauli exclusion principle: no two fermions can occupy the same quantum state.

French and Krause argue in *Identity in Physics* (2006) that quantum particles are best understood as "non-individuals" — entities that lack the identity conditions required to count as individual objects in the classical sense. The wave function for two identical particles is a symmetrized combination that treats them as indistinguishable. There is no matter of fact about which particle is which; there are only the combined quantum state and its structural properties.

OSR takes the argument a step further: not only are quantum particles non-individuals, but there are no individual objects at the fundamental level at all. The fundamental ontology of physics consists entirely of structural features — mathematical relations, symmetry groups, gauge fields — rather than objects instantiating properties. Objects, if they figure in the ontology at all, are derivative; they are "nodes" in structural networks, individuated by their structural role rather than by any intrinsic nature. In quantum field theory, this is even more natural. The basic ontology is fields, not particles. Particles are excitations of fields — localized disturbances in an underlying quantum field — and the electron has no sharp boundary, no intrinsic identity independent of its field-theoretic context. OSR reads QFT as supporting the view that the fields, defined by their transformation properties under relevant gauge symmetries, are fundamental, while individual particles are derivative and transient.

OSR has natural affinities with mathematical structuralism (Resnik, Shapiro): mathematical objects like numbers are positions in abstract structures, not self-subsisting Platonic items. The number 2 is whatever plays the role of 2 in a structure satisfying the Peano axioms. OSR extends this insight to physical reality. "Being an electron" is a matter of occupying a certain structural position in the quantum field — having mass mₑ, charge -e, spin ½, and the relevant interaction structure. There is no further question of what the electron is "intrinsically" beyond the structural description.

Ladyman and Ross develop their view within a "naturalistic metaphysics" constrained by fundamental physics. Their "Principle of Naturalistic Closure" holds that metaphysics should be continuous with and constrained by the best science. Higher-level structures are accommodated through a "real patterns" approach, derived from Dennett: higher-level structures are real insofar as they are patterns in the lower-level structure that cannot be more efficiently described at the lower level.

## Primary Challenges

The most fundamental objection is metaphysical: relations are paradigmatically polyadic entities — they hold between relata. A relation without relata is not a relation but a property, and properties themselves seem to require property-bearers. To assert that there are only relations, with no non-relational items relating, appears to be a category mistake. French's response is that OSR does not eliminate relata but reconceives them as "thin" objects defined by their structural role — positions in chess characterized only by their roles in the game, not by any intrinsic feature of the pieces. Critics reply with a dilemma: either the thin objects have some intrinsic features (even if minimal), in which case OSR is not purely structuralist; or they have no intrinsic features, in which case it is unclear what "object" means.

The underdetermination problem cuts in an unexpected direction. OSR is motivated partly by quantum mechanical underdetermination — quantum particles cannot be assigned determinate identity conditions. But this underdetermination cuts both ways: we are underdetermined between an interpretation that takes particles as individuals (with weaker versions of identity) and one that takes them as non-individuals. Quantum mechanics does not uniquely mandate the OSR ontology.

Spacetime structure in general relativity provides a further test case. On OSR, spacetime points are not genuine individuals but positions in the spacetime structure. But what is spacetime structure without the points? The manifold is defined as a collection of points with topological and metrical relations; eliminating the points eliminates the manifold. OSR advocates typically respond that the metric field is the genuine structural entity, with points defined as intersections of metric relations — but this response is technically complex and has not achieved consensus.

Finally, even granting that quantum particles are non-individuals at the fundamental level, ordinary objects — tables, cats, persons — seem to be genuine individuals. If OSR holds only at the fundamental level, it provides no full account of how individual-seeming objects arise from the fundamental non-individual structure. The "real patterns" account gestures at this connection but leaves considerable work to be done.
