# Important Terms and Ideas: The Scientific Realism Debate

**Scientific Realism**
The view that our best scientific theories are approximately true and that the unobservable entities they posit (electrons, genes, fields) genuinely exist. Scientific realism holds that the success of science provides evidence for the truth of its theoretical claims, not just for its predictive utility.

**Constructive Empiricism**
Van Fraassen's anti-realist alternative: the aim of science is empirical adequacy—producing theories that correctly describe observable phenomena. We should accept theories as empirically adequate without believing in their unobservable posits. Belief should track the observable; agnosticism is appropriate for the unobservable.

**The No-Miracles Argument**
Putnam's argument for realism: the predictive success of science (especially novel predictions) would be a miracle—an astronomically improbable coincidence—if our theories were not at least approximately true. The best explanation of success is approximate truth.

**The Pessimistic Meta-Induction**
Laudan's historical argument: the history of science shows a pattern of successful theories later overturned, with their unobservable posits abandoned (caloric, phlogiston, ether). By induction over this history, our current successful theories are probably false in their theoretical claims too.

**Structural Realism**
Worrall's position: we should be realist about the mathematical structure of theories (the equations, the relations they describe) but remain agnostic about the intrinsic nature of what instantiates that structure. Structure is preserved across theory change; unobservable nature is not.

**Empirical Adequacy**
Van Fraassen's criterion for accepting a scientific theory: a theory is empirically adequate if what it says about observable phenomena is true—it correctly describes all past, present, and future observable behavior. This is a weaker standard than truth about unobservables.
