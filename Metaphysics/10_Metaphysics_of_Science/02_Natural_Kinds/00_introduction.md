# Natural Kinds

When Mendeleev arranged the elements in his periodic table, he was not merely organizing data by convenience; he was carving nature at its joints — grouping elements according to properties that reflect genuine shared structure in the world. When biologists classify organisms into species, they claim to be discovering real groupings that exist prior to and independent of their classifications. When physicians diagnose diseases, they assume that the diagnostic categories they use — depression, schizophrenia, diabetes — carve the biological and psychological landscape according to real divisions rather than imposing arbitrary boundaries.

This assumption — that the world has a natural kind structure that our best classifications track — is the central claim of natural kinds realism. Natural kinds are categories whose members genuinely resemble each other in ways that go beyond our classificatory interests; they are the kinds that support inductive inference, ground explanatory laws, and figure in the causal-mechanical explanations of science. Gold, water, electron, tiger — these are natural kinds because their members share deep, explanatorily relevant properties that scientific investigation progressively reveals.

The challenge to natural kinds realism comes from multiple directions. If natural kinds have real essences — intrinsic, necessary properties that all and only members of the kind share — then we need an account of what these essences are and how we come to know them. But the history of science suggests that natural kind boundaries are often revised, and that what counts as the same kind is more context- and interest-sensitive than strict essentialism allows. Species, in particular, resist essentialist treatment: there may be no intrinsic property that all and only members of a biological species share.

Psychiatric and social kinds raise the sharpest questions. Is depression a natural kind with a biological essence? Or is it a heterogeneous cluster of conditions that our diagnostic practices group together for practical reasons? Is race a natural kind? These are not merely academic questions; the answers bear on how we understand disease, how we design treatments, and how we interpret social inequalities.

This chapter examines natural kinds theory and its limits, working from the clearest cases (elements, molecules) through the contested cases (biological species, psychiatric conditions, social kinds).
