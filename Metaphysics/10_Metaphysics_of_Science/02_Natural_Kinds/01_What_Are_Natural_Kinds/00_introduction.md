# What Are Natural Kinds?

The concept of a natural kind marks what we believe to be a real distinction in the world — a distinction that nature has made, not one that we have imposed. Gold is a natural kind; things-I-noticed-this-morning is not. The distinction matters scientifically: inductive generalizations project only over natural kinds (learning about gold teaches us about other gold samples; learning about things-I-noticed-this-morning teaches us nothing about similar collections). It matters semantically: natural kind terms like "water" and "gold" have their reference fixed not by description but by something like ostension and the nature of the referred-to kind.

But what makes a kind natural? The essentialist answer — a natural kind is one defined by a real essence, a set of intrinsic, necessary properties that all and only members of the kind possess — has great intuitive appeal and significant philosophical tradition behind it. The discovery that water is H₂O is not merely the discovery of a correlation; it is the discovery of water's essence. Any sample of water is essentially H₂O; any sample of H₂O is necessarily water. The natural kind is defined by its essential constitution.

Several dimensions of difficulty complicate the essentialist picture. In biology, species seem not to have essential properties in the traditional sense: any given property of a species (even a molecular one) may be absent from some members, and the history of evolution shows that what counts as the "essential" property of a species can change over time. In chemistry, the boundaries of even the most paradigmatic natural kinds (like elements) are less sharp than essentialism suggests: isotopes of the same element differ in mass and in many physical properties. In social science, the question of whether social kinds (races, genders, mental disorders) have real essences is deeply contested.

This section examines the philosophical analysis of natural kinds, the essentialist tradition, and the alternative accounts that have been developed in response to biological and social examples.
