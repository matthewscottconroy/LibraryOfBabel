# Essentialism About Natural Kinds

Before the development of modern chemistry, "water" was associated with a cluster of familiar properties: the odorless, colorless liquid that fills oceans, quenches thirst, dissolves salts, freezes and boils at characteristic temperatures. These are the properties speakers used to identify water. But when chemistry revealed that water is H₂O — a molecule consisting of two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom by polar covalent bonds — something striking followed: the observable properties of water turned out to be consequences of that molecular structure. The polarity of the molecule explains the high boiling point, the dissolution of ionic compounds, the cohesion of water droplets, and the anomalous expansion of ice. The observable properties are symptoms of the molecular structure, not the essence itself. The essence grounds the properties.

This is the central claim of essentialism about natural kinds: each natural kind has an essence — a set of properties necessary and sufficient for membership in the kind, and from which the other properties of its members follow. The position is associated with Kripke and Putnam's semantic essentialism and with the scientific essentialism developed by Brian Ellis, Caroline Lierse, and Alexander Bird.

## Kripke-Putnam Essentialism

Saul Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1972) and Hilary Putnam's "The Meaning of Meaning" (1975) jointly established the contemporary framework. Natural kind terms like "gold," "water," and "tiger" are rigid designators — terms that refer to the same kind in every possible world. The reference is fixed by the actual properties of paradigm members, not by the descriptions associated with the terms in users' minds.

Kripke's argument: if "water" rigidly designates the actual substance with molecular structure H₂O, then any possible world containing a substance that lacks H₂O but resembles water phenomenologically contains something that is not water. Water is necessarily H₂O, not contingently. This gives us a category of necessary a posteriori truths: truths that are necessary once discovered, though they could not be known a priori.

Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment presses the point further. On Twin Earth, there is a liquid that behaves exactly like water but has molecular structure XYZ rather than H₂O. Despite phenomenological and functional indiscernibility, what Twin Earthers call "water" is not water. The extension of "water" is determined by the actual chemical structure, not by functional role or phenomenal appearance. Natural kind terms pick out kinds by their intrinsic constitution. This is the famous conclusion that meaning "ain't in the head": the reference of a natural kind term is partly determined by facts about the world, not solely by facts about the speaker's mental states.

## Scientific Essentialism: Ellis and Bird

Brian Ellis and Caroline Lierse developed a systematic scientific essentialism in the 1990s, elaborated by Ellis in *Scientific Essentialism* (2001). The core thesis: the fundamental natural kinds recognized by science have essences that consist in their intrinsic causal powers and dispositions. The essence of gold (element 79) includes its disposition to conduct electricity, to dissolve in aqua regia, to resist corrosion, to have a characteristic spectrum, and to undergo specific nuclear reactions. These are not contingent features gold merely happens to have; they are necessary features that gold must have, given what gold is.

Alexander Bird's *Nature's Metaphysics* (2007) develops the connection between essentialism and dispositionalism. If natural kinds are essentially characterized by their causal powers, and causal powers are irreducible dispositions — not Humean categorical bases — then the laws of nature express necessary truths about the powers of kinds rather than contingent regularities. Laws are metaphysically necessary in the sense that any world containing the same natural kinds would have the same laws. Electrons must repel each other (they could not fail to do so without ceasing to be electrons), and this is why the Coulomb law holds. Laws are not imposed from outside on an otherwise flexible nature; they are internal to the natures of the kinds.

The formal structure: let K be a natural kind and E(K) = {P₁, P₂, …, Pₙ} be K's essence. Scientific essentialism maintains:

- **Necessary membership**: □(∀x)(x ∈ K → x has all properties in E(K))
- **Sufficient membership**: □(∀x)(x has all properties in E(K) → x ∈ K)
- **Grounding**: The non-essential properties of K-members are grounded in E(K) — they obtain in virtue of the essential properties.
- **Modal stability**: The essential properties of K are preserved across possible worlds: any world containing a K contains entities with E(K).

## Objections

Three objections press hard. First, underdetermination of essence: the Kripke-Putnam framework fixes reference by actual molecular structure, but there are choices about which structural features count as essential. Is H₂O the essence of water, or is some more fundamental quantum mechanical description? How do we distinguish essential from accidental structural features? The framework tells us that there is an essence without telling us how to read it off from the physics.

Second, multiple realizability: if higher-level kinds (pain, belief, gene) can be realized by multiple lower-level structures, they cannot have a single intrinsic essence. Pain is not identical to C-fiber firing because pain can be realized differently in different organisms. Essentialism works well for chemical and physical kinds but faces challenges for biological and psychological kinds — which is precisely where the HPC framework (discussed next) enters.

Third, Quine's nominalism: Quine argued that there are no genuine essences; kind membership is always relative to a conceptual scheme and cannot be made scheme-independent. Natural kinds are at best useful idealizations — good for scientific practice — without deep metaphysical significance. This challenge has not been widely accepted by contemporary metaphysicians, but it represents an important tradition of resistance to essentialist metaphysics that any full account must address.
