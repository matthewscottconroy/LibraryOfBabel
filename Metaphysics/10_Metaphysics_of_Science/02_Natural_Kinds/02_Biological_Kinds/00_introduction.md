# Biological Kinds

Biological taxonomy — the classification of living things into species, genera, families, and higher taxa — raises distinctive philosophical challenges for the theory of natural kinds. Traditional essentialism assumes that natural kind members share intrinsic, necessary properties; but biological organisms within a species are notoriously variable, no single property is shared by all members, and the boundaries between species are often indeterminate. Evolution means that the properties of a species can change over time; hybridization means that an individual can have features of multiple species; and horizontal gene transfer in bacteria complicates the notion of a single ancestral lineage.

The "species problem" — the difficulty of defining what a species is — reflects these challenges. Over two dozen species concepts have been proposed, each capturing different aspects of what biologists care about in taxonomic classification. The biological species concept (Mayr) defines species by reproductive isolation: organisms belong to the same species if they can interbreed and produce fertile offspring. The phylogenetic species concept defines species by common ancestry and shared derived characters. The ecological species concept defines species by their ecological niche. Each concept carves nature differently.

The philosophical question is whether species are natural kinds in any robust sense. Ghiselin and Hull argued in the 1970s that species are not natural kinds at all; they are historical entities — individuals with temporal parts, not classes with essential properties. Tiger is not a property; it is a concrete lineage of organisms related by descent. This "species are individuals" thesis has important implications: if species are individuals, then claims about species ("Tigers are carnivores") are not general claims about a kind but particular claims about a historically specific lineage.

Contemporary philosophers of biology have developed "HPC" (homeostatic property cluster) theory as an alternative to essentialism: natural kinds are defined not by necessary and sufficient conditions but by clusters of properties that tend to co-occur due to underlying causal mechanisms.
