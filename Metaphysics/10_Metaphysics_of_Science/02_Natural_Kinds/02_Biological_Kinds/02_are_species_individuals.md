# Are Species Individuals?

We say "tigers are orange" the way we say "gold is yellow" — as if we are predicating a property of a kind. But notice what else we say: that tigers *evolved*, that tigers might *go extinct*, that tigers *diverged* from a common ancestor. These are not things one says about abstract kinds. Gold the element did not evolve; it is not going extinct; it did not diverge from anything. The tiger lineage originated in a particular geographic region at a particular time, spread through space, and is now declining. These are the sorts of things we say about particular historical entities, not about universals.

This observation drives one of the most provocative proposals in philosophy of biology, associated with Michael Ghiselin (1974) and David Hull (1978, 1980): species are not kinds but individuals — historical entities with a spatiotemporal location, a beginning and an end. Rather than tiger being a kind to which individual tigers belong as instances, individual tigers are *parts* of the species *Panthera tigris*, as cells are parts of an organism, or citizens are parts of a nation.

## The Individuality Thesis

We need two relations. **Instantiation** (∈): x is a member of kind K if x instantiates K. This holds between Socrates and *humanity*, or between a particular gold sample and *gold*. **Composition** (⊑): x is a part of individual I if x composes I. This holds between a cell and the organism it composes.

The species-as-individuals thesis maintains: for any species S and any organism o that belongs to S, the relation between o and S is composition (⊑), not instantiation (∈). The organism is part of the species-individual; it is not an instance of a species-kind.

Four lines of evidence support this interpretation. First, spatiotemporal location: species originate in particular geographic regions at particular times, spread through space, and go extinct. Abstract kinds lack spatiotemporal location. Gold the kind is neither located in space nor has a beginning or end; gold the element exists wherever and whenever there are atoms of atomic number 79. Second, evolution and change: species evolve, their morphological, behavioral, and genetic properties changing over time. Kinds do not change their essential properties. If tigers gradually evolved to become herbivores over ten thousand years, "tiger" would still refer to the lineage *Panthera tigris*, but the lineage would have changed its properties — this is coherent for an individual but not for a kind defined by carnivory. Third, speciation and extinction: a species comes into existence through speciation and ceases to exist through extinction or phylogenetic transformation. Abstract kinds do not come into or go out of existence. Fourth, the absence of essentialism: individual organisms vary enormously within a species, and there is no property necessary and sufficient for being a member of *Homo sapiens* across the full history of the lineage — including our australopithecine ancestors, transitional forms, and possible future descendants. The absence of a plausible essence supports treating the species as a historical entity rather than a kind.

## Implications for Biology

If species are individuals, biological taxonomy is not an investigation of natural kinds but a science of individuals — a project of tracing evolutionary lineages and identifying the historical entities composing the tree of life.

Several implications follow. Generalizations about species ("tigers are carnivores") are not laws but descriptions of the properties of particular individuals. Laws of nature range over kinds; historical generalizations range over individuals. If biology lacks natural kinds, it lacks genuine biological laws in the strict sense — its generalizations are contingent historical claims, not nomological necessities. This does not mean biology has nothing interesting to say; historical science is genuine science. It means biology's generalizations have a different modal status than the laws of chemistry.

The species-as-individuals thesis also supports thinking of natural selection as operating on individuals (lineages, populations) rather than on abstract kinds. Biological classification, on this view, is an exercise in evolutionary history rather than in the discovery of natural kinds. A cladistic taxonomy is a historical narrative tracing the branching history of lineages.

## Objections

The individuation problem remains: if species are individuals, what makes two temporally separated populations parts of the same species-individual? The question is at least as difficult as the species problem itself, and the answer (causal-historical continuity) faces the same vagueness problems that plagued the concept we were trying to clarify.

Explanatory loss is also a concern. If "tiger" names an individual rather than a kind, we lose the explanatory capacity associated with kind-membership. We cannot explain why tigers have stripes by appeal to their being tigers — we can only cite the actual history that produced tigers with stripes. This seems to sacrifice genuine explanatory depth in favor of metaphysical tidiness.

And there is the middle-level consideration: even granting that species are individuals, there may be natural kinds at other biological levels — population-level kinds defined by gene frequencies, or molecular-level kinds defined by genetic sequences. The species-as-individuals thesis does not eliminate natural kinds from biology; it relocates kindhood to different levels of biological organization.

The most nuanced position may be that species are sui generis entities — neither purely abstract kinds nor purely concrete individuals, but entities that have features of both. The metaphysical frameworks developed for chemistry and for ordinary objects may simply be inadequate for characterizing the distinctive ontology of biological species.
