# Important Terms and Ideas: Biological Kinds

**Biological Species Concept**
Mayr's definition: a species is a group of interbreeding or potentially interbreeding natural populations reproductively isolated from other such groups. This is the most widely used species concept in zoology but faces difficulties with asexual organisms and hybridization.

**Species as Individuals**
Ghiselin and Hull's thesis: species are not natural kinds (sets of organisms sharing essential properties) but spatiotemporally bounded individuals—historical lineages with parts (member organisms) rather than instances. This makes species unrepeatable and means species membership is a historical relation, not an essential property.

**Phylogenetic Species Concept**
A species is the smallest diagnosable cluster of individual organisms sharing a unique combination of characters; species are defined by their phylogenetic history rather than interbreeding capacity. This concept multiplies species counts and is common in systematic biology.

**Ecological Species Concept**
A species is a lineage occupying an adaptive zone—a set of conditions and resources that support a particular life strategy. Species boundaries are determined by ecological roles rather than reproductive isolation.

**Species Pluralism**
The view that no single species concept is correct for all biological purposes; different concepts track different real biological features, and pluralism is the appropriate methodological response. Kitcher and Ereshefsky defend versions of species pluralism.

**Essentialism about Biological Kinds**
The thesis that biological kinds have essential properties shared by all members—a microstructural basis for kind membership. Most philosophers of biology reject essentialism about species, given the variability within species and the species-as-individuals thesis.
