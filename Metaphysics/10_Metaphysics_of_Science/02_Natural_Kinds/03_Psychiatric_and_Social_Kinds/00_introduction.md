# Psychiatric and Social Kinds

The category of natural kinds extends into domains — psychiatry, sociology, economics, anthropology — where the question of whether the categories track real, mind-independent structure becomes most contested. Are depression, schizophrenia, and autism natural kinds, or are they human constructions imposed on a continuous spectrum of human variation? Are races, genders, and social classes natural kinds, or are they social constructions that classify people for social purposes without tracking any real, intrinsic distinction?

Psychiatric kinds present a particular challenge. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM) classifies mental disorders by symptom clusters, with limited grounding in underlying neurobiological mechanisms. Whether the categories of the DSM correspond to genuine natural kinds — kinds with a discoverable biological essence — or are conventional classifications that facilitate treatment without tracking natural structure, is an open question. Some argue that psychiatric kinds are HPC kinds: defined by homeostatic clusters of symptoms and mechanisms that tend to co-occur, without the kind of essential unity that physical natural kinds possess.

The concept of "looping kinds" — introduced by Hacking — applies particularly to social and psychiatric kinds. When a kind is identified and named, the label changes how members of the kind think about themselves and behave; this changed behavior changes the kind; which changes the label's application. Unlike natural kinds such as gold (which does not change when we name it), psychiatric and social kinds are responsive to their classification. This "interactive" character of social kinds distinguishes them from the "indifferent" kinds of natural science.

Racial and gender categories raise the question of whether they are natural kinds, social constructions, or something in between. Racial categories do not correspond to any underlying genetic divisions in the human species; yet racial classification has real social effects and grounds real patterns of experience and disadvantage. Gender categories are similarly contested: are they biological, psychological, or social, and are they kinds at all in the metaphysically interesting sense?

This section examines psychiatric and social kinds, the concept of looping kinds, and the implications of social constructionism for the theory of natural kinds.
