# Social Kinds

Take a piece of paper with numbers on it. Set it down in the right place at the right time before the right person and it discharges a debt. Legally and socially, something has happened. No natural process has occurred — the same physical transfer in a different context, or with a different currency, or in a society that does not recognize this form of obligation, produces nothing of the sort. Social kinds present a puzzle that physical kinds do not: their existence seems to depend on collective human attitudes in a way that the existence of electrons or tiger lineages does not. If everyone stopped treating a piece of paper as money, it would cease to be money. If everyone stopped believing in electrons, electrons would continue to exist with their charge and mass.

Social kinds are the categories of social entities: money, governments, universities, marriages, corporations, social roles, and institutions. Their metaphysical status raises distinctive questions. On one hand, social facts are clearly real and causally efficacious — money buys goods, governments pass laws, marriages create legal obligations. These effects cannot be explained without appealing to the social facts. On the other hand, social facts seem to depend on human attitudes and practices in a way that distinguishes them from physical or biological facts.

## Searle's Theory of Social Ontology

John Searle's *The Construction of Social Reality* (1995) and *Making the Social World* (2010) provide the most influential systematic account. Searle argues that social facts are constituted by *collective intentionality* — the shared, mutually recognized attitudes of individuals.

The key mechanism is the **Status Function Declaration**: social facts are created when a community collectively accepts that some entity X counts as Y in context C. A piece of paper (X) counts as a twenty-dollar bill (Y) in the context of the United States economic system (C). The counting-as relation assigns a status function to the physical entity — the function of serving as a medium of exchange — that the entity lacks by virtue of its physical properties alone. Status function declarations are self-referential: the act of passing a law creates the legal fact; the exchange of vows creates the marriage. These are performative declarations rather than descriptions of pre-existing facts.

Social facts are therefore ontologically subjective — their existence depends on subjects with intentional states — but epistemically objective: "The Federal Reserve is the central bank of the United States" is objectively true even though the Federal Reserve exists only in virtue of collective acceptance.

## Supervenience and Social Facts

A natural physicalist approach holds that social facts supervene on physical and biological facts: there can be no difference in social facts without some underlying difference in physical or biological facts. Formally:

- **Weak social supervenience**: Necessarily, if two possible worlds are physically identical, their social facts are identical.
- **Strong social supervenience**: For any social fact f, f is necessitated by some physical or biological fact.

Money exists only when there are physical tokens (coins, bills, electronic records) and organisms that relate to those tokens in the appropriate way. The physical facts do not constitute the money facts in isolation — a piece of paper is not money in virtue of its physical properties alone — but they provide the base on which the social facts supervene.

The relationship between supervenience and reduction is complex: supervenience does not entail reduction. Social facts might supervene on physical facts without being reducible to them, because the same social fact can be realized by many different physical configurations. "Money" picks out a functional role (medium of exchange) that can be realized by gold, paper, seashells, or electronic signals.

## Haslanger's Social Structuralism

Sally Haslanger's *Resisting Reality* (2012) develops a different approach, focused on social structures rather than collective intentionality. On Haslanger's account, social kinds are defined by the positions individuals occupy in systems of social power and practice. A social kind is not merely a collection of individuals who share some property or are treated similarly; it is a set of positions in a social structure that distributes power, resources, and constraints.

This social structuralist account has implications for the normative question of how social kinds should be understood and changed. If social kinds are defined by structural positions rather than by collective attitudes, changing social kinds requires changing social structures, not merely changing attitudes. This matters for the practical politics of race and gender, as discussed in the next file.

## Ontological Parsimony and the Normative Dimension

A physicalist will press for elimination or reduction: perhaps social facts just are physical facts about human behavioral dispositions. A twenty-dollar bill just is a piece of paper that humans are disposed to exchange for goods, accept as legal tender, and treat as more valuable than a scrap of paper. On this deflationary account, there is no irreducible social entity "money" — just physical entities and human behavioral dispositions.

Critics of deflationism respond that this loses the normative dimension of social facts. Money is not merely something humans happen to exchange; it is something they *ought* to accept as legal tender, something that *counts* as a valid payment, something with a normative status that cannot be captured by purely behavioral descriptions. The ought or counts-as cannot be reduced to an is.

The autonomy of social science is implicated here. If social kinds are merely conventional groupings without causal unity, social science generalizations will be at best empirical regularities without the modal force of genuine laws. Philip Pettit and others have argued that collective behavior can exhibit patterns not predictable from individual behavior alone, supporting the irreducibility of social kinds. The "superorganism" model treats social entities (nations, markets, corporations) as genuine agents with properties not reducible to those of their members. Whether this requires genuine ontological irreducibility or can be maintained within a reductive framework is an ongoing debate at the intersection of social ontology, philosophy of social science, and the broader question of levels of nature.
