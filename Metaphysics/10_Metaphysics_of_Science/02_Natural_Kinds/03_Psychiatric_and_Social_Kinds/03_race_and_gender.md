# Race and Gender as Social Kinds

The Human Genome Project produced a result that surprised many people: roughly 85–90% of human genetic variation occurs *within* traditionally defined racial groups, and only 10–15% *between* them. If races were biologically fundamental kinds, we would expect the opposite — most variation should occur between races. Human genetic variation turns out to be clinal, continuously varying across geography rather than corresponding to discrete groups. The clusters that emerge from genomic analysis depend on the parameters of the analysis rather than on natural discontinuities in the data. What follows from this for the metaphysics of race?

## Race

The abolitionist conclusion (K. Anthony Appiah, *In My Father's House*, 1992) is that racial categories do not correspond to biological natural kinds, and racial terms should therefore be abandoned. There are no races in any scientifically robust sense; racial categories of common usage are at best rough approximations of geographic ancestry clusters with no deeper biological significance.

Many philosophers resist this conclusion, not by defending racial essentialism but by arguing that race is a genuine social kind even if it is not a biological one. Social constructivism about race holds that races are real — they have genuine causal effects on people's lives and opportunities — while being constituted by social practices and structures rather than biological facts.

Haslanger's *Resisting Reality* develops an explicit account: to be raced (as white, black, etc.) is to be positioned in a social structure in which one's (perceived) physical appearance correlates with differential treatment, access to resources, and exposure to constraints. Race is not defined by biology but by this social positioning. It is real because the social positioning is real and causally efficacious — it produces measurable differences in health outcomes, wealth, educational attainment, and exposure to violence.

Formally: x belongs to racial group R if and only if x is perceived to have certain physical characteristics associated with R, and this perception systematically affects how x is treated within the relevant social system, positioning x in a hierarchical social structure.

Critical race theory (Bell, Crenshaw, Delgado) emphasizes that race is not merely a social kind but a political one: racial categories were constructed and are maintained in the service of systems of domination. Charles Mills's *The Racial Contract* (1997) argues that white supremacy is built into the foundational political arrangements of Western modernity. On this view, racial kinds are constitutively normative: they define who counts as a full moral and political subject.

A pluralist position holds that there are multiple legitimate uses of racial categories tracking different things. "Race" in the biological sense tracks geographic ancestry and associated genetic variation. "Race" in the social sense tracks social positioning and the historical legacy of racial categorization. "Race" in the experiential sense tracks how individuals understand their own racial identity. Different contexts call for different senses: in medicine, socially defined race may be a relevant variable because it correlates with exposure to racial discrimination and its health consequences, without corresponding to a biological natural kind; in genetics, ancestry (not social race) is the relevant variable for disease risk.

## Gender

The standard feminist philosophical view, crystallized in Simone de Beauvoir's formulation "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman," holds that gender is socially constructed — a system of roles, expectations, behaviors, and hierarchies imposed on individuals on the basis of perceived sex. Gender is distinguished from biological sex: sex is (roughly) defined by chromosomal, hormonal, and anatomical characteristics; gender is a social and cultural category. Biological sex is (at least partly) a natural biological kind (though complicated by intersex conditions, hormonal variation, and the distinction between chromosomal, gonadal, and phenotypic sex); gender is not.

Haslanger's structural account defines gender by position in systems of social power: to be a woman is to be systematically subordinated along some dimension (economic, political, legal, social) and to be "marked" as a target for this treatment by observed or imagined bodily features presumed to be evidence of a female's biological role in reproduction. Gender is not a matter of inner experience or biological essence but of social positioning. This structural account has both descriptive and normative force: it explains the systematic character of gender inequality — the pattern of women's subordination is built into the social structure — and implies that changing gender requires structural change, not merely attitudinal change.

Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) offers a more radical account: gender is not a property that individuals have but a performance that constitutes the subject. Gender norms are reproduced through repeated, ritualized acts — dressing, speaking, moving, presenting oneself — that create the appearance of a natural gender underlying the performance. There is no pre-gendered self that then performs gender; the performance constitutes the gendered subject. This challenges the very concept of gender identity: if gender is constituted by performance rather than expressing a pre-existing identity, the notion of a "real" gender beneath the performance is an illusion.

## Transgender Experience and the Normative-Descriptive Entanglement

The existence and experiences of transgender individuals have reinvigorated the metaphysics of gender. If gender is purely social positioning (Haslanger), then being a woman is a matter of occupying the relevant social position — which does not straightforwardly accommodate the experience of trans women, who may not be positioned as women in Haslanger's structural sense but who nonetheless understand themselves as women and seek recognition as such. Alternative accounts emphasize gender identity — a person's inner sense of their gender — as a genuine and irreducible feature of psychological reality. Gender is partly a social phenomenon (defined by social roles and norms) and partly a psychological phenomenon (defined by a person's self-understanding and experience). The two dimensions can come apart.

Both race and gender illustrate a feature distinctive of social kinds: they are simultaneously descriptive and normative. To classify someone as a woman or as black is not merely to assign them to a descriptive category but to position them within normative structures — systems of expectation, obligation, privilege, and constraint. The descriptive and normative aspects cannot be cleanly separated. Understanding what race and gender are requires understanding how these categories function in social life, and that functional role is irreducibly normative. This has implications for the question of whether race and gender should be "abolished" or "revised." If racial and gender categories are merely descriptive, abolition means ceasing to categorize along those dimensions. If the categories are normatively constituted — defined by the hierarchical social relations they organize — then abolition requires dismantling the hierarchical relations themselves, not merely abandoning the vocabulary.
