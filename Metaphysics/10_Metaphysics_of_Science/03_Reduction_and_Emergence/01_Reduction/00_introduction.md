# Reduction

Is chemistry reducible to physics? Is biology reducible to chemistry? Is psychology reducible to neuroscience? Reduction — the derivation of the laws and ontology of a higher-level science from those of a lower-level science — is one of the central programs in the philosophy of science and a central aspiration of scientific naturalism. If reduction is possible, science converges toward a single unified theory; the special sciences are merely convenient abbreviations for complex physical descriptions.

Nagel's classical account of reduction holds that the laws of the reduced theory are derived from the laws of the reducing theory, given "bridge principles" that connect the predicates of the two theories. The Mendelian law of independent assortment (genes on different chromosomes assort independently) is "reduced" to molecular genetics by bridge principles connecting "gene" to "DNA sequence," "allele" to "variant sequence," and so on. The reduction shows how the higher-level law is explained by the lower-level mechanism.

Multiple realizability — the fact that the same higher-level property (pain, gene, predator) can be realized by different lower-level configurations — threatens Nagel-style reduction. If there is no unique lower-level predicate that corresponds to each higher-level predicate, the bridge principles cannot be stated in the simple form that Nagel's account requires. Putnam's original multiple realizability argument was directed against type identity theory; but it extends to the reduction of any higher-level science whose predicates are functional — defined by causal role rather than physical constitution.

Responses to multiple realizability include: local reduction (different reductions for different realizers, without a single unified reduction); functional reduction (reducing the functional kind to the disjunction of its physical realizers); and the claim that multiple realizability undermines type reduction but not token reduction. Kim's "causal exclusion" argument suggests that non-reductive physicalism faces a dilemma: either higher-level properties are causally efficacious (and hence reducible) or they are not (and hence epiphenomenal).

This section examines reduction — its theoretical attractions, the multiple realizability objection, and the prospects for scientific unity without strict inter-theoretic reduction.
