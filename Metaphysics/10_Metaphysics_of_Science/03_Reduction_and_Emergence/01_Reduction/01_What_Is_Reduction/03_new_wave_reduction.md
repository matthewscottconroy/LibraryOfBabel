# New Wave Reduction

Nagelian reduction requires strict derivability — the laws of the higher-level theory must follow logically from those of the lower-level theory plus bridge laws. This is a demanding standard that virtually no actual reduction in science satisfies. Classical thermodynamics is not strictly derivable from statistical mechanics; classical genetics is not strictly derivable from molecular biology; folk psychology is not derivable from neuroscience. The natural response is not to abandon reductionism but to revise it: real reductions are approximate, structure-preserving mappings rather than strict logical derivations.

This is the central modification introduced by new wave reduction, associated with Paul Churchland (*Scientific Realism and the Plasticity of Mind*, 1979), Clifford Hooker (*A Realistic Theory of Science*, 1987), and later John Bickle (*Psychoneural Reduction*, 1998). The key move: replace Nagel's derivability condition with a weaker structural analog condition. Instead of requiring that the laws of T₂ be logically derivable from T₁ plus bridge laws, new wave reduction requires that there exists an *analog* T₂* of T₂ that is both approximately derivable from T₁ and sufficiently similar to T₂ to count as its reduction image.

## The Smoothness Spectrum

The similarity between T₂* and T₂ is characterized by structural analogy: the two theories agree on the kinds they recognize (within a tolerance), on the relations they posit among those kinds, and on their observational predictions (within a margin of approximation). This modification accommodates approximation directly: real reductions involve corrected and idealized versions of the higher-level theory, not the exact theory as formulated.

New wave reduction introduces a spectrum of reduction quality. **Smooth reduction**: T₂* is highly similar to T₂ and derivable from T₁. The reduction is clean, and the higher-level theory is largely vindicated — as in the reduction of classical thermodynamics to statistical mechanics. **Bumpy reduction**: T₂* differs significantly from T₂; the reduction involves substantial correction of the higher-level theory. Some posits are revised or eliminated — as in the bumpier reduction of classical genetics to molecular biology, where gene concepts require significant revision. **Replacement**: T₂* is so different from T₂ that T₂ is effectively abandoned rather than reduced — as the Churchlands predict may happen to folk psychology.

This spectrum allows for a graduated assessment of reduction attempts rather than the binary succeed/fail of Nagel's model. Whether a particular reduction is smooth or bumpy is an empirical question about how much the higher-level theory must be corrected when the lower-level account is available.

## Bickle's Ruthless Reduction

John Bickle's "ruthless reduction" program develops the most aggressive application. Bickle argues that folk psychology and cognitive psychology should be reduced — and substantially corrected — by molecular neuroscience. The reduction is ruthless because it does not try to preserve the higher-level theory intact; it allows for the elimination of concepts that do not map onto lower-level processes and the replacement of higher-level explanations with mechanistic accounts at the cellular and molecular level.

Bickle's examples include the neuroscience of memory consolidation. The theory that memory is consolidated during sleep through hippocampal replay, confirmed by molecular studies of synaptic potentiation (LTP), provides a case where the mechanistic, molecular-level account is the genuinely explanatory one. The cognitive-level description ("the memory trace consolidates") is retained as a summary but does not capture the explanatory mechanism. This ruthless approach challenges the non-reductive physicalist claim that higher-level descriptions have autonomous explanatory value.

## The Theory-Reduction versus Mechanism Debate

New wave reduction is often linked to the mechanistic turn in philosophy of science (Machamer, Darden, Craver). Mechanistic explanation identifies the components of a system, their activities, and their organization — the mechanism — that produces the phenomenon to be explained. Reduction, on the mechanistic account, consists in identifying the mechanism underlying a higher-level phenomenon. This differs from the Nagelian model: mechanisms are not logical derivations from laws but causal stories about component activities. Carl Craver's *Explaining the Brain* (2007) develops the most systematic version, arguing that neuroscientific explanation is constitutively mechanistic — to explain a neurological phenomenon is to identify the mechanism producing it, where the mechanism consists of entities and activities at multiple spatial and temporal scales.

On multiple realizability: new wave reduction partially addresses the problem. For each species or system that instantiates pain, there is a species-specific reduction — human pain to C-fiber firing in humans, molluscan pain to ganglionic processes in mollusks. Each reduction is local, not universal. This is not Nagelian (no universal bridge law) but is reductive in the new wave sense. Whether local reduction is sufficient depends on what we want from reduction. If we want to explain why pain as such causes avoidance across all its realizers, local species-specific reduction leaves this unexplained — the explanation of the general pattern requires appeal to the higher-level kind. If we want only to explain each particular instance, local reduction may suffice.

## The Problem of Explanatory Autonomy

If the molecular story is the truly explanatory one, why do scientists continue to use higher-level descriptions? And why do higher-level sciences — ecology, psychology, economics — make successful predictions without being formulated at the lower level? New wave reductionists have two responses. First, pragmatic: higher-level descriptions are tractable and useful even if not truly explanatory; lower-level descriptions are too complex to be practically deployed. Second, eliminativist: eventually, as lower-level sciences mature, higher-level sciences will be replaced or substantially revised — the persistence of higher-level descriptions is a temporary feature of our epistemic situation.

Critics, including advocates of scientific pluralism (Dupré, Cartwright), argue that higher-level explanations are genuinely explanatory and irreplaceable, not merely pragmatic tools. Cartwright's *The Dappled World* (1999) argues that different sciences employ different models and idealizations appropriate to different ranges of phenomena, and that no single science is uniquely privileged as the "true" description of the world. The debate between new wave reductionism and scientific pluralism is thus a debate about the architecture of scientific understanding itself.
