# The Hierarchy of Levels

We have multiple sciences, each apparently successful in its domain. Physics explains the behavior of fundamental particles and fields. Chemistry explains chemical bonds, reactions, and equilibria. Biology explains natural selection, fitness, and development. Psychology explains beliefs, desires, and rational inference. Economics explains markets, prices, and equilibria. Notice that these sciences do not simply duplicate each other's work — each seems to describe something real that the others do not directly capture. Notice also that the entities of each science are composed of entities from the level below: molecules are composed of atoms, cells of molecules, organisms of cells. This combination of compositional dependence and explanatory distinctness is the starting point for the picture of levels of nature.

The hierarchical picture organizes the sciences by levels of composition: fundamental physics (quarks, leptons, gauge bosons, quantum fields), atomic and molecular physics and chemistry, cell biology (organelles, cells, macromolecules), organism biology (multicellular organisms, organ systems), ecology and evolutionary biology (populations, ecosystems, lineages), psychology and cognitive science (mental states, cognitive processes, behavior), and social science (institutions, economies, cultures). Each level is related to the level below by composition and is characterized by its own distinctive kinds, laws, and explanatory concepts.

## What the Hierarchy Gets Right

The hierarchical picture has several attractive features. It accommodates the existence and success of the special sciences without collapsing them into physics: each science describes phenomena at its own level with its own conceptual apparatus, and its success supports the view that phenomena at that level are real and organized by genuine kinds and laws. The hierarchy also provides a natural account of explanatory grounding: higher-level laws hold because of lower-level processes. The ideal gas law holds because of molecular kinetics; evolutionary laws hold because of population genetics and molecular mechanisms. Despite the plurality of sciences at different levels, the hierarchy provides a vision of their unity — all sciences ultimately describe aspects of the same physical world, organized at different scales and levels of complexity.

## Challenges to the Standard Picture

The hierarchical picture also raises philosophical challenges. The levels do not correspond to sharply separated domains: biochemistry overlaps with both chemistry and biology; cognitive neuroscience spans psychology and neuroscience; evolutionary psychology crosses evolutionary biology and cognitive science. The distinctions among levels are matters of degree and scientific interest rather than sharp ontological divisions.

What grounds the hierarchy? Possible grounds include: compositional complexity (higher levels involve more complex organized entities); temporal scale (higher-level phenomena operate on longer timescales); functional organization (higher levels are defined by functional roles rather than material composition); or evolutionary priority (higher levels emerged later in the history of the universe). None of these criteria yields a unique, well-defined hierarchy, and the combination is not principled enough to settle boundary cases.

The eliminability worry runs deeper. If higher levels are "nothing over and above" lower levels — if everything is ultimately physical — one might conclude that higher-level descriptions are merely convenient approximations, eliminable in favor of the lower-level description. But if psychological descriptions add nothing to the physical description, psychology is not a genuine science but a heuristic. And the cascading multiple realizability problem complicates the picture further: a thought can be realized by different neural configurations, and a neural configuration by different molecular configurations. This cascading multiple realizability means higher levels are genuinely autonomous in the sense that they are not determined by any unique lower-level configuration.

## Formal Characterization: Supervenience and Grounding

The levels of nature can be formally characterized using the supervenience relation. Let F be a family of physical properties and G a family of higher-level properties. G supervenes on F if:

- **Local supervenience**: For any two objects x and y, if x and y are identical with respect to all F-properties, they are identical with respect to all G-properties.
- **Global supervenience**: Any two possible worlds identical with respect to all F-facts are identical with respect to all G-facts.

The hierarchy corresponds to a nested series of supervenience relations: biological properties supervene on chemical properties, which supervene on physical properties. The supervenience relations are asymmetric: biological properties supervene on physical properties, but not vice versa. This formal characterization captures the dependency relations without presupposing reduction — higher-level properties depend on lower-level properties (no change in the higher without a change in the lower) without being identical to or derivable from them.

Contemporary metaphysics supplements supervenience with metaphysical grounding. Grounding is a relation of metaphysical determination: the fact that [this organism is alive] is grounded in facts about its biochemical processes, cellular organization, and metabolic activity. Grounding is asymmetric, irreflexive, and transitive; unlike supervenience, it is a determination relation that explains why the higher level is as it is, not merely a covariation relation. The hierarchy of levels corresponds to a hierarchy of grounding: social facts grounded in psychological facts, grounded in biological facts, grounded in chemical and physical facts. At the base are fundamental physical facts — grounded in nothing further, assuming physicalism is true.

Despite its conceptual problems, the hierarchical picture is enormously productive as a methodological framework. It directs scientists to look for mechanisms at lower levels explaining higher-level phenomena, and to look for patterns at higher levels constraining and organizing investigation of lower-level mechanisms. The hierarchy is thus best understood not as a fixed metaphysical truth about the structure of reality but as a heuristic organizational framework — a map of the sciences that guides inquiry and highlights explanatory relations among different domains, while remaining open to revision as those sciences develop and their interrelations become clearer.
