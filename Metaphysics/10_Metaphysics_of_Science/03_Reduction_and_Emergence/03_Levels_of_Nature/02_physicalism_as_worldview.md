# Physicalism as a Worldview

Here is a datum that is easy to overlook because it is so familiar: no serious scientific explanation of any natural phenomenon invokes vital forces, disembodied souls, or non-physical mental substances. Neuroscientists do not posit spirits when they explain behavior. Biochemists do not invoke vital essences when they explain metabolism. The working assumption of virtually all scientific research is that the world is physical — that everything that exists is composed of physical stuff, interacting via physical forces, subject to physical laws. This working assumption is physicalism, and it has been enormously productive.

But formulating physicalism precisely turns out to be harder than it looks. "Everything is physical" is the slogan; the slogan requires unpacking.

## Formulations of Physicalism

**Token physicalism** is the most minimal form: every entity is a physical entity, composed of physical parts, located in physical spacetime, interacting via physical forces. This excludes non-physical substances (souls, vital forces, abstract objects with causal powers). It is the most widely accepted form of physicalism and is compatible with multiple realizability: even if mental states are not identical to physical states, they are realized in physical systems.

**Type physicalism** (the type identity theory) goes further: every type of higher-level state is identical to some type of physical state. Pain-type states are identical to some neural type. This stronger form supports full Nagelian reduction, since identity provides the strict bridge laws required. Type physicalism has largely been abandoned because of the multiple realizability problem — if pain is multiply realizable, there is no single physical type identical to pain.

**Supervenience physicalism** is weaker and more widely held: all facts supervene on physical facts. Any two possible worlds that are physically identical are identical in all other respects. Formally:

- □(∀P)(∀Q)[(∀x)(Px ↔ Qx) → (∀F)(Fx_P ↔ Fx_Q)]

where P and Q range over physical worlds, F over all facts, and the modality is metaphysical. Supervenience physicalism accommodates multiple realizability, since the same higher-level fact can supervene on different lower-level physical facts in different worlds.

**Grounding physicalism** is more metaphysically precise: all facts are grounded in physical facts. Grounding is a hyperintensional, asymmetric, irreflexive determination relation, while supervenience is an extensional covariation relation. Two sets of facts can be related by supervenience without grounding (if both are independently fundamental). Grounding physicalism entails that physical facts are the ultimate grounds of all facts, with no higher-level facts being fundamental.

## The Problem of What is "Physical"

Each formulation requires specifying what counts as "physical" — which turns out to be surprisingly difficult. **Physics-defined physicalism** takes physical facts to be those described by fundamental physics — quantum field theory, general relativity, and whatever future fundamental physics reveals. But our current physics may be incomplete (dark matter, dark energy, quantum gravity are not fully understood), making characterization in terms of current physics potentially too restrictive. And if physicalism is defined by whatever future physics says, it seems trivially true — anything real will presumably be included in the ultimate physics. This is Hempel's dilemma: if "physical" is defined by current physics, physicalism is probably false; if by ideal physics, physicalism is trivially true. Neither horn is satisfactory.

**Spatial-temporal physicalism** holds that physical entities are those that are spatiotemporally located. This excludes abstract mathematical objects but may be too permissive — it does not obviously exclude vital forces or souls that are spatiotemporally located. **Causal closure physicalism** holds that physical entities are those involved in the causal network described by physics, associated with the thesis that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause. If causal closure is true, non-physical causes are causally redundant.

## Supervenience, Non-Reductive Physicalism, and Kim's Challenge

Supervenience physicalism is widely accepted as a minimal commitment. But supervenience is not sufficient for full-blooded physicalism. Epiphenomenalism is compatible with supervenience: higher-level properties might supervene on physical properties while having no causal powers of their own. Property dualism is compatible with supervenience: Chalmers argues that phenomenal properties supervene on physical properties (via psychophysical laws) while being genuinely distinct from physical properties.

Non-reductive physicalism is the most popular contemporary position: everything is physical (in the token sense), higher-level properties supervene on physical properties, but higher-level properties are not identical to physical properties and cannot be reduced to them. This position accepts the multiple realizability argument against type identity while maintaining physicalism through supervenience.

Non-reductive physicalism faces Kim's causal exclusion problem: if physical causation is sufficient for all physical effects, and higher-level properties are not identical to physical properties, the causal relevance of higher-level properties is threatened. We are faced with a choice between higher-level properties being causally inert or physical events being causally overdetermined. Several responses have been developed. Yablo's determinable/determinate response: higher-level properties are determinables of their physical realizers (pain is a determinable of which C-fiber firing is a determinate), and determinables can be causally relevant even when their determinates are causally sufficient. Interventionist responses: higher-level causal claims capture counterfactual dependencies not captured by lower-level claims, even if lower-level causation is sufficient. Contextualist responses: the levels at which causation operates are context-relative, and there is no uniquely privileged level at which "real" causation occurs.

## Challenges from Mathematics and Consciousness

Physicalism faces special challenges from two domains. Mathematical objects — numbers, sets, functions — seem to be real and to play essential roles in physics, which is formulated in mathematical language. But mathematical objects are abstract: not spatiotemporally located, not causally efficacious in the ordinary sense. If they are not physical, physicalism seems false. Nominalists respond by denying the existence of abstract mathematical objects; Platonists argue that mathematical objects are real but non-physical, requiring revision of physicalism.

Consciousness is the deepest challenge. Even a complete physical description of the brain seems to leave open why there is subjective experience at all. If phenomenal properties are not identical to physical properties — if zombies are possible — physicalism is false. Physicalists have developed multiple responses: denying zombie conceivability, arguing that phenomenal properties are functional properties (functional physicalism), or accepting that phenomenal properties are identical to physical properties even though we cannot know this a priori (type B physicalism). The open questions do not undermine the broad scientific consensus that the world is physical — they indicate that our philosophical articulation of physicalism requires further refinement, not that physicalism is false.
