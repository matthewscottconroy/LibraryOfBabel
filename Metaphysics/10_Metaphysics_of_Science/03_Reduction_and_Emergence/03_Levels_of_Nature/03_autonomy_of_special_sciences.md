# Autonomy of the Special Sciences

Consider the economic law connecting money supply to price level: if the money supply increases without a corresponding increase in output, the price level will rise. This is a genuine law — it supports counterfactuals, it is projectible, and it grounds causal explanations. Now ask what a physical reduction of this law would look like. A monetary exchange can be realized by the exchange of gold coins, paper currency, electronic bank transfers, credits in a local exchange system, or any number of other physical transactions. There is no single physical predicate that is necessarily coextensive with "is a monetary exchange." Any bridge law connecting "monetary exchange" to a physical predicate will be either false (if strict) or uselessly disjunctive. The economic law is therefore not derivable from physical laws. But it is a genuine law nonetheless. Something has to give in our picture of the unity of science.

Jerry Fodor's influential 1974 paper "Special Sciences (or: The Disunity of Science as a Working Hypothesis)" argued that the special sciences — biology, psychology, economics, geology — are autonomous from physics and cannot be reduced to it. The argument turned on multiple realizability. It has been enormously influential in philosophy of mind and philosophy of science and continues to generate debate.

## Fodor's Argument

The argument proceeds in stages. **Stage 1**: Reduction requires strict bridge laws connecting higher-level to lower-level predicates. A strict bridge law is a universal biconditional: ∀x(P₂x ↔ P₁x), where P₂ is a higher-level predicate and P₁ is a lower-level predicate. **Stage 2**: Special science kinds are multiply realizable. The disjunction of all physical predicates coextensive with a given special science predicate is not a natural kind predicate — it is not projectible and generates no further physical laws. So bridge laws connecting "monetary exchange" or "pain" to any physical predicate will be either false (if strict) or useless (if disjunctive). **Stage 3**: Despite the failure of reduction, the special sciences formulate genuine laws — laws that are projectible, support counterfactuals, and ground genuine causal explanations.

**Conclusion**: The special sciences are autonomous from physics. Their laws cannot be derived from physical laws, and their natural kinds are not identical to physical kinds. The unity of science is not a methodological imperative; the disunity of science is a working hypothesis that accommodates the genuine autonomy of higher-level sciences.

We should be clear about what the autonomy thesis claims and what it does not. Fodor is not claiming that special science entities are non-physical — he accepts token physicalism. He is not claiming that special science laws are more nomologically necessary than physical laws. He is claiming that special science kinds do not correspond to physical kinds (no type identity, no bridge laws); that special science laws are not derivable from physical laws; and that special science explanations are genuinely explanatory, not merely approximate summaries of physical processes. The autonomy is epistemic and ontological, but not causal: mental events are causally efficacious by virtue of their physical realizations, even if not by virtue of their psychological characterization in isolation.

## Ceteris Paribus Laws and Their Challenges

The laws of the special sciences are typically ceteris paribus (all else being equal) laws, not strict exceptionless laws. "If the money supply increases, prices rise — other things being equal" is the form. The ceteris paribus clause covers interfering factors that can prevent the law from holding in any particular case. Fodor argued that ceteris paribus laws are genuine laws because they capture real counterfactual dependencies — even if the law fails in a particular case due to some interfering factor, it correctly specifies what would happen if the interfering factor were absent.

Critics (Schiffer, Pietroski and Rey) have argued that if ceteris paribus laws can be indefinitely qualified, they risk vacuity: a law that holds "other things being equal" holds trivially if "other things being equal" means "whenever the law holds." Fodor and his defenders respond that the ceteris paribus clause specifies the *kind* of interfering factor — it must be physicalistically specifiable — even if it does not enumerate them.

## Responses to the Autonomy Thesis

Several distinct responses have been developed. **Reductionism about kinds** (Kim and others): multiple realizability is not as widespread as Fodor suggests. Many apparent higher-level kinds can be identified with lower-level kinds — perhaps at an intermediate level rather than at the level of fundamental physics. The mental kind "pain" might be identified with a neural kind at the level of functional-computational description. This species-specific or local reduction avoids the multiple realizability problem at the cost of giving up cross-species generalizations.

**Eliminativism**: if special science kinds cannot be reduced, they should be eliminated. If folk psychology's kind "belief" does not correspond to any neural kind, there are no beliefs — folk psychology should be replaced by neuroscientific vocabulary. The Churchlands' eliminative materialism accepts the autonomy argument as showing that reduction is impossible but draws the eliminativist rather than the autonomy conclusion.

**Pluralism** (Dupré, Cartwright): the special sciences do not form a single unified hierarchy but a genuinely pluralistic landscape of sciences, each with its own methods, kinds, and laws, none privileged as the "real" description. Not only is psychology autonomous from physics, but there is no single unified "physics" from which everything could in principle be derived.

**Mechanistic integration** (Craver): the special sciences are neither autonomous nor reducible in Nagel's sense. Instead, they are integrated through mechanistic explanation — higher-level phenomena are explained by lower-level mechanisms, but this is not reduction (the higher level is not derived from the lower), and the higher level retains genuine explanatory status (the mechanism explanation shows how the higher-level phenomenon is produced).

The autonomy debate connects to laws of nature more broadly. If the special sciences are autonomous, their laws are genuine laws — not corollaries of physical laws. This means there are laws at multiple levels: physical laws, chemical laws, biological laws, psychological laws, economic laws. D. M. Armstrong's account of laws as relations among universals provides a framework: if each natural kind is a genuine universal and laws are relations among universals, then biological laws are relations among biological universals (fitness, selection, heritability), not logical consequences of physical universals. Alexander Bird's dispositional essentialism connects: if biological natural kinds have essential dispositions, biological laws express necessary actualization of those dispositions, giving biological laws the same modal force as physical laws. Whether this picture is defensible depends on whether biological, psychological, and economic entities can be shown to instantiate genuine universals with essential powers — a demanding metaphysical commitment that not all advocates of the autonomy thesis are willing to make.
