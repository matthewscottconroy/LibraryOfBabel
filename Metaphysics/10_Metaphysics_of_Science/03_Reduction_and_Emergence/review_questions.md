# Review Questions — Reduction and Emergence

1. Nagelian reduction requires both connectability (bridge laws linking vocabularies) and derivability (logical entailment of the higher-level theory). Why does it matter whether bridge laws are read as identity claims or as biconditionals? What hangs ontologically on the difference between saying that temperature is mean kinetic energy versus that temperature is nomically coextensive with mean kinetic energy?

2. Multiple realizability is the central challenge to Nagelian reduction: if pain can be realized by C-fiber firing, ganglionic processes, or silicon circuits, no strict bridge law can connect "pain" to any unified physical predicate. How does Kim's causal exclusion argument show that the problem runs deeper than the mere absence of bridge laws, and why does non-reductive physicalism struggle to respond?

3. New wave reduction replaces derivability with approximate structural analogy, introducing a spectrum from smooth to bumpy reduction. Does this modification rescue reductionism or concede too much to the autonomy of higher-level sciences? Identify a case where reduction is genuinely smooth and one where it is genuinely bumpy, and explain what determines which side of the spectrum a case falls on.

4. Weak emergence is defined by the absence of a compressing theory — the macro-level property can be derived in principle from the micro-level, but only by running the full simulation, with no shortcut. What does this entail for the relationship between explanation and reduction? Can a phenomenon be weakly emergent and yet also exhaustively explained by the lower level, or does the absence of a compressing theory indicate residual explanatory autonomy?

5. Strong emergence requires that higher-level properties have causal powers not derivable from or reducible to lower-level causal powers. The causal closure of the physical seems to rule this out. Reconstruct the dilemma: either strong emergence is epiphenomenal or it generates overdetermination. How compelling is the history of science objection — that apparent explanatory gaps have repeatedly been bridged — against invoking strong emergence for consciousness?

6. Philip Anderson's "More is Different" and the renormalization group analysis of universality suggest that higher-level collective descriptions track something physically real that is invisible from the level of fundamental physics. How does universality — the same critical behavior in systems with completely different microscopic constituents — challenge the naive reductionist picture? Does this constitute evidence for ontological emergence, or only for the indispensability of higher-level descriptions?

7. Fodor's autonomy thesis holds that special science kinds are genuinely multiply realizable and therefore irreducible, yet he accepts token physicalism. Is this combination stable? Reconstruct the tension: if every special science token is a physical token, and if physical causal closure holds, how can special science kinds have autonomous causal explanatory power without either collapsing into physics or becoming epiphenomenal?

8. The hierarchy of levels picture organizes the sciences by compositional complexity and grounding relations — biological properties are grounded in chemical properties, which are grounded in physical properties. Compare supervenience and grounding as tools for characterizing this hierarchy. Does the grounding framework capture something that supervenience misses, and does it help resolve the question of whether the special sciences are genuinely autonomous or merely convenient approximations?
