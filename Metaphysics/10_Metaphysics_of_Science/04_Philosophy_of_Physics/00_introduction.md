# Philosophy of Physics

Physics is our deepest empirical science — the science that investigates the most fundamental constituents of the physical world and the laws that govern them. Its foundational theories — quantum mechanics and general relativity — are extraordinarily successful: quantum mechanics is tested to the precision of one part in a billion; general relativity has passed every empirical test thrown at it. Yet these two theories are inconsistent with each other at the foundational level, and neither has an agreed-upon interpretation that tells us what the world described by the theory is actually like.

The measurement problem in quantum mechanics is the central foundational puzzle. Quantum systems are described by wave functions that evolve deterministically according to the Schrödinger equation and that can represent superpositions of multiple states simultaneously. Yet when we measure a quantum system, we always get a definite outcome — a single value, not a superposition. The formalism says the system is in a superposition of states; our measurements say it is in one definite state. What happens in measurement? Does the wave function collapse? Does it always evolve unitarily, generating many branches of an ever-branching many-worlds universe? Do hidden variables restore determinism and definiteness? Each answer generates a different picture of reality, and no consensus has emerged.

Thermodynamics raises the puzzle of the arrow of time. The second law of thermodynamics — that entropy increases in closed systems — is directional: it distinguishes the past from the future. But the microscopic laws of physics (quantum mechanics and general relativity) are time-reversal invariant: they work equally well in both temporal directions. How does the thermodynamic arrow emerge from the symmetric microscopic physics? This question connects the philosophy of physics to the philosophy of time examined in Unit 6.

Quantum gravity — the attempt to reconcile quantum mechanics and general relativity — suggests that both theories are approximations to something more fundamental, possibly involving a discrete structure of spacetime or a relational account of geometry and time that does not presuppose any background spacetime. The philosophy of physics here encounters questions at the limits of current science, where metaphysical speculation and physical research are genuinely intertwined.
