# The Philosophy of Quantum Mechanics

Quantum mechanics is the most empirically successful physical theory ever developed; it has been tested to extraordinary precision and underpins an enormous range of modern technology. Yet it is also a theory whose interpretation is profoundly contested: physicists and philosophers disagree about what quantum mechanics says the world is like in a way that has no parallel in classical physics. The measurement problem — the question of what happens when a quantum system "collapses" to a definite value upon measurement — has resisted resolution for nearly a century.

The core of the measurement problem: quantum mechanics describes the state of a system by a wave function — a superposition of possible values for each observable quantity. According to the Schrödinger equation, this superposition evolves continuously and deterministically. But when we measure a quantum system, we always find a definite value — not a superposition. The wave function "collapses" to a definite value upon measurement. The problem is that the Schrödinger equation gives no account of when or how collapse occurs; and if we try to include the measuring apparatus in the quantum description, we get a superposition that includes apparatus states — which never seem to occur in reality.

The major interpretations of quantum mechanics each resolve this puzzle differently. The Copenhagen interpretation holds that quantum mechanics describes only observable phenomena, not any underlying reality; the wave function is not a description of an objective state but a tool for predicting measurement outcomes. Everett's many-worlds interpretation holds that the wave function never collapses; all possible measurement outcomes are realized, each in a different "branch" of the universe. Bohm's pilot wave theory holds that there is a real particle following a definite trajectory, guided by the wave function; what we call "collapse" is just our updating of our information about the particle's trajectory. GRW theory posits a genuine, physical process of spontaneous collapse.

This section examines these interpretations and their philosophical implications for the ontology of quantum mechanics, the nature of determinism, and the role of the observer in physical theory.
