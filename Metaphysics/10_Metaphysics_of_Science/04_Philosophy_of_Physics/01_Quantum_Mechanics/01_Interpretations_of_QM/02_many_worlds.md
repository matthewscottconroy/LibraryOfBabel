# Many-Worlds Interpretation

The Schrödinger equation is linear and deterministic. If it is universally valid — if it governs all physical evolution without exception, including the process of measurement itself — then there is no collapse, and the outcome of every quantum measurement leaves the world in a superposition. When a spin measurement is performed on a superposition (1/√2)(|↑⟩ + |↓⟩), the combined state of the particle, apparatus, and observer evolves to:

(1/√2)(|↑⟩_particle ⊗ |"up" reading⟩_apparatus ⊗ |saw up⟩_observer + |↓⟩_particle ⊗ |"down" reading⟩_apparatus ⊗ |saw down⟩_observer)

Hugh Everett III proposed in his 1957 doctoral thesis "Relative State Formulation of Quantum Mechanics" that we should simply accept what the Schrödinger equation gives us. Both terms in this superposition represent real, co-existing branches of the universal wave function. There is a branch containing an observer who recorded spin-up and a branch containing an observer who recorded spin-down. Both branches are equally real. Neither constitutes the "actual" outcome; there is no collapse. What we experience as a single measurement outcome is simply the perspective of observers within one branch. The many-worlds interpretation (MWI) takes quantum mechanics at its mathematical word.

## Decoherence and Branch Structure

Early presentations of MWI were vague about what constitutes a "branch" and why branches do not interfere. Decoherence provides the answer: interaction with the environment rapidly suppresses interference between macroscopically distinct branches. Once a quantum system has become entangled with macroscopic numbers of environmental degrees of freedom, the different branches become effectively non-interfering — they decohere into distinct "quasi-classical" histories.

Wojciech Zurek's program of "einselection" (environment-induced superselection) explains why the preferred basis for branching is the position/classical basis rather than an arbitrary quantum basis: the environment selects states that are most stable against environmental monitoring — "pointer states" — and these correspond to the macroscopically definite states in which we describe the world. The branching structure of MWI is not an additional postulate but a consequence of decoherence: branches are decoherent, effectively non-interacting components of the universal wave function, individuated by their entanglement structure with the environment.

## Ontological Commitments

MWI is ontologically austere in one sense — it posits only one fundamental thing, the universal wave function evolving by the Schrödinger equation — and ontologically extravagant in another sense — it posits the existence of an enormous, perhaps infinite, number of parallel branches, each containing a full copy of the observable world. The universal wave function exists in configuration space — a space of extremely high dimension (3N dimensions for N particles). This raises the question of how a wave function in configuration space gives rise to the three-dimensional world we observe. MWI advocates have debated whether the wave function should be regarded as the fundamental ontology (monism about the wave function) or whether there is a more fundamental description in three-dimensional space from which the wave function derives.

Ladyman and Ross, from an ontic structural realist perspective, argue that the branching structure of the MWI wave function is precisely the kind of structural ontology that OSR predicts: there are no individuals, only structures — in this case, the branching structure of the universal wave function. MWI and OSR are thus natural allies. Wallace argues that branches are *real but not fundamental*: they are emergent entities, individuated by decoherence. A branch is real in the same sense that a tiger or a planet is real — as an emergent pattern in the underlying quantum dynamics — even though it is not fundamental.

## The Probability Problem

The deepest challenge for MWI is the probability problem. If all outcomes occur with certainty in some branch, what is the meaning of the Born rule probability P(x) = |⟨x|ψ⟩|²? In standard quantum mechanics, the Born rule gives the probability of finding a particular outcome in a single measurement. In MWI, there is no single outcome — all outcomes are realized. The Born rule cannot be interpreted as the probability that outcome x will occur, since all outcomes occur.

Branch counting fails: probabilities are not determined by the number of branches because the number of branches is not uniquely defined — the Schrödinger evolution produces a continuous superposition, not a discrete set of branches. Deutsch and Wallace have argued that rational agents within an Everettian universe should assign the Born rule probabilities to outcomes because this is the uniquely rational credence-assignment strategy for agents who accept MWI and are uncertainty-neutral among branches. Wallace's *The Emergent Multiverse* (2012) develops this in detail, deriving the Born rule from axioms of rational agency (including measurement neutrality and state supervenience on branch weight). Critics (Albert, Kent) argue that this decision-theoretic derivation cannot be genuine, since it presupposes the probabilities in the notion of "branch weight" before their normative significance has been established.

A persistent question concerns personal identity: if branches are real, why should I care about what happens to me in future branches? The MWI perspective seems to undermine personal identity across time, since "I" will become all the future-branch descendants of my current state. The rational self-concern underpinning prudential reasoning requires that there be facts about which future person is me — facts that MWI cannot straightforwardly supply. This is not merely a philosophical nicety; it bears on whether the decision-theoretic derivation of the Born rule can get off the ground.

## MWI and Scientific Realism

MWI is often presented as the most straightforwardly realist interpretation of quantum mechanics: it takes the wave function at face value as a description of physical reality, without adding collapse mechanisms or hidden variables. The wave function is real, its evolution is exactly governed by the Schrödinger equation, and all measurement outcomes are real. But MWI's realism comes at a cost: the reality of all branches is ontologically extravagant, and the wave function in configuration space is not a straightforward description of the three-dimensional physical world. Whether MWI is the most natural realist interpretation depends on how one weighs ontological parsimony at the fundamental level against the proliferation of parallel worlds.
