# Bohmian Mechanics

In 1952, David Bohm produced something that most physicists had believed impossible since John von Neumann's 1932 "proof": a fully deterministic interpretation of quantum mechanics, empirically equivalent to the standard theory, in which particles have definite positions at all times. The wave function is real; the particles are real; the quantum probabilities arise from ignorance of initial conditions rather than from fundamental indeterminism. Bohm revived an approach de Broglie had sketched in 1927, developed it fully, and produced what John Bell would later call the clearest available interpretation of quantum mechanics.

Bohmian mechanics (also called pilot wave theory or de Broglie-Bohm theory) posits two fundamental ontological components. First, **particles with definite positions**: at all times, every particle has a definite, precise position in three-dimensional space. Quantum uncertainty in position arises from ignorance of the exact initial position, not from ontological indeterminacy. Second, **the wave function**: ψ(q, t), where q = (q₁, q₂, …, qₙ) is the configuration of all N particles, is a real physical field that exists in configuration space (3N-dimensional). The wave function evolves according to the Schrödinger equation exactly as in standard quantum mechanics.

The wave function acts as a "pilot wave" that governs particle motion via the **guidance equation**:

dQₖ/dt = (ℏ/mₖ) Im(∇ₖψ/ψ)|_{q=Q(t)}

where Q(t) = (Q₁(t), …, Qₙ(t)) is the actual configuration of all particles. The velocity of each particle is determined by the gradient of the phase of the wave function evaluated at the actual particle configuration.

## Resolution of the Measurement Problem

In Bohmian mechanics, the measurement problem is *dissolved* rather than solved: particles always have definite positions, and measurement outcomes are determined by the actual positions of the particles in the measuring apparatus. There is no collapse; the wave function evolves by the Schrödinger equation throughout. When a spin measurement is performed on a particle in a superposition, the actual configuration of the apparatus particles evolves according to the guidance equation, channeling them into one definite outcome configuration — depending on the actual initial positions of all the particles. The wave function evolves into a superposition of outcome-branches (as in MWI), but only one branch is "occupied" by the actual particles. That branch determines the measurement outcome.

The quantum probabilities arise from the distribution of actual particle positions. Bohmian mechanics posits an *equivariant* distribution of initial particle positions: the probability distribution over the initial configuration Q₀ is given by |ψ(Q₀, t₀)|². Given this quantum equilibrium distribution, the statistical predictions of Bohmian mechanics exactly match those of standard quantum mechanics. The Born rule is not a fundamental postulate but a consequence of the quantum equilibrium distribution of initial conditions.

## Empirical Equivalence and Nonlocality

Bohmian mechanics is *empirically equivalent* to standard quantum mechanics — every prediction of standard quantum mechanics is also a prediction of Bohmian mechanics, given quantum equilibrium. No experiment can distinguish them. This has a philosophical implication: quantum indeterminism is not forced by the empirical data. If Bohmian mechanics is correct, quantum randomness is epistemic (arising from ignorance of initial conditions) rather than ontic (fundamental). The deterministic, particle picture is empirically equivalent to the indeterministic, wave-function-collapse picture.

The guidance equation is explicitly *nonlocal*: the velocity of particle k at time t depends on the positions of all other particles at time t, regardless of their spatial separation. This nonlocality is not an objection but a consequence of Bell's theorem, which proved that any theory reproducing quantum mechanical predictions for entangled particles must be nonlocal. Bell himself was a strong advocate of Bohmian mechanics and regarded its explicit nonlocality as a virtue: it makes the nonlocality of quantum mechanics transparent rather than hidden in the formalism.

The nonlocality does not allow faster-than-light communication. The nonlocal effects are screened by the quantum equilibrium distribution: even though the pilot wave is nonlocal, the statistical distribution of outcomes is such that no observable signal can propagate faster than light.

## The Wave Function and Configuration Space

A conceptually challenging aspect is the ontological status of the wave function. The wave function exists in 3N-dimensional configuration space, not in three-dimensional physical space — yet it is supposed to be a real physical entity that guides the particles. Bohmians have developed several responses. The **nomological view** (Dürr, Goldstein, Zanghì): the wave function is not a material field but a law of nature — it determines how particles move without itself being a physical entity in any space. Bohmian mechanics then has a clean ontology: particles in three-dimensional space, governed by a law. The **multi-field view** (Hubert, Romano): the wave function is a kind of "multi-field" existing in three-dimensional space by being defined over particle positions. **Configuration space realism**: the wave function is a field in 3N-dimensional configuration space, which is the fundamental arena of physical reality.

## Challenges and Philosophical Significance

Extending Bohmian mechanics to quantum field theory is technically challenging. QFT is not naturally a particle theory — it is a theory of fields in which particles are excitations. Several approaches have been developed (Bell's "beable" field theory, Dürr's approach to QFT), but none has achieved the simplicity and elegance of the non-relativistic particle version. Relativistic extensions involve complexities not present in the non-relativistic case, because the guidance equation is nonlocal in a way that makes it difficult to reconcile with special relativity. Bohmian mechanics also assumes that position is the preferred observable — the one with always-definite values — a choice that must be justified and that is not obviously unique.

For the metaphysics of science, Bohmian mechanics illustrates how the same empirical data can be accommodated by radically different theoretical pictures. The empirical equivalence of Bohmian and Copenhagen mechanics shows that ontological interpretation is not uniquely determined by empirical data — providing a concrete case study for debates about underdetermination and the limits of scientific realism. And Bohmian mechanics demonstrates that determinism and quantum mechanics are compatible: quantum indeterminism is not logically required by the formalism but is one interpretational choice among others.
