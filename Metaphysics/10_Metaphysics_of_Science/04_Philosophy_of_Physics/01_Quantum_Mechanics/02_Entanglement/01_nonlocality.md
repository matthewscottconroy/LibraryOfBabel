# Quantum Nonlocality

Suppose two particles are prepared in an entangled state and sent to detectors that are far apart — so far that no signal traveling at the speed of light could travel from one detector to the other before the measurements are completed. The measurements are made simultaneously (in some reference frame), and the results are compared. The correlations between the results turn out to be stronger than any local theory could produce. No pre-arranged agreement between the particles before they separated — no "hidden variable" scheme — can reproduce the correlations that quantum mechanics predicts. This is Bell's theorem, and its experimental confirmation has established quantum nonlocality as a real feature of the physical world.

John Bell proved in 1964 that any hidden variable theory that is both *deterministic* and *local* must satisfy certain statistical inequalities for the correlations between measurements on spatially separated entangled particles. The proof assumes: **Locality** — the outcome of a measurement at location A does not depend on what measurement is performed at location B, provided A and B are spacelike separated. **Hidden variables** — variables λ determine the outcomes of measurements, with quantum probabilities arising from ignorance of λ.

Under these assumptions, the correlations between measurements at A and B must satisfy the CHSH inequality:

|⟨AB⟩ + ⟨AB'⟩ + ⟨A'B⟩ - ⟨A'B'⟩| ≤ 2

where A, A' are measurement settings at location A and B, B' at location B. Quantum mechanics predicts violations of this inequality for entangled particles. For a maximally entangled two-particle state (a singlet state), the quantum prediction for the left-hand side is 2√2 ≈ 2.83 — exceeding the local hidden variable bound of 2. This is not a small effect; it is large enough to be reliably detected.

## Experimental Confirmation

The CHSH inequality was first tested by Freedman and Clauser in 1972 and more carefully by Aspect, Grangier, and Roger in 1982. Subsequent experiments have progressively closed the "loopholes" that could allow local hidden variable explanations. The **locality loophole** — that detector settings at A might signal the settings at B — was closed by choosing detector settings randomly and fast enough that the choice could not have signaled between A and B at the speed of light. The **detection loophole** — that selective detection might bias the results — was closed by experiments using ion traps with nearly 100% detection efficiency. The **freedom of choice loophole** has been partially addressed by experiments using random number generators driven by astrophysical sources, guaranteeing that correlations between settings could not have been established by any common cause within the past thousand years. The most comprehensive experiments (Hensen et al., 2015; Giustina et al., 2015; Shalm et al., 2015) have closed all major loopholes simultaneously, providing decisive evidence that quantum correlations violate local realism.

## What Nonlocality Is Not

We need to be precise about what Bell's theorem establishes. Nonlocality does not allow faster-than-light communication: although the correlations between measurement outcomes are nonlocal, they cannot be used to send information. The outcome of each individual measurement at A is random; only when the results at A and B are combined (by a classical, light-speed-limited channel) do the correlations become visible. Nonlocality is not a refutation of all hidden variable theories — it refutes *local* hidden variable theories. It does not rule out nonlocal hidden variable theories, like Bohmian mechanics, in which correlations are produced by nonlocal causal influences. And quantum nonlocality is not telepathy or any form of mystical connection: the correlations are statistical and carry no subjective states or information.

## Metaphysical Implications

**The failure of local realism**: "Local realism" combines two assumptions — that physical systems have definite properties independent of measurement (realism), and that these properties can be influenced only by events in their local past light cone (locality). Bell's theorem shows that the quantum correlations are incompatible with the conjunction. Different interpretations sacrifice one or the other: Copenhagen sacrifices realism about pre-measurement properties; Bohmian mechanics sacrifices locality (particles have definite positions, but the guidance equation is nonlocal); many-worlds sacrifices the assumption that there is a single definite outcome.

**Holism and non-separability**: Entangled quantum systems have joint properties — described by their combined quantum state — that are not reducible to properties of the individual systems. The quantum state of two entangled particles is not a product of two single-particle states; it is an irreducibly two-particle state. This quantum non-separability challenges the mereological principle that the properties of a whole are determined by the properties of its parts. It supports priority monism (Schaffer) over conventional part-prior mereology: the whole is, in some sense, prior to the parts.

**Implications for causation**: The nonlocal correlations cannot be explained by ordinary causal stories — causes propagating from common sources at subluminal speeds. Several philosophical responses have been developed. Retrocausality: Price and Wharton propose that the correlations can be explained if quantum mechanics involves backward-in-time causal influences — the entangled particles are causally connected across time from the future measurement back to the source. Common cause failure: Bell's theorem shows that the principle of common cause (Reichenbach) fails for quantum correlations — there is no common cause in the past light cone of both measurements. Non-causal correlation: van Fraassen holds that quantum correlations are simply irreducibly non-causal — a kind of physical coherence that cannot be analyzed into causal terms.

Quantum nonlocality also connects to the quest for quantum gravity. Whether a deeper theory will fundamentally alter our understanding of nonlocality is an open question. Some approaches to quantum gravity suggest that nonlocality may be even more fundamental at the Planck scale, with spacetime itself emerging from entanglement structure — in which case the nonlocality of quantum mechanics is not an anomaly to be explained away but a window into the deep architecture of reality.
