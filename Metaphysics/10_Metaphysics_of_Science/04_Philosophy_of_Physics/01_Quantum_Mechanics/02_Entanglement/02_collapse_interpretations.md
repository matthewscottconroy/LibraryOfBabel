# Collapse Interpretations

If we are not willing to accept the many-worlds proliferation of branches, and not willing to postulate Bohmian hidden variables, the remaining option is to modify the Schrödinger equation itself — to add a physical collapse process that selects one definite outcome from the superposition. Collapse interpretations hold that the wave function physically collapses upon measurement, and that the Schrödinger equation alone is incomplete. They share a commitment to a single, definite physical world rather than Everett's branching multiverse.

## The Copenhagen Interpretation

The Copenhagen interpretation, associated with Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg, and Wolfgang Pauli, is the original and most widely taught interpretation and dominated physics pedagogy for most of the twentieth century. Its core commitments: complementarity (physical systems cannot be simultaneously described by mutually exclusive descriptions — wave and particle, position and momentum — and the appropriate description is contextual); the classical-quantum divide (the world is divided into the quantum domain, described by wave functions, and the classical domain, described by classical physics); collapse upon measurement (when a quantum system interacts with a classical measuring apparatus, the wave function collapses to an eigenstate of the measured observable, described by a separate collapse rule, not the Schrödinger equation); and a refusal to ask what the quantum system is doing between measurements.

Bohr's distinctive insistence was that quantum mechanics describes our knowledge of physical systems, not the systems themselves — making Copenhagen a form of anti-realism about the quantum domain. A persistent difficulty is identifying where the boundary between quantum and classical worlds lies — the Heisenberg cut. The cut is not fixed: as quantum mechanics has been applied to increasingly large systems, the boundary has been pushed toward macroscopic scales, threatening to disappear entirely. Schrödinger's cat is the limiting case. Copenhagen requires a cut but cannot say where it is.

John von Neumann's *Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics* (1932) formalized the collapse postulate. He distinguished **Process 1** (collapse: the non-linear, stochastic collapse upon measurement) and **Process 2** (unitary evolution: the linear, deterministic Schrödinger evolution between measurements), and proved that the Heisenberg cut can be placed anywhere between the quantum system and the observer without changing observable predictions. This suggests the quantum-classical boundary is a pragmatic choice in the formalism rather than a physical distinction — an uncomfortable conclusion that fueled subsequent searches for a more principled account.

## Objective Collapse Theories (GRW and CSL)

Objective collapse theories supplement the Schrödinger equation with a stochastic collapse mechanism that operates continuously and independently of observers, resolving the measurement problem without appealing to measurement or observation.

**GRW theory** (Ghirardi, Rimini, Weber, 1986): The GRW model adds a stochastic term to the Schrödinger equation. Each particle has a small spontaneous probability per unit time (approximately 10⁻¹⁵ per second) of undergoing a "hit" — a localization event in which its wave function is multiplied by a narrow Gaussian centered at a random position. For a single particle, such hits are rare. For macroscopic objects composed of ~10²³ particles, the cumulative hit rate ensures essentially instantaneous localization. A Schrödinger's cat composed of ~10²³ atoms would be localized to a definite state in ~10⁻⁸ seconds — far too fast to observe a superposition. **CSL theory** (Continuous Spontaneous Localization) provides a relativistically appropriate continuous version in which collapse is described by a stochastic differential equation that drives the wave function continuously toward position eigenstates.

Objective collapse theories have several notable features. They are **empirically testable**: unlike Copenhagen and many-worlds, they make predictions that differ in principle from standard quantum mechanics. The collapse mechanism produces a small amount of energy and momentum spreading not predicted by the unmodified Schrödinger equation. Experiments have constrained the parameters of GRW/CSL significantly, though confirmation or refutation has not yet been achieved. They are also straightforwardly **realist**: there is a definite physical world, and wave function collapses are real physical events occurring without reference to any observer.

The **tail problem** poses a challenge for these theories: GRW and CSL do not produce perfectly sharp localizations — the wave function always retains exponentially small "tails" extending throughout all of space. No system is ever in a perfectly definite state. Albert and Loewer's "fuzzy link" addresses this by stipulating that a system has a determinate value of an observable when the probability of a different value is below some threshold.

## QBism and Relational Quantum Mechanics

QBism (Quantum Bayesianism), associated with Fuchs, Mermin, and Schack, takes a very different approach: the wave function is an agent's subjective probability assignment — a representation of an agent's beliefs about future experience, not a description of objective reality. "Collapse" is simply Bayesian updating upon learning the measurement outcome. Quantum mechanics is a theory about agents' experiences, not about the objective physical world. QBism dissolves the measurement problem by denying that the wave function represents physical reality: there is nothing to collapse because the wave function was never a real physical object. Critics argue that QBism does not explain the success of quantum mechanics at making predictions about the external world, and that the relationship between the agent's subjective experience and external physical reality is left opaque.

Relational quantum mechanics (Rovelli, 1996) holds that the quantum state of a system is not absolute but relative to an observer or reference system. Different observers may assign different quantum states to the same system, and there is no view-from-nowhere quantum state. Collapse is relative to an observer: the wave function collapses for the measuring apparatus when the measurement occurs, but from the perspective of a third party who has not interacted with the apparatus, no collapse has occurred. Relational QM avoids absolute collapse while preserving a definite-outcome perspective for each observer — at the cost of there being no observer-independent fact about whether the wave function has collapsed.

Together, collapse interpretations span a range of positions: from the pragmatic agnosticism of Copenhagen, through the physically modified dynamics of GRW and CSL, to the subjective-Bayesian stance of QBism. Each trades one kind of conceptual cost for another, and the choice among them implicates deep questions about the nature of physical reality, scientific realism, and the role of observers in quantum mechanics.
