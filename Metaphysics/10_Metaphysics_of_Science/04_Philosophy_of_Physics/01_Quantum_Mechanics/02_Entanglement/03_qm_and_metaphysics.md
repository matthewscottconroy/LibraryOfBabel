# Quantum Mechanics and Metaphysics

Quantum mechanics is not merely a physical theory with philosophical implications at the margins. It challenges some of the most basic assumptions built into traditional metaphysics: that objects have determinate properties at all times, that distinct objects are in principle distinguishable, that the whole is determined by its parts, that causation is a local affair, and that time has a determinate direction at the level of fundamental law. Each of these challenges opens onto a live debate in metaphysics that would not be nearly as pressured without quantum mechanics.

## Properties and Superposition

In classical physics and most traditional metaphysics, properties are conceived as determinate: for any object x and property P, either Px or ¬Px. The law of excluded middle applies. Quantum mechanics challenges this at least twice over.

A particle in a superposition of spin-up and spin-down is not spin-up and not spin-down; it is in an indefinite spin state. Several responses are possible. On the **eigenvalue-eigenstate link**, a system has a determinate value of observable O if and only if its state is an eigenstate of the corresponding operator Ô. A particle in a spin superposition genuinely lacks any determinate spin value — this is the orthodox quantum view. On **contextual values**, a particle has a property only relative to a context of measurement; spin value is relational, holding between the particle and the measuring apparatus. On **dispositional properties**, what is fundamental is the *disposition* to produce a certain measurement outcome with a certain probability, not a categorical property of having a definite value.

Notice that the reality of quantum properties connects to the scientific realism debate. If particles genuinely have indefinite properties, our ordinary assumption that the world is made of objects with determinate properties is fundamentally mistaken. OSR draws the further conclusion that properties are not had by objects but are structural — relations rather than monadic features.

## Individuality and Quantum Statistics

Classical objects are individuals: they have numerical identity persisting through time; any two distinct objects are distinguishable in principle by their different spatiotemporal locations or intrinsic properties; and Leibniz's Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (PII) holds. Quantum particles violate PII in characteristic ways. **Bosons** (photons, gluons, the Higgs boson) obey Bose-Einstein statistics: multiple bosons can occupy the same quantum state, and when two bosons are in the same state, there is no fact about which particle is which. **Fermions** (electrons, quarks) obey Fermi-Dirac statistics and the Pauli exclusion principle. Even for fermions in different states, the wave function is antisymmetrized: ψ(q₁, q₂) = -ψ(q₂, q₁). The state is defined over the combined configuration, not over individual particles.

French and Krause argue that quantum particles are best understood as *non-individuals*: entities for which no principle of numerical identity applies. This requires a revision of set-theoretic logic underlying standard metaphysics — since set theory presupposes well-defined individuals — and motivates the development of quasi-set theory. The metaphysical debate: should we conclude (a) that quantum particles are individuals with weak forms of PII (defined by their relational rather than intrinsic properties), (b) that quantum particles are non-individuals, or (c) that OSR is correct and there are no individuals at all?

## Entanglement, Holism, and the Parts-Whole Relation

Quantum entanglement challenges the mereological principle of separability: the idea that the state of a composite system is determined by the states of its parts. For two entangled particles in a singlet state, the joint quantum state |Ψ⁻⟩ = (1/√2)(|↑↓⟩ - |↓↑⟩) cannot be written as a product of single-particle states. The entangled state is irreducibly holistic — it represents properties of the pair that are not properties of either particle individually.

Jonathan Schaffer's priority monism holds that the one fundamental entity is the universe as a whole, with all parts being derivative from the whole. Quantum entanglement supports this: the total quantum state of the universe is fundamental, and individual particles are derivative features of the total state. Quantum entanglement also violates mereological supervenience: the joint state of two entangled particles is not determined by the states of the individual particles, even if we include their mutual relations. The entangled state is primitive and cannot be derived from any combination of component-level information. This provides a concrete physical example of non-supervenient relations — relations holding between particles without being grounded in any intrinsic properties of the relata.

## Causation, Time, and Modality

Standard quantum mechanics (in most interpretations) is indeterministic: the Born rule gives probabilities but no fact determines which outcome occurs. This challenges accounts of causation requiring determinism, and forces a probabilistic account: A causes B if A raises the probability of B in the right way. The precise formulation of probabilistic causation is contested.

Bell's theorem shows that quantum correlations cannot be explained by local causal models. Either there is nonlocal causation (Bohmian mechanics), or the correlations are not causal at all (van Fraassen's "correlations without cause"). The standard quantum mechanical framework is also time-symmetric — the Schrödinger equation is equally valid if the time coordinate is reversed — yet the Born rule collapse (or decoherence-driven branching in MWI) is future-directed. The origin of this time asymmetry must be traced to the initial conditions of the universe rather than any asymmetry in the fundamental laws.

On quantum modality: in MWI, all outcomes are actual in different branches, blurring the possible-worlds framework — there are no genuinely possible but not actual outcomes, only actual outcomes in different branches. Bird's dispositional essentialism suggests that the laws of quantum mechanics are metaphysically necessary: they express the essential dispositions of fundamental quantum entities. If electrons essentially have charge -e, then in any world containing electrons, the electromagnetic interactions obey the same laws. And the connection to free will: quantum indeterminism creates no direct space for libertarian free will, since random quantum events are no more under the agent's control than deterministic ones. The connection between quantum indeterminism and freedom remains elusive.

Attempts to reconcile quantum mechanics with general relativity face the "problem of time": the Wheeler-DeWitt equation governing quantum states of the universe in some formulations is a constraint equation with no explicit time variable. In such a framework, time itself may be emergent rather than fundamental — a feature of our classical approximation to a timeless quantum reality. This would mean that even our most basic experience of temporal direction is not built into the fundamental structure of the world.
