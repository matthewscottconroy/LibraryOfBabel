# Parts and Wholes

Consider the following question: when do some things compose a further thing? Your hand has five fingers. Together they compose a hand. Your hand and your arm compose a limb. Your body is composed of limbs and a torso and a head. This much seems relatively clear. But what about your left ear and the Eiffel Tower: do they compose a further thing — the scattered object consisting of your left ear plus the Tower? If you say no, what principle distinguishes this case from the case of your hand and fingers? If you say yes, you seem committed to an enormous proliferation of arbitrary and gerrymandered objects.

This is the Special Composition Question, posed with clarity by Peter van Inwagen: under what conditions do some objects compose a further object? The question is more difficult than it looks. Universalism — the view that any plurality of objects whatsoever compose a further object — is logically elegant and avoids the need for a principled distinction between composing and non-composing pluralities. But it requires us to accept an ontology populated by trillions of gerrymandered objects, many of which seem to exist merely by convention. Nihilism — the view that no composition ever occurs, that there are only mereological simples and no composite objects at all — avoids the proliferation but seems to deny the existence of tables, trees, and people.

Van Inwagen's own answer — that composition occurs if and only if the activities of the objects constitute a life — is conservative: it accepts organisms as genuine composites but denies the existence of artifacts like tables and chairs. This seems to match some of our intuitions while violating others: it is hard to see why biological integration should be the unique condition for composition.

Classical mereology — the formal theory of the part-whole relation developed by Leśniewski and Leonard-Goodman — provides a rigorous framework within which to pose and evaluate these questions. Its axioms (transitivity of parthood, supplementation principles) and the notion of mereological sum generate precise consequences that can be evaluated against our intuitions about cases. This chapter develops classical mereology, the Special Composition Question, and the major positions in the debate about when composition occurs.
