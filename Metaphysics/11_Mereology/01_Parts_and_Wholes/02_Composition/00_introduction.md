# Composition

When do some things compose something? The question seems trivially easy — a table is composed of its legs and top — but philosophical examination reveals it to be one of the most difficult questions in metaphysics. The problem is not that we cannot identify paradigm cases of composition; it is that we cannot articulate a principled, non-arbitrary condition that determines exactly when composition occurs and when it does not.

Van Inwagen's formulation of the Special Composition Question makes the problem precise. Consider any things: the atoms in this table, the particles in the Milky Way, the cells in your body. Under what conditions is there a further thing — something composed of those things? The "universalist" (or "all-fusionist") says: always — for any things, there is their fusion. The "nihilist" says: never — composition never really occurs; what seems to be composite objects are just arrangements of simples. The "restricted" view says: sometimes — composition occurs under some conditions but not others.

Van Inwagen's own answer — composition occurs when and only when the composing things constitute a life — is restricted and motivated by a principle of parsimony: we should posit composite objects only where there is something that genuinely unifies the parts, something over and above a mere spatial arrangement. Living things have this unity because their parts participate in life-sustaining activities. Tables, rocks, and mountains do not — they are mere aggregates, not genuine unities.

The challenge for the restricted view is to specify the composition condition without either falling into arbitrariness or being forced to admit all the composite objects the universalist accepts. Why should "constituting a life" be the magic condition? The response must explain what is special about life from an ontological point of view, and this connects to the broader metaphysics of biological organisms.

This section examines the Special Composition Question, van Inwagen's restricted view, universalism, and nihilism, with attention to the arguments that support each position.
