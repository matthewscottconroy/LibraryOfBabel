# Mereological Nihilism

The most radical answer to the Special Composition Question is that composition never occurs — or more precisely, that the only composite objects are trivial ones with a single "part," namely themselves. On this view, when we say "there is a table in the corner," we are not quantifying over any composite entity called a table. There are many atoms arranged tablewise, and our ordinary language describes that arrangement without committing us to an additional object over and above the atoms.

**Nihilism (strong form):** ¬∃x ∃y PP(y, x)    — every object is an atom; nothing has proper parts

**Nihilism (weak form, van Inwagen's characterization):** ∃y [the xs compose y] ↔ ∃!x (x is among the xs)    — a plurality of atoms composes nothing further

Both formulations have the same upshot: the only objects are the fundamental simples. Everything else is a manner of speaking.

## Nihilism's Formal Advantages

Nihilism has a clean answer to the SCQ: the conditions for composition (for y distinct from each x) are *never* satisfied. This gives it striking formal virtues. Since composition never occurs, there is no need to specify conditions for when it does — no risk of arbitrary or vague conditions. Since composition never occurs, there are no borderline cases of composite objects, no question of whether the proto-cloud composes a cloud or the embryo composes a person. All existential questions reduce to questions about atoms. And since there are no composite objects, there are no statues and no lumps to coincide — the statue-lump puzzle simply evaporates.

Ontological parsimony follows: where universalism posits 2ⁿ − 1 objects for n fundamental simples (every non-empty subset has a fusion), nihilism posits exactly n. The saving is dramatic. Quine's razor — do not multiply entities beyond necessity — is satisfied in an extreme form.

## The Paraphrase Strategy

Nihilists must explain why ordinary sentences like "there is a table in the room" seem true if tables don't exist. The standard strategy is *paraphrase*: such sentences are strictly false but communicate truths expressible in an ontologically correct language:

| Ordinary claim | Nihilist paraphrase |
|---|---|
| "There is a table here" | "There are atoms arranged tablewise here" |
| "The table has four legs" | "Some of the tablewise-arranged atoms are leg-wise arranged" |
| "The table was built in 1990" | "Atoms began being arranged tablewise in 1990" |
| "The same table that was here yesterday is here today" | "The atoms arranged tablewise here today are suitably related to atoms arranged tablewise here yesterday" |

The last paraphrase is illustrative of a deeper challenge: nihilism must handle persistence claims about "the same table" by invoking continuity relations among atoms, not identity of any composite object.

## The Main Objection: Persons Don't Exist

The most pressing objection is that nihilism seems to entail that persons don't exist — and that is almost impossible to accept. If no composite objects exist, then I don't exist (since I am composed of atoms), and the sentence "I am thinking about mereology" makes a false existential presupposition.

Nihilists respond in several ways. The *bite-the-bullet response* (Dorr, Unger): people, like tables, are constructs. Strict ontological truth is expressed in a fundamental language that quantifies only over simples. The sentence "I exist" is technically false in the language of strict ontology but communicates a true proposition — that certain atoms are arranged person-wise. Coherent, but requiring willingness to accept that virtually all ordinary existential commitments are false at the level of fundamental ontology.

Sider's *priority nihilism* response distinguishes between what ordinary language quantifiers range over (a restricted domain including tables and persons by convention) and what exists when quantifiers are unrestricted. At the unrestricted level, only atoms exist. The *grounding response* distinguishes fundamental from derivative existence: tables and persons *derivatively* exist — their existence is grounded in the arrangement of atoms — but they do not *fundamentally* exist as further entities. Critics argue this is unstable: either derivative existence is real existence (and nihilism is false) or it is not (and ordinary talk is simply false).

## The Paraphrase Completeness Problem

A serious technical objection is that the paraphrase strategy cannot be completed. Many claims about composite objects resist translation into atomistic terms. "Mary is thinking about the Eiffel Tower": if the Eiffel Tower doesn't exist, the paraphrase "Mary's mental states are directed at iron atoms arranged tower-wise" does not obviously preserve the content of the original thought. Intentional contexts are famously not extensional.

Counterfactuals are similarly resistant: "If the table were red, it would be visible from here." The counterfactual concerns a non-existent object (the table), and paraphrasing it into claims about atoms requires specifying how "red tablewise-arranged atoms" feature in counterfactual scenarios — which seems to require the very concept of a composite that nihilism forswears.

## The Epistemic Argument for Nihilism

Some philosophers argue for nihilism on explicitly epistemic grounds:

- P1. If composite objects exist, our beliefs about which atoms compose which object could systematically be wrong — there is no reliable mechanism for detecting composition events at the macro-scale.
- P2. We have no reliable mechanism for detecting the presence or absence of such composition events.
- P3. If we lack a reliable detection mechanism, and positing something provides no additional explanatory power, we should not posit it.
- C. We should not posit composite objects.

The key premise is P3. Critics question it: the existence of composite objects *does* provide explanatory power — it explains why macro-level regularities hold, why there are genuine causal powers at the macro-level, and why persons and their intentional states are real.

Nihilism remains a minority position partly because the paraphrase strategy is difficult to carry out consistently and partly because most philosophers want their ontology to include persons. But it represents the logically clean terminus of the case against unrestricted composition. It shows us how far one can go while keeping a formally rigorous position — and thereby reveals, by contrast, exactly what cost every other position is implicitly paying.
