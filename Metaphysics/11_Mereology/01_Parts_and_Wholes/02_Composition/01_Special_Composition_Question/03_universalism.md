# Mereological Universalism

If we are going to admit any objects at all — if we are going to say there are atoms and they stand in relations — then what principled reason could there be for refusing to acknowledge their sum? You believe in a and b. Each is there, real, occupying its region of the world. Why not a + b? If the parts are already there, what would it cost to admit the whole?

This is the core intuition behind mereological universalism, also called unrestricted composition. For any non-empty collection of objects, there exists a further object that is their mereological sum. The Eiffel Tower and your left thumb compose something. Any *xs* whatsoever compose a *y*. This is the answer to the Special Composition Question built into classical mereology.

**Universalism (Universal Composition, UC):**

∀φ [∃x φ(x) → ∃z ∀y (O(y, z) ↔ ∃x (φ(x) ∧ O(y, x)))]

For any non-empty condition φ, there is a fusion of all the φ-ers. Equivalently, for any objects a and b, a + b exists; for any finite or infinite collection, their sum exists.

## The Innocence Argument (Lewis)

David Lewis's central defense in *Parts of Classes* (1991) is the *innocence argument*:

- P1. If you accept two objects a and b, you are already committed to a portion of reality that includes both a and b.
- P2. The mereological sum a + b is nothing over and above a and b together — it is just them, described collectively in the singular.
- P3. If a + b is nothing over and above a and b, then accepting a + b costs no additional ontological commitment.
- C. Given any objects, their sum may be admitted without additional ontological cost.

The *parity corollary* follows: if you accept a and b but refuse to accept a + b, you draw an arbitrary distinction. The sum is exactly as real as its parts; refusing it is ontologically capricious.

Van Inwagen objects that Lewis's claim begs the question: whether the sum is "nothing over and above" its parts is precisely what is at issue. If it is, then admitting it does increase ontological commitment; if it isn't, then perhaps the sum is *identical* to the parts — the composition-as-identity thesis, which Lewis does not endorse in its full form. Lewis's reply is that ontological commitment must be distinguished from ontological burden: we are committed to a + b when we are committed to a and b, because the sum is just them described differently. The sum is not a further addition to the fundamental inventory; it is a reconfiguration of what was already there.

## The Vagueness Argument (Sider)

Theodore Sider's *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001) offers an independent argument:

- P1. If restricted composition is true, there are cases where it is *indeterminate* whether some xs compose a y.
- P2. For composition to be indeterminate, there would have to be *ontological vagueness* — vagueness in what exists, not merely in how we describe what exists.
- P3. There is no ontological vagueness: existence is not vague, and the question "does y exist?" always has a determinate answer.
- C. Restricted composition is false.
- C2. Since nihilism is independently implausible (it denies persons), universalism is true.

The crucial premise is P3. Sider defends it by arguing that vagueness is always semantic — a matter of indeterminate reference or vague predicates — never ontological. Some philosophers accept ontological vagueness and thus reject P3; Gareth Evans's famous 1978 argument against vague objects attempts to show that the supposition of vague identity leads to contradiction, though this argument has been disputed. If ontological vagueness is coherent, restricted composition remains on the table.

## The Apparent Costs of Universalism

Universalism commits us to gerrymandered objects: the fusion of the rings of Saturn and a pebble on a Welsh beach, the fusion of all red objects currently visible from Earth. These objects seem to lack causal unity, qualitative coherence, or any feature we normally associate with objecthood. Lewis's response is that the discrimination between natural and gerrymandered objects is not *ontological* but *qualitative*. Natural objects are those whose parts share many properties and stand in natural causal relations; gerrymandered objects are those whose parts share few and interact little. Universalism does not deny this qualitative distinction — it insists that both natural and gerrymandered objects exist. The notion of *naturalness* (Lewis's "perfectly natural properties") does the work of distinguishing the scientifically and practically significant objects from the arbitrary fusions, without any metaphysical distinction in what exists.

There is also the statue-lump problem. If the statue and the lump are both fusions of the same atoms, universalism seems to entail they are identical (by extensionality) — precisely the conclusion constitution theorists dispute. The four-dimensionalist response is that the statue and lump have different temporal parts and are therefore different fusions.

The universalism-physics connection is worth noting. Four-dimensionalism and universalism fit naturally with special relativity's picture: in relativistic spacetime, "objects" are naturally understood as spacetime regions or functions from spacetime points to properties. The mereological sum of any spacetime regions is itself a spacetime region — universalism is naturally built into the spacetime picture. Resisting universalism means positing metaphysical constraints on which spacetime regions "count" as genuine objects, constraints with no natural home in the physics.

## Markosian's Brutal Composition

Ned Markosian's "Brutal Composition" (1998) argues that the SCQ has no informative answer: some collections compose objects and others don't, but there is no general condition that distinguishes them. Composition facts are *brute* — metaphysically fundamental and not explicable in terms of anything more basic. This is distinct from universalism: Markosian thinks restricted composition is true, but the restriction has no principled characterization.

The objection is that this concedes arbitrariness: if composition facts are brute, they are unexplained. Why do *these* atoms compose a table and *those* do not? Markosian accepts this result and argues that bruteness in ontology is no more troubling than bruteness elsewhere — the brute laws of nature, the brute charge of the electron. Whether this analogy holds is itself a point of debate.

Universalism is thus not merely a claim about physical objects but a structural position with implications for the foundations of mathematics, the metaphysics of modality, and the theory of ontological commitment. Lewis exploited the parallel with ZF set theory's unrestricted comprehension schema in *Parts of Classes* to give a mereological reconstruction of set theory. The success of that project — and its difficulties, particularly around the singleton function — illuminates both what mereology can do and what remains irreducibly set-theoretic. The debate with nihilism and restricted composition is one of the generative engines of contemporary analytic metaphysics.
