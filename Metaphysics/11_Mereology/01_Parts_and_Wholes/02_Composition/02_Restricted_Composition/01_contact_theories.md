# Contact Theories of Composition

The most intuitive answer to the question "under what conditions do some things compose a further thing?" is also the simplest: they compose something when they are touching. A table exists because its legs are in contact with its top, the fibers of the wood are bonded within each part, and so on down. When parts are separated — a disassembled table lying in pieces across a warehouse — there is a natural reluctance to say the table still exists. Contact appears necessary for the existence of the composite.

Among the restricted composition views, contact theories are the most in keeping with ordinary thought. According to a simple contact theory:

∃y [the xs compose y] ↔ C*(xs)

where C*(xs) means "the xs are connected by a chain of pairwise contacts." A table composes something because its parts touch in a chain; your pencil does not compose an object with the moon because there is no such chain between them.

There is an immediate subtlety about what "contact" means. One could require *direct* geometric touching (contact as zero-distance), or allow *force-mediated* connection — if you hold a string taut between two separate blocks, forces are transmitted even though the blocks don't touch. A strict contact theory says the blocks compose nothing together (unless we count the string); a looser version counts force-mediated connection.

## Van Inwagen's Objections

Van Inwagen raises a series of objections to contact theories in *Material Beings* that are broadly considered decisive.

The first is the *arbitrary threshold* problem. Two atoms touching compose something; two atoms a nanometer apart do not? At the subatomic level, "touching" is not a precise notion — there is no sharp distance at which quantum wavefunctions cease to overlap. Any contact theory must either pick an arbitrary distance threshold (which seems metaphysically unmotivated) or admit that contact is vague (which generates vagueness in existence). The formal version: let d(x, y) be the minimum distance between any point in x and any point in y. A contact theory requires ∃ε > 0 such that:

∃y [the xs compose y] ↔ for any two xᵢ, xⱼ among the xs, there is a chain xᵢ = z₁, z₂, …, zₙ = xⱼ with d(zₖ, zₖ₊₁) < ε

There is no principled value of ε. Any choice is arbitrary.

Second, *physical incoherence*. Quantum mechanics suggests that particles interact through fields rather than through literal geometric contact. The notion of strict geometric contact — two particles occupying adjacent spatial regions with no gap — may be physically incoherent. Contact theories requiring the classical notion of touching may have no application to the actual physical world.

Third, *discontinuous generation and destruction*. Contact theories imply that when two clay balls are squeezed together, a new composite object comes into existence the moment they touch; when pulled slightly apart, it ceases to exist. Small motions of fundamental particles would constantly generate and destroy objects. For any configuration of atoms slightly below the contact threshold, no composite exists; for a configuration slightly above, one suddenly exists. This sharp ontological discontinuity for arbitrarily small physical differences seems to place too much metaphysical weight on a tiny spatial gap.

Fourth, *odd modal consequences*. A table would cease to exist if any two of its molecules were moved apart by more than ε — even if the table looks and functions exactly as before. This makes ordinary objects far more fragile than we ordinarily suppose.

## More Sophisticated Alternatives

More sophisticated contact theories replace simple touching with richer notions. *Fastening* (van Inwagen's term): the xs compose something when they are interlocked in a way that resists separation — glued parts, screwed parts, interlocked parts. More demanding than mere contact, more in keeping with ordinary intuitions. But van Inwagen ultimately concludes he cannot make "fastening" precise enough to be a genuine composition-enabling relation.

*Continuous causal connection*: the xs compose something when there is continuous causal influence among them. This is broader than geometric contact — gravity can provide continuous causal connection over large distances — but more selective than mere co-presence. The problem is that continuous gravitational influence connects every massive object in the universe with every other. On this view, the universe is one big composite object, which is no better than universalism.

*Structural integrity*: the xs compose something when they are arranged in a configuration that resists disruption — a stable equilibrium they collectively maintain. This is an improvement over simple contact but requires specifying what counts as "stable equilibrium" in a way that does not reintroduce arbitrary thresholds.

## The Deep Lesson

Van Inwagen's analysis shows that no contact-style restriction can be made principled without invoking something more than geometric or physical proximity. The deeper lesson he draws is that any principled restriction must appeal to a *robust structural or functional property* — not a property of the arrangement of parts in space, but a property of what the parts collectively *do*. This is why he opts for *life* as the composition-enabling relation: life is not a spatial property but a functional-biological one, involving the parts constituting a unified causal process rather than merely being adjacent. Whether this move is ultimately successful is the question taken up in the discussion of organicism.
