# Organicism

Van Inwagen's own answer to the Special Composition Question is called *organicism*: some xs compose something y if and only if the activity of the xs constitutes a *life*. On this view, the only genuine composite objects in the world are living organisms. Tables, chairs, planets, and rivers are not single things at all — they are many things arranged in characteristic ways.

## The Formal Statement

Let **L(xs)** mean "the joint activity of the xs constitutes a life" — the xs collectively participate in a single, self-sustaining, self-regulating biological process. Then:

∃y [the xs compose y] ↔ L(xs) ∨ ∃!x (x is among the xs)

Composition occurs just when the xs are caught up in a life, or when there is only one of them (the single atom trivially "composes" itself). The ontological upshot: the only composite objects are organisms. All other pluralities of atoms — tables, chairs, mountains, planets — are arrangements, not things.

## The Motivation

Van Inwagen wants to avoid universalism, which generates implausible gerrymandered objects, while also avoiding nihilism, which denies the existence of persons. Life gives him both. Persons genuinely exist as composite objects: the person is not merely atoms arranged person-wise but a genuine further thing — the organism — that has those atoms as parts. At the same time, tables and chairs do not exist as genuine composite objects; there are only their atomic constituents, arranged in characteristic configurations.

The key to the view is the contrast between genuine functional unity and mere arrangement. Consider a living cell: its atoms collectively sustain metabolic processes — they exchange energy, maintain a membrane, replicate genetic material. Any subset of atoms affects all the others; the activity is irreducibly joint. The cell does things that no proper subset of its atoms does. Now consider a heap of sand: the grains do nothing collectively. Each grain is doing what it would do anyway — sitting, responding to gravity. There is no process the grains are collectively engaged in; their "togetherness" is incidental. A table falls in the same category: the atoms are in a stable configuration, but they are not *doing* anything jointly. They are merely arranged. Van Inwagen insists that arrangement is not enough; joint *activity* is required, and only living organisms exhibit the right kind.

The core argument makes this explicit:

- P1. Composition requires a principled condition — not any arbitrary collection composes something.
- P2. The only non-arbitrary condition available (that avoids the arbitrariness and vagueness objections to contact, fastening, and similar conditions) is functional unity.
- P3. The only kind of functional unity that is robust, non-conventional, and non-arbitrary is biological life.
- C. Composition occurs if and only if the composing objects constitute a life.

Premise P3 does the most work. Van Inwagen argues that other candidates for functional unity — computational processes, machine functioning, social institutions — are either conventional (defined by our practices) or reducible to biological-like functional unity. A computer functions in the relevant sense only because it is used by persons; a society functions because it is composed of persons. Life is the foundational case.

## Objections

The most common objection is the most obvious: tables don't exist on this view, and that seems plainly false. Van Inwagen accepts the implication with equanimity. Our ordinary talk of tables is useful and communicates truths, but those truths are expressed by sentences like "there are atoms arranged tablewise in the corner." Strictly speaking, "there is a table in the corner" is false. Critics find this almost impossible to accept — the existence of tables seems more certain than any metaphysical theory that denies it. Van Inwagen's response is that philosophy sometimes forces revision of common sense when common sense conflicts with theoretical principles.

The *vagueness of life* objection is more pointed. Is a virus alive? A prion? A self-replicating RNA molecule? A computer program that reproduces itself? The boundary of life is vague, and if L(xs) is vague, then "the xs compose something" is vague — which generates exactly the vagueness in existence that Sider's argument targets. Van Inwagen acknowledges this and maintains that vagueness in the biological domain is more tractable than vagueness in the mereological domain — it is an empirical problem with empirical resources. Critics are not satisfied.

There is also the *decomposing organisms* objection. What happens when an organism dies? For a moment after death, all the same atoms are present and arranged the same way. Van Inwagen must say that the composite object — the organism — ceases to exist the moment the life ceases, even before any macroscopic change. And colonial organisms complicate the picture further: a Portuguese man-of-war consists of polyps that are in some ways separate organisms and in others parts of a larger one; a mycorrhizal network connects many trees in a forest. "Constituting a life" may itself be a vague and multiply-applicable predicate.

A notable consequence is that organicism *entirely eliminates* the ontology of artifacts. There are no tables, chairs, buildings, ships, tools, or machines as composite objects. Van Inwagen argues that practices involving artifacts can be understood in terms of atom-arrangements — the table-owner owns the atoms arranged tablewise — but critics argue that this paraphrase loses something essential about how object-level predicates function.

## Organicism and Personal Identity

One feature that makes organicism attractive is that it preserves personal identity in a robust sense. Persons are organisms, and organisms are genuine composite objects with determinate identity conditions provided by biological continuity. This contrasts favorably with bundle theories (persons are collections of experiences) or nihilism (persons don't exist as composite objects). Organicism gives persons a metaphysically respectable status that neither universalism — which makes them just one among infinitely many fusions — nor nihilism — which eliminates them — provides.

For van Inwagen, the SCQ is ultimately a question about what kinds of things matter. Organisms, as bearers of lives, are the paradigm case of things that matter — and that is the deepest motivation for organicism, beneath all the formal machinery.
