# Problems with Restricted Composition

Every restricted composition view — the claim that composition occurs under some conditions but not others — faces a battery of objections that have led many philosophers to conclude that only the extreme positions (universalism or nihilism) are tenable. Understanding these objections is essential for appreciating why the mereology debate has the shape it does.

## The Arbitrariness Problem

Whatever condition you specify — contact, life, functional organization, causal unity — one can always ask: why *that* condition? Why does composition occur when parts are touching but not when they are 0.001 millimeters apart? Any line drawn will appear arbitrary unless backed by a deep metaphysical explanation of what makes that property composition-enabling.

Suppose the restriction is stated as:

∃y [the xs compose y] ↔ R(xs)

For R to be non-arbitrary, there must be a *metaphysical ground* for R being composition-enabling — some property of R that explains why, when R holds of the xs, they compose something, while when R fails, they don't. The candidates each face distinctive difficulties. *Causal unity* (R(xs) iff the xs form a causal unit — each part affecting all others) implies that large-scale gravitational systems, entire galaxies, are single objects. *Structural integrity* (R(xs) iff the xs maintain a stable equilibrium) is vague and threshold-dependent. *Emergence* (R(xs) iff new properties emerge from the combination) is itself contested and vague. *Teleological unity* (R(xs) iff the xs jointly have a telos — a function or goal) seems to require prior reference to intentional agents who assign functions.

Each candidate either is vague, arbitrarily specific, or reduces to another restricted-composition view with the same problems.

## The Vagueness Problem (Sider's Argument)

Theodore Sider's most influential objection, developed in *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001), turns on the unacceptability of vague composition:

- P1. If restricted composition is true, then for any composition-enabling condition R, there are borderline cases — situations where it is indeterminate whether R(xs).
- P2. If it is indeterminate whether R(xs), it is indeterminate whether the xs compose something, i.e., indeterminate whether ∃y [the xs compose y].
- P3. If ∃y [the xs compose y] is indeterminate, there is ontological vagueness — vagueness in what exists.
- P4. There is no ontological vagueness: ∃y [the xs compose y] is either determinately true or determinately false.
- C1. There are no borderline cases for any composition-enabling condition R.
- C2. Since any natural candidate for R has borderline cases, no natural candidate is correct.
- C3. Restricted composition is false.

Sider defends P4 by arguing that vagueness is always semantic — a matter of our representations having indeterminate meaning — never ontic. If the xs either compose something or they don't, there is a fact of the matter even if we cannot know it. Ontological vagueness would require there to be no fact of the matter about whether a certain entity exists, which is incoherent.

The objection to P4 comes from philosophers who accept ontological vagueness. Evans's 1978 argument against vague identity (which would support P4) has been widely disputed — the property *being such that it is indeterminate whether it is identical to a* may not be a genuine property that Leibniz's Law can operate on. If ontological vagueness is coherent, Sider's argument fails.

## The Proliferation Problem

Even granting a non-vague condition R, there will be many near-misses — things that barely fail to meet R. Consider a proto-organism that falls just below the threshold: it has self-repair mechanisms that work 49% of the time. Just on the other side is an organism whose mechanisms work 51% of the time. On a restricted view, there is a metaphysical chasm between these two cases: one is a genuine composite object; the other is merely many atoms. For any threshold n in a continuous ordering, the difference between adjacent cases is infinitesimal, but the difference in ontological status — exists as a composite vs. doesn't — is maximal. Sharp thresholds in ontological status for continuous variations in physical properties seem to violate a principle of proportionality.

## The Explanatory Burden

Restricted composition views seem to require an explanation of *why* some configurations of matter generate an extra entity while others do not. This explanatory burden — what makes composition-enabling properties special — is not faced by universalism (which says fusion always happens) or nihilism (which says it never does). Both are parsimonious about explanatory demands; they posit exceptionless principles that require no case-by-case explanation. Restricted composition requires both a general principle and an explanation of why that principle picks out the relevant configurations.

The emergentist response is that composition is an *emergent* phenomenon — certain configurations give rise to genuinely new causal powers and properties that the individual parts lack, and this emergence grounds composition. The objection is that emergentism faces its own explanatory challenges: what makes a property emergent rather than merely higher-level? The standard criterion is downward causation, but whether higher-level properties can cause changes in lower-level physical states is itself deeply contested.

## The Restriction-Independence Problem

For any proposed restriction R, we can describe cases that satisfy R but where intuitively no composite object exists, and cases that fail R but where intuitively one does. Two bacteria in temporary metabolic cooperation might satisfy a liberal version of the life-condition, but we would not say they compose a single organism. Conversely, a person in a persistent vegetative state may fail to satisfy a stringent version of the life-condition, but we may still want to say the person exists. A loosely assembled machine (parts in contact but held together only by friction) seems to be a composite even though its parts could be easily separated; a tightly integrated chemical compound clearly does too. No single condition tracks our intuitions cleanly.

## The Best Responses Available

Restricted-composition theorists are not without resources. Markosian ("Brutal Composition," 1998) argues that composition facts are brute — they have no further explanation. Some collections compose objects and others don't, and that is a fundamental fact about the world. This response accepts the arbitrariness and explanatory burden objections but argues that they apply equally to any foundational fact, including the brute laws of nature. Fine, Baker, and others argue for *sortal-relative composition*: composition is always relative to a sortal concept or kind, and the demand for a single condition R is a demand for something that doesn't exist. A third option is *epistemic modesty*: perhaps the correct R exists but is unknown to us, discoverable through future science or metaphysical investigation.

Each of these responses is costly. The problems with restricted composition explain why the landscape is polarized between universalism and nihilism rather than settled around a sensible middle position. The middle ground, it turns out, is genuinely difficult to occupy. That is itself a philosophically significant result.
