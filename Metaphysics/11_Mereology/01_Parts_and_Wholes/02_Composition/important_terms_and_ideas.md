# Important Terms and Ideas: Composition

**Special Composition Question (SCQ)**
Van Inwagen's question: under what conditions do some objects (the xs) compose a further object (a y)? The possible answers range from Nihilism (never) through various restricted views (only when the xs are in contact, bonded, or constitute a life) to Universalism (always).

**Universalism (Unrestricted Composition)**
The view that for any collection of objects whatsoever, there exists their mereological sum. Lewis defends this: the table, the distant star, and the number 7 together compose an object—albeit a scattered, bizarre one. Universalists argue that composition is ontologically innocent.

**Nihilism (Mereological Nihilism)**
The view that no objects have proper parts—the only things that exist are mereological atoms (simples). All apparent macroscopic objects (tables, persons) are really just arrangements of simples. Nihilism is revisionary but has the advantage of avoiding puzzles about composition and identity.

**Restricted Composition**
The view that composition obtains under some but not all conditions. Van Inwagen's answer: only when the simples constitute a life. Others: when the parts are in contact, bonded by cohesive forces, or form a functional unity. The challenge is to state the restriction without arbitrary cutoffs.

**Vagueness Argument for Unrestricted Composition**
Sider's argument: if composition is restricted, there must be a sharp or vague boundary between cases where composition occurs and cases where it does not. Sharp cutoffs are implausible (why exactly four particles?); vague composition facts are also problematic. Therefore composition is unrestricted.

**Ontological Innocence of Mereology**
Lewis's claim: mereological sums do not add to our ontological commitments because they just are the things already in our ontology arranged a certain way. The sum of Alice and Bob is not a third entity over and above Alice and Bob—it is them, collectively.
