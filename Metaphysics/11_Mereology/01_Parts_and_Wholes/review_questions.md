# Review Questions — Parts and Wholes

1. Classical mereology takes parthood as a single primitive and derives all other mereological concepts from it. What structural commitments follow from treating parthood as a partial order, and why does formalizing these commitments make certain metaphysical puzzles—such as the statue-lump case—more tractable rather than less?

2. Lewis argues that mereological sums are "ontologically innocent"—admitting a + b costs nothing beyond already believing in a and b. What does this claim depend on, and why do critics argue that it begs the question at issue rather than settling it? Is there a coherent notion of "ontological cost" that would allow us to evaluate this claim neutrally?

3. Mereological extensionality (the theorem that objects sharing all proper parts are identical) follows from strong supplementation but not from weak supplementation alone. What work does strong supplementation do that weak supplementation cannot, and why does this distinction place the statue-lump puzzle at the heart of the whole axiomatic framework rather than at its periphery?

4. Van Inwagen argues that every proposed restricted composition condition—contact, fastening, cohesion, functional organization—either is vague, arbitrarily specific, or collapses into something else. Evaluate this diagnosis: is there a candidate he dismisses too quickly, or does his survey establish that the very form of a restricted composition answer is unprincipled?

5. Mereological nihilism and mereological universalism are structurally opposite answers to the Special Composition Question, yet both are motivated in part by the same argument—Sider's vagueness argument. Trace how the argument leads toward each extreme and explain whether this convergence reveals a genuine logical pressure or a gap in the argument's premises.

6. Van Inwagen's organicism preserves the existence of persons as genuine composite objects while eliminating tables and chairs. Assess this asymmetry: does the appeal to "constituting a life" mark a principled ontological distinction, or does it reflect a prior commitment to the importance of persons that is imported into mereology rather than derived from it?

7. The atomism-versus-gunk question is formally independent of the core mereological axioms—both atomistic and atomless classical mereology are consistent systems. What does this independence reveal about the scope of classical mereology as a foundational theory? Does the formal neutrality count in favor of the theory or against it as an account of the actual structure of reality?

8. Leśniewski's nominalist motivation for mereology was to replace set-theoretic membership with concrete parthood relations, thereby avoiding abstract objects. How well does classical mereology, with its unrestricted fusion principle, actually serve this goal? Does the fusion of all red objects constitute something clearly less abstract than the set of all red objects, and what hangs on the answer?
