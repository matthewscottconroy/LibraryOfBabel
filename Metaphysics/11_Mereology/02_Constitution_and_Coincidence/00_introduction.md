# Constitution and Coincidence in Mereology

The mereological approach to coincidence — the apparent fact that two distinct objects can share all their parts — faces a structural problem that brings out the deepest tensions in the metaphysics of material objects. If constitution (a lump of clay constituting a statue) is a relation distinct from identity, and if two things can be constituted by the same parts, then mereological simples must appear in the fundamental ontology without any of the familiar composites being derivable from them by standard mereological principles. This forces a choice: either revise mereology, or deny coincident objects, or accept a non-standard mereological framework.

The constitution relation — holding between the clay and the statue, between the river of water molecules and the river, between the biological organism and the person — is presented by its defenders (Baker, Haslanger, Johnston) as a real relation of material constitution that is not identity. The clay constitutes the statue because it plays the statue-role in the right circumstances; the statue exists as a distinct entity because it has different persistence conditions from the clay. This allows us to count correctly: there is one lump and one statue, both of which occupy the same region of space.

Critics press the problem of spatial coincidence: if the lump and the statue share all their parts and all their spatial locations, what distinguishes them as numerically distinct? The four-dimensionalist offers one answer: they share their spatial parts but differ in their temporal parts — the lump existed before the statue and will exist after it. The modal differentialist offers another: they differ in their modal properties, properties that do not reduce to spatial or temporal parts. Whether modal properties can do this work without generating further puzzles is a question this chapter pursues.

Vagueness in mereology adds another dimension: are the boundaries of composite objects determinate, or is there genuine indeterminacy about which atoms are parts of which composites? The connection between vagueness and composition, and between vagueness and identity, raises questions that span formal logic, metaphysics, and the philosophy of language.
