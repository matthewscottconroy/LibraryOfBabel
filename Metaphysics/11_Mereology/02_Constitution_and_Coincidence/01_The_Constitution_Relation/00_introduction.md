# The Constitution Relation in Mereology

The lump of clay and the statue it constitutes present a puzzle for mereology as much as for the general theory of identity. On a standard mereological view, the lump and the statue have exactly the same parts at any time they coincide — the same clay molecules, arranged in the same way. If mereological extensionality holds — if things with the same parts are identical — then the lump and the statue are identical. But if they are identical, they must have the same modal properties; yet they do not: the statue cannot survive squashing, while the lump can.

This puzzle is the constitution problem from a mereological angle. The mereological framework forces a choice: either reject extensionality (allowing that the lump and the statue are two things with exactly the same parts) or accept identity (collapsing the distinction between the lump and the statue). The first option preserves our intuitions about persistence and modal properties at the cost of revising classical mereology; the second option preserves classical mereology at the cost of counterintuitive identity claims.

Four-dimensionalism offers a mereological resolution. On the four-dimensional view, material objects extend through time as well as space; they have temporal parts just as they have spatial parts. The lump and the statue are not spatially coincident three-dimensional objects; they are distinct four-dimensional objects that overlap — share temporal parts — during the period when the clay constitutes the statue. The lump's temporal parts include the clay before and after the statue; the statue's temporal parts are confined to the period when the clay is shaped as the statue. No spatial coincidence of distinct three-dimensional objects is required.

This section examines the constitution problem in mereological terms, the standard responses (reject extensionality, embrace four-dimensionalism), and what each implies for the broader theory of composition and persistence.
