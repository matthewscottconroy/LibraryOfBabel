# The Constitution Relation

The sculptor finishes her work. She steps back, and there in front of her is a bronze statue. The lump of bronze that she has been working with for months is still there — it occupies the same region of space, has the same mass, the same color, the same atomic composition. Two things, one place. Or is it two things? That is the question.

Constitution theory says it is two things. The lump *constitutes* the statue without being identical to it. This requires a relation — constitution — that is neither identity nor ordinary mereological composition, but something irreducibly between them.

## The Core Argument for Distinct Constituters

The standard argument for constitution-without-identity appeals to Leibniz's Law. The statue s and the lump of bronze c appear to differ in their properties:

- P1. s would be destroyed by melting — statues cannot survive being melted and recast
- P2. c would not be destroyed by melting — lumps of bronze survive changes of shape
- P3. ∀x ∀y [x = y → ∀F (Fx ↔ Fy)]     (Leibniz's Law)
- C. s ≠ c

Similarly for historical properties: c existed before t (when the sculptor began working with the bronze in a pre-statue form), while s did not exist before t (statues come into being only when the relevant shape and artistic intention are realized). Two different histories, one spatial location. The statue and the lump are distinct.

## Lynne Rudder Baker's Constitution View

Lynne Rudder Baker is the most systematic contemporary defender, developing the view in *Persons and Bodies* (2000) and *The Metaphysics of Everyday Life* (2007). The central claims:

**Constitution is asymmetric:** The lump constitutes the statue, but not vice versa. Asymmetry is grounded in *kind-dependence*: statues depend for their existence on being constituted by some material object with the right properties, while lumps do not depend for their existence on constituting any artwork.

**Constitution involves borrowed properties:** When the lump constitutes the statue, the statue *borrows* many of its properties from the lump — its mass, its shape, its bronze color. The lump's physical properties are available to the statue by virtue of the constitution relation, without the statue having those properties independently.

Formally, let Con(x, y) mean "x constitutes y":

- ∀x ∀y [Con(x, y) → ¬Con(y, x)]     (asymmetry)
- ∀x ¬Con(x, x)     (irreflexivity)
- ∀x ∀y [Con(x, y) → x ≠ y]     (constitution is not identity)
- ∀x ∀y [Con(x, y) → ∀t (Loc(x, t) ↔ Loc(y, t))]     (co-location)

Constitution is not identity because it is asymmetric; identity is symmetric. Constitution is not ordinary composition because two mereologically coincident objects can have all the same parts yet be distinct.

Baker also distinguishes *primary-kind* properties from other properties. Each object has a primary kind that determines its essential nature: the statue's primary kind is artwork; the lump's primary kind is bronze-stuff. Primary-kind properties are essential — the statue would cease to exist if it ceased to be an artwork of the relevant type; the lump would not. This framework handles the Leibniz's Law arguments about modal properties: the statue and the lump differ in their primary-kind properties, and primary-kind properties are genuine properties that generate legitimate Leibniz's Law conclusions.

The sortal-relativist objection is that "would be destroyed by melting" is not a genuine property of an object but a way of describing the object relative to a sortal — true of the thing *qua statue* and false of it *qua lump*, with one thing being described in two ways. Baker's reply is that sortals track metaphysically real properties: the kind *artwork* is a genuine natural kind with its own identity conditions, grounding the modal differences in real differences between distinct objects.

## Constitution and Mereological Composition

We should be clear about how constitution differs from mereological composition. In classical mereology, if x has exactly the same parts as y, then x = y (extensionality). Constitution theory denies this: the statue and the lump have the same parts but are distinct. This means constitution theory is committed to rejecting mereological extensionality.

But how can two distinct objects have the same parts? Baker's answer: they belong to different primary kinds, and kind-membership is a genuine metaphysical property not reducible to mereological structure. The atoms compose a lump *and* a statue because "composing a lump" and "composing a statue" are different facts, grounded in different kinds.

Kit Fine offers a more technically developed response in "Things and Their Parts" (1999): *relativized mereology*. Parthood is not a single two-place relation P(x, y) but a family of relations P_K(x, y) — parthood relative to a principle of unity K. An atom is part of the statue *under the unity principle for artworks* and part of the lump *under the unity principle for material stuff*. Extensionality holds within each sortal domain — no two artworks with the same artwork-parts are distinct; no two lumps with the same lump-parts are distinct — but cross-domain comparison is illegitimate.

This is technically clever, but it may be *ad hoc*. The multiplicity of parthood relations seems to be introduced precisely to handle the coincidence puzzle, without independent motivation. What determines whether z is an artwork-part of s? If it is determined by the artist's intentions or aesthetic conventions, mereology has become dependent on intentional and normative facts in a way foreign to the formal discipline. Fine's reply is that the principles of unity are metaphysically real, not conventional — the artwork principle is determined by the kind *artwork*, a genuine natural kind with its own identity conditions.

## Historical Development

The constitution relation entered analytic philosophy largely through debates about personal identity and artifact identity. Thomas Hobbes raised the Ship of Theseus puzzle; John Locke distinguished between *person* and *organism* as different kinds with different identity conditions. In contemporary analytic philosophy, Michael Rea's "Sameness Without Identity" (1997) distinguishes constitution from identity and defends it as a basic metaphysical relation. Baker's systematic development applies it to persons. David Wiggins's *Sameness and Substance* (1980) and *Sameness and Substance Renewed* (2001) develop sortal essentialism — on which objects are individuated by their sortals — generating many of the same results as constitution theory but with different metaphysical machinery.

The constitution view is now a major position in the debate over coincident objects, material constitution, and the ontology of artifacts. Its central difficulty is making the borrowed-property story sufficiently clear to do the metaphysical work required of it without either collapsing into identity or dissolving the constituted object into a shadow of its constituter.
