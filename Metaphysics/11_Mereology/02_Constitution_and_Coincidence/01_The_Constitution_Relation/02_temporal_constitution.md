# Temporal Constitution

The constitution puzzle is not confined to spatial cases. Consider your own body. The atoms of which you are composed are gradually replaced — through metabolic processes, cellular renewal, the ordinary turnover of biological matter. Over a long enough period, not a single atom from when you were born remains. Yet there is a strong intuition that *you* have persisted through all of this. What is the relation between the person you are now and the material that currently constitutes you? And what is the relation between today's constituting matter and yesterday's?

This is the *temporal* dimension of the constitution puzzle: at each time, some matter constitutes an object, but no single portion of matter *is* that object, and the constituting matter changes while the object — supposedly — remains.

## The Puzzle of Gradual Replacement

Suppose an object O begins at t₀ composed of matter M₀. Over time, the matter is gradually replaced:

- At t₀: O is composed of M₀ (all original matter)
- At t₁: O is composed of M₁ (90% original, 10% new)
- At t₂: O is composed of M₂ (50% original, 50% new)
- At tₙ: O is composed of Mₙ (0% original, 100% new)

Intuitively, O at each stage is the same object — the same ship, the same person, the same tree. But the matter has been entirely replaced. O ≠ M₀ and O ≠ Mₙ, since M₀ and Mₙ are entirely distinct portions of matter, yet O is supposedly the same object at both times. Using Con(M, O, t) to mean "M constitutes O at t":

- Con(M₀, O, t₀)
- Con(Mₙ, O, tₙ)
- M₀ ≠ Mₙ     (entirely distinct matter)
- O at t₀ = O at tₙ     (same object throughout)

The relation between O and its matter at each time is not identity — but it must be something. Constitution theorists say it is the constitution relation, temporal version: what constitutes an object can change over time without destroying the object's identity.

## The Ship of Theseus

The Ship of Theseus sharpens the puzzle with two competing outcomes. The ship has its planks replaced one by one over years. At the end of this process, we have S₁ — the functionally continuous ship, still in the harbor, with all new planks. Intuitively, S₁ seems to be the original Ship of Theseus.

But suppose someone has been collecting the old planks and, after all have been replaced, reassembles them into a ship S₂ in the original configuration. Now there are two ships:

- S₁: functionally continuous, spatiotemporally continuous, with all new planks
- S₂: made of the original planks, recently assembled

Which is the original? The formal structure makes the tension precise: either Con(Mₙ, Theseus, t₁) (new matter constitutes the ship — S₁ is the original) or Con(M₀, Theseus, t₁) (original matter constitutes the ship — S₂ is the original). But M₀ ≠ Mₙ and S₁ ≠ S₂, so we cannot have "Theseus" picking out a single ship on both readings.

Baker and other constitution theorists handle temporal cases by arguing that what constitutes an object can change over time without destroying its identity, so long as changes are gradual and respect the continuity conditions appropriate to the object's primary kind. An artifact remains the same artifact through gradual replacement of material if the replacement preserves the functional role and the object's history within human practices. S₂ is not the original ship: once the planks were disassembled and the ship ceased to exist as a functional unity, no subsequent reassembly can recreate the original. S₂ is a new ship made of old planks.

David Wiggins's treatment in *Sameness and Substance* (1980) connects this to a more general thesis: every object belongs to a sortal with its own identity conditions, and identity through time is always identity *qua* a particular sortal. Persons are re-identified by psychological and bodily continuity; organisms by biological continuity; artifacts by functional and historical continuity; masses of matter by material continuity. The Ship of Theseus puzzle arises because we apply the person-criterion (continuity of functional role) and the mass-of-matter criterion (continuity of composition) simultaneously, and they give different verdicts. Wiggins's response is that artifact identity is determined by the artifact-criterion — functional-historical continuity — so S₁ is the original.

## The Perdurantist Alternative

Perdurantists handle temporal constitution by dissolving it into mereology. On this view, the ship at t₀ is a temporal part of the ship-worm; the ship at tₙ is a different temporal part. The ship-worm is the mereological sum of all its temporal parts. The matter that constitutes the ship at t is not a temporal part of the ship but rather a matter-worm that occupies the same spacetime region as the ship-temporal-part at t. There is no constitution relation in the proprietary sense — just different four-dimensional objects (the ship-worm, the matter-worm₀, the matter-worm₁, …) that overlap in certain spacetime regions.

The Ship of Theseus puzzle dissolves: S₁ and S₂ are simply two different four-dimensional worms. The question "which is the original?" becomes "which four-dimensional worm is the one we are calling 'the Ship of Theseus'?" — a question about the referent of a name, not about metaphysical identity.

The endurantist critique is that this changes the subject. The intuition behind the puzzle is that *the same ship* persists through time, not that a series of distinct temporal parts are suitably related. Endurantists insist that the identity relation — the *very same* ship — is what matters, and the perdurantist reconstruction in terms of temporal-part overlap loses this.

## Temporal Constitution and Personal Identity

The puzzle has special urgency for persons. The gradual replacement of neurons and bodily matter over a lifetime raises the question: what makes me the same person as the person born three decades ago? Baker's answer: I am an organism with a first-person perspective, and my identity is determined by the continuity appropriate to the kind *person*. My constituting matter changes while I — the person — persist, because persons are not individuated by their material constitution but by biological-psychological continuity and the first-person perspective. The relation between me and my matter at each moment is constitutional, not identical.

Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) destabilizes this response by arguing that personal identity facts may not be what matters in survival. What matters is psychological continuity, not strict identity. If Parfit is right, the question "is this person the same person?" may be less metaphysically weighty than we thought, and the stakes of temporal constitution puzzles for persons may be lower than they appear. Whether that is a vindication of constitution theory or a dissolution of the problem it is meant to solve remains contested.
