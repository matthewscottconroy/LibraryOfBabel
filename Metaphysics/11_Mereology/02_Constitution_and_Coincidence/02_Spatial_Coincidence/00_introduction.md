# Spatial Coincidence

Can two distinct objects occupy exactly the same region of space at the same time? This question is among the most counterintuitive in metaphysics, yet the best arguments seem to force an affirmative answer. The lump of clay and the statue occupy the same region; the person and her body occupy the same region; the river and the quantity of water that constitutes it at a given moment occupy the same region. If all these apparently distinct objects are genuine existents, then spatial coincidence is not just possible but commonplace.

Impenetrability — the principle that no two distinct material objects can occupy the same spatial region — is deeply intuitive. We think of impenetrability as a basic physical fact about matter. But impenetrability may be a physical principle about physical objects of the same kind: two lumps of clay cannot occupy the same space; two statues cannot; but a lump of clay and a statue can, if they are distinct kinds of thing and the lump constitutes the statue. The principle of impenetrability would then be restricted to objects of the same material kind.

This raises the question of what determines material kind membership, and what the relevant principle of impenetrability applies to. If the principle applies to all material objects simpliciter, then coincident objects are impossible. If it applies only to objects of the same kind, then coincidence between objects of different kinds is possible — but then the principle is no longer a simple, basic physical fact; it requires appeal to kinds and their natures.

The ontological cost of spatial coincidence is the multiplication of entities: if the lump and the statue are both real and coincident, then any region of space may be occupied by many distinct objects (the lump, the statue, the atoms, the molecules, the cosmic worm, and so on). This "many-objects" ontology is uncomfortable but perhaps unavoidable given the considerations that motivate coincidence.

This section examines spatial coincidence — the cases that seem to require it, the principle of impenetrability and its limits, and the theoretical costs of accepting or rejecting coincident objects.
