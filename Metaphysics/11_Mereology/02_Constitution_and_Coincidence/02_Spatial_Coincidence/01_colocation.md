# Colocation

We normally assume that two distinct objects cannot occupy exactly the same region of space at the same time. This is part of what distinguishes material objects from properties: properties can be co-instantiated in a region; objects cannot co-occupy it. The statue occupies the region R; the lump occupies R. If the statue and lump are distinct — as constitution theory holds — then we have two numerically distinct objects in the same place at the same time. Colocation.

**Formally:** Let Loc(x, r, t) mean "object x occupies region r at time t." Colocation asserts:

∃x ∃y ∃r ∃t [Loc(x, r, t) ∧ Loc(y, r, t) ∧ x ≠ y]

Two numerically distinct objects occupying the same region at the same time. The constitution theorist is committed to this in the case of the statue and the lump: Loc(s, R, t) and Loc(c, R, t) and s ≠ c for all t during which the statue exists.

We can put the challenge more sharply: *strong colocation* holds that the statue and lump not only occupy the same region but share all their spatial parts — every spatial sub-region contains the same parts of each. This follows if we accept that they are made of the same atoms, each of which has a determinate location.

## Colocation and Leibniz's Law

If s and c occupy the same region r and share all their spatial parts, how can they differ in any property? Leibniz's Law says: if x = y, then x and y share all properties. Contraposing: if x and y differ in some property, then x ≠ y.

Baker accepts that s ≠ c. The challenge is to explain how they can differ in any property if they are colocated and share all physical structure. Her answer: objects have properties partly in virtue of their *kind*, and kinds carry distinct identity and modal conditions. The statue has the property *would be destroyed by melting* because statues, qua artworks with particular aesthetic form, cannot survive being melted and recast. The lump has the property *would not be destroyed by melting* because lumps, qua material stuff, can survive changes of shape. These are genuine property differences — the Leibniz's Law argument that establishes s ≠ c invokes them.

But this seems to say that two objects with the same intrinsic physical properties — same spatial location, same atomic composition, same shape, same mass, same color — can differ in their modal properties. Many philosophers find this hard to accept because modal properties are often thought to *supervene* on categorical, non-modal properties. If the statue and lump have all the same categorical properties, how can they differ modally?

Baker's response is that kinds are not merely nominal or conventional: they are metaphysically real, and membership in a kind is a genuine categorical property. "Being an artwork" is a genuine categorical property of the statue, instantiated in virtue of historical, intentional, and social facts, and this categorical difference grounds the modal difference. The modal difference is not brute but explained by the categorical difference in kind-membership.

## The Exclusion Principle and Its Revision

We operate with what might be called the *Exclusion Principle*: two distinct objects cannot occupy exactly the same region of space at the same time. Constitution theory must revise this. The natural grounds for Exclusion are:

1. *Material impenetrability*: two material objects cannot be in the same place — this is sometimes treated as a near-conceptual truth about materiality.
2. *Physical common sense*: when we put one brick on another, the top brick is on top of, not inside, the bottom brick.
3. *Causal individuation*: if two objects were exactly colocated, they would have all the same causal effects, and we cannot causally distinguish them.

Baker's responses are targeted. Impenetrability is a physical law about material objects of the *same kind* forcing each other apart. The statue and lump are not two lumps — they are a lump and an artwork, and impenetrability governs objects of the same kind, not objects of different ontological categories. Physical common sense about bricks does not straightforwardly generalize to pairs of objects from different categories. And causal individuation is complicated because the statue and lump do agree in all their non-modal categorical properties — their difference is in modal properties, not current causal powers.

## The Counting Problem

A particularly vivid challenge is the problem of counting. If I point to the region R occupied by the statue and lump and ask "how many objects are here?", what is the correct answer?

If one: the constitution theorist is wrong that there are two numerically distinct objects. If two: the region R contains two objects each with mass 5 kg, and the region's total mass is... 5 kg? 10 kg? This seems incoherent.

Baker's response: the correct count depends on the *count question*. How many statues? One. How many lumps? One. How many physical objects? If this means "how many objects of any kind?", the answer is two (one of each kind). If it means "how many physical space-occupiers?", there is only one space-occupier, though it instantiates two kinds. On mass: two colocated objects of the same mass do not have 10 kg in the region because the statue borrows its mass from the lump. There are not two independent mass-distributions but one, realized by the lump and borrowed by the statue. Mass is not additive across constituted and constituting objects.

## Colocation and the Theory of Space

Colocation also raises challenges for the theory of space itself. If two distinct objects can occupy the same spatial region, then spatial location is not an *individuating* relation — it does not tell us how many things are in a region. One alternative is to think of space not as a container with fixed "slots" but as a field of properties, where "occupying a region" is a matter of instantiating certain spatial properties. On this view, two objects can share spatial properties without "being in the same slot" — the spatial properties are multiply instantiated, just as a color can be multiply instantiated at a region by different objects.

Colocation thus connects to broader questions about the metaphysics of space — whether space is a container (substantivalism), a system of relations between objects (relationalism), or a field of properties — and the debate about colocation partly turns on which background theory of space one accepts.
