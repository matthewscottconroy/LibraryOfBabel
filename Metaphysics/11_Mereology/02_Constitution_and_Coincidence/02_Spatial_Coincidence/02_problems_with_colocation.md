# Problems with Colocation

Accepting that two distinct objects can occupy the same place at the same time generates a series of difficulties that critics have pressed against constitution theory. These problems range from abstract metaphysical puzzles about properties to concrete difficulties about counting, mass, and the unity of mind.

## The Problem of Material Coincidence

When you touch the statue, you also touch the lump. When you paint it gold, both objects are painted. The two share all their current qualitative properties — same color, same temperature, same shape, same spatial location, same atomic composition. How do they differ?

Constitution theorists say they differ in their modal and historical properties. But this generates a further problem: modal and historical properties are often thought to be *grounded in* categorical, non-modal properties. If the statue and lump have all the same categorical properties, they should have all the same modal properties (by supervenience). Two consequences are possible:

**(A)** Modal properties do not supervene on categorical properties — a significant revision of widely-held views about the relationship between modality and actuality.

**(B)** The statue and lump differ in some categorical property after all — presumably kind-membership — and kind-membership is a categorical property not determined by physical structure alone but by historical, intentional, and social facts.

Baker accepts (B): being an *artwork* is a genuine categorical property, instantiated in virtue of historical-intentional facts, not physical structure alone. Critics find this puzzling: how can historical-intentional facts about the artist's activity ground a current categorical property of an object that seems to be purely physical? The answer cannot be entirely in the physics, so something more is needed — and constitution theory is committed to explaining what that something is.

## The Too Many Objects Problem

If the statue and lump are distinct, then in region R there is something that weighs 5 kg (the statue) and something that weighs 5 kg (the lump). Do these two objects together weigh 10 kg? Obviously not — the region weighs 5 kg total. But then two objects share a single mass, which is puzzling. For any extensive physical quantity Q — mass, charge, volume — if two colocated objects both have value q, they should together have value 2q. But they don't. Colocation thus seems to violate the additivity of physical quantities.

Baker's response: extensive quantities are not additive across constituted and constituting objects because the statue *borrows* its mass from the lump. There are not two independent mass-distributions in the region; there is one, which the lump has intrinsically and the statue has derivatively (via borrowing). Mass additivity holds within levels (two lumps weigh twice as much as one), but not across constitutional levels.

The objection to borrowing is that it is a metaphor in need of cashing out. What is the underlying metaphysical mechanism? If the lump's mass explains the statue's mass through the constitution relation, then the statue's having-mass is metaphysically posterior to the lump's having-mass. But then the statue doesn't independently have the property mass — it has it derivatively. This seems to suggest that the statue is not a full-blooded object with intrinsic physical properties but a "shadow" of the lump, a purely relational entity. Constitution theorists resist this reading, but the pressure is real.

## The Too Many Thinkers Problem

Applied to persons, colocation generates a particularly troubling puzzle. If a person P and a human organism O are distinct but colocated, and both have brains, then there are two thinkers in my skull. Both process the same information; both have the same beliefs, desires, and experiences; both undergo the same mental events. Two complete thinkers, both thinking "I am now thinking about mereology."

Baker's response: the person has a first-person perspective *essentially* — what makes something a person is having a first-person perspective. The organism does not have a first-person perspective essentially; it has it *derivatively*, by virtue of constituting a person. So there are not two independent thinkers; there is one thinker (the person) and one organism that thinks-derivatively by virtue of constituting the thinker.

The objection is pointed: if the organism thinks only derivatively, in what sense does it think at all? Genuine thought seems to require a subject who has mental states intrinsically. If the organism only has thoughts derivatively, the thought is not really "the organism's thought" — and yet the organism has a perfectly good brain that is producing those thoughts in the ordinary physical sense. The asymmetry between the person's intrinsic thinking and the organism's derivative thinking is difficult to maintain consistently.

## The Property Inheritance Problem

Consider the statue's color. The lump is bronze-colored in virtue of having bronze atoms with certain electron configurations. The statue is bronze-colored. The statue's being bronze-colored is grounded in the lump's being bronze-colored via the constitution relation.

But what is the statue's being bronze-colored? It cannot be an independent instantiation of the property *bronze-colored* — that would give the region two independent instantiations of the color property, returning us to the too-many-objects problem. It must be a *derivative* or *borrowed* instantiation. But then the statue's color is a relational property: it is bronze-colored in virtue of standing in the constitution relation to something that is intrinsically bronze-colored.

If the statue's physical properties are all relational in this way — all borrowed — then the statue is not a full-blooded object with intrinsic physical properties but a purely relational entity that "borrows" all its physical character from its constituter. This is a slide toward *eliminativism* about the statue: since it has no physical properties of its own, perhaps it is not an additional object over and above the lump but merely the lump under a different description. This is precisely what the monist about constitution (Lewis, four-dimensionalists) maintains. Baker must resist this slide, but doing so requires an account of property-borrowing that preserves the statue's genuine object-hood without making it an independent mass-bearing entity.

## The Fission Problem

Colocation invites a further structural problem: what would happen if a single matter-object constituted two distinct kind-objects simultaneously? Suppose a single lump of bronze is simultaneously shaped into an abstract sculpture and serves as a doorstop. The lump now constitutes both a sculpture and a doorstop. Three objects are in the same region: the lump, the sculpture, and the doorstop. The too-many-objects problem is multiplied.

Baker's response is that not every functional role generates a distinct kind-object. Being a doorstop is not a robust enough kind; being an artwork is. The criterion she offers is roughly: a kind generates a distinct constituted object if membership in it is essential to the object, makes the object subject to distinct identity and persistence conditions, and grounds a genuine first-person perspective (for persons) or a genuine social/institutional role (for certain artifacts). Whether this criterion is principled enough to block proliferation while admitting the cases constitution theorists want remains contested.

## Alternative Responses

The accumulation of problems with colocation has led some philosophers to prefer different solutions. Four-dimensionalism (Sider, Lewis) dissolves coincidence by multiplying temporal parts: the statue and lump are distinct four-dimensional worms that share all their spatial parts at each moment of co-existence but differ in their temporal extent. Sortal monism (Wiggins) holds that there is only one object here, falling under two sortals simultaneously, with identity being sortal-relative rather than constituting genuine numerical distinctness. Eliminativism denies that there are two objects to be colocated — either the lump is real and the statue is just the lump under an artwork-description, or vice versa. Each alternative has its own costs, and the debate continues to be one of the most active areas of contemporary metaphysics.
