# The Four-Dimensionalist Solution to Coincidence

If the statue and the lump seem to be "two things in one place," four-dimensionalism offers a diagnosis: we are confusing a three-dimensional cross-section with the full four-dimensional reality. On the four-dimensional picture, objects are extended through time as well as space — they have temporal parts just as they have spatial parts. The statue and the lump are both four-dimensional entities, "worms" stretching through spacetime. Their apparent coincidence dissolves when we recognize that they are distinct four-dimensional worms that overlap in three dimensions but not in four.

## The Core Proposal

Let a *temporal part* of object O at time t be the maximal part of O that exists at t — the "time-slice" of O's four-dimensional worm:

**Temporal part:** TP(s, O, t) =df P(s, O) ∧ ∃!r [Loc(s, r, t)] ∧ ∀t' [∃r' Loc(s, r', t') → t' = t]

(s is a temporal part of O at t iff s is a part of O, s exists only at t, and every spatial part of O at t is a part of s.)

The four-dimensionalist analyzes coincidence as follows. The statue s and the lump c are distinct four-dimensional wholes. They *overlap* in three-dimensional space at each moment of the statue's existence — sharing all their instantaneous temporal parts during that period. But they differ as four-dimensional totalities:

- The lump c has temporal parts extending from t₀ (pre-casting) to tₙ (destruction)
- The statue s has temporal parts extending only from t₁ (casting) to t₂ (destruction of statue)
- t₀ < t₁, so the lump has temporal parts — TP(c_{[t₀, t₁)}, c, _) — that the statue lacks

Therefore, by mereological extensionality:

∃z [PP(z, c) ∧ ¬PP(z, s)]

and hence c ≠ s. The statue and lump are mereologically distinct — they differ in their temporal parts — even though they share all spatial parts during the period of co-existence.

## The Resolution of Leibniz's Law Arguments

The four-dimensionalist handles the modal and historical differences without positing a special constitution relation. The historical difference is simply a matter of temporal extent: the lump's four-dimensional worm extends before t₁ but the statue's does not. The modal difference is analyzed as a claim about the statue's modal counterparts across possible worlds: the statue s would be destroyed by melting because no possible world contains a worm that is the continuation of s across a melting-event and still counts as a statue (by the sortal conditions for *statue*). The lump c would survive melting because there are possible worlds containing a worm that continues c through a melting-event and still counts as a lump. These modal claims are grounded in the sortal conditions combined with the four-dimensional worm theory of persistence — no primitive constitution relation required.

Define: s₁ and s₂ are *temporarily coincident* iff ∃t ∀r [Loc(s₁, r, t) ↔ Loc(s₂, r, t)]. The four-dimensionalist's argument: temporary coincidence is compatible with mereological distinctness (differing in temporal parts), so temporary coincidence does not imply identity. The appearance of "two things in one place" is a perspective artifact of looking at a temporal cross-section.

Contrast this with the endurantist situation. An endurantist says that objects are wholly present at each moment — there are no temporal parts. At each moment t, the statue and lump both exist, share all their material, and are wholly present. There is nothing further to differentiate them. The endurantist must either accept genuine coincidence with all its difficulties (constitution theory), deny that there are two objects (monism), or deploy some other theoretical resource (sortal-relative identity, essentialism) to block the identity inference.

## Key Texts and Proponents

David Lewis (*On the Plurality of Worlds*, 1986; *Parts of Classes*, 1991) defends the worm theory and argues that temporal parts are as natural as spatial parts — if you believe a road has spatial parts (the northern section, the southern section), you should believe an object has temporal parts (the early stage, the late stage).

Theodore Sider's *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001) is the most systematic contemporary defense. Sider adds the vagueness argument for four-dimensionalism and develops the formal mereology of temporal parts in detail, arguing that four-dimensionalism is not only compatible with but required by taking mereological extensionality seriously as a metaphysical principle.

Critics include Judith Jarvis Thomson ("Parthood and Identity Across Time," 1983) and Mark Johnston ("Is There a Problem About Persistence?," 1987). Thomson argues that positing temporal parts multiplies entities unnecessarily and that the puzzles they are meant to solve can be handled by distinguishing uses of identity claims. Johnston argues that the four-dimensionalist "solution" does not address the genuine puzzle of coincidence but merely relocates it.

## The Costs

The four-dimensionalist solution is technically the most successful in preserving the formal virtues of classical mereology, especially EXT. Its costs are paid in ontological currency rather than logical currency.

Our ordinary concept of a material object does not include temporal parts as components. When I examine my hand, I do not encounter a temporal-part-of-a-hand; I encounter my hand, wholly present. Temporal parts are a philosopher's theoretical device, not a datum of experience.

Moreover, the solution may be explanatorily unsatisfying. Sider's four-dimensionalism tells us that the apparent coincidence dissolves because the statue and lump have different temporal extents. But why are there two distinct objects here rather than one? The four-dimensionalist answer — "because their four-dimensional worms differ in extent" — seems to push the question back. We can still ask: what determines that there is a statue-worm in addition to the lump-worm?

Four-dimensionalism combined with universalism also generates an enormous number of four-dimensional worms — one for every possible temporal "cutout" from the four-dimensional structure of the world. This ontological liberality bothers many.

The table below summarizes how each major position handles the central questions:

| Solution | Status of EXT | Temporal Parts | Constitution Relation | Verdict on s = c |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classical mereology | Preserved | Optional | Not needed | s = c (if same parts) |
| Four-dimensionalism | Preserved | Required | Not needed | s ≠ c (differ in temporal parts) |
| Constitution theory | Violated | Not needed | Required | s ≠ c (by constitution) |
| Sortal monism | Modified | Not needed | Not needed | Sortal-relative |
| Nihilism | Vacuously true | Not needed | Not needed | Neither s nor c exists |

The four-dimensionalist solution is formally elegant. Whether that elegance is worth the ontological commitment to temporal parts — and whether it really answers the question rather than transforming it into a different question — is what the debate is about.
