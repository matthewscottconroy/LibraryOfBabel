# Exercises: Spatial Coincidence

1. **The grounding problem.** If the statue and the lump are made of exactly the same matter and have the same shape, what grounds the fact that the statue has the property *would be destroyed by melting* while the lump does not? Assess at least two proposed solutions.

2. **Four-dimensionalist dissolution.** Reconstruct the four-dimensionalist response to the coincidence puzzle. Does it genuinely dissolve the puzzle, or does it relocate it? (Consider: are temporal parts themselves entities that can coincide?)

3. **Evans's argument.** Gareth Evans argued that vague objects are impossible (because if a and b are vaguely identical, b has the property "is vaguely identical to a" while a does not, contradicting Leibniz's Law). Does this argument extend to show that coincident objects are impossible? Why or why not?

4. **The problem of too many.** Peter Unger's "problem of the many" holds that a cloud is composed of many almost-identical collections of water droplets, each of which seems to compose a cloud. How is this related to (but different from) the statue-lump problem? Does your preferred solution to one generalize to the other?

5. **Hylomorphism.** Aristotle held that a statue is a form-matter composite, where the form (shape + function) and the matter (bronze) are principles rather than objects. Can a neo-Aristotelian hylomorphic account of the statue avoid the coincidence puzzle? What would it say about the lump?

6. **Common sense.** Which option—many coincident objects, no coincident objects (eliminativism), or four-dimensional non-coincidence—fits best with common sense? Is fitting common sense a relevant theoretical virtue here?
