# Important Terms and Ideas: Spatial Coincidence

**Spatial Coincidence**
Two distinct objects occupy exactly the same spatial region at the same time. The statue and the clay are a canonical example: if they are distinct objects (as constitution theorists hold), they coincide spatially. This challenges the intuition that two things cannot be in exactly the same place at the same time.

**The No-Two-Things-in-One-Place Principle**
The intuitive principle that two numerically distinct material objects cannot occupy exactly the same spatial region at the same time. Spatial coincidence violates this principle; those who accept coincidence must reject or restrict it.

**Relative Identity**
Wiggins's view: identity is always relative to a sortal kind. The statue and the clay are the same lump but not the same statue. There is no absolute, sortal-neutral identity—two things can be the "same F" without being the "same G." This view allows "coincidence" without genuine numerical distinctness under all sorts.

**Contingent Identity**
Gibbard's proposal: the statue and the clay are identical in the actual world (they are the same thing) but could have been distinct (in some possible world, the clay exists without the statue). This preserves the intuition of numerical identity in the actual case while allowing modal divergence.

**Four-Dimensionalist Solution**
The temporal parts strategy: distinct four-dimensional objects can share a three-dimensional temporal stage. The statue and the clay are distinct four-dimensional worms that happen to share a spatial stage at a time, but they differ in their other temporal stages—coincidence is harmless.

**Coincidentalism**
The acceptance of genuine spatial coincidence: numerically distinct objects can occupy the same spatial region at the same time. Coincidentalists argue that the no-two-things-in-one-place principle is false and that sortal properties explain how two things can be co-located.
