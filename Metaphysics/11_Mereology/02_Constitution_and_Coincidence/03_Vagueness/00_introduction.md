# Vagueness in Mereology

Mereology and vagueness intersect in troubling ways. The formal theory of mereology — with its precise axioms about parts, wholes, and composition — presupposes that questions of parthood and composition have determinate answers. Is this molecule a part of this organism? Is this cell a part of this brain? Do these scattered objects compose anything? Classical mereology assumes that each of these questions has a definite yes or no answer. But our ordinary concepts of part and whole are vague: there are borderline cases where it is genuinely unclear whether something is a part of something else.

The Sorites paradox applies directly to composition. If we start with a complete organism and begin removing cells one by one, at which point does the organism cease to exist? No single cell removal makes the difference between existence and non-existence; but many removals together clearly do. This is a Sorites series for existence of a composite object, and it has the same structure as other Sorites paradoxes: no single step makes the difference, yet the aggregate of steps does.

Lewis's response was to accept that there are indefinitely many objects corresponding to the many ways of drawing the boundary around what is "clearly part" of the organism. This "many-objects" response preserves classical mereology by multiplying objects rather than admitting vagueness. The counterintuitive conclusion — that ordinary objects like organisms are not single objects but huge families of slightly different objects, each with a different determinate boundary — is a significant theoretical cost.

The alternative — accepting vagueness in composition — challenges the classical framework. A mereology that admits vague parthood must give up classical bivalence and the sharp boundaries that formal mereology presupposes. Fuzzy mereology — mereology with degrees of parthood — has been developed, but it faces difficulties in developing a coherent formal theory.

This section examines vagueness in mereology, the Sorites paradox applied to composition, and the competing strategies for handling the indeterminacy.
