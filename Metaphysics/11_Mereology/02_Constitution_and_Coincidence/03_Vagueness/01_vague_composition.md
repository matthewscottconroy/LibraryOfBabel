# Vague Composition

When does a cloud of water vapor become a cloud? When does a collection of cells become a person? These are not merely questions about our concepts — or so the advocate of vague composition maintains. They are questions about what exists. Vague composition is the thesis that there are cases where it is neither determinately true nor determinately false that some objects compose a further object. If genuine, this would mean that the existence of composite objects can be vague: not merely that we are uncertain whether something exists, but that there is no fact of the matter.

## The Problem Stated

Restricted composition says: ∃y [the xs compose y] ↔ C(xs), where C is some composition-enabling condition. Now suppose C is vague — there are cases where it is indeterminate whether C(xs). Call such a case a *borderline composition case*: a collection of objects xs such that there is no fact of the matter about whether C(xs).

The vague composition thesis asserts that for such borderline cases, there is no fact of the matter about whether ∃y [the xs compose y]. In classical logic, ∃y [the xs compose y] is either true or false. Vague composition says that in borderline cases, neither: the sentence has no determinate truth-value.

Three positions are available:

1. **Semantic vagueness:** The vagueness is in our predicates or concepts, not in the world. There is a fact of the matter about whether the xs compose something, but our concept of composition (or "cloud," or "person") is imprecise in a way that prevents us from determining it.

2. **Epistemic vagueness:** There is a sharp fact of the matter, but we cannot know it. The xs either determinately compose something or determinately don't; we just can't tell which.

3. **Ontological vagueness:** There is no sharp fact of the matter. The vagueness is in the world, not merely in our representations.

## Supervaluationism Applied to Composition

Supervaluationism (Fine 1975, Keefe 2000) analyzes vagueness as semantic: a sentence is *super-true* iff it is true on all admissible sharpenings of the relevant vague predicates, *super-false* iff false on all, and *indeterminate* otherwise.

Applied to composition: "The xs compose a y" is indeterminate in borderline cases because there are multiple admissible ways to sharpen the composition-enabling condition C, and on some sharpenings the xs compose something while on others they don't. Let C be vague and 𝒮 be the set of admissible sharpenings {C₁, C₂, …}. For each sharpening Cᵢ:

- If Cᵢ(xs): on this sharpening, ∃y [the xs compose y] is true
- If ¬Cᵢ(xs): on this sharpening, ∃y [the xs compose y] is false

In borderline cases: ∃i Cᵢ(xs) and ∃j ¬Cⱼ(xs). The sentence is indeterminate. There is still a fact of the matter about the underlying physical situation; what is vague is only the predicate C that we use to describe when composition occurs.

The advantage is that classical logic is preserved within each sharpening. The disadvantage is that the meta-semantic story about admissible sharpenings requires an account of what makes a sharpening "admissible" — and if this is itself vague, the supervaluationist analysis is threatened by a regress.

## Sider's Argument Against Vague Composition

Sider's most influential argument against restricted composition turns on the unacceptability of vague composition:

- P1. If restricted composition is true, there are borderline cases of composition — cases where ∃y [the xs compose y] has no determinate truth-value.
- P2. If ∃y [the xs compose y] has no determinate truth-value, there is ontological vagueness — vagueness in what exists.
- P3. There is no ontological vagueness (vagueness is always representational, never ontic).
- C1. There are no borderline cases of composition.
- C2. Composition is either always occurring (universalism) or never occurring (nihilism).

Premise P3 is defended by the claim that ontological vagueness is incoherent: if it is indeterminate whether y exists, there is no fact of the matter about existence — but this requires the world to have "gaps" in it, and gaps in the world make no sense for existence. Either y is there or it isn't; there are no "maybe-entities."

Gareth Evans offered a related argument in 1978 that vague identity is incoherent. Suppose it is indeterminate whether a = b. Then b has the property *being such that it is indeterminate whether it is identical to a*. But a does not have this property (it is determinately identical to itself). So by Leibniz's Law, a ≠ b. But we assumed it was indeterminate whether a = b — contradiction. Applied to composition: if it were indeterminate whether y exists (as a composition of the xs), then there would be a vague identity claim generating Evans-style problems.

Three objections to Sider's argument are available. The supervaluationist denies P2: vague composition does not entail ontological vagueness — indeterminacy is a semantic fact about our representations, not a metaphysical fact about the world. Some philosophers directly deny P3 and accept ontological vagueness, disputing Evans's argument on the grounds that the property *being such that it is indeterminate whether it is identical to a* may not be a genuine property Leibniz's Law can operate on. And epistemicists (Williamson, Sorensen) deny P1: there are no genuine borderline cases, only cases where the sharp truth is unknowable.

## Ontological Vagueness

The most controversial position is that vagueness in composition is *ontological*. This view has been developed most carefully by Elizabeth Barnes and Ross Williams, who argue that ontic vagueness can be understood model-theoretically: there are multiple ways the world could determinately be (multiple "precisifications"), and in some of these the xs compose something and in others they don't. Unlike the supervaluationist, the Barnes-Williams view holds that this is a fact about the world itself, not about our representations of it.

This is a contentious position but one that takes seriously the intuition that composition puzzles — when does an embryo become a person? — are not just problems of conceptual precision but genuine metaphysical puzzles about what exists.

## The Interaction with the SCQ

The topology of the debate is tight. If composition is always (universalism) or never (nihilism) occurring, there are no borderline cases. Only restricted composition generates genuine ontological borderlines — which is one of Sider's arguments for moving to an extreme view:

- Accept restricted composition → face vague composition
- Accept vague composition → face the vague-existence problem
- Reject vague existence → move to universalism or nihilism

The middle ground is genuinely difficult to occupy without accepting either epistemic vagueness (sharp facts, no access) or semantic vagueness (sharp world, vague concepts). The philosophical pressure toward extreme positions in the SCQ debate is partly explained by the power of Sider's argument. If he is right that there is no vagueness in existence, then every restricted composition view is in trouble — and the choice becomes how to pay the price of universalism or nihilism.
