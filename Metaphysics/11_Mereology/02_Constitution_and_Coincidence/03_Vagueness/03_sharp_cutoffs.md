# Sharp Cutoffs

Every restricted composition view — every view that says composition occurs under some conditions but not others — faces a structural problem: whenever the relevant conditions vary continuously, there must be a transition from composing to not-composing. Either that transition is gradual (vague) or it is sharp. Both options seem problematic. This is the problem of sharp cutoffs.

## The Structural Dilemma

Let C be a composition-enabling condition. Suppose C varies continuously along some dimension — the degree of molecular bonding, the extent of metabolic activity, the distance between particles. Then there are sequences of cases:

Case₁, Case₂, Case₃, …, Caseₙ

such that adjacent cases are nearly indistinguishable but the first clearly fails C and the last clearly satisfies C. This is a *sorites series* for C. Restricted composition entails: for some k, Caseₖ is a case where composition occurs, and Caseₖ₋₁ is not. This is the sharp cutoff: between two nearly identical cases, a fundamental ontological difference — an additional composite object comes into existence.

The dilemma:
- If no sharp cutoff: the transition is gradual, hence vague. But vague composition seems incoherent or at least deeply problematic (as Sider argues).
- If sharp cutoff: it is arbitrary. Why at this precise point rather than the adjacent one? The physical difference between adjacent cases is infinitesimal; the ontological difference — existence vs. non-existence of a composite — is maximal.

## The Sorites Argument Applied to Composition

The sorites argument exploits continuous variation to refute the application of a predicate. Applied to composition:

- P1. 1 atom cannot compose a further object.
- P2. ∀n: if n atoms cannot compose a further object, then n+1 atoms cannot.
- C. No collection of atoms can compose a further object.

Restricted compositionists must reject P2. But rejecting P2 requires saying that for some n, n atoms cannot compose while n+1 can — which is the sharp cutoff. This seems to require placing enormous metaphysical weight on the addition of a single atom.

## Sider's Exploitation of the Dilemma

Sider's argument in *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001) uses this structure against all restricted composition views:

- P1. If composition is restricted, then as we vary the features of the xs along a continuous dimension, there is a sharp transition from composing to not-composing (given no ontic vagueness).
- P2. Any such sharp transition would be metaphysically arbitrary — it requires a fact about the world that cannot be grounded in the continuous physical variation.
- P3. Metaphysically arbitrary facts (facts that cannot be grounded in any physical difference) do not exist.
- C. Composition is not restricted.

Premise P3 is the key and most controversial claim. Sider grounds it in a principle of metaphysical proportionality: the metaphysical facts should be proportional to (grounded in) physical facts. If the physical difference between adjacent cases is infinitesimal, the metaphysical difference should also be minimal, not maximal.

## Epistemicism About Sharp Cutoffs

Timothy Williamson's *Vagueness* (1994) and Roy Sorensen's *Blindspots* (1988) argue for thoroughgoing epistemicism: wherever there is apparent vagueness, there is actually a sharp fact that we cannot determine. Applied to composition, this means:

- There is a precise number n of atoms such that n atoms in a heap arrangement compose a heap and n−1 do not.
- There is a precise moment t such that at t the embryo composes a person and at t−ε it does not.
- There is a precise distance d such that atoms within distance d compose an object and atoms at distance d + ε do not.

These are sharp facts determined by the nature of the world and the norms governing our concepts. Our inability to locate the sharp boundary is not because there is none but because our epistemic access to the facts is limited by the nature of vague language. We cannot consistently know where the sharp boundary falls because knowing it would require a kind of semantic self-knowledge that is constitutively unavailable.

The appeal: epistemicism preserves classical logic, avoids ontic vagueness, and allows restricted composition to be coherent. The price is accepting sharp cutoffs that seem metaphysically arbitrary. Even if epistemicism is plausible for predicates like "bald" or "tall" — where there genuinely seems to be a fact about whether someone is bald, just not one we can determine — it is harder to accept for composition. Is there really a precise number of molecules that makes the difference between a cloud existing and not existing? This seems to require the world to have an implausibly precise macro-scale structure that nothing in physics suggests is there.

## The Moderate Response: Composition and Convention

A third response denies that composition facts are subject to sorites arguments in the same way that ordinary predicates are. Whether n atoms compose a table is not a purely factual question but one partly determined by human conventions and practices. There is no sharp cutoff because composition for artifacts is not a metaphysical fact but a matter of how we categorize.

The xs compose a table iff we — speaking as competent users of "table" — would count the xs as a table under ideal epistemic conditions. Since "table" is a vague predicate in a broadly pragmatic language, there are borderline cases. The vagueness is not ontic but a feature of our classification practices.

The objection is that this makes the existence of tables conventional and hence mind-dependent. If tables exist only because we classify things as tables, then tables are not part of the mind-independent furniture of the world — which is a significant revision of ordinary ontological commitments.

## Consequences for the Mereology Debate

The sharp-cutoffs problem has two main consequences. First, it reinforces the case for extreme positions. If every restricted-composition view faces arbitrary sharp cutoffs (or vague composition), and both seem unacceptable, the only alternatives are the views that make composition exceptionless. Universalism avoids sharp cutoffs by having composition occur everywhere; nihilism avoids them by having it occur nowhere.

Second, it motivates the search for a "natural" composition-enabling condition with a principled non-arbitrary threshold. Van Inwagen's life-condition is one attempt: life is a natural phenomenon with a threshold that, even if somewhat vague, is not arbitrary in the same way that spatial proximity is — the biological sciences give us resources for determining where organisms begin and end. Whether biological naturalness is sufficient to overcome the sharp-cutoffs objection is a further question connecting mereology to the philosophy of biology.

The sharp-cutoffs problem is therefore not merely a formal puzzle but a substantive challenge whose resolution depends on deep questions about the relationship between continuous physical variation and discrete ontological structure. That relationship is not well understood, and progress on it would illuminate much of what is most contested in the metaphysics of composition.
