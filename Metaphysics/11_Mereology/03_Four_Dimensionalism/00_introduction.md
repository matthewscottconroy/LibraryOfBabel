# Four-Dimensionalism

Four-dimensionalism extends the mereological framework from space to time: just as ordinary objects have spatial parts (a left half and a right half, a top and a bottom), they also have temporal parts (a yesterday-stage and a today-stage, a childhood part and an adult part). Objects are four-dimensional wholes, extended through time just as they are extended through space, and what exists at any given moment is a temporal slice of the whole object rather than the whole object itself.

The motivation for four-dimensionalism comes from multiple directions. It provides an elegant solution to the problem of temporary intrinsics: if the same object is straight at one time and bent at another, it cannot have these incompatible intrinsic properties simply. The four-dimensionalist resolves this by saying that the straight-at-t1 temporal part and the bent-at-t2 temporal part are distinct objects with distinct properties; there is no contradiction. It also provides resources for handling change of composition over time (the Ship of Theseus, the gradually replaced organism) and for understanding how organisms survive radical physical change.

Perdurantism — the view that objects persist by having different temporal parts at different times — contrasts with endurantism — the view that objects are wholly present at each time they exist. The choice between them is not merely terminological: they generate different predictions about persistence, different accounts of the relationship between objects and their stages, and different implications for the philosophy of time. An eternalist four-dimensionalist treats time as analogous to space in a way that a presentist cannot; the connection between four-dimensionalism and the B-theory of time is tight.

Stage theory, associated with Sider and Hawley, proposes that what ordinary object-terms refer to are instantaneous stages rather than four-dimensional worms. "You" refers to your present stage, not to the temporally extended entity. This has counterintuitive consequences but may handle some problems that worm theory faces. This chapter develops the full range of four-dimensionalist positions and evaluates them against the central problems of persistence and change.
