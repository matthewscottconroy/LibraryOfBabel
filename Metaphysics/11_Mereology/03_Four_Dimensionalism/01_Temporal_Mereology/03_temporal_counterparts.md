# Temporal Counterparts

Lewis's modal realism handles claims like "you could have been taller" by invoking counterpart theory: there is a possible-world individual that is your counterpart — sufficiently similar to you in the relevant respects — and that individual is taller. You could have been taller not because you are literally present in another world, but because a close enough stand-in for you is taller there. The temporal counterpart relation is the same idea applied to the time-dimension rather than the world-dimension: "you were a child" is analyzed as "there is a past individual that is your temporal counterpart and that is a child."

## Background: Lewis's Modal Counterpart Relation

Lewis's modal realism in *Counterfactuals* (1973) and *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986) analyzes de re modal claims using counterpart theory. A modal counterpart of an individual x in world w is an individual x' in some other world w' that resembles x in relevant respects and is the most similar such individual in w'. The relevant respects of similarity and their weighting are context-sensitive.

**Formal definition of modal counterpart:** CC(x, y, w, w') iff:
1. x exists in w and y exists in w'
2. y resembles x in the contextually relevant respects
3. y is sufficiently similar to x — no other individual in w' resembles x as much as y does (or: y is sufficiently similar, depending on the version)

## The Temporal Counterpart Relation

The temporal analog: TC(s, s') iff:
1. s exists at time t and s' exists at time t' ≠ t
2. s' resembles s in the contextually relevant respects
3. s' is sufficiently similar to s and appropriately connected through time — spatiotemporally continuous causal chain from s to s', or suitably related psychological states, depending on the kind of entity

For *persons*, the relevant respects include psychological continuity (memories, beliefs, desires, personality), bodily continuity (the same or suitably continuous body), and spatiotemporal continuity (unbroken physical causal chain). For *artifacts*, the relevant respects are functional continuity (the artifact continues to play its role), material continuity (the same or continuously related material), and historical continuity (the artifact is recognized as continuous within human practices).

## Analyzing Persistence Claims

On the stage view, all persistence claims are analyzed via the temporal counterpart relation:

**"John was a child."** (Said now, John being the present stage s at t)

∃s' [t' < t ∧ TC(s', s) ∧ Child(s')]

**"John will be happy."**

∃s'' [t'' > t ∧ TC(s'', s) ∧ Happy(s'')]

**"John is the same person as the person who was born thirty years ago."**

∃s''' [TC(s, s''') ∧ s''' is-born-at(t₀) ∧ t₀ = thirty-years-ago]

This is not a strict identity claim — s ≠ s''' since they exist at different times — but a temporal counterpart claim.

## Context-Sensitivity

A philosophically important feature of the stage view is that the temporal counterpart relation is *context-sensitive*. In different contexts, different features are salient for temporal counterpart-hood, generating different verdicts about persistence claims.

Consider the Ship of Theseus. In a context where functional continuity is salient: S₁ (the functionally continuous ship) is the temporal counterpart of the original ship at t₀, and the persistence claim "the ship that is now in the harbor is the same ship as the one that sailed out" comes out true. In a context where material continuity is salient: S₂ (the ship made of the original planks) is the temporal counterpart of the original, and the persistence claim comes out true — but for S₂, not S₁.

The stage view thus allows the Ship of Theseus to be handled by context-sensitivity rather than by a single determinate fact. For proponents, this is a virtue: it captures the genuine context-dependence of our intuitions. For critics, it is a bug: it seems to deny that there is a single determinate fact about ship identity, which is what most people who raise the puzzle are asking about.

Consider also a brain transplant: a person's brain is transplanted from body B₁ to body B₂. In a context where psychological continuity is salient, the person-stage in B₂ is the temporal counterpart of the person-stage in B₁. In a context where bodily continuity is salient, the body-stage B₁ that remains (without a brain) is the temporal counterpart of the original. The stage view captures the genuine ambiguity in our intuitions without forcing a single metaphysically determined verdict.

## Advantages and Objections

The stage view's handling of ordinary predication is literal: stages have properties simply, without temporal relativization. "John is happy" means John (the present stage) is happy — full stop. This is a genuine advantage over the worm view's implicit time-indexing.

The most pressing objection is that the stage view makes ordinary identity claims across time strictly false. "I am the same person who was born thirty years ago" is false on the stage view: I (the present stage) am not identical to any stage from thirty years ago. Sider's response is that ordinary speakers' truth-conditions for "I am the same person as..." are captured by the temporal counterpart analysis. When ordinary speakers make this claim, what makes it true or false in their usage is just that the relevant stages are temporally counterpart-related. The sentence is not false; it is true under the correct analysis.

The counterpart relation also needs specification: which respects are relevant, and how similar is "sufficiently"? The specification must avoid circularity — it cannot appeal to the notion of being the same object. The response is that the counterpart relation is specified by the kind of object in question and the context of inquiry. Context-sensitivity is a feature, not a bug.

One significant objection notes that psychological-continuity accounts of personal identity are already available to endurantists (Locke, Parfit, Shoemaker) — without positing temporal parts or stages. The stage view's handling of personal identity can be achieved within an endurantist framework, so the stage view does not have a distinctive advantage here. The response is that the stage view provides a *uniform* treatment of all persistence claims in terms of temporal counterpart relations and a single framework, while endurantist accounts of personal identity are not obviously generalizable to artifacts, masses of matter, and other kinds of object.

## Temporal Counterparts and the Formal Mereology

On the stage view, there is no worm — stages are not parts of a persisting whole. The mereological axioms govern parts and wholes, but stages are not parts of worms (since there are no worms). The formal structure is given by the temporal counterpart relation, defined outside the mereological framework. This means the stage view requires a dual formal apparatus: mereology for the spatial parts of stages, and temporal counterpart theory for relations among stages.

Some regard this duality as an inelegance. Stage theorists regard it as an accurate representation of the different formal structures underlying spatial composition and temporal persistence — two genuinely different kinds of thing that should not be forced into a single mold.
