# The Perdurance vs. Endurance Debate

Here is the most fundamental question about persistence: when you meet someone again after ten years, is the person before you *literally the same person* — numerically identical, the very same individual — or merely a later stage of a four-dimensional entity that includes both the earlier and the later encounter as temporal parts? This is the perdurance-endurance debate, one of the central disputes in the metaphysics of persistence.

## Defining the Positions

**Perdurantism:** An object O *perdures* through time iff O has distinct temporal parts at different times, and what makes O a single persisting thing is that these parts are all parts of the same four-dimensional worm:

O perdures iff ∀t₁ ∀t₂ [O exists at t₁ ∧ O exists at t₂ ∧ t₁ ≠ t₂ → ∃s₁ ∃s₂ (TP(s₁, O, t₁) ∧ TP(s₂, O, t₂) ∧ s₁ ≠ s₂)]

At any two distinct times, O has distinct temporal parts. The person at t₁ and the person at t₂ are *numerically distinct* temporal parts of the same four-dimensional worm.

**Endurantism:** An object O *endures* through time iff O is wholly present at each time of its existence — there are no temporal parts; the whole object exists at each moment:

O endures iff ∀t [O exists at t → ∀y (y is a part of O → y exists at t)]

At any time O exists, all of O's parts exist at that time. O is fully located at each moment, not merely a temporal cross-section of a four-dimensional whole.

**A third option — exdurantism (stagism):** On the stage view, what exists at any time is only the momentary stage. Objects do not perdure (they have no temporal extent) nor endure (they are not wholly present through time); they *exdure* — they are instantaneous but related to other instantaneous stages by temporal counterpart relations.

A useful test: does the person who exists on Monday have the *very same* numerical identity as the person who exists on Tuesday? The perdurantist says no — they are distinct temporal parts of the same worm, related by mereological parthood. The endurantist says yes — the very same individual, wholly present on both days. The stage theorist says no — Monday-stage and Tuesday-stage are not identical, but are related by the temporal counterpart relation.

## Historical Background

The debate traces to ancient metaphysics. Heraclitus's view that one cannot step into the same river twice suggests something like perdurantism: the river is never the same river because it is constantly changing. Aristotle's treatment of substance as persisting through change, possessing properties at times, reflects something closer to endurantism. In the modern period, Locke's distinction between masses of matter (identified by their material composition) and organisms (identified by their functional organization) anticipates the thought that different kinds of objects may have different identity conditions. Kant's treatment of substances as enduring through time and possessing properties at-a-time supports endurantism.

McTaggart's distinction between the A-series (past-present-future, dynamic time) and the B-series (earlier-later, static time) is relevant: B-series theorists naturally favor perdurantism (objects are spread across the B-series), while A-series theorists may prefer endurantism (the present is metaphysically privileged). In contemporary analytic philosophy, the debate was sharpened by Lewis in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986) and *"Rearrangement of Particles: Reply to Lowe"* (1988), and systematized by Sider's *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001).

## Why the Debate Matters

The two views generate different predictions across several cases. On *change*: if I stand still and then sit, the perdurantist says my standing temporal part has the property *standing*, my sitting temporal part has the property *sitting*, and these are distinct objects so there is no contradiction. The endurantist says I, one object, first had the property *standing* and then had the property *sitting*; these are instantiated at different times, so there is no contradiction either — but the solution is relational or time-indexed rather than mereological.

On *coincidence*: perdurantism resolves coincidence by multiplying temporal parts; endurantism either must accept genuinely coincident objects (constitution theory) or find a different solution.

On *personal identity*: perdurantism gives precise mereological answers (are these stages parts of the same worm?); endurantism must appeal to qualitative continuity criteria — psychological continuity, bodily continuity — as grounds for identity, without reducing identity to mereological composition.

## Presentism and Eternalism

The perdurance-endurance debate is orthogonal to but connected with the presentism-eternalism debate. *Presentism* holds that only the present exists; past and future objects do not exist, they either existed or will exist but do not currently exist. *Eternalism* holds that all times exist equally; past and future objects exist just as much as present ones; the present is merely our temporal perspective.

Perdurantism pairs naturally with eternalism — the four-dimensional worm needs to exist at past and future times to have parts there. Endurantism is compatible with both presentism (only the present object exists, wholly present at the present) and eternalism (the object exists at all its times, wholly present at each). Mark Johnston and Judith Jarvis Thomson are prominent endurantists. David Lewis and Theodore Sider are the canonical perdurantists. The debate continues with important contributions from Sally Haslanger, Mark Heller, and Dean Zimmerman, among others, and shows no signs of resolution.
