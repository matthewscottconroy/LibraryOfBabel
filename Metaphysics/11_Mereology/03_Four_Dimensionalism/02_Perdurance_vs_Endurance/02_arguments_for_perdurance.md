# Arguments for Perdurance

Several powerful arguments favor perdurantism. They range from formal arguments about the logic of intrinsic properties to considerations about coincidence, vagueness, and the relationship between mereology and physics.

## The Argument from Temporary Intrinsics (Lewis)

This is Lewis's most influential argument, presented in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986). A poker is straight on Monday and bent on Tuesday. *Straight* and *bent* are intrinsic properties — properties that an object has in virtue of how it is itself, not in virtue of its relations to other things. If the poker is one and the same entity present at both times, as endurantism holds, it seems to have both *straight* and *bent* — incompatible properties. Contradiction.

Let S(x) mean "x is straight" and B(x) mean "x is bent," where these are *simpliciter* properties (not relativized to times). Classical logic says: ¬∃x [S(x) ∧ B(x)]. If the poker-on-Monday and the poker-on-Tuesday are identical (as endurantism holds), then S(poker) ∧ B(poker) — contradiction.

Endurantists have three main responses. *Adverbialism*: the poker has the property *straight* on Monday, meaning it has the property *straightly-on-Monday* — a time-indexed property that is not incompatible with *bentedly-on-Tuesday*. Lewis's objection: this makes intrinsic properties relational — the poker's straightness at t is a relation between the poker and t. But intrinsic properties are supposed to be had in isolation, not in relation to anything else. *Relations to times*: the poker stands in the *being-straight-at* relation to Monday and the *being-bent-at* relation to Tuesday. Lewis's same objection: these are relations, not intrinsic properties. *Presentism*: only the present exists, so there is no past bent-poker. Lewis's objection: presentism is independently problematic (it conflicts with special relativity) and solves the puzzle by eliminating one of the objects involved rather than explaining how change is possible.

The perdurantist solution is clean: the Monday-stage is straight simpliciter; the Tuesday-stage is bent simpliciter. These are distinct objects, so there is no contradiction:

- TP(poker_{Mon}, poker, Mon) ∧ S(poker_{Mon})
- TP(poker_{Tue}, poker, Tue) ∧ B(poker_{Tue})
- poker_{Mon} ≠ poker_{Tue}
- ¬∃x [S(x) ∧ B(x)]     (no single object is both) ✓

Intrinsic properties are instantiated by temporal parts simpliciter. No time-indexing or relationalization is needed.

## The Argument from Mereological Extensionality

Mereological extensionality (EXT) states: two objects with exactly the same proper parts are identical. The perdurantist argument:

- P1. EXT is true.
- P2. The statue and the lump have different temporal parts (the lump's parts precede the statue).
- P3. By EXT, if two objects differ in any part, they are distinct.
- C. The statue and the lump are distinct — as constitution intuitions require — but without any need for a special constitution relation.

The perdurantist thus preserves EXT while accommodating the commonsense distinction between statue and lump. The endurantist who accepts constitution must give up EXT; the perdurantist does not.

## The Vagueness Argument (Sider)

The vagueness argument for perdurantism follows directly from the argument for universalism and against restricted composition:

- P1. If composition is restricted, there are borderline cases of composition.
- P2. Borderline cases of composition entail ontological vagueness.
- P3. Ontological vagueness is impossible.
- C. Composition is unrestricted (universalism).
- P4. Combined with universalism: for any collection of temporal stages (arranged in any spacetime pattern), there is a four-dimensional worm that is their fusion.
- C2. Perdurantism is true: every persisting object is a four-dimensional worm, and there are worms corresponding to every non-empty collection of stages.

This argument does not merely show that temporal parts exist; it shows that every collection of stages generates a worm, making the space of four-dimensional objects as rich as possible.

## The Argument from Special Relativity

Special relativity represents objects as four-dimensional structures in spacetime. In relativistic physics, there is no absolute simultaneity — what counts as "the same time" is reference-frame-relative. The correct physical description of an object is a *world-tube*: the set of spacetime events in the object's history. The "object at a time" is a cross-section of the world-tube at a given reference frame's notion of "at time t" — which varies across frames.

Formally, an object O is represented by its world-tube WT(O): a four-dimensional region of spacetime. The "instantaneous spatial extent" of O at time t (in frame F) is a three-dimensional cross-section:

WT_F(O, t) = {p ∈ WT(O) : p is simultaneous with t in frame F}

This cross-section is what the perdurantist calls the *temporal part* of O at t (in frame F). The perdurantist's framework maps naturally onto the physics. Endurantism must make sense of an object being "wholly present at a moment" in a framework where simultaneity is frame-relative — which is at best awkward and at worst incoherent.

## The Coincidence Argument

Perdurantism dissolves coincidence puzzles by giving the statue and lump different temporal parts. This dissolution is elegant and formally precise. The endurantist must either accept genuine spatial coincidence (constitution theory) with its difficulties, or adopt revisionary semantics (sortal-relative identity), or make some other costly move.

- P1. Intuition: the statue and lump are distinct.
- P2. Classical mereology with temporal parts: if they are distinct, they must differ in some part.
- P3. Claim: they differ in temporal parts (the lump has pre-casting temporal parts the statue lacks).
- C. They are distinct, and EXT is preserved.

This argument works if temporal parts are genuine parts. If they are, perdurantism gives a principled and formally precise account of the distinctness of coincident objects — an account that the endurantist has no counterpart to within classical mereology.

## The Puzzle of Change in Properties of Occupation

Consider a region of space R occupied by object O at t₁ and empty at t₂. If O endures, then O exists at both times and has the property "occupies R" at t₁ but lacks it at t₂. Is this property intrinsic or relational? If intrinsic: O has incompatible intrinsic properties at different times — the temporary intrinsics problem again. If relational: O's spatial properties are its relations to spacetime regions, which are permanent and atemporal. O permanently occupies-R-at-t₁ and permanently fails-to-occupy-R-at-t₂. This makes spatial properties time-indexed relations, which is uncomfortable for endurantism.

The perdurantist handles this naturally: the temporal part of O at t₁ occupies R simpliciter; the temporal part at t₂ does not exist in R. Spatial occupation is an intrinsic property of temporal parts, not a relation between a persisting object and a spacetime region.
