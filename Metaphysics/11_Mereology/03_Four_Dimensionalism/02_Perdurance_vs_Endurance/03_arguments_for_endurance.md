# Arguments for Endurance

Despite the theoretical appeal of perdurantism, many philosophers defend the endurantist view that objects persist by being wholly present at each time of their existence. The arguments for endurance are partly defensive — showing that the problems perdurantism is meant to solve can be solved otherwise — and partly offensive, showing that perdurantism itself has unacceptable costs.

## The Argument from Strangeness: The Default View

Our ordinary concept of a material object does not include temporal parts as components. When I examine my hand, I do not encounter a temporal-part-of-a-hand; I encounter my hand, wholly present. Temporal parts are a philosopher's invention. They are posited as a theoretical device, not discovered as constituents of ordinary experience.

This methodological point matters. Revisionary theories bear a heavier argumentative burden: if the ordinary view can solve the puzzles that motivate perdurantism, we should prefer it. Perdurantism is not the natural reading of ordinary discourse about objects, and the case for it must be strong enough to overcome the default.

The perdurantist can agree that temporal parts are not found in ordinary experience and still insist that they are theoretically required — as electrons are required to explain the behavior of matter, despite not being encountered in ordinary experience. The endurantist's reply is that the analogy fails. Electrons are posited to explain *empirical* phenomena — the observed behavior of matter and radiation. Temporal parts are posited to solve a philosopher's puzzle (the problem of change) that can be solved in other ways. The empirical success of physics does not support temporal parts.

## The Solvability of Temporary Intrinsics Without Temporal Parts

Lewis argues that the problem of temporary intrinsics requires temporal parts. But endurantists argue the problem is solvable otherwise.

The *time-indexed properties response* (Johnston, Haslanger): "straight" and "bent" are not unrelativized properties but relations to times. The poker stands in the *being-straight-at* relation to Monday and the *being-bent-at* relation to Tuesday. These are not incompatible — they have different relata:

S(poker, Monday) ∧ B(poker, Tuesday) — no contradiction.

Lewis objects that this makes intrinsic properties relational, betraying the very intrinsicness that motivates the puzzle. The endurantist reply, developed by Haslanger in "Endurance and Temporary Intrinsics" (1989), is that the objection assumes properties are instantiated *simpliciter* (atemporally) — which is precisely the perdurantist assumption. Once we allow time-indexed instantiation, the problem does not arise. Properties are instantiated *at times*: the formal framework replaces Fx with F(x, t). This is not making properties relational in any objectionable sense — it is recognizing that property-instantiation is temporally located. The poker's shape at Monday is not a relation between the poker and Monday; it is the poker's shape, understood as temporally located.

The *presentist response* (Markosian, Craig): if only the present exists, there is no past bent-poker and no future bent-poker. The present poker is straight. No incompatibility arises because there is nothing to be bent. Lewis's objection is that presentism cannot account for truths about the past — it requires a special account of how past-directed claims can be true now. The presentist reply is that past truths are true in virtue of the current world's history: the present world has a past in which the poker was bent, and this grounds the past-tense truth without requiring a past object to be bent.

## The Argument from Personal Identity and Moral Responsibility

Many philosophers find the perdurantist account of personal identity phenomenologically and ethically inadequate.

If my current temporal part is what acts and suffers, then my past temporal parts have existed, acted, and are metaphysically inert. But responsibility for past actions seems to belong to *me* — the agent who acted. On the perdurantist view, I am responsible for my past actions because my current temporal part is part of the same worm as the past temporal part that acted. This seems to make responsibility a matter of mereological overlap — a purely formal, non-moral relation.

The endurantist response: I am the very same individual who acted in the past. My responsibility is grounded in numerical identity: the person now present is the person who acted. This is what moral responsibility requires — not mereological overlap but genuine identity.

The perdurantist reply is that the worm-analysis is not merely formal: it captures what we care about when we hold people responsible. We care that the current person is psychologically continuous with the past actor — same memories, dispositions, character — and worm-membership captures this when the worm is defined by the right continuity conditions.

## The Argument from Presentness

Objects are paradigmatically present — they are here, now, before us. When we interact with an object, we interact with the whole object, not a temporal slice of it. The perdurantist says the object we interact with is a time-slice; the "whole object" is a four-dimensional worm that extends into the past and future and is not itself present before us.

The endurantist argument:

- P1. We interact directly with material objects — we perceive, touch, move, and are causally affected by them.
- P2. Our perceptual and causal access to objects is always at the present time.
- P3. If we access the whole object at the present time, the whole object must be present at the present time.
- C. Objects are wholly present at the present time — endurantism is true.

The perdurantist objection to P3: we do not access "the whole object" in perception — we access its current temporal slice. The four-dimensional worm extends beyond our perceptual access. This is no different from the fact that we perceive the front surface of an object without perceiving its back: we do not thereby access the "whole" object in perception, yet the object has a back.

The endurantist reply: the analogy to the back surface fails. The back of the object is causally connected to the front and can in principle be perceived by moving around the object. Past and future temporal parts, however, cannot be causally accessed from the present — they are causally inert relative to present perception. There is a fundamental asymmetry between spatial hiddenness (the back surface) and temporal hiddenness (temporal parts) that the analogy misses.

## The Argument from Four-Dimensionalism's Revisionary Character

Four-dimensionalism is a philosopher's invention, and revisionary theories bear a heavier argumentative burden. Endurantism is the default view — what ordinary speakers, non-philosophers, and most scientists assume when they talk about persisting objects.

- P1. Default metaphysical positions should be accepted unless there is compelling reason to revise them.
- P2. Endurantism is the default view of persistence.
- P3. The arguments for perdurantism (from temporary intrinsics, coincidence, vagueness) can be answered within an endurantist framework.
- C. We should accept endurantism.

The status of P3 is the central question of the entire debate. The endurantist argues that the temporary intrinsics problem can be solved by time-indexing properties, the coincidence puzzle can be handled by constitution theory or sortal-relative identity, and the vagueness argument can be resisted by accepting epistemic vagueness or moderate ontological vagueness. Whether these endurantist solutions are adequate — whether they solve the problems without introducing new problems equally serious — remains contested. The perdurance-endurance debate is one of the most live issues in contemporary metaphysics, and there is no clear consensus verdict.
