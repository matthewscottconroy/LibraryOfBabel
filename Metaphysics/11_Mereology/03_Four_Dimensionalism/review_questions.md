# Review Questions — Four-Dimensionalism

1. Lewis's argument from temporary intrinsics holds that endurantism requires either making intrinsic properties relational (indexed to times) or accepting contradictions. Haslanger's time-indexed response denies that relativizing instantiation to times makes properties relational in any objectionable sense. Who has the stronger position here, and does resolving this dispute require a prior account of what "intrinsic" means?

2. The worm view and the stage view both accept temporal parts, but they differ over which entities are the primary bearers of properties and what ordinary persistence discourse is really about. The worm view makes ordinary identity claims literally true; the stage view makes them strictly false but contextually appropriate. Which is the greater theoretical cost—revisionary semantics or revisionary ontology—and does the choice between the two views ultimately come down to which cost you prefer?

3. Temporal counterpart relations are explicitly context-sensitive: what counts as the same ship or the same person depends on which continuity conditions are salient in a given context. Does this context-sensitivity make the stage view more or less realistic as a metaphysical theory of persistence? Compare the stage view's handling of the Ship of Theseus with the constitution theorist's handling of the same case from Chapter 2.

4. Perdurantism pairs naturally with mereological universalism: just as any collection of objects composes a spatial fusion, any collection of temporal stages composes a four-dimensional worm. What are the ontological consequences of accepting both simultaneously? Does the combination resolve the coincidence problem in a satisfying way, or does it replace one puzzle (two objects, one place) with another (an unbounded proliferation of worms)?

5. The argument from special relativity holds that objects in relativistic spacetime are naturally understood as four-dimensional world-tubes, making temporal parts the physically apt ontological unit. How compelling is this argument as a case for perdurantism specifically rather than merely for the claim that our best physics uses a spacetime framework? Could an endurantist accept special relativity without accepting temporal parts?

6. Endurantists argue that the problems motivating perdurantism—temporary intrinsics, coincidence, vagueness—can each be solved within an endurantist framework, so that theoretical conservatism favors endurance. Is this a sound application of a principle of parsimony, or does it misidentify which theoretical virtues matter? Consider whether the perdurantist's solutions are more unified even if individually each endurantist solution is adequate.

7. On the worm view, moral responsibility is grounded in mereological overlap: I am responsible for my past action because my current temporal part and the acting temporal part are parts of the same worm. Assess whether this is a genuine account of responsibility or merely a re-description that leaves the normatively important question—why being parts of the same worm matters morally—unanswered.

8. Four-dimensionalism is proposed as a solution to both the coincidence problem (Chapter 2) and the problem of change (this chapter), and it also underwrites the vagueness argument against restricted composition (Chapter 1). Stepping back across all three chapters: does four-dimensionalism function as a genuinely unified theoretical framework that resolves problems from independent directions, or does its success in each area rely on importing assumptions—particularly about temporal parts and universalism—that are themselves in need of justification?
