# Domain Survey: Truth and Reality

## Overview

Truth and reality mark the interface between what we say and think on one hand, and what the world is like on the other. The philosophical theory of truth asks: what is it for a proposition, belief, or sentence to be true? Is truth a substantive property that relates representations to the world (as correspondence theory holds), or is it a thin, redundant property such that to say "it is true that snow is white" adds nothing to "snow is white" (deflationism)? Is truth internal to a coherent system of beliefs (coherence theory), or is it constituted by what would be ideal practice (pragmatism)? These are not merely terminological choices — they carry commitments about the nature of reality, the possibility of knowledge, and the relationship between mind and world.

The **correspondence theory** of truth — the view that a proposition is true if and only if it corresponds to a fact (or states of affairs, or portion of reality) — is the intuitive default. It captures the common sense that when I say the cat is on the mat, my statement is true if and only if the cat is, in fact, on the mat. But unpacking "correspondence" and "fact" quickly generates hard metaphysical questions: what are facts? Are they entities in the world distinct from the objects and properties they are "about"? Russell and Wittgenstein (early) posited facts as basic ontological entities (atomic facts consisting of objects standing in relations); Armstrong developed a sophisticated theory of states of affairs as truthmakers. Deflationary critics argue that positing facts as truth-making entities is metaphysically idle — it adds nothing substantive to the statement itself.

**Deflationism** (or "minimalism") about truth holds that the truth predicate serves primarily a logical/expressive function — enabling quantification and generalization over propositions — but adds no substantive content. "It is true that snow is white" is equivalent to "snow is white"; the truth predicate is "disquotational." Quine's disquotation, Horwich's minimal theory, and Brandom's inferentialist account all fall under this broad category. The challenge for deflationists is to explain why truth plays such a central role in our epistemic and metaphysical practices if it is merely a thin logical operator.

**Metaphysical realism and anti-realism** about truth involves not just the correspondence/deflationism debate but also questions about whether truth is mind-independent (external-world realism) or in some way responsive to epistemic conditions (anti-realism, epistemic theories of truth). Putnam's internal realism holds that truth is what ideal rational inquiry converges to; Dummett's anti-realism holds that truth is warranted assertibility and that the world is constrained by our epistemic access to it. These positions intersect with the realism debate in metametaphysics (Domain 09) but have their own distinctive motivations from philosophy of language, mathematics, and logic.

## Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

### Correspondence Theory

**Classical correspondence** (Aristotle, medieval *adaequatio intellectus et rei*, Russell 1912): A proposition is true if and only if it corresponds to a fact. The correspondence is a real metaphysical relation between the proposition and the fact.

- *Russell's logical atomism*: Elementary propositions (atomic statements of the form *a is F* or *aRb*) are true if and only if there exists an atomic fact consisting of the object(s) and property/relation. Complex propositions are built from elementary ones by logical connectives; their truth conditions are determined by truth-functional rules.

- *Wittgenstein's Tractatus*: "A proposition is a picture of reality." Propositions share logical form with the facts they depict; atomic facts are combinations of objects; the world is the totality of facts.

- *Austin's speech act version*: A statement is true when the type of situation it is correlated with by demonstrative conventions is one that actually exists.

**Truthmaker theory** (Armstrong, Bigelow, Fox): The correspondence theory is made precise via the notion of a *truthmaker* — an entity (or entities) whose existence necessitates the truth of the proposition. For every true proposition *p*, there exists an entity *T* such that necessarily, if *T* exists, *p* is true. 

- Candidates for truthmakers: states of affairs (Armstrong), tropes (Mulligan, Simons, Smith), facts, objects, or even abstract propositions themselves.
- *Truthmaker maximalism*: Every truth has a truthmaker. This generates puzzles for negative truths ("there are no unicorns"), universal generalizations, and modal truths.
- *Truthmaker necessitarianism* (Armstrong): Truthmaking is a necessary relation — if *T* makes *p* true, then in every world where *T* exists, *p* is true.

**Problems for correspondence theory**:
- *The regress* (Bradley): If truth requires a correspondence relation between proposition and fact, does this relation itself require a further fact? An infinite regress seems to follow.
- *Negative truths*: What is the truthmaker for "there are no unicorns"? No positive entity seems to make this true; Armstrong posited "totality facts" (it is a fact that the totality of positive facts is exhausted by...), but these seem odd.
- *Moral truths*: If moral truths are true, what are the moral facts that correspond to them? Naturalists posit natural facts; non-naturalists posit sui generis moral facts.

### Deflationary Theories

**Disquotation** (Quine): The truth predicate has only a disquotational role — to "ascend" from using a sentence to mentioning it, and vice versa. "Snow is white" is true ↔ snow is white. Truth is merely a device for semantic ascent and descent; it does not describe a substantive property.

**Prosentential theory** (Grover, Camp, Belnap): "It is true" functions as a prosentence — a pronoun for sentences — not as a predicate attributing a property. "That's true" is like "he did it" — it inherits its content from an antecedent sentence rather than ascribing a property.

**Minimalism** (Horwich 1990): All that can be said about truth is captured by the infinite set of "equivalence biconditionals": *⟨p⟩ is true ↔ p*. Truth is not a substantive property; the equivalences are trivially true; there is no unified theory of truth beyond the schema.

**Deflationist challenge**: If truth is merely disquotational, why does it matter whether our beliefs are true? Why should we aim for truth rather than mere coherence or utility? Deflationists must explain the normative role of truth without invoking a substantive property.

### Coherence Theory

**Idealist coherence theory** (F. H. Bradley, Joachim): A proposition is true if it coheres with a comprehensive, maximally coherent system of propositions. Truth is internal coherence of the system.
- *Problem*: multiple non-coherent systems may each be internally coherent; the coherence criterion fails to determine which system is the true one. Also, coherence seems to allow truths that contradict each other (two internally coherent but mutually inconsistent systems).

**Neo-pragmatist coherence** (Sellars, Davidson on coherence of beliefs with web of belief): Coherence plays an important epistemological role — justification is coherence — but this is not a theory of what truth is.

### Pragmatist Theories

**Peirce**: Truth is the opinion that ideal inquiry would converge to in the long run. A proposition is true if it would be assented to by all rational inquirers given unlimited evidence and discussion.

**Putnam's internal realism** (1981): Truth is idealized rational acceptability — what rational inquiry would accept under ideal conditions. Truth is not "out there" waiting to be discovered; it is constituted by what ideal inquiry would validate.
- *Putnam's later self-criticism*: Putnam came to regard this as a form of anti-realism and moved toward "natural realism."

**Rorty's neo-pragmatism**: Truth is what it is good for us to believe. There is no useful distinction between truth and warranted assertibility; the vocabulary of truth adds nothing.

### Realism and Anti-Realism

**Metaphysical realism**: There is a mind-independent reality; truth consists in correspondence to that reality; a proposition can be true even if we are unable to verify it. Realism presupposes bivalence (every proposition is either true or false).

**Dummett's anti-realism**: Truth is not mind-independent correspondence; a proposition is true only if we are able in principle to verify it. For some domains (mathematics, the past), there are propositions that may be neither verifiable nor refutable — and for these, truth and falsity do not apply. Anti-realism supports **intuitionist logic** (where the Law of Excluded Middle fails for some propositions) rather than classical logic.

**Wright's pluralism about truth** (1992): Truth may be different things in different domains. In mathematics, truth may be minimal; in perceptual judgment, truth may involve correspondence; in ethics, truth may be superassertibility. There is no single property that all truths share.

## Key Arguments and Counterarguments

### The Liar Paradox

"This sentence is false." If true, it is false; if false, it is true. This is the simplest form of the Liar.

**Tarskian response**: Truth is defined hierarchically; "true" in English applies only to sentences in an object language, not to sentences of the metalanguage used to define truth. The Liar sentence, which uses "true" self-referentially, is not well-formed in the stratified hierarchy.

**Paraconsistent responses**: Accept true contradictions (dialetheism — Priest); some sentences are both true and false; classical logic must be revised to accommodate this.

**Revision theory** (Gupta, Herzberger): Truth is defined by revision rules; the truth value of the Liar sentence "revises" indefinitely without settling; this is a feature, not a bug, of the Liar.

### The Slingshot Argument

Church and Quine's "slingshot" attempts to show that if correspondence theory is correct, there is at most one fact — the Great Fact — that all true sentences correspond to:

(1) The Morning Star is the Morning Star. (trivially true)
(2) The Morning Star is the Evening Star. (true, a posteriori)
(3) Substituting co-referring terms: (1) and (2) have the same reference (the planet Venus) so they correspond to the same fact.
(4) Similarly, any two true sentences describe the same fact.

**Response**: Structured propositions contain constituents that determine which facts they can correspond to; fine-grained proposition identity blocks the substitution steps. (Barwise and Perry's situation theory; structured propositions views.)

### Davidson's Argument Against Correspondence

Davidson (1969): Suppose sentences are true by "correspondence" to facts. Facts must be fine-grained enough to distinguish different truths; but fine-grained facts collapse into Fregean senses, and then correspondence theory adds nothing to a description theory of meaning that we already have.

**Truthmaker response**: Correspondence is analyzed via truthmaking, not via a Russellian picture theory. The truthmaking relation is not a mapping of sentences onto facts but an ontological dependence relation between existing entities and propositions.

### The Motivation for Truthmaker Theory (Armstrong)

**P1.** Every true proposition is true because of how things are in the world (truth is not primitive and fundamental; it supervenes on being).
**P2.** For each truth, there must be something in the world that makes it true — that is, its existence necessitates the truth of the proposition.
**P3.** The truthmakers are the ontological ground of truth; they are what truth is responsive to.
**C.** For every truth *p*, there exists a truthmaker T such that necessarily: if T exists, p is true.

**Deflationist response**: P1 is correct (truth supervenes on being), but this does not require truthmakers as distinct entities. The supervenience of truth on being is guaranteed by the equivalence schema without positing facts or states of affairs.

## Key Papers and Books

1. **Tarski, Alfred, "The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages" (1933; English 1956)** — The technical foundational paper; establishes the semantic definition of truth via satisfaction; widely discussed in philosophy and logic.

2. **Armstrong, D. M., *Truth and Truthmakers* (2004)** — Systematic defense of truthmaker theory; develops the theory of states of affairs as truthmakers and responds to objections for negative truths and modal truths.

3. **Horwich, Paul, *Truth* (1990, 2nd ed. 1998)** — The definitive defense of minimalism about truth; argues that the equivalence schema exhausts everything that can be said about truth.

4. **Dummett, Michael, *Truth and Other Enigmas* (1978)** — Collected papers developing the anti-realist view; meaning-theoretic argument for intuitionist logic in mathematics.

5. **Wright, Crispin, *Truth and Objectivity* (1992)** — Develops pluralism about truth; argues that different domains warrant different notions of truth.

6. **Davidson, Donald, "True to the Facts" (1969)** — The slingshot argument against facts as truth-relata; influential attack on correspondence theory.

7. **Bigelow, John, *The Reality of Numbers* (1988)** — Develops truthmaker theory for mathematics; the first systematic application of truthmaking to abstract objects.

8. **Beall, J. C. and Restall, Greg, *Logical Pluralism* (2006)** — Argues that there are multiple legitimate logical systems corresponding to different truth-preservation relations; connects truth to logical consequence.

## Live Debates and Open Questions

1. **Truthmakers for modal truths**: "It is possible that there are dragons" is true. What makes it true? Lewis: the existence of possible worlds where there are dragons. Actualists: abstract states of affairs, sets of propositions. The truthmaker for modal claims is a central puzzle.

2. **Negative truths and the problem of absence**: "There are no unicorns" is true. What makes it true? Armstrong's totality facts; Lewis's plurality of worlds none of which contain unicorns; Quine: eliminate reference to the unicorn-absence by paraphrase. This is one of the most difficult problems for truthmaker theory.

3. **Pluralism about truth**: Wright's proposal that truth is superassertibility in some domains and correspondence in others faces the challenge of giving a unified account of how these different "truths" connect.

4. **The relationship between truth and meaning**: Tarski's semantic definition of truth gives truth conditions for sentences; Davidson proposed to use truth conditions as the basis for a theory of meaning. The direction of analysis — from meaning to truth, or from truth to meaning — is contested.

5. **Truth gaps and truth gluts**: Gaps (sentences that are neither true nor false) arise for the Liar, for statements about non-existent referents, for future contingents. Gluts (sentences that are both true and false) arise on dialethic views of the Liar. What are the implications for the metaphysics of truth?

## Connections to Other Domains

**Truth and Ontology (Domain 01)**: Truthmaker theory is an explicitly ontological thesis — it posits facts, states of affairs, or other entities as the truthmakers for truths. The debate between truthmaker maximalism and deflationism is partly a debate about the ontological commitment required by a theory of truth.

**Truth and Universals/Particulars (Domain 02)**: If predicates like "is red" are true of things, what makes them true? Realists about universals posit the universal redness as a truthmaker; nominalists must give a truthmaker (the class of red things, or the individual's resemblance to paradigms) without invoking universals.

**Truth and Metametaphysics (Domain 09)**: The debate between metaphysical realism (truth is mind-independent) and anti-realism (truth is constrained by epistemic access) directly parallels the debate between heavyweight ontological realism and deflationism. Wright's pluralism about truth corresponds to pluralism about ontological questions.

**Truth and Philosophy of Religion (Domain 13)**: If God is omniscient, God believes all and only truths. The nature of divine omniscience depends on the extent of truths (are there truths about the future? about counterfactuals of creaturely freedom?). The truthmakers for these truths — or their absence — connects directly to discussions of divine foreknowledge and Molinism.

**Truth and Modality (Domain 05)**: Modal truths ("it is possible that there are talking donkeys") require truth-making. Lewis provides the most systematic truthmaking account for modal truths (possible worlds as concrete entities); actualists and modalists must give alternative accounts.
