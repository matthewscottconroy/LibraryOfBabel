# Truth and Reality

"Snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white. This Tarski biconditional looks trivial — almost tautological. But it conceals a substantive and contested question: what is it that *makes* the sentence on the left correct? Is there a relation — correspondence — between the sentence and the world, such that truth consists in the sentence's matching or picturing some feature of reality? Or is truth a matter of coherence — of fitting into a comprehensive and consistent system of beliefs? Or is it perhaps less metaphysically loaded than either of these: perhaps "is true" is just a device for endorsing statements, adding nothing to their content, requiring no deep analysis because it does not name a real property at all?

These are the questions of the theory of truth, and they have consequences that extend far beyond the philosophy of language. If truth is correspondence, we are committed to there being facts — features of reality — that truthbearers correspond to or fail to correspond to, and this commitment shapes the entire ontological landscape. If truth is coherence, we face the question of which system of beliefs is the coherent one and whether coherence can be achieved without input from a mind-independent world. If truth is deflated away — if there is nothing more to being true than being assertable — we face the challenge of explaining how science makes genuine discoveries and how our statements answer to a world they did not create.

The stakes of the debate about truth are made vivid by the question of realism: is there a mind-independent reality that our beliefs and theories are about? Realism says yes; various forms of anti-realism say no, or at least no in a way that vindicates the robust independence the realist has in mind. This question — perhaps the most fundamental in all of philosophy — has been debated under the headings of idealism, pragmatism, verificationism, and constructivism, and its resolution (if it has one) determines what we mean by "the world" when we say that true statements correspond to it.

This unit examines truth and its relationship to reality across four chapters. The opening chapter surveys the major **theories of truth**: correspondence theory, which locates truth in a relation between propositions and facts or states of affairs; coherence theory, associated with the idealist tradition and with certain recent epistemological views; and deflationary or minimalist theories, which hold that the T-biconditionals exhaust the content of the truth predicate and that no deeper analysis is needed or available. Each theory illuminates something and faces characteristic objections, and the debate among them is far from settled.

**Truthmaking** shifts focus from the analysis of truth to a prior ontological question: what are the features of the world that make truths true? The truthmaker principle holds that for every truth, there is something in the world whose existence necessitates that truth. Truthmaker maximalism extends this to every truth without exception; restricted accounts limit the principle. The ontological commitments involved — states of affairs, tropes, essences — connect truthmaking to the deepest questions of ontology examined in Unit 1.

**Realism and anti-realism** develops the major positions in the debate about the mind-independence of reality. Idealism in its various forms — British idealism, Kantian idealism, contemporary neo-idealism — holds that reality is in some sense constituted by mind or experience. Pragmatism, in the tradition of Peirce, James, and Dewey, identifies truth with what is good to believe, or with what inquiry converges on in the long run. The contemporary realism/anti-realism debate, framed by Dummett's semantic anti-realism, turns on whether truth transcends evidence.

**Metaethics and reality** brings the abstract debate about truth and realism to the domain of ethics, asking whether moral truths are objective features of the world (moral realism), expressions of attitudes without truth conditions (expressivism), systematically false (error theory), or deserving of a more nuanced treatment (quasi-realism). Parallel questions arise in aesthetics. The metaethical debate shows that questions about truth and reality are not merely theoretical; they concern the standing of our most valued judgments.

Truth, in the end, is where philosophy and the world meet.
