# Theories of Truth

What is truth? The question has been posed since antiquity and seems both obvious and elusive. "Snow is white" is true, and snow is in fact white — the connection between them appears direct. But when we try to say exactly what the connection is, the difficulties multiply.

Correspondence theory holds that truth is a relation between a truthbearer (a sentence, proposition, or belief) and reality: a proposition is true when it corresponds to a fact, matches a state of affairs, or accurately represents how things are. This view captures the intuition that truth is mind-independent — that what makes a statement true is not our believing it but how the world actually is. The challenge is to say what correspondence amounts to: how does a proposition "correspond to" a fact? Is correspondence a genuine relation, or a merely figurative way of saying that the proposition accurately represents the world?

Coherence theory holds that truth consists not in correspondence to an independent world but in membership in a coherent system of beliefs. A belief is true if it coheres with — is consistent with and supported by — the other beliefs in a comprehensive system. This view has attractions for those skeptical of the idea of a mind-independent world against which beliefs can be checked, but it faces the objection that a coherent system of beliefs might nonetheless be systematically false.

Deflationary (or minimalist) theories hold that there is nothing deep to explain about truth. The predicate "is true" serves a logical function — it allows us to endorse statements we cannot specify explicitly, to generalize over propositions — without naming any real property. "Snow is white is true" says nothing more than "snow is white"; the truth predicate adds no content. Deflationists argue that the T-biconditionals exhaust the content of our truth talk and that no substantive theory of truth is needed.

This chapter develops each theory carefully, evaluates the objections to each, and asks whether there is a notion of truth robust enough to do the work that metaphysics, science, and ethics require of it.
