# What Is Correspondence?

Consider the simplest possible case. It is raining. I say "it is raining." We want to say that what makes my statement true — not just useful, not just coherent with other things I believe, but *true* — is something about the rain itself. The rain is doing the work. This intuition, as old as systematic philosophy, is the seed of the correspondence theory: a proposition is true when it accurately represents how things actually are in the world, and false when it does not.

Aristotle gave it canonical expression: "to say of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is, is false, while to say of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not, is true" (*Metaphysics* Γ.7, 1011b25). The scholastic tradition rendered this as *veritas est adaequatio rei et intellectus* — truth is the conformity of the mind to things. The *ordo rationis* (the order of thought) is true when it matches the *ordo realis* (the order of things). Simple enough, and deeply appealing. The task of modern correspondence theory has been to give it enough philosophical precision to count as a genuine theory rather than just a compelling slogan.

**Specifying the relation**

That task requires answering two questions: what are the *relata* — what kind of thing stands on each side of the correspondence relation — and what is the relation itself? The left-hand relatum is typically a proposition, belief, statement, or sentence. The right-hand relatum — the worldly item that the proposition matches or fails to match — is variously taken to be a *fact*, a *state of affairs*, or a *trope*. And the relation itself is described, with varying precision, as "representing," "picturing," "matching," or "being made true by."

The formal point can be put sharply. Tarski's T-schema gives us: T(⌜p⌝) ↔ p. The correspondence theorist wants more than this: not merely that "the rose is red" is true iff the rose is red, but that there is an entity in the world whose existence accounts for that biconditional. The substantive elaboration is: T(⌜p⌝) ↔ ∃F(Corresponds(⌜p⌝, F) ∧ Obtains(F)). Truth is not just disquotation; it is the obtaining of a worldly correlate.

Three accounts of correspondence have been influential. First, *structural isomorphism*: the structure of a proposition mirrors the structure of a fact. This is the Tractarian view. A proposition "Fa" contains an element for the object *a* and an element for the property *F*, combined in a way that represents their combination in reality. Second, *reference and satisfaction*: Davidson's truth-theoretic semantics grounds correspondence in Tarskian reference and satisfaction. A singular term refers to an object; a predicate is satisfied by objects; truth is derived from these semantic relations without positing facts as further entities. Third, *truthmaker necessitation*: *p* is true iff there exists an entity T such that T's existence necessitates *p*'s truth (T ⊩ p). This weakens structural isomorphism while preserving the basic correspondence insight.

Bertrand Russell and G. E. Moore revived correspondence theory in the early twentieth century as part of their revolt against British Idealism. Russell's logical atomism held that true propositions correspond to atomic facts — the world is a totality of such facts, and logical structure mirrors propositional structure. The young Wittgenstein's *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* elaborated this into a "picture theory": a proposition is a logical picture of a possible fact, and truth consists in the picture's matching the actual fact. "The picture is a fact" (TLP 2.141); "A proposition is a picture of reality" (TLP 4.01). The structure of the picture — the way its elements are combined — maps onto the structure of the depicted state of affairs.

Alfred Tarski's "The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages" (1933, Polish; 1956, English) gave correspondence theory a formal grounding. Tarski's material adequacy condition requires that any acceptable definition of truth entail all instances of the T-schema: T(⌜p⌝) ↔ p. His recursive definition of satisfaction — a sentence is true in a model iff satisfied by all sequences — was widely taken as vindicating the correspondence intuition by showing how truth conditions track the structure of the world. Tarski himself was cautious about whether his formal achievement captured the pre-theoretical notion of correspondence; philosophers like J. L. Austin and Karl Popper were less cautious.

**A case in the argument**

We can put the core correspondence argument more formally. Consider "the rose is red."

- P1: For any proposition *p*, *p* is true if and only if there exists a fact *F* such that *p* corresponds to *F*.
- P2: The rose's being red is a fact — it obtains in the world.
- P3: The proposition "the rose is red" corresponds to the fact of the rose's being red.
- C: Therefore, "the rose is red" is true.

Notice that the theory's appeal lies precisely in making explicit what the simple rain example relies on implicitly. Most people, on reflection, think that what makes "snow is white" true is something about snow and whiteness — not internal coherence of a belief system, not pragmatic utility. Correspondence theory codifies this.

**Austin, Wright, and the limits of the theory**

J. L. Austin's "Truth" (1950) revived correspondence in a more sophisticated form, connecting it to speech-act theory. Austin distinguished the *demonstration* relation — the sentence type conventionally correlates with a type of state of affairs — from the *correlation* relation — the particular historic state of affairs that a given statement is about. A statement is true when the state of affairs it correlates with is of the type the sentence conventionally correlates with. This two-stage account grounds correspondence in linguistic convention rather than metaphysical structure and avoids positing facts as structured entities.

P. F. Strawson challenged Austin in the same proceedings, arguing that truth is a performative rather than a descriptive act — to call a statement true is to confirm or agree with it, not to assert a correspondence relation. Austin replied that Strawson's account cannot explain how truth relates to the world. The exchange helped crystallize what distinguishes correspondence theory from deflationism.

Crispin Wright's *Truth and Objectivity* (1992) argues that correspondence theory is best understood as one truth-property among several rather than as a universal theory. For domains where truths have a Wide Cosmological Role — where they figure in the explanation of observable phenomena beyond the discourse itself — correspondence may be the appropriate truth-property. The truth of "electrons have charge" explains why experimental devices behave as they do. But aesthetic truths, mathematical truths, and perhaps moral truths may not carry this cosmological role to the same degree. Wright's pluralism preserves correspondence for the domains where it is most compelling while permitting other accounts elsewhere.

A persistent problem concerns the structure of propositions themselves. If propositions are abstract objects — Fregean thoughts, sets of possible worlds — what gives them a structure that can "correspond" to facts? Frege held that true and false sentences both refer to objects (the True and the False), which sidesteps correspondence but abandons the theory's intuitive content. Russell's multiple-relation theory of judgment (1910–1913) attempted to relate the judging mind directly to the constituents of a fact without positing propositions as intermediate entities, but faced Wittgenstein's objection: the theory has no resources to exclude judging nonsense.

The trajectory from Aristotle to Austin illustrates a consistent pressure: specifying the correspondence relation without circularity or regress. "Water is H₂O" is true because it corresponds to the chemical fact that water molecules consist of two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom. The proposition's terms refer to natural kinds; the predicate expresses a structural relation of molecular composition; the fact — the molecular structure of water — exists independently of the proposition and necessitates its truth. In Dummett's phrase, the truth of "water is H₂O" is recognition-transcendent: it was true before Lavoisier discovered it and would be true even if no one had ever discovered it. Whether such cases show the theory is correct, or merely that the correspondence intuition is compelling, is the question the history of the theory keeps putting to us.
