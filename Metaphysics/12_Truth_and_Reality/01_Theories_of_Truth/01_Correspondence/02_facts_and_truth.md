# Facts and Truth

The correspondence theory of truth posits *facts* as the worldly relata to which true propositions correspond. A fact is a feature of reality — a way things are — that serves as the truthmaker for a true proposition. When "the cat is on the mat" is true, there is a fact — the cat's being on the mat — that makes it true. This much seems intuitive enough. The difficulty begins when we ask what a fact actually is.

One option is to treat facts as structured complexes: the fact that *a* is *F* is an entity composed of the object *a* and the property *F*, combined in some structured way. This is Russell's logical atomism: the world consists of atomic facts, each composed of a particular and a universal in a relation. True propositions correspond to existing facts; false propositions correspond to nothing. A second option, associated with Wittgenstein's *Tractatus*, treats facts as obtaining states of affairs: "The world divides into facts" (TLP 1.2); "What is the case — a fact — is the existence of states of affairs" (TLP 2). The advantage is ontological parsimony: states of affairs are structured entities, but the structure is given by the combinatorial possibilities of objects, not by any further "fact-entity."

**Three pressures on facts**

We can formalize the basic account as follows:

- P1: A proposition *p* is true iff there exists a fact *F* such that *p* corresponds to *F* and *F* obtains.
- P2: Facts are structured complexes consisting of objects instantiating properties or standing in relations.
- P3: The fact that the cat is on the mat consists of the cat, the mat, and the relation of being-on, combined in the appropriate structure.
- P4: This fact obtains (the cat is, in fact, on the mat).
- C: Therefore, "the cat is on the mat" is true.

Let F(a, R, b) denote the fact that *a* stands in relation *R* to *b*. Then: T(⌜aRb⌝) ↔ Obtains(F(a, R, b)).

Neat — but several difficulties bite immediately. The first is what we can call the *reference problem*. What makes a proposition correspond to one fact rather than another? Davidson pressed this point sharply: facts are just true propositions in disguise — the fact that the cat is on the mat is individuated by the proposition "the cat is on the mat." If so, the correspondence relation is circular: what makes *p* correspond to *F* is that *F* is the fact described by *p*, which presupposes that *p* is true.

Second, consider *negative facts*. If "the cat is not on the mat" is true, what fact does it correspond to? The absence of the cat from the mat? Russell at various points accepted negative facts — a position he described at the 1918 lectures as forced on him despite his discomfort, and which audiences received "with a sort of feeling that such things are monstrous." Others propose that negative truths are made true by the totality of positive facts. Armstrong's totality state of affairs is one version: the world contains a higher-order fact that all actual facts are all there are, which rules out the cat's being on the mat when it is not.

Third, *molecular facts*. What corresponds to "either it is raining or the sun is shining"? There seems to be no single disjunctive fact in reality. The correspondence theorist must either accept complex facts, analyze molecular truths in terms of atomic ones, or concede that correspondence applies only to a basic class of truths. Wittgenstein tried to dissolve logical facts by treating logical truths as tautologies — true in virtue of logical form rather than in virtue of any fact — but this leaves the problem of molecular empirical truths unresolved.

**Davidson's slingshot**

Donald Davidson, following Frege and Church, developed a powerful challenge to the entire enterprise. The "slingshot" argument purports to show that if we accept facts as entities to which propositions correspond, and if standard logical manipulations are valid, then all true propositions correspond to the same fact — the Great Fact. The argument uses intersubstitution of co-extensive descriptions and logically equivalent sentences. If "the cat is on the mat" and "the morning star is the evening star" are both true, and if co-extensional terms can be substituted salva veritate, then any two true sentences can be transformed into each other by a chain of truth-preserving substitutions — suggesting that all truths "correspond to" one undifferentiated entity.

Defenders of correspondence (Barwise and Perry, Mulligan et al.) reject one of the slingshot's premises, typically by insisting that facts are individuated intensionally, not extensionally, so that co-extensional terms cannot be freely substituted *salva facto*. The debate turns on whether facts are fine-grained enough to do the work the theory assigns them. Barwise and Perry's situation semantics offers one escape: situations are partial information states — not complete possible worlds but "chunks" of reality — individuated by their partial content. A situation makes "the cat is on the mat" true without being the same situation as the one that makes "the sun is a star" true. This allows correspondence without the slingshot collapse, but it requires a rich ontology of partial states of reality.

The individuation problem is closely related. How are facts individuated? If facts are individuated extensionally — by the objects they involve — then all truths correspond to the same fact. If facts are individuated intensionally — by the propositions that describe them — then facts are individuated by propositions, which makes the correspondence relation circular. Fine-grained situation semantics is one escape from this dilemma, but not the only one.

**Escape routes and their costs**

These difficulties have led some philosophers to prefer truthmaking accounts that do not invoke facts as structured entities. The truthmaker theorist (Armstrong, Lowe, Rodriguez-Pereyra) distinguishes the truthmaker relation from the correspondence relation: a truthmaker T makes proposition *p* true iff T's existence necessitates *p*'s truth (T ⊩ *p*), without requiring structural isomorphism. On this view, the state of affairs of the cat's being on the mat is a truthmaker — but so might be the cat itself for the proposition "the cat exists" — and properties or tropes might serve as truthmakers without there being "facts" in the Russell-Wittgenstein sense.

The deflationist response (Quine, Horwich) is more radical: deny that facts do any explanatory work at all. "'The cat is on the mat' is true" says no more than "the cat is on the mat" — invoking facts adds nothing except an apparent ontological commitment that cannot be cashed out. The T-schema T(⌜p⌝) ↔ *p* generates an implicit definition of truth that does not require facts or correspondence as further ontological posits.

Lynch's truth pluralism (*True to Life*, 2004) offers yet another response: facts as truthmakers are appropriate for some domains — science, history — but not others. In scientific domains, there are mind-independent facts, and correspondence is the right account of truth. In ethical or mathematical domains, a different truth-property may be more appropriate. The facts-based conception of correspondence is retained where it has the most philosophical justification.

Consider the proposition "the electron has negative charge." Under the correspondence theory, this is true because it corresponds to the fact that electrons instantiate the property of having charge −1e. The fact is structured: the electron kind instantiates HAVING-NEGATIVE-CHARGE. Kripke and Putnam argued that "electron" is a rigid designator and having charge −1e is an essential property of electrons — so the fact is not contingent but necessary. The truthmaker for "electrons have charge" is, on this reading, the essence of the electron kind, not a contingent state of affairs. This connects the facts-based correspondence theory to the metaphysics of natural kinds in ways that both enrich and complicate it.

Formal semantics — Montague grammar, possible-worlds semantics — provides one way to specify truth conditions without explicitly invoking facts as entities. In a Montague grammar, the semantic value of a sentence is a function from possible worlds to truth values. "The cat is on the mat" is true at a world *w* iff the individual denoted by "the cat" stands in the on-the-mat relation to the individual denoted by "the mat" at *w*. No facts are explicitly mentioned. Whether models in the formal-semantic sense correspond to mind-independent facts is a further, metaphysical question — and truthmaker theory re-enters as the metaphysical complement: the formal semanticist gives the truth condition; the truthmaker theorist asks what entity in the actual world grounds the satisfaction of that condition.
