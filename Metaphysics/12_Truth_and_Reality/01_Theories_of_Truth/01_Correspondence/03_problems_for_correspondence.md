# Problems for Correspondence

The intuitive appeal of the correspondence theory is real, and something like it underlies most ordinary thinking about truth. But when we try to give it philosophical precision, a cluster of serious objections emerges — objections that have driven many philosophers toward deflationism or pragmatism. They target different components of the theory: its ontology of facts, its account of the correspondence relation, and its scope.

**Negative truths**

Start with a case that looks deceptively easy: "Sherlock Holmes does not live in Edinburgh." It is true. What fact does it correspond to? The non-existence of Holmes in Edinburgh? But absences, unlike presences, are not among the positive structural features of reality — they are not things we can point to, weigh, or locate. Russell's acceptance of negative facts was widely lampooned; he acknowledged at the 1918 lectures that audiences reacted "with a sort of feeling that such things are monstrous." Yet his options were limited.

We can put this more sharply:

- P1: The correspondence theory requires that every true proposition correspond to a fact.
- P2: "There are no dragons" is true.
- P3: If "there are no dragons" corresponds to a fact, there must be a fact whose obtaining constitutes the non-existence of dragons.
- P4: Negative facts (non-existence facts) are not among the positive structural features of reality.
- C: Either the correspondence theory is incomplete, or it must accept negative facts at a significant ontological cost.

Armstrong's response is the totality state of affairs: the world contains a higher-order fact that the total inventory of positive facts is exhaustive — that there is nothing beyond the actual facts. Negative truths are grounded in this totality fact together with the positive facts. The problem is that the totality fact is itself a truth, which would seem to require its own truthmaker — and the regress threatens.

**The correspondence relation and the regress**

This regress has a more general form. If "P corresponds to F" is itself a truth, does it correspond to a meta-fact? If so, "P corresponds to F corresponds to G" corresponds to some further fact H — and so on. The deflationist response is to treat the regress as a reductio: "corresponds to" is doing no real work, and truth is just a predicate we apply when we assert propositions. Horwich (1990) makes this point explicit: the T-schema T(⌜p⌝) ↔ p generates an implicit definition of truth that does not require facts or correspondence as further ontological posits.

Specifying the correspondence relation without circularity is itself a formidable task. The theory must specify (a) what propositions are, (b) what facts are, and (c) what correspondence is — each in non-circular terms. Propositions are contested entities: are they structured abstract objects, sets of possible worlds, sentence-meanings, or something else? Facts, as we have seen, may just be true propositions in disguise. And the correspondence relation typically receives only a gestural characterization — "matches," "pictures," "represents" — that falls short of genuine philosophical analysis.

**Abstract and mathematical truth**

"7 is prime" appears to be true. Under the correspondence theory, it must correspond to a mathematical fact. But mathematical facts, if they exist, are abstract and causally inert. What is the correspondence relation between a proposition and an abstract mathematical structure? The theory seems best suited to concrete empirical claims, and requires supplementation or modification for abstract domains — logical truths, modal truths, aesthetic truths. The same worry arises for each, with different details.

**The identity theory**

F. H. Bradley and more recently Jennifer Hornsby have argued that the correspondence relation collapses into identity: a true proposition just *is* the fact it supposedly corresponds to. Hornsby (1997) puts the point sharply: the fact that Fido is a dog and the true thought that Fido is a dog have the same content. Their identity eliminates the need for a correspondence relation while preserving the intuition that truth is tied to reality. This is not refutation so much as dissolution: if the theory reduces to the identity theory, it tells us something correct — true thoughts and the facts they express have the same content — but it no longer illuminates the correspondence relation, since there is no longer a relation but an identity.

**Davidson's challenge**

Donald Davidson, though sympathetic to a form of correspondence intuition, argued in "True to the Facts" (1969) that no coherent account of facts can sustain the correspondence theory. His slingshot argument — which we examined in the previous file — purports to show that all truths "correspond to" the same fact, the Great Fact, if we apply standard logical manipulations to the correspondence relation. The circularity problem reinforces this: facts are individuated by the propositions they make true, which makes the correspondence relation circular. Davidson's own alternative — a Tarskian truth theory — characterizes truth by the totality of T-sentences, grounding semantic content in the structure of a compositional truth theory rather than in a correspondence relation with facts.

**Wright's pluralism as a response**

Crispin Wright's *Truth and Objectivity* (1992) offers a pluralist response to these difficulties. Rather than seeking a single theory of truth across all domains, Wright identifies a minimal truth predicate (satisfying the T-schema, the Equivalence Schema, the norm of Correspondence, and the principle of Embedding) and then asks whether different domains exhibit Cognitive Command (all disagreement is in principle traceable to cognitive divergence) and Width of Cosmological Role (the domain's truths play a substantive explanatory role in our account of the world). Science exhibits full-blown correspondence; aesthetic discourse may not. Wright's pluralism partially rehabilitates correspondence for the domains where it has the most plausibility while conceding that it is not a universal theory.

The key distinctions in the correspondence debate can be captured formally:

- Naive correspondence: T(⌜p⌝) ↔ ∃F(Corresponds(⌜p⌝, F) ∧ Obtains(F))
- Truthmaker version: T(⌜p⌝) ↔ ∃T(T ⊩ p)   [where T ⊩ p iff □(T exists → p)]
- Tarski's schema: T(⌜p⌝) ↔ p   [the deflationary reading]
- Davidson's no-facts version: truth is characterized by the totality of T-sentences, which determine the semantic content of the object language via radical interpretation

Notice what separates these positions. The correspondence theorist's distinctive commitment is to the first: facts are genuine entities, correspondence is a genuine relation, and truth is explained in terms of both. The truthmaker version weakens the structural isomorphism requirement while preserving the ontological commitment to worldly truth-grounders. Deflationism eliminates facts and correspondence relations altogether. Davidson's approach eliminates facts but preserves a substantive notion of truth.

Consider "Holmes did not live in Edinburgh." Under a naive correspondence theory, this negative truth about a fictional character requires a negative fact. The analysis runs: "Holmes" fails to refer to any actual person; the proposition asserts something about a non-existent entity; whatever makes it true must be something about the actual absence of Holmes in Edinburgh. Armstrong would say the totality state of affairs — the fact that the world contains only the actual historical persons and no Holmesian one — is the truthmaker. But this example shows how the problem of negative truths is entangled with fictional reference and negative existentials in a way that makes it especially recalcitrant.

These problems have not killed correspondence theory — it retains serious defenders who think the problems can be managed — but they explain why alternatives continue to attract adherents. The contemporary consensus, insofar as there is one, is that naively formulated correspondence theory is untenable: the problems of facts, correspondence, and regress are decisive against it as a complete theory. But the correspondence intuition survives in truthmaker theory and in the realist strand of Davidsonian semantics. Philosophers like Armstrong, Lowe, and Rodriguez-Pereyra have rehabilitated the correspondence insight without the full apparatus of Russellian facts. The debate has shifted from "correspondence or not?" to "what kind of worldly grounding does truth require, and what entities provide it?"
