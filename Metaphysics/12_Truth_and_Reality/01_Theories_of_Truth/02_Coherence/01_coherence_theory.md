# The Coherence Theory of Truth

We never compare our beliefs to bare, unmediated reality. When we verify a claim, we do so against a background of other claims — theories, observations, inferential norms. There is no "view from nowhere" from which to perform the comparison that correspondence theory requires. This epistemological observation is the starting point for coherentism: if what we can actually assess is the internal fit of our beliefs with one another, then perhaps this is what truth itself consists in. A proposition is true if and only if it coheres with a specified system of beliefs.

Coherence theorists typically specify coherence in terms of consistency and mutual support: a proposition is true if it is consistent with, and ideally implied or explained by, a comprehensive system of other beliefs. The "system" is variously characterized as the totality of currently held beliefs, an ideally complete and rationally ordered belief system, or the beliefs of an idealized inquirer at the limit of inquiry. The formal core: *p* coheres with *S* iff (i) *p* is consistent with *S*, (ii) S ∪ {p} is more unified than *S* alone, and (iii) *p* follows from or is supported by other members of *S*. Coherence so defined admits of degrees, which suggests that truth, on this theory, admits of degrees — a consequence that some coherentists accept and others find troubling.

We can state the core argument as follows:

- P1: A proposition *p* is true iff *p* coheres with the specified belief system *S*.
- P2: Coherence is a relation involving consistency, mutual entailment, and explanatory integration.
- P3: The proposition "water boils at 100°C at sea level" coheres with physics, chemistry, and the broader scientific system.
- C: Therefore, "water boils at 100°C at sea level" is true.

**Idealist roots and epistemological appeal**

The view has significant historical associations with British Idealism. Bradley, Bosanquet, and Joachim developed coherentism in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as part of a broader idealist metaphysics according to which reality is rational and mind-constituted. If reality is rational through and through, then truth — conformity with reality — must be rational coherence. Harold Joachim's *The Nature of Truth* (1906) is the locus classicus: the coherence of a proposition with a systematic whole of knowledge is not merely evidence for its truth but constitutive of it. A completely coherent system would be absolutely true; partial coherence yields partial or approximate truth.

The appeal of coherentism is partly epistemological and partly metaphysical, and these motivations are worth separating. The epistemological motivation, which does not require full idealist metaphysics, runs through Neurath's "boat" metaphor: we cannot step outside our system of beliefs to compare it with bare reality; we must rebuild the boat plank by plank from within. Quine's holism inherits this insight: our beliefs face experience "as a corporate body," not proposition by proposition, so that revision is always a matter of adjusting the system. BonJour (1985) developed a coherentist theory of epistemic justification on similar grounds: a belief is justified when it is part of a coherent system, and this is what we can actually assess in practice.

The metaphysical motivation goes further: if our access to truth is always mediated by our conceptual schemes and background theories, perhaps truth itself is constituted by those relational facts rather than by some unverifiable mind-world match. This is the move that opponents find most contentious.

**Contemporary coherentism and deflationism**

Contemporary coherentism is most prominent in epistemology — coherentist theories of justification — rather than in truth theory proper. Philosophers like Keith Lehrer have developed coherentist accounts of knowledge and rational belief. The metaphysical version — truth as coherence with a belief system — remains associated primarily with idealist traditions.

Lynch's pluralism about truth (*True to Life*, 2004) suggests that coherence may be the right truth-property for some domains — mathematics, perhaps ethics — while correspondence is right for others, reviving coherentism in a more modest form. Lynch's "alethic functionalism" holds that truth is the property that plays the truth-role: the norm of assertion, the aim of inquiry, the explainer of why true beliefs guide action. Different domains realize this role differently, and coherence is one legitimate realization.

Deflationism and coherentism agree that there is no "view from nowhere" from which to compare propositions with unmediated reality. But they disagree about what follows. The deflationist concludes that truth is merely a device of endorsement, adding nothing beyond the T-schema. The coherentist concludes that truth is a genuine property — the property of coherence — but one internal to the system of beliefs rather than relational between beliefs and an external world.

Consider the geological claim "the Earth is approximately 4.5 billion years old." Under the coherence theory, this is true because it coheres with a vast, interconnected system: radiometric dating methods, nuclear physics, stellar evolution theory, plate tectonics, the fossil record, and the observed abundance of heavy elements in the solar system. We verify geological age claims not by "looking" at the Earth's formation directly — impossible — but by seeing how our dating methods cohere with the rest of physics and chemistry. The coherentist holds that this coherence is what makes the claim true, not a direct comparison to a mind-independent past event. The correspondence theorist responds: the coherence of our evidence is evidence for a correspondence that holds independently. The Earth is 4.5 billion years old regardless of our belief system; coherence is a reliable indicator of this correspondence, not its constitution.

Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) offers an implicitly coherentist picture of scientific truth. Within a scientific paradigm, truth is determined by coherence with the paradigm's theoretical commitments, methodological norms, and exemplary problem-solutions. Anomalies that do not cohere are initially accommodated; only when they accumulate does a paradigm shift occur. Kuhn's "incommensurability" thesis — that different paradigms are not comparable from a neutral standpoint — takes the coherentist implication farthest: truth within a paradigm is coherence with the paradigm's standards; across paradigms, truth cannot be univocally defined.

The coherence theory thus survives not as a complete account of truth but as a partial account in certain domains — one component of a pluralist picture that does justice to the variety of human cognitive practice. The historical trajectory from Bradley's Absolute to contemporary pluralism illustrates both the problems with maximalist coherentism and the residual appeal of the core coherentist insight: truth is always truth-within-a-system, and a system's internal relations are philosophically significant in ways that pure correspondence theory cannot fully capture.
