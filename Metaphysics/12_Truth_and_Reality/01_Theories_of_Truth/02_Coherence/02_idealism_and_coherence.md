# Idealism and Coherence

Understanding the alliance between the coherence theory of truth and idealist metaphysics is essential for grasping both the appeal and the limits of coherentism. British Idealism — dominant in British philosophy from roughly 1870 to 1920 — held that reality is fundamentally mental or spiritual in character: the world as it is "in itself" is constituted by thought or experience, not something external and independent that thought merely represents. For such a view, the correspondence theory faces an insuperable problem. It posits a mind-independent reality to which our beliefs must correspond, but the nature of this reality can only be accessed through further thought. Any attempt to compare our beliefs to "bare reality" is itself a cognitive act, mediated by concepts.

**The Kantian background**

This is the lesson Kant drew in the *Critique of Pure Reason*: we can never step outside our cognitive framework to inspect reality as it is in itself. The categories of the understanding — causality, substance, quantity — and the forms of intuition — space, time — structure experience from the inside. The "thing in itself" (*Ding an sich*) is the thought-independent residue that thought cannot reach.

Kant himself did not draw the coherentist conclusion. He retained a notion of empirical truth as the agreement of cognition with its object, within the domain of possible experience. But his successors — Fichte, Schelling, Hegel — radicalized the Kantian position by arguing that the *Ding an sich* is an empty, incoherent notion that should be eliminated. If thought constitutes its object through and through, then reality is rational through and through, and truth is nothing but the internal rational structure of thought.

**Bradley's absolute coherentism**

F. H. Bradley's *Appearance and Reality* (1893) articulates this most forcefully. For Bradley, the Absolute — reality as it truly is — is a completely unified, coherent whole. Ordinary objects, causal relations, space and time are all "appearances": real as appearances, but not fully real, because they admit of internal contradictions when examined philosophically. The Absolute is what remains when all contradictions are reconciled — a seamless, unified, rational whole.

This metaphysics had direct implications for truth. If reality is rational through and through, then truth — correspondence with reality — must be understood as coherence with the rational structure of the Absolute. Partial truths — scientific laws, common-sense claims — are approximately true, participating in the Absolute's coherence to different degrees. Bradley's "degree of truth" doctrine follows: truth is not binary but scalar. The degree of truth of *p* in system *S* can be represented as T_degree(p, S) = f(C(p, S)), where C(p, S) is the coherence measure — a value between 0 and 1 tracking consistency, mutual support, and explanatory integration. Full truth corresponds to C = 1; no finite proposition achieves this because all finite propositions introduce the distinctions (subject-predicate, part-whole, particular-universal) that the Absolute transcends.

We can formalize Bradley's position:

- P1: Reality (the Absolute) is a fully unified, rational, coherent whole.
- P2: Truth is conformity with reality.
- P3: Conformity with a fully unified, rational, coherent whole is nothing other than coherence.
- C: Therefore, truth is coherence with the Absolute.

The weaker version, which does not require the Absolute but only the epistemological point:

- P1: We can never step outside our conceptual scheme to compare beliefs directly with unmediated reality.
- P2: The only test available to us for evaluating beliefs is their coherence with other beliefs.
- P3: If the only accessible test of truth is coherence, truth is best understood as coherence.
- C: Therefore, truth is coherence.

**The input objection and its handling**

The idealist-coherentist view faces the obvious objection pressed by Russell and Moore: it collapses the distinction between what we believe and what is true. A paranoid delusion can be internally coherent; does that make it true? Idealists typically respond that the relevant belief system must be idealized — not the actual beliefs of any thinker, but the beliefs that would be held by an ideally rational inquirer at the limit of inquiry. This moves coherentism closer to Peirce's pragmatist conception of truth and partly defuses the objection, at the cost of making the "system" less concrete. Critics ask: what determines what the idealized system would contain? If the answer is "whatever beliefs correctly represent reality," then the coherentist is helping herself to a prior notion of truth that undermines the coherentist analysis.

G. E. Moore's "Refutation of Idealism" (1903) challenged the Berkeleyan premise *esse est percipi* by distinguishing the act of perception from its object. When I perceive blue, there is both the act of perceiving and the blue that is perceived; these are distinct. Moore's argument reinstates the gap between thought and reality that idealists sought to close. Bradley can acknowledge the distinction between act and object at the level of appearance, while insisting that at the level of the Absolute, the distinction dissolves. The analytic tradition largely accepted Moore's refutation as decisive; the continental tradition developed more nuanced accounts that do not obviously fall to Moore's argument.

**Hegel's dynamic coherentism**

Hegel's *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) and *Science of Logic* (1812–1816) developed the idealist-coherentist framework beyond Bradley. For Hegel, the Absolute is not a static rational whole but a dynamic process: the self-unfolding of Spirit (*Geist*) through the history of thought and culture. Truth is not a fixed system but the movement of the concept through its determinations — partial truths are superseded (*aufgehoben*) as the concept develops. "The whole is the true" (*das Wahre ist das Ganze*) means that no partial truth is complete; truth is the entire logical system in its self-articulation.

This dynamic coherentism generates a distinctive account of error: error is not mere falsity — correspondence to a non-existent fact — but a one-sided, incomplete perspective that has not yet been integrated into the developing Whole. Scientific theories are not simply wrong or right; they are more or less adequate expressions of the concept at a particular stage of inquiry.

Josiah Royce (*The World and the Individual*, 1900) developed a version of Absolute Idealism in the American context that explicitly linked coherence, truth, and community. For Royce, the Absolute is an all-inclusive knower — God — who grasps all truths in a single act of infinite cognition. Truth for finite knowers is partial coherence with this infinite system. The community of interpretation is the medium through which finite minds approach the Absolute — a position that anticipates Peirce's communal account of truth and Brandom's social inferentialism.

**The idealist legacy in analytic philosophy**

The idealist-coherentist tradition left important legacies even in the tradition that rejected it. The coherence theory of justification (BonJour, Lehrer) retains the epistemological core without the idealist metaphysics. Quinean holism — the view that beliefs face the tribunal of experience as a web — inherits the coherentist intuition about the interconnection of beliefs. The Sellarsian critique of the "myth of the given" — the idea that there are brute, uninterpreted, epistemically foundational data — echoes the idealist rejection of any non-conceptual contact with bare reality.

Green's critique of Hume anticipates themes later developed by Sellars. Green argued that Hume's associationist account of mind — the view that mental life consists of sequences of impressions and ideas governed by habits of association — cannot account for the unity of experience. The rational unity of experience requires something that is not itself a bundle — a self-conscious principle that is more than any sequence of empirical states. This connects to idealism's broader critique of naturalism: naturalism tries to explain mind and knowledge in terms of sequences of physical or psychological events, but no such sequence can generate the rational unity and normativity that characterize genuine knowledge.
