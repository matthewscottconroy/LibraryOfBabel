# Objections to Coherence Theory

The coherence theory of truth faces several serious objections that have prevented it from gaining wide acceptance in contemporary analytic philosophy. They attack the theory at multiple levels: its capacity to connect truth to the world, its ability to select among competing systems, and its internal consistency. Understanding these objections also helps explain why most philosophers dissatisfied with correspondence turn to deflationism rather than coherentism.

**The input objection**

The most powerful objection is that coherence theory severs the connection between truth and the world. If truth consists in coherence with a belief system, then any internally consistent fiction is "true." A well-constructed fantasy novel is internally coherent. Does that make its claims true?

We can put this more precisely:

- P1: On the coherence theory, *p* is true iff *p* coheres with system *S*.
- P2: The propositions in Tolkien's *The Lord of the Rings* form a highly coherent system *S\**.
- P3: "Gandalf is a wizard" coheres with *S\**.
- C1: By the coherence theory, "Gandalf is a wizard" is true.
- C2: But "Gandalf is a wizard" is not true.
- C3: Therefore, the coherence theory yields false results.

The coherentist's standard reply is that *S* must be the actual belief system of the actual community of inquirers, which includes empirical inputs. Fictional propositions are true relative to the fictional system but not relative to the actual-world system. The objector responds: this reply presupposes that empirical inputs have a special epistemic status — that perceptions which conflict with the fictional system "win" — and this special status seems to require that perception tracks mind-independent reality, reinstating correspondence by the back door.

**The plurality objection**

There can be multiple, mutually incompatible but internally coherent systems of belief. Ptolemaic astronomy and Copernican astronomy may each have been internally coherent at some historical stage; they cannot both be true. If truth is coherence, how do we choose between them? The correspondence theorist has a ready answer: only one system corresponds to the astronomical facts. The coherentist must invoke some further criterion — simplicity, comprehensiveness, predictive success — but these seem to be epistemic virtues rather than truth-constitutive properties.

BonJour (1985) acknowledged that coherentism requires an account of what makes one coherent system better than another without invoking correspondence to mind-independent facts. His response was to invoke "observation requirements" — a coherent system must include a subclass of cognitively spontaneous beliefs (perceptual beliefs that arise without inference) that are treated as inputs. But this response makes coherentism a hybrid view, dependent on a non-coherentist account of why those spontaneous beliefs have authority.

Nicholas Rescher (*The Coherence Theory of Truth*, 1973) developed a sophisticated neo-coherentist response. Coherence, on his account, is not about consistency among all propositions but about selecting the most defensible subset of a body of candidate truths. When faced with a set of propositions that are collectively inconsistent, we accept the maximally coherent subset that includes the strongest, most specific, and most confirmed propositions. This gives priority to observational inputs without presupposing they "correspond" to an independent reality. The response to the plurality objection: the selection criterion is maximization of "collective strength" — Copernican astronomy won because it included more specific predictions, more independently confirmed posits, and ultimately greater explanatory power. Ptolemaic astronomy was coherent but not maximally so.

**The regress objection**

Coherence is a relation among propositions. For "*P* is true," the coherence theory says this is true if *P* coheres with the system *S*. But "*P* coheres with *S*" is itself a proposition — is it true? Only if it coheres with some further system *S'*. And so on. Either the regress terminates in something non-coherentist (a brute fact about coherence relations) or it generates an infinite regress, undermining the theory's claim to completeness.

One response: coherence is a property of systems, not of individual propositions. The regress does not arise because we are not evaluating propositions one by one but evaluating whether a whole system is coherent. This response faces a further difficulty: what makes a whole system coherent? The answer — the mutual support of its members — seems to use "support" in ways that presuppose a notion of truth (if *p* supports *q*, then the truth of *p* increases the probability of *q*), which again threatens circularity.

**Bradley's internal criticism**

F. H. Bradley himself came to see a difficulty that is worth dwelling on. The Absolute, as a completely coherent system, would include all truths — and including all truths seems to require corresponding to reality after all. Coherence at its limit collapses into correspondence. Bradley's *Essays on Truth and Reality* (1914) acknowledged that "coherence" itself is only intelligible against a background of something that "is" or "holds" — which is essentially a correspondence notion. This suggests that coherentism and correspondence may be more complementary than rival.

This internal critique is powerful precisely because it comes from the theory's most distinguished defender. If coherence at its limit collapses into correspondence, then coherentism as a *replacement* for correspondence fails — it can at best be a criterion of, or evidence for, truth, not its constitution.

**Wright's response**

Crispin Wright (1992) proposes that the objections to coherentism (and to correspondence) point toward a minimal account of truth. The T-schema T(⌜p⌝) ↔ p is analytically true and captures everything essential; the dispute between coherentists and correspondence theorists is a dispute about what *further* properties truth has in particular domains. For domains where inquiry is governed by Cognitive Command — where all disagreements are in principle resolvable by better evidence — something like correspondence obtains. For domains where this does not hold, a more pluralist account may be appropriate. Wright's pluralism vindicates a restricted coherentism without the problematic claim that coherence is the nature of truth universally.

An important observation connects coherentism to the formal apparatus: the T-schema T(⌜p⌝) ↔ p is compatible with coherentism. The biconditional does not specify what makes *p* true; it specifies the formal relationship between truth-ascription and assertion. A coherentist can accept the T-schema while maintaining that what it is for *p* to be true is for *p* to cohere with the relevant system. The debate between coherentism and correspondence is a debate about what fills in the left-hand side — what the property of truth consists in — not about whether the T-schema holds.

Consider the historical scientific claim "phlogiston was released during combustion." This was part of a highly coherent 18th-century chemical system: it cohered with observations of combustion, rusting, respiration, and the properties of "dephlogisticated air." On the coherence theory, it was — at that time — approximately true. Lavoisier's new chemistry broke this coherence by introducing oxygen and providing a more comprehensive and internally consistent system. On the coherence theory, the phlogiston claim became false when the broader chemical system changed. The correspondence theorist says it was always false; there was never any phlogiston. The coherentist says "always false" presupposes a mind-independent fact (the absence of phlogiston) that cannot be accessed without appealing to our current best theory — which is itself a coherent system. The debate is ultimately about whether "false" means "not cohering with any possible idealized system" or "not corresponding to a mind-independent state of affairs." Neither side can claim an easy victory.
