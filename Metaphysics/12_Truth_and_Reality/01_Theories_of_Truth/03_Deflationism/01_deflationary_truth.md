# Deflationary Truth

When I say "it is true that snow is white," am I saying anything more than "snow is white"? The deflationist's answer is: not really. Deflationism about truth is the family of views that holds that "truth" does not name a substantive property of propositions or beliefs. There is no deep metaphysical relation — whether correspondence or coherence — that underlies true attributions. Saying "that is true" does little more than express agreement with the proposition in question, or "disquote" it, or generalize over propositions.

**The T-schema as the core**

The starting point is a logical observation: the equivalence schema, or "T-schema," captures everything essential about truth. For any proposition *P*, it is true that *P* if and only if *P*. "It is true that snow is white" is equivalent to "snow is white." The truth predicate adds no semantic content; it is a device for endorsing or quantifying over propositions rather than a predicate that attributes a property.

Tarski's formal version: T(⌜p⌝) ↔ p, where ⌜p⌝ is the name (quotation) of the sentence *p*. Every instance of this schema is analytic — true in virtue of the meaning of "true." Tarski's material adequacy condition requires that any adequate definition of truth entail all T-schema instances. Deflationists read this as showing that truth is fully characterized by the T-schema: there is nothing more to say.

We can put the deflationist argument as follows:

- P1: The T-schema T(⌜p⌝) ↔ p is analytic and exhausts the meaning of "true."
- P2: Any substantive account of truth (correspondence, coherence) must explain more than the T-schema explains.
- P3: Everything we need "true" to do — generalization, endorsement, blind quotation — can be done by the T-schema alone.
- C: There is no substantive property of truth for a theory to explain; deflationism is correct.

The deflationary move has a clean philosophical payoff: it dissolves many traditional puzzles about truth. The correspondence theorist agonizes over what facts are and how propositions correspond to them; the deflationist says these questions rest on a mistake. "Snow is white" is made true by snow being white, not by some further relation between the proposition and a fact-about-snow.

**The varieties of deflation**

Deflationism has several variants, distinguished by what they say the truth predicate does.

*Redundancy theory* (Frege and Ramsey): "It is true that P" is simply redundant — it means the same as P. Frank Ramsey wrote in 1927: "It is true that Caesar was murdered" means no more than "Caesar was murdered." On this view, "true" is logically idle. The objection: "true" cannot be merely redundant, because it appears in sentences where it cannot simply be dropped: "Everything she said is true" cannot be rendered without truth-talk if we don't know what she said.

*Disquotationalism* (Quine, Leeds): "True" is a device for semantic ascent — for talking about sentences rather than directly about the world. "It is true that snow is white" ascends to the meta-level and then descends again. The function of truth-talk is primarily to generalize and to enable "blind endorsement": "everything Newton said about motion is true."

*Minimalism* (Paul Horwich, *Truth*, 1990): the T-schema for propositions is all we need to understand truth; there is no further analysis. Each proposition has a trivial "propositional truth condition" — the proposition that snow is white is true iff snow is white — and these collectively define what truth is. Horwich calls this "the minimal theory." Everything else philosophers have wanted to say about truth can, he argues, be derived from the minimal theory together with other relevant facts about belief, inquiry, and assertion.

*Prosententialism* (Grover, Camp, and Belnap, 1975): "true" functions as a prosentence-forming operator. Just as pronouns replace noun phrases ("it"), prosentences replace sentence-length expressions. "That's true" is a prosentence that inherits its content from the proposition it stands in for, much as "he" inherits its reference from an antecedent noun phrase.

What deflationists share is the denial that truth is a substantive, explanatory property — one that could serve as the basis for a theory of knowledge, reference, or meaning. We can rank the deflationary positions by how much they strip away:

- *Redundancy theory*: T(⌜p⌝) is literally synonymous with *p*; "true" is eliminable without loss.
- *Disquotationalism*: T(⌜p⌝) is governed by the T-schema, which exhausts its meaning; "true" is a device for semantic ascent and generalization.
- *Minimalism*: The minimal theory MT = {T(⌜p⌝) ↔ p : *p* a proposition} is the complete theory of truth. No substantive property is named.
- *Prosententialism*: "That's true" is a prosentence inheriting content from its linguistic antecedent; "true" is not a predicate at all.

**The deflationary critique of substantive theories**

A central deflationist motivation is what Quine called the "disquotational platitude" and Horwich extended into a critique of substantive theories: any substantive account of truth adds something beyond the T-schema that cannot be independently justified. If "snow is white" is true iff snow is white — the T-schema — and that is the complete account, then any further claim that truth consists in correspondence to the fact of snow's whiteness, or in coherence with our belief system, or in pragmatic cash value — is an unwarranted addition.

The deflationist's challenge to substantive theories: specify what the additional claim adds that is not already captured by the T-schema, and show that this addition is needed to explain something the T-schema alone cannot explain. Deflationists argue that no such explanation is forthcoming; substantive theories generate explanatory illusions rather than genuine explanations.

Lynch (*True to Life*, 2004) argues that deflationism is wrong because it cannot account for the role truth plays in explaining success (why true beliefs reliably guide action) and in characterizing inquiry (we aim at truth, not merely coherence or utility). Lynch defends pluralism: truth is a functional property, "playing the truth role," but this role is realized differently in different domains — by correspondence in empirical discourse, by superassertibility in moral discourse, and so on. This challenge to deflationism is examined further in the file on challenges to deflationism.

Under deflationism, "'Caesar crossed the Rubicon' is true" says exactly the same as "Caesar crossed the Rubicon." The truth predicate adds nothing. The question "what makes it true?" is answered simply by: Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon — not by a further fact or correspondence relation. The deflationist is not committed to denying that Caesar crossed the Rubicon; she is committed only to denying that this truth-making requires anything beyond what is already contained in the historical claim itself. Truth is not an additional layer of ontology; it is a logical device for talking about propositions economically.
