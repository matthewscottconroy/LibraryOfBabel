# Challenges to Deflationism

Deflationism about truth is widely influential in contemporary analytic philosophy, but it faces a battery of objections that its critics regard as serious — and that deflationists must address. The challenges come from several directions: the normativity of truth, its role in explanation, the Liar paradox, and the content of truth-generalizations.

**The normativity objection**

Truth is a norm of inquiry and assertion: we aim at truth, we criticize beliefs for being false, we defer to the true over the merely agreeable. If truth is just a logical device for endorsement or disquotation, what explains this normative role? The realist about truth can say: truth is a substantive property — correspondence with reality — and we value it because it makes our beliefs reliable guides to action. The deflationist must account for normativity without invoking a substantive truth property.

The argument more precisely:

- P1: Rational inquiry is governed by the norm: assert only what is true.
- P2: Norms require properties to underwrite them; if "true" names no substantive property, the norm has no metaphysical basis.
- P3: The deflationist holds that "true" names no substantive property.
- C: The deflationist cannot adequately explain the normativity of truth-directed inquiry.

Paul Horwich (1990, 2010) responds that the normativity of truth follows from the normativity of rational belief together with the T-schema. If I rationally ought to believe *p* when *p* is warranted by my evidence, and if "p is true iff p" is analytic, then I rationally ought to believe *p* only when *p* is true — which generates the norm "aim at truth" without invoking a substantive truth property. Critics find this derivation too quick: the T-schema does not itself explain *why* truth-conducive methods — logic, evidence-gathering, peer consultation — are appropriate methods, unless truth already has a substantial connection to the world.

Michael Lynch (*True to Life*, 2004) argues that this is where deflationism fails. A property that plays the role of a norm of inquiry must be such that it can explain why truth-conducive methods are reliable means to it. If truth is merely disquotational, there is no explanation of why methods that track the world are truth-conducive — the connection between inquiry and the world is left unexplained.

**The explanatory role objection**

Truth seems to figure in explaining why things work. "My belief that the train leaves at noon caused me to arrive on time because my belief was true." The correspondence theorist explains this by the fact that a true belief represents the world accurately, enabling reliable action. The deflationist, for whom "true" is just a disquotational device, seems unable to give this explanation.

The challenge is sharpest for the following inference pattern:

- P1: If a belief is true, then (in normal conditions) acting on it leads to success.
- P2: My belief that *p* is true.
- C: Acting on my belief that *p* leads to success.

For this to be an *explanation* of success, P1 must express a substantive connection between truth and the world. If truth is merely disquotational, P1 reduces to: "if *p*, then (in normal conditions) acting on the belief that *p* leads to success" — which is just a generalization about the causes of success, not an explanation in terms of truth. Some deflationists bite the bullet, denying that truth has a special explanatory role. Others (Field, 1994) argue that "is true" can figure in causal explanations without being explanatory *in virtue of* a substantive property, because the truth predicate generalizes over propositions whose specific content is what actually does the causal work.

**The Liar paradox**

The Liar sentence *L* ("This sentence is false," equivalently "¬T(⌜L⌝)") generates contradiction when we apply the T-schema: T(⌜L⌝) ↔ ¬T(⌜L⌝). This is a theorem of classical logic plus the unrestricted T-schema, which is the deflationist's central commitment. Any adequate theory of truth must handle the Liar. Most deflationist theories restrict the T-schema — by type-theoretic or hierarchical approaches following Tarski, by paraconsistent logic following Priest, or by truth-value gaps following Kripke.

The difficulty for deflationism specifically: the principled justification of any restriction on the T-schema seems to require a substantive account of what makes a T-sentence legitimate — why some propositions cannot serve as their own truth-conditions. This takes us beyond what the T-schema alone can deliver. Horwich acknowledges this and holds that the minimal theory needs to be supplemented by a theory of which propositions express concepts, which he takes to be a separate matter. Critics argue this concession significantly reduces the explanatory self-sufficiency of the minimal theory.

**The content objection**

If "P is true" just means P, then sentences like "Everything Aristotle said is true" would have to be unpacked as an infinite conjunction of claims. This seems tractable formally: ∀s(Aristotle-asserted(s) → T(s)). But the T-schema, applied instance by instance, reduces this to ∀s(Aristotle-asserted(s) → p_s), where p_s is the specific content of each claim *s*. The deeper worry: can deflationism account for the *semantic connection* between "true" and the world? If "Snow is white" is true iff snow is white, and snow is white because snow reflects most visible wavelengths, then there is a connection between the truth of the sentence and the physics of snow. The deflationist says this connection is just the composition of (i) "snow is white" is true iff snow is white, and (ii) snow is white. There is no additional "semantic" connection. Realists argue that step (ii) presupposes a relation between the sentence "snow is white" and actual snow — a reference relation — that is itself a substantive semantic fact not captured by the T-schema.

**Davidson's challenge**

Donald Davidson's later work ("The Structure and Content of Truth," 1990) drew a distinction between the *formal* and the *substantial* theory of truth. The formal theory — Tarski's recursive definition — tells us how truth distributes across complex sentences given the truth of atoms. The substantial theory must tell us *what* makes atomic sentences true. Davidson held that interpretation — radical interpretation, the project of simultaneously assigning meanings and beliefs — is the proper context for truth. A Tarskian truth theory for a language, when confirmed by radical interpretation, provides a substantial theory of truth in that language, without invoking "correspondence" or "facts" as further entities. This position is neither deflationist nor robust-realist; it occupies a middle ground that many find productive.

**Lynch's functionalist response**

Lynch argues that the challenges to deflationism motivate pluralism about truth rather than either full deflationism or robust realism. The truth predicate picks out a functional property — the property of "playing the truth role" — which is characterized by norms like: truth is the aim of inquiry, true belief is reliable in action, believing *p* to be true is warranted only when *p* satisfies appropriate epistemic conditions. The deflationist is right that the T-schema is part of what characterizes the truth role. But the T-schema alone does not explain why playing the truth role is what we aim at in inquiry, or why truth-conducive methods are appropriate methods. For that, we need to understand truth as a genuine property — not a trivial one — even if it is realized differently in different domains.

Blackburn's quasi-realism in metaethics (see Topic 04) is instructive for deflationism generally. Blackburn starts from an expressivist base and "earns the right" to talk of moral truths. This mirrors a move available to the deflationist: start from the view that "true" is an expressive or endorsing device, and then explain how it comes to behave like a predicate attributing a property, including entering into inferences, being governed by norms, and playing explanatory roles. Whether this quasi-realist strategy fully vindicates the truth predicate, or merely shows that we can get by with a deflationary surrogate, is a central open question.

Consider "'The Battle of Hastings occurred in 1066' is true." Under deflationism, this is equivalent to "The Battle of Hastings occurred in 1066." We need not posit any fact of the battle's occurrence beyond what the historical claim itself asserts. The realist asks: what is needed is an account of why the Battle of 1066 makes our assertion true *now* — a relation between past events and present linguistic acts that the T-schema does not supply. This is the reference relation (Kripke-Putnam causal chain) or the truthmaker relation (the historical event necessitates the truth of the proposition). Deflationism dissolves the question; realism answers it. Whether dissolution is the right response is what is at issue.
