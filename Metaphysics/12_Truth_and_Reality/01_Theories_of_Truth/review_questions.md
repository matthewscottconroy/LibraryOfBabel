# Review Questions — Theories of Truth

1. The correspondence theory insists on a worldly correlate for every true proposition, while the deflationist claims the T-schema says everything there is to say about truth. Both philosophers can accept "snow is white is true iff snow is white" — so what, precisely, is their disagreement? Is it a disagreement in ontology, in the role of explanation, or in something else?

2. Davidson's slingshot argument is supposed to show that all true propositions correspond to the same "Great Fact." Identify which premise defenders of correspondence most plausibly reject, and explain what commitment they must take on to block the argument. Does rejecting that premise simply push the problem back a level?

3. The coherence theory gains much of its appeal from the epistemological claim that we never compare beliefs to "bare reality." But the chapter distinguishes the epistemological motivation from the metaphysical one. Can someone accept the epistemological point — Neurath's boat — without accepting that truth itself is coherence? What additional step is required, and is it well-motivated?

4. Both the coherence theory and deflationism deny that truth requires a special mind-world correspondence relation. Yet they arrive at this from very different starting points and reach very different conclusions about what truth is. Trace exactly where the two views part ways, and evaluate which handles the normativity of truth-directed inquiry more convincingly.

5. Bradley's own internal criticism of coherentism holds that coherence at its limit collapses into correspondence. If this self-undermining objection has force, does it show that coherentism can be at most a criterion of truth rather than its nature? What would a committed coherentist have to say to resist this conclusion?

6. The chapter presents three variants of deflationism — redundancy theory, disquotationalism, and minimalism — that differ primarily over the truth-bearer and the scope of the T-schema. Disquotationalism restricts truth to sentences of one's own language; minimalism extends it to language-independent propositions. What philosophical price does each pay, and which price is more tolerable?

7. Lynch argues that deflationism cannot account for why true beliefs reliably guide successful action — that a merely disquotational truth predicate leaves the connection between inquiry and the world unexplained. Construct the best deflationary response to this objection, then evaluate whether it is adequate.

8. Wright's pluralism about truth is offered as a response to the failures of both correspondence and coherence theories. Consider whether pluralism avoids those failures or merely relocates them. In particular: if different domains have different truth properties, what unifies these different properties under the concept "truth" at all?
