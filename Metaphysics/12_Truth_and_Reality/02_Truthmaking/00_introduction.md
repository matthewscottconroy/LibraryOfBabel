# Truthmaking

Why is it true that snow is white? The obvious answer — because snow is white — seems like a tautology. But the truthmaking theorist turns this obvious answer into a substantive ontological thesis: there is something in the world — a truthmaker — whose existence necessitates the truth of the proposition "snow is white." Snow itself (a white thing), or the fact of snow's being white, or the state of affairs of snow's being white — these are candidates for the truthmaker of the proposition.

The truthmaker principle holds that every truth has a truthmaker: for every true proposition, there is something in the world whose existence necessitates that proposition's truth. This principle connects truth to ontology in a direct way: to ask what makes a proposition true is to ask what ontological commitment the truth of that proposition requires. Truthmaker theory is thus not merely an account of truth but a research program for ontology: by asking what makes various propositions true, we determine what must be in the ontological inventory.

Truthmaker maximalism — the view that every truth has a truthmaker — generates significant pressure on the ontology. What makes negative existentials true? "There are no unicorns" is true, but what makes it true? There is no unicorn to be the truthmaker; the absence of unicorns seems to be what is relevant, but absences are not obviously entities. What makes modal truths true? "It is possible that there are unicorns" is true, but if modal realism is false and there are no concrete possible worlds, what makes it true? The truthmaker for this proposition seems elusive.

This chapter develops truthmaker theory from its foundations, examines the case for and against maximalism, and explores the connections between truthmaking and the broader ontological commitments examined throughout this textbook. The relationship between truthmaking and grounding — two forms of metaphysical dependence — is a particularly fruitful area of investigation.
