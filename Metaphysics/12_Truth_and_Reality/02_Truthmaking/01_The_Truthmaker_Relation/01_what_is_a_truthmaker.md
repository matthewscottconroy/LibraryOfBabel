# What Is a Truthmaker?

Consider the proposition "the rose is red." It is true. But what makes it true? Not just in the thin sense given by the T-schema — "the rose is red" is true iff the rose is red — but in the deeper sense of what in the world *grounds* that truth. The deflationist says there is nothing more to say. But many philosophers are unsatisfied: it seems as though there ought to be something — the rose's redness, some feature of reality — in virtue of which the proposition holds. This is the intuition that truthmaker theory codifies.

The truthmaker principle, in its canonical form: for every truth *p*, there exists an entity T such that necessarily, if T exists, then *p* is true. This is written:

T ⊩ p iff □(T exists → T(p))

The relation ⊩ ("makes true") is thus a modal relation: a truthmaker *necessitates* its proposition. Truthmakers are typically facts, states of affairs, tropes, or events — concrete or abstract features of reality — rather than propositions themselves.

The intuition behind truthmaking is that truth is not "free-floating." If "the rose is red" is true, that truth is grounded in something: the rose's redness — perhaps a particular color trope instantiated by the rose. The rose's redness makes the proposition true; in a world where the rose lacks this property, the proposition is false.

**Truthmaking versus correspondence and deflationism**

We can sharpen what the theory says by contrast:

- P1: If a proposition *p* is true, there must be something in the world in virtue of which *p* is true.
- P2: Mere consistent assertibility or coherence with a belief system is not sufficient to ground truth — truth must be grounded in reality.
- P3: The entity in virtue of which *p* is true is the truthmaker for *p*.
- C: Every truth has a truthmaker — a worldly entity whose existence necessitates *p*'s truth.

Truthmaker theory is importantly different from both correspondence theory and deflationism. Unlike the correspondence theory, it does not require a structural "matching" between propositions and facts — a truthmaker simply needs to necessitate the proposition, however this is achieved. There need not be any structural isomorphism between the proposition "the rose is red" and the trope that is its truthmaker: the trope makes the proposition true by existing, not by "mirroring" the propositional structure. Unlike deflationism, truthmaker theory insists that truth is genuinely grounded in the world and that this grounding has metaphysical import. The T-schema does not capture the full story, because the right-hand side — *p* — itself requires grounding. The deflationist says "snow is white" is true because snow is white; the truthmaker theorist agrees but adds: and snow's being white is itself grounded in some entity (a state of affairs, a trope) whose existence makes it so. The "in virtue of" relation is ineliminable.

D. M. Armstrong (*Truth and Truthmakers*, 2004) has been the most systematic defender. Armstrong argues that states of affairs are the canonical truthmakers: the state of affairs of the cat's being on the mat exists just in case the cat is on the mat, and necessitates the truth of "the cat is on the mat." C. B. Martin, Barry Smith, E. J. Lowe, and Mulligan, Simons, and Smith (in their influential 1984 paper "Truth-Makers") have contributed significantly to the framework.

Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra's *Resemblance Nominalism* (2002) offers a precise formulation: T is a truthmaker of *p* iff T is a minimal entity whose existence necessitates *p*. "Minimal" excludes entities that necessitate *p* only because they contain as a part something sufficient for *p*. The minimality condition prevents trivial truthmakers: the Great Conjunction of all truths is not a truthmaker for "the cat is on the mat," because it contains far more than is needed.

**Ontological discipline**

One major implication of truthmaker theory is that it imposes ontological discipline. If we assert "electrons have spin," we are committed to there being a truthmaker — some entity whose existence makes this true. If we assert "torturing children is wrong," we are committed to there being a truthmaker for this moral claim — a moral fact, a natural fact that grounds the wrongness, or something else. This is one reason truthmaker theory is philosophically rich: it connects the metaphysics of truth to the ontology of particular domains and forces explicit decisions about what we believe to exist.

The supervenience connection is relevant here. If the moral supervenes on the natural — if there can be no moral difference without a natural difference — then moral truths have their truthmakers partly in natural states of affairs. The supervenience principle:

□(x)(M(x) → ∃φ (N(φ)(x) ∧ □(N(φ)(x) → M(x))))

where M is a moral property and N(φ) is some natural base. Truthmaker theory requires that the natural base be the truthmaker, or that some entity grounding the supervenience relation serves as the truthmaker for the moral truth.

Truthmaker theory also bears directly on the metaphysics of properties. If properties are universals — abstract, repeatable entities instantiated by many particulars — then states of affairs are natural truthmakers for predicative truths. If properties are tropes — concrete, unrepeatable particulars — then tropes are the truthmakers. If properties are merely sets of similar particulars (extreme nominalism), then predicative truths lack individual truthmakers. Armstrong uses truthmaker theory to argue for universals: only universals (together with particulars in states of affairs) can be truthmakers for predicative truths that hold across many particulars. The nominalist must find alternative truthmakers or abandon the truthmaker principle for such truths.

Ross Cameron (*The Moving Spotlight*, 2015; various papers) has developed a sparse truthmaker ontology in which only the fundamental, "ontologically basic" truths have truthmakers. Derived truths — truths that are metaphysically grounded in fundamental truths — do not need their own truthmakers; they inherit their truth from the fundamental truths that ground them. This connects truthmaker theory to the broader debate about grounding: the fundamental-derivative distinction in ontology maps onto the truthmaker-grounded distinction in truth theory. Cameron's view allows for an ontologically austere truthmaker theory that does not require a dedicated truthmaker for every truth, while still maintaining that truth is not "free-floating."

Consider the paradigm case in detail. "The rose is red" is a predicative proposition ascribing redness to a particular rose. Three truthmaker candidates: (A) Armstrong's state of affairs — the state of affairs [the rose's instantiating redness]. This entity exists exactly when the rose is red; its existence necessitates the proposition. The state of affairs contains the rose and the universal REDNESS as constituents, combined in the instantiation relation. (B) Martin's trope — the rose's-redness, a particular color trope that is a part of the rose. This trope exists exactly when the rose is red; its existence necessitates the proposition without requiring the universal REDNESS as a further entity. (C) The rose itself — but this fails because the rose exists in worlds where it is not red. The rose alone does not necessitate the proposition. The comparison shows that truthmaker theory imposes ontological decisions: accepting states of affairs commits us to universals and the instantiation relation; accepting tropes commits us to a different category of entity; accepting neither requires a different account of how particular truths are grounded.

The truthmaker principle faces its greatest challenge with negative truths ("there are no dragons"), universal generalizations ("all ravens are black"), and modal truths ("it is possible for gold to be liquid"). These cases are examined in the subsequent files. The challenge is that their truthmakers — if they have any — seem to be "absences," totalities, or merely possible entities, none of which sit easily in a positive, actual ontology.
