# Truthmaker Maximalism

Truthmaker maximalism is the thesis that every true proposition has a truthmaker — that every truth is made true by some entity in the world. This is the strongest form of truthmaker theory, and has been defended most systematically by D. M. Armstrong (*Truth and Truthmakers*, 2004) and, with some qualifications, by Barry Smith, E. J. Lowe, and others.

Formally: ∀p (T(p) → ∃T (T ⊩ p)). Every true proposition is entailed by the existence of some entity. Armstrong's motivation is blunt: "If a proposition is true, the world must be such as to make it true. This, I think, is a datum for the whole inquiry." Truthmaking without maximalism — allowing some truths to float free of any worldly entity — concedes too much to the deflationist. If some truths need no truthmakers, the maximalist argues, then the deflationist's position looks attractive: truth is just a predicate we apply to propositions without grounding in the world.

The motivation for maximalism is a principle of "ontological honesty": if something is true, the world must be such as to make it true; truth cannot outrun what exists. If "there are seven planets" is true, that truth is grounded in the seven planets themselves. If "electrons have charge" is true, the truthmaker is the fact that electrons instantiate the property of having charge.

Putting the argument schematically:

- P1 (Truthmaker Principle): For every truth *p*, there is an entity T whose existence necessitates *p*'s truth.
- P2 (Maximalism): The Truthmaker Principle applies to *every* truth without exception.
- P3: For any true proposition *p*, no matter how abstract or negative, the world must contain something in virtue of which *p* is true.
- C: The world contains not only the positive, concrete states of affairs corresponding to positive atomic truths, but also totality states of affairs, essential structures, or other entities sufficient to ground every truth.

**The case of positive contingent truths**

Maximalism is most plausible for positive contingent truths. "The cat is on the mat" is true, and its truthmaker is the state of affairs of the cat's being on the mat. Armstrong holds that states of affairs — not just particulars or universals separately — are the canonical truthmakers. The particular cat cannot alone make "the cat is on the mat" true, because the cat exists in possible worlds where it is not on the mat. The universal being-on-the-mat cannot alone make it true, because it exists in worlds where some other object is on the mat. Only the state of affairs — the cat's being on the mat — exists in exactly the worlds where the proposition is true.

**The totality state of affairs**

Maximalism faces its most serious challenges when applied to negative truths and universal generalizations. "There are no unicorns" is true, but what makes it true? There is no unicorn-free-zone entity; unicorns simply do not exist. Armstrong's response is the *totality state of affairs*: a further fact that the actual distribution of kinds is *all* the distribution there is — that the actual cats, dogs, electrons, and so on are *everything*. This totality fact is a higher-order state of affairs that makes negative existentials true by ruling out the existence of anything not in the actual totality.

The totality fact T_total has a distinctive logical structure: T_total obtains iff the world contains exactly the states of affairs S₁, S₂, ..., Sₙ and no others. It functions as a "stopper" that prevents the world from containing entities beyond the actual. Formally:

T_total ⊩ ¬∃x (Unicorn(x))

because T_total's existence entails that nothing beyond the actual totality exists, and unicorns are not in the actual totality. A similar structure handles universal generalizations: "All ravens are black" is made true by the individual facts about each actual raven together with the totality fact that closes off the possibility of non-black ravens.

The totality state of affairs is contested. Critics argue: (a) it is mysterious — a "higher-order fact" about the world's completeness that is unlike the ordinary categorical states of affairs that Armstrong's ontology is built on; (b) it is not obviously "actual" in the same sense as ordinary states of affairs; and (c) postulating it makes maximalism less parsimonious than advertised, because the totality fact is not entailed by any finite conjunction of positive facts. David Lewis and Peter Simons pressed that the totality fact is an ad hoc addition — if we are willing to posit any entity needed to make maximalism work, the theory becomes unfalsifiable.

**Modal truths**

A related problem arises for modal truths: "water could have been a different compound" is true (or arguably true), but what makes it true? Possible states of affairs are not actual entities; they cannot serve as truthmakers in the standard sense. Armstrong's "combinatorialist" account (*A Combinatorial Theory of Possibility*, 1989) holds that all genuine possibilities are combinations of actual elements — actual particulars, actual universals, actual relations. "There could have been a golden mountain" is true because gold and mountain are actual universals that can be jointly instantiated. The truthmaker for this modal claim is the actual universals GOLD and MOUNTAIN together with the combinatorial possibilities they generate.

Critics respond: combinatorialism cannot accommodate "alien" properties — properties not instantiated anywhere in the actual world. If it is genuinely possible that there be properties of a fundamentally different kind from any actual property, those possibilities outrun the combinatorial space defined by actual elements. Armstrong's response is that we should be agnostic about alien properties and that our modal intuitions about them may be unreliable. This is a significant concession.

**Maximalism and deflationism**

Truthmaker maximalism and deflationism are natural opposites. Deflationism holds that the T-schema exhausts the content of truth; maximalism holds that for every true *p*, there is an entity T whose existence necessitates *p*. These two claims are not straightforwardly contradictory — the deflationist can accept that snow's being white necessitates "snow is white" (the right-hand side of the T-schema), while denying that this requires positing "states of affairs" or "facts" as further ontological entities. The dispute is thus partly ontological (does the necessitating entity belong to a distinct ontological category?) and partly about what explanatory work the truthmaker relation is supposed to do.

Armstrong's response: the deflationist's acceptance that "snow is white" is true because snow is white is already a commitment to the existence of something — snow, its whiteness, and (tacitly) the state of affairs of snow's being white. The maximalist makes this commitment explicit. The deflationist makes it implicit by treating "snow is white" as a brute description without asking what grounds it.

The maximalist thesis can be expressed with two formal principles:

*Necessitation*: T ⊩ p iff □(∃T → p), where the modality is metaphysical necessity.

*Maximalism*: ∀p (T(p) → ∃T (T ⊩ p))

Together these entail: for any truth *p*, there is a metaphysically necessary connection between the existence of some entity T and the truth of *p*. No truth floats free of the world in the sense that its truth would be unexplained by any actual entity.

These problems have led some truthmaker theorists (Rodriguez-Pereyra, Cameron) to adopt *sparse truthmaking*: only a restricted class of truths require truthmakers, and the rest can be understood in other ways — as grounded in, or derivable from, the truths that do have truthmakers. On this view, negative truths and generalizations may be grounded in positive facts rather than directly made true by dedicated entities. Despite these difficulties, maximalism remains the benchmark version of truthmaker theory against which more modest alternatives are measured.

Consider "All water contains oxygen." Under maximalism, the truthmaker for this universal claim is not merely the actual water molecules and their oxygen atoms, but the totality state of affairs together with the facts about each actual water molecule. Alternatively, the second-order nomic state of affairs N(WATER, CONTAINS-OXYGEN) — the nomic relation between the natural kind WATER and the property CONTAINS-OXYGEN — is a candidate maximalist truthmaker: it necessitates the universal claim by grounding a law, not merely a regularity. Armstrong prefers the nomic state of affairs: laws of nature are second-order states of affairs relating universals, and "All water contains oxygen" is made true by the law connecting WATER and OXYGEN-CONTAINING, which is a genuine entity in the ontology.
