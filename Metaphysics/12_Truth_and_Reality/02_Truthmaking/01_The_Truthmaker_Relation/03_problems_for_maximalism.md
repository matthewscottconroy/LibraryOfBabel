# Problems for Maximalism

Truthmaker maximalism — the claim that every truth has a truthmaker — encounters a family of problems that motivate either abandoning maximalism in favor of a restricted truthmaker principle or revising the theory significantly. These problems reveal the limits of any theory that insists on grounding every truth in some actual, positively existing entity.

**The problem of negative truths**

"There are no dragons" is true, but there is no dragon-excluding entity in the world; the world simply lacks dragons. Armstrong's totality state of affairs is supposed to do the work, but critics argue that this totality fact is itself mysterious — it is a higher-order entity with no clear ontological basis. We can put the problem as follows:

- P1: Maximalism: every true proposition *p* has a truthmaker T such that □(T exists → p).
- P2: "There are no dragons" is true.
- P3: If "there are no dragons" has a truthmaker, there must be an entity T such that □(T exists → there are no dragons).
- P4: No positive entity has this property — any actual entity could in principle coexist with a dragon.
- C1: The only candidate is a totality state of affairs that "closes off" the world — but such a state of affairs is mysterious and unlike ordinary states of affairs.
- C2: Therefore, maximalism either fails for negative truths or requires ontological resources that undermine its parsimony.

The deeper difficulty: the totality state of affairs must "encompass" all actual facts and nothing more. But what makes it true that the totality state of affairs exists and is complete? That is itself a truth, which would require a further truthmaker — and the regress threatens.

**The problem of general truths**

"All ravens are black" poses a similar problem. The individual ravens and their blackness together make each instance true, but they do not make the generalization true — there might be more ravens beyond the actual ravens whose blackness has been checked. Armstrong invokes the totality fact again. Alternatively, one might hold that universal generalizations are true in virtue of the truthmakers of their instances plus the totality fact — but this requires careful formulation to avoid circularity.

A parallel worry: "Every electron has spin" is a law of nature (on a realist view of laws). Armstrong's own account of laws is that they are second-order states of affairs relating universals — the universal ELECTRON stands in the nomic relation N to the universal HAVING-SPIN. This second-order state of affairs is the truthmaker for the law. But critics (van Fraassen, Lewis) question whether second-order states of affairs relating universals are genuine entities or merely a posit tailored to solve the problem.

**Modal truths and alien properties**

A related problem arises for modal truths: "water could have been a different compound" or "it is possible that there be a dragon" seem to be truths, but their truthmakers (possible entities, possible worlds) are not obviously actual. Lewis's modal realism provides truthmakers for modal truths — "it is possible that p" is made true by the existence of a possible world at which *p* is true — but at the cost of a vast ontology of concrete possible worlds. Armstrong's combinatorialist alternative grounds modal truth in actual categorical properties, but faces the problem of alien properties and is widely regarded as giving an impoverished modal ontology.

Fine's essence-based approach offers an alternative: some modal truths are grounded not in possible worlds but in the essences of actual entities. "Necessarily, water is H₂O" is true in virtue of the essence of water — what water is — not in virtue of any possible-worlds structure. This gives truthmakers for some necessary truths without invoking possible worlds, but it requires a robust account of essence that is itself contested.

**Mathematical and ethical truths**

If "7 is prime" and "torturing children is wrong" are true, what are their truthmakers? Platonic abstract entities? Neural states? The truthmaking framework can seem to presuppose a settled metaphysics of abstract objects and normativity that is itself contested. If mathematical Platonism is false — there are no abstract mathematical objects — then mathematical truths either have non-Platonic truthmakers (some form of structuralism or fictionalism) or they lack truthmakers altogether, undermining maximalism.

For ethics, the problem is Mackie's queerness argument in a new guise: moral facts, if they exist, would have to be queer entities — neither natural nor abstractly mathematical. The formal structure of the moral supervenience constraint:

□ ∀x ∀y ((∀P ∈ N: P(x) ↔ P(y)) → (∀M ∈ Moral: M(x) ↔ M(y)))

where N is the set of natural properties and Moral is the set of moral properties. Moral supervenience on the natural is widely accepted but does not by itself determine whether there are moral truthmakers or whether the moral truths are grounded in natural states of affairs.

**Sparse truthmaking and grounding**

These problems have led truthmaker theorists (Rodriguez-Pereyra, Cameron) to adopt *sparse truthmaking*: only a restricted class of truths require truthmakers, and the rest can be understood in other ways.

Cameron's "truthmaker gaps": it is acceptable for some truths to lack truthmakers, provided they are grounded in — metaphysically explained by — truths that do have truthmakers. Negative truths are grounded in positive truths: "there are no dragons" is grounded in the totality of positive facts about what does exist, not in a dedicated totality state of affairs. The grounding approach: rather than requiring truthmakers for every truth, require *grounds*. A truth *p* is grounded in truths q₁, ..., qₙ iff the q's metaphysically explain *p* — they are why *p* is true. Grounding allows for grounded truths that lack their own truthmakers, provided they are derivable from truths with truthmakers. This weakens maximalism to "every ungrounded truth has a truthmaker," which is a more defensible position.

Theodore Sider (*Writing the Book of the World*, 2011) argues that truthmaker theory implicitly presupposes a notion of *fundamentality* or *naturalness*: some descriptions of the world cut reality at its joints, while others do not. The truthmakers for truths are determined by the fundamental, joint-carving features of reality, not by every logically possible description. This is why we do not say that the proposition "either snow is white or grass is green" has a dedicated disjunctive truthmaker — the disjunction does not carve at a joint. A more principled maximalism would hold that fundamental truths — those whose predicates carve nature at its joints — have truthmakers, and derivative truths are grounded in fundamental truths without dedicated truthmakers of their own.

Joseph Melia argues that the truthmaker principle should be understood as a constraint on metaphysical theories rather than as a universal claim about all truths. The principle is: if your metaphysical theory posits certain truths as fundamental, those truths must have truthmakers. Derived truths do not require their own truthmakers. This frees maximalism from its most problematic commitments — totality states of affairs, second-order nomic states — while preserving its core insight that fundamental truth is grounded in the world.

The debate between maximalists and sparse truthmaker theorists tracks a broader debate in metaphysics about the priority of positive versus negative facts, the legitimacy of totality posits, and the relationship between truthmaking and the more general notion of metaphysical grounding.

Consider the problem through the lens of presentism — the view that only present entities exist. Truths about past individuals — "Socrates was mortal," "Socrates does not exist" — are difficult to ground because Socrates is not a present entity; his past existence cannot serve as a current truthmaker. Options: (a) accept "haecceity properties" (the property of being identical to Socrates) that exist currently and ground truths about Socrates; (b) accept that some truths are made true by past entities that no longer exist, which modifies the standard necessity account of truthmaking; (c) give up presentism in favor of eternalism (all times equally real, so Socrates exists at his time and makes the truths about him true). The problem illustrates how truthmaker maximalism interacts with the metaphysics of time: the right theory of truthmaking depends partly on whether the ontology is presentist or eternalist.
