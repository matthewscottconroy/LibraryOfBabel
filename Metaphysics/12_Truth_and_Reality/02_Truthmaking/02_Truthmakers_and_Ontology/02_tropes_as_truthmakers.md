# Tropes as Truthmakers

States of affairs have a commitment built into them: they require a "nexus" or "tie" to bind their constituents — a particular to a universal in the instantiation relation. Critics find this nexus mysterious. An alternative approach replaces states of affairs with *tropes* — particular property instances — as the primary truthmakers, and claims that no such binding relation is needed.

A trope is a concrete, particular instantiation of a property: the particular redness of this rose is a trope, distinct from the redness of any other object. Tropes are not universals — they are particular, not repeatable — and not ordinary particulars — they are property-instances, not substance-things. Every feature of reality is a particular instance, a concrete quality located at a particular region of spacetime. The rose's redness is exactly as red as the rose is red, it is located where the rose is, and it could not be the redness of any other object.

Tropes resemble each other: the redness of this rose and the redness of that rose are two distinct tropes that stand in a resemblance relation. On the "resemblance nominalist" version developed by Rodriguez-Pereyra, universals are analyzed as maximal resemblance classes of tropes — what we call "redness" is the class of all tropes that maximally resemble each other in respect of color-redness. This allows for general truths without invoking universals as further entities.

**The trope theory of truthmaking**

On the trope theory, what makes "the rose is red" true is the rose's-redness: a particular trope that is both a part of the rose and a particular instantiation of the type redness. The trope exists just in case the rose is red; its existence necessitates the proposition. Formally:

Let t_r be the trope that is the rose's particular redness. Then: □(t_r exists → the rose is red). So t_r ⊩ "the rose is red."

Unlike Armstrong's states of affairs, tropes are often thought to be ontologically more economical: a world of tropes and their bundles may need fewer fundamental kinds of entities than a world of objects, properties, and states of affairs. There is no need for a separate "tie" or "nexus" between a particular and a universal, because a trope just *is* a particular property instance — it does not require a further binding relation.

The argument for tropes as truthmakers can be put more precisely:

- P1: Truthmakers must necessitate their propositions and must be causally and spatiotemporally real entities.
- P2: States of affairs are abstract structures whose causal role is questionable.
- P3: Tropes are concrete, spatiotemporally located, causally efficacious property-instances.
- P4: The existence of the rose's-redness trope t_r necessitates "the rose is red."
- C: Tropes are better candidates for truthmakers than states of affairs.

The trope approach has been developed by C. B. Martin, Keith Campbell (*Abstract Particulars*, 1990), and D. H. Mellor. Martin in particular argued that tropes (or "particular qualities") are the real categorical basis of causal powers and that they serve as truthmakers more naturally than abstract states of affairs. Campbell's term "abstract particulars" captures the dual character of tropes: abstract in that they are "ways" things are (not things themselves), particular in that they are unrepeatable instances. G. F. Stout and D. C. Williams laid the historical groundwork: Williams in "The Elements of Being" (1953) called tropes "abstract particulars" — "abstract" not in the sense of non-spatiotemporal but in the sense of being an "abstracted" aspect of a concrete thing.

**Advantages of trope truthmaking**

Tropes are concrete, located in spacetime, and causally efficacious in ways that Armstrong's states of affairs are not obviously so. If the rose's redness can participate in causal processes — causing the perception of redness, causing the bee to be attracted — it is a more robustly "real" entity than an abstract state of affairs. Tropes earn their place in a naturalistic ontology by being causally relevant.

A second advantage: trope ontology avoids the Bradley regress problem. Because a trope just is a particular property-instance, there is no need for a "nexus" to bind it to its bearer. The trope is inherently "attached" to the object whose property it is — it is numerically identical with the instance, not a separate entity requiring binding.

One of the strongest arguments for tropes as truthmakers comes from the philosophy of causation. Particular causal events — this match's lighting — seem to involve particular property instances, not universals. The match's-flammability (a trope) together with the oxygen-presence (a trope) and the striking (a trope) are the causally relevant entities. If causation is particular — if causes are individual events or tropes — then the truthmakers for causal truths are tropes, not states of affairs involving universals. This gives the trope ontology a significant advantage over Armstrong's universals-based account.

**Objections**

Tropes still face the problem of negative truths. The trope theory's truthmaker for "this rose is not blue" cannot simply be the absence of a blue trope — absences are not positive entities. Trope theorists must invoke something like the totality of actual tropes, parallel to Armstrong's totality state of affairs. This difficulty is shared by all positivity-based truthmaker theories: negative truths require something that "closes off" the positive ontology.

The *bundling problem* is also pressing. If ordinary objects are bundles of tropes — their many property-instances — the unity of the bundle requires explanation. What holds the tropes of a single object together? Compresence relations — the tropes of a single object are all compresent in the same spatiotemporal region — are one answer, but compresence itself must either be a trope or a universal, reopening questions about the nature of the unifying relation.

A third challenge concerns relational propositions. "The rose is to the left of the vase" is made true by what trope? A relational trope is a trope of the rose-and-vase pair — an entity that is the particular instance of the left-of relation between these two things. Relational tropes are philosophically strange: they seem to be located at neither the rose nor the vase but at both, or "between" them.

**The formal ontology**

The trope ontology can be represented formally as:

- Tropes: t₁, t₂, ... (particular property instances)
- Compresence: Co(tᵢ, tⱼ) (two tropes belong to the same object or event)
- Resemblance: Res(tᵢ, tⱼ, d) (tropes tᵢ and tⱼ resemble each other in respect d)
- Objects: bundles of compresent tropes

The truthmaker assignment:
- TM("the rose is red") = t_r   (the rose's-redness trope)
- TM("something is red") = ∃tᵢ (Res(tᵢ, t_r, redness) ∧ Actual(tᵢ))
- TM("the rose is redder than the violet") = ⟨t_r, t_v⟩ together with the resemblance ordering

The economy: one category (tropes) replaces three (particulars, universals, states of affairs). However, the resemblance relation and the compresence relation may themselves require tropes or universals to ground them, threatening the parsimony.

Rodriguez-Pereyra's resemblance nominalism uses tropes as truthmakers while analyzing general truths in terms of resemblance. "The rose is red" is made true by the rose's-redness trope; "something is red" is made true by the existence of any trope that maximally resembles the rose's-redness trope in the relevant respect. Universals, on this view, are not entities in the ontology — they are "constructed" from resemblance classes of tropes. The truthmaker for "redness exists" is not a universal but the collection of all red-resembling tropes.

This approach is attractive in its parsimony: one category of entity does the work of particulars, universals, and facts. But critics (Armstrong, Sider) argue that resemblance nominalism faces a vicious regress: what makes two tropes resemble? A further trope of resemblance? And what makes two resemblance-tropes resemble? The regress threatens unless resemblance is treated as a primitive, which risks making the theory unexplanatory at the foundational level.
