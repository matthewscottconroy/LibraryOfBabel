# Modal Truthmakers

Consider the proposition "it is possible that there be a golden mountain." It seems true — there is no contradiction in the idea. But what makes it true? For positive contingent truths, the truthmaker is an actual state of affairs. For possibility claims, the truthmaker would seem to be a possible-but-non-actual state of affairs — something that does not exist but could. This is the central challenge: modal truths resist the standard truthmaker treatment.

The truthmaker principle for modal truths: T ⊩ ◇p iff □(T exists → ◇p). Whatever makes "it is possible that there be a dragon" true must be an actual entity whose existence is sufficient for that possibility. For necessity claims, "necessarily, water is H₂O," the truthmaker would seem to be whatever ensures that the truth holds across all possible worlds. Neither type of entity fits comfortably into an ontology of actual states of affairs.

**Lewisian modal realism**

The most direct solution is David Lewis's modal realism (*On the Plurality of Worlds*, 1986). Possible worlds are concrete entities as real as the actual world, so they can serve as straightforward truthmakers. "◇p" is true iff there exists a possible world *w* such that *p* is true at *w*. The possible world *w* is the truthmaker.

The argument from truthmaking to Lewisian modal realism:

- P1: The truthmaker principle: every truth has a truthmaker.
- P2: Modal truths (possibility, necessity) are genuine truths.
- P3: The only entities that could serve as truthmakers for modal truths are possible worlds.
- P4: Possible worlds must be actual entities (not mere abstractions) to serve as genuine truthmakers.
- C: Possible worlds are concretely existing entities.

The cost is steep: Lewis's worlds are causally and spatiotemporally isolated from one another. They are real but inaccessible to us. Many philosophers regard this as an ontological price too high to pay for a smooth truthmaker account.

**Armstrong's combinatorialism**

Armstrong takes a different approach. He argues that modal truths about contingent matters are made true by actual categorical properties of actual objects, together with laws of nature. "It is possible for gold to be liquid" is made true by the actual properties of gold atoms and the actual laws governing phase transitions: these categorical facts are sufficient to ground the modal truth, without invoking non-actual possible states.

This "combinatorialist" approach (*A Combinatorial Theory of Possibility*, 1989) holds that all possibilities are combinations of actual or possible recombinations of actual elements. A possible world is a "recombination" of actual particulars and universals into new configurations. The truthmaker for "there could be a golden mountain" is the combinatorial space generated by the actual universals GOLD and MOUNTAIN — the possibility is grounded in what those universals are, not in any non-actual entity.

Formally:

- P1: The combinatorial space of possible worlds is determined by the actual particulars and universals and their possible recombinations.
- P2: "It is possible that there be a golden mountain" is true because gold and mountain are actual universals that can be jointly instantiated.
- P3: The truthmaker for this modal claim is the actual universals GOLD and MOUNTAIN.
- C: Modal truths have actual truthmakers — the categorical properties that define the combinatorial space of possibilities.

Critics press the problem of "alien" properties — properties not instantiated anywhere in the actual world. If there could be a color fundamentally unlike any actual color, that possibility seems genuine but outstrips the combinatorial space of actual universals. Armstrong's response is that we should be agnostic about alien properties and that our modal intuitions about them may be unreliable. This is a significant concession.

**Essence-based accounts**

Fine's essence-based approach provides an alternative for necessary truths. Necessary truths are grounded in the essences of actual entities. "Necessarily, all triangles have three sides" is true in virtue of the essence of triangularity — it is part of what it is to be a triangle to have three sides. No possible-worlds machinery is needed; the necessity flows from the nature of the triangle.

For *de re* modality: "necessarily, Hesperus is Phosphorus" is true because it is part of the nature of Hesperus (as a particular object — Venus) to be identical to itself — and Hesperus is Phosphorus. The necessity is grounded in the essence of the identity relation and the essence of the object, not in cross-world identity.

The formal representation: let E_x be the essence of entity *x*. "□Fx" is grounded in E_x when "*x* is *F*" is true in virtue of E_x. Essential necessities are asymmetrically grounded in essences; they are not merely correlations across possible worlds. Fine's advantage: essence grounds necessary truths without invoking possible worlds. "Necessarily, water is H₂O" is true because of the essence of water, which is an actual entity (the nature of water), not a transworld structure. This is ontologically more parsimonious than Lewisian possible-worlds semantics and more explanatory than mere de re necessity claims.

**Modal fictionalism**

A third approach avoids truthmakers for modal claims by treating modal discourse as fictional. Rosen (1990) proposes: "◇p" means "according to the fiction of possible worlds, there is a world at which *p*." Modal claims are "true in the fiction" without requiring real possible worlds as truthmakers. This is compatible with there being no truthmakers for modal truths: modal truths are like fictional truths, true relative to a representation, not true simpliciter.

Modal fictionalism avoids the ontological costs of both Lewis and Armstrong, but it faces the "Humphrey objection" (Lewis): we use modal talk to say that Humphrey could have won the election — not that some counterpart of Humphrey wins in some world. Our concern is with Humphrey himself, not a fictional surrogate. Fictionalism seems to change the subject.

**Actualist possibilism**

Plantinga's actualism holds that possible worlds are maximal consistent states of affairs or propositions — abstract entities that exist actually (they are abstracta, which are actual). "It is possible for there to be a golden mountain" is made true by the existence of the abstract state of affairs [a golden mountain exists], which is an actually existing abstract entity — a possible-but-non-obtaining state of affairs. Every proposition is either true or false, and every state of affairs either obtains or fails to obtain, but every proposition and state of affairs exists necessarily. The truthmaker for modal claims is the necessarily existing abstract structure of propositions and states of affairs.

This brings the discussion full circle to the connection between modal truthmaking and the debates over Platonism, abstracta, and the nature of possible entities. Lewis's concrete possible worlds, Armstrong's combinatorial space of actual elements, Fine's essences, and Plantinga's abstract maximal states of affairs represent four very different strategies for grounding modal truth in what actually exists. Each strategy has its distinctive costs; none commands consensus. The challenge of modal truthmakers is one of the clearest illustrations of how the question "what makes a truth true?" connects to the deepest and most contested questions in metaphysics.
