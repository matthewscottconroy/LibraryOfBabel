# Ethical Truthmakers

If moral realism is true — if there are genuine moral truths — then truthmaker theory invites the question: what makes moral claims true? "Torturing children for fun is wrong" is, most of us think, true. What is its truthmaker?

The question is harder for ethics than for empirical claims because moral properties are not obviously part of the natural causal order. We cannot observe moral facts with our senses; they do not seem to participate in causal processes; they do not fit neatly into the physical world. These features of moral discourse generate the classic concerns of metaethics. Mackie's "queerness argument" holds that if there were objective moral facts, they would be unlike anything else in our ontology. The truthmaker version of this concern runs as follows:

- P1: Every truth requires a truthmaker — an entity in the world in virtue of which it is true.
- P2: If "torturing children is wrong" is true, there must be an entity T such that T's existence necessitates the wrongness of torturing children.
- P3: Moral properties (wrongness, goodness, rightness) are not natural properties — they are not color, mass, charge, or any other naturalistically described feature.
- P4: Non-natural moral entities are metaphysically queer — unlike anything else in our ontology.
- C: Either (a) moral claims lack truthmakers (error theory), (b) moral properties are natural (naturalism), or (c) we accept queer non-natural moral entities (non-naturalism).

**Naturalistic moral realism and truthmakers**

For the moral realist who accepts truthmaker theory, *naturalistic moral realism* holds that moral properties are identical to or constituted by natural properties — perhaps pleasure, flourishing, or preference-satisfaction. On this view, the truthmaker for "X is good" is the natural state of X (its causing pleasure, or its constituting flourishing). Cornell realism (Sturgeon, Railton, Brink) holds that moral properties are natural properties, though not necessarily simple or non-disjunctive. "Goodness" names a natural property — perhaps something like "being what promotes flourishing in beings like us." The truthmaker for "this act is good" is the natural state of affairs constituting the act's promoting flourishing.

The supervenience constraint is central here. Moral supervenience: any two situations that are identical in all natural respects are identical in all moral respects. Formally:

□ ∀x ∀y ((∀φ ∈ N: φ(x) ↔ φ(y)) → (∀μ ∈ M: μ(x) ↔ μ(y)))

where N is the set of natural properties and M is the set of moral properties. If moral properties supervene necessarily on natural properties, then natural states of affairs are at least sufficient for moral truths — a natural state of affairs can serve as a truthmaker for a moral truth by necessitating it via supervenience.

But supervenience alone does not establish naturalism. Supervenience is compatible with non-naturalism: it might be that every moral fact supervenes on natural facts, while moral properties are nonetheless irreducibly non-natural. The non-naturalist says: yes, fixing the natural facts fixes the moral facts, but not because moral facts are natural — rather because the moral supervenes on the natural by a necessary connection that is itself non-natural.

**Non-naturalist moral realism and queer truthmakers**

*Non-naturalistic realism* (associated with G. E. Moore, Russ Shafer-Landau, and Derek Parfit) holds that moral properties are sui generis — neither natural nor supernatural but a distinct domain of normative facts. On this view, the truthmaker for "torturing children is wrong" is a non-natural moral fact. Truthmaker theory then requires that non-natural moral facts exist and that our propositions can be made true by them — a commitment many find too extravagant.

Parfit (*On What Matters*, 2011) embraces normative non-naturalism: there are irreducibly normative truths, and these truths are not made true by natural facts. Their truthmakers are normative facts themselves. The epistemological problem for non-naturalism: how do we know non-natural moral facts if they are causally inert? The standard response (Audi, Shafer-Landau) is moral intuitionism: we have a faculty of moral perception or intuition that is not reducible to sensory perception but is reliable for the domain of moral facts.

**Error theory and the absence of truthmakers**

The error theorist (Mackie) denies that there are truthmakers for moral claims — there are no moral facts, so positive moral claims are systematically false. The falsity is systematic because moral language purports to describe mind-independent moral properties that do not exist.

Joyce's extension (*The Myth of Morality*, 2001): moral claims incorporate an implicit commitment to "categorically binding" authority — they claim that their verdicts apply independently of our desires and contingent social arrangements. But no such categorical authority exists; therefore, moral claims as ordinarily made are all false. The pragmatic value of moral discourse (its role in coordinating behavior, managing cooperation) is preserved even if the discourse is technically false.

**Quasi-realism and constructivism**

The quasi-realist (Blackburn) tries to earn the right to speak of "moral truths" while denying that they are made true by mind-independent facts: moral truth is a pragmatic or assertoric notion, not a metaphysical one. "Torturing children is wrong" is true in the sense that it represents a stable, reflectively endorsed attitude that survives critical scrutiny — but there is no worldly entity (fact, state of affairs, moral property) that is its truthmaker.

Moral constructivism (Korsgaard, Street, Scanlon) offers a middle path. Moral truths are "constructed" from facts about rational agency or rational agreement — they are not mind-independent facts but are not merely expressions of arbitrary preference either. What makes "gratuitous cruelty is wrong" true is that any rational agent, from the perspective of practical reason, would endorse a principle prohibiting gratuitous cruelty. The truthmaker under constructivism: a normative fact about practical reason — the fact that rational agency, properly understood, endorses the prohibition of gratuitous cruelty. This is not a Mackian queer entity (it is grounded in the nature of rationality) but it is not a straightforwardly natural entity either.

The formal structure of moral truthmaking under naturalistic moral realism:

M(a) ← N(a)   (moral fact M(a) is grounded in natural fact N(a))

The grounding relation: M(a) < N(a) means N(a) is the metaphysical explanation of M(a) — not merely that N(a) necessitates M(a) (supervenience), but that N(a) is why M(a) holds. For non-naturalism: no such grounding relation holds; moral facts are primitive and ungrounded in natural facts (though they supervene on them).

Truthmaker theory thus exerts pressure on metaethics: it forces a decision about whether moral realism can deliver the right kind of entities to serve as truthmakers, or whether the appearance of moral truth must be explained without genuinely moral truthmakers.

Consider "The Hiroshima bombing was morally wrong." Under naturalistic realism: the truthmaker is the natural fact of the mass death of civilians, the suffering caused, the absence of military necessity as strict justification, and the violation of principles of proportionality and non-combatant immunity. These natural facts constitute the wrongness by virtue of a naturalistic reduction or by moral supervenience. Under non-naturalism: the truthmaker is a non-natural moral fact — the property of wrongness instantiated by the bombing event. The natural facts ground the moral fact without reducing it; the wrongness is real and irreducible. Under error theory: there is no truthmaker; "the Hiroshima bombing was morally wrong" is, strictly speaking, false — there are no moral facts. What is true is the natural fact of mass death; the moral verdict is a projection of our attitudes. Under quasi-realism: the moral verdict represents a stable, reflectively endorsed attitude of condemnation that passes tests of coherence, universalizability, and informed reflection. It is "morally true" in the quasi-realist sense without requiring a mind-independent moral truthmaker.
