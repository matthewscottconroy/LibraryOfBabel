# Mathematical Truthmakers

Mathematical truths present a particularly sharp challenge for truthmaker theory. "7 is prime" and "there are infinitely many prime numbers" are paradigmatically true — if anything is — but what makes them true? What are the truthmakers for mathematical propositions?

Paul Benacerraf's "Mathematical Truth" (1973) posed the dilemma that frames the debate. Any adequate account of mathematical truth must satisfy two conditions: (i) a *semantic* condition — mathematical sentences have the same semantic structure as ordinary empirical sentences, with terms referring to objects and predicates expressing properties; (ii) an *epistemic* condition — there must be a plausible account of how we come to know mathematical truths. Platonism satisfies (i) but struggles with (ii); nominalist alternatives satisfy (ii) but struggle with (i).

Reformulated as a truthmaking challenge:

- P1: Mathematical truths are genuine truths.
- P2: Every truth has a truthmaker.
- P3: Mathematical truthmakers, if they exist, must be entities whose existence necessitates mathematical truths.
- P4: Abstract mathematical entities (numbers, sets, functions) are the natural candidates.
- P5: But abstract entities are causally inert — they cannot causally interact with our cognitive faculties.
- C: Either (a) we abandon the epistemic access requirement (Platonism), or (b) we find non-abstract truthmakers (nominalism), or (c) we deny that mathematical sentences have truthmakers (fictionalism or deflationism about mathematical truth).

**Platonism and mathematical truthmakers**

The Platonist answer is that mathematical objects (numbers, sets, functions) exist as abstract entities, and mathematical truths are made true by facts about these abstract objects. "7 is prime" is made true by the number 7's having the property of primeness. The challenge for Platonism is epistemic: how do we come to know about abstract, causally inert mathematical entities? We cannot perceive numbers; they do not interact causally with the physical world. Gödel famously invoked mathematical intuition — a quasi-perceptual faculty that "sees" mathematical truths directly. Contemporary defenders (Maddy, on some readings) hold that we perceive sets in the physical world (we perceive the set of three apples when we perceive three apples), giving Platonism a naturalistic epistemic dimension.

Frege's logicism held that mathematical truths are logical truths, and their truthmakers are logical facts — truths of pure logic. Frege's system was inconsistent (Russell's paradox), but neo-logicism (Wright, Hale) revives Frege's approach using second-order logic and Hume's Principle as a "safe" abstraction principle. Hume's Principle: the number of Fs = the number of Gs iff the Fs and Gs are in one-one correspondence. This abstraction principle, together with the logical framework, is sufficient to derive the Peano axioms. The truthmakers for arithmetic truths are logical relations among concepts — not abstract numbers over and above the logical structure.

**Nominalism and its variants**

The nominalist denies that abstract mathematical objects exist. If so, there are no Platonic truthmakers for mathematical claims. Nominalists pursue several alternatives.

*(a) Fictionalism (Field)*: Mathematical claims are systematically false (or strictly speaking, not true in a model of physical reality), useful fictions without truthmakers. Hartry Field's *Science Without Numbers* (1980) argues that mathematics is indispensable not because mathematical claims are true but because they are conservative — adding mathematical axioms to a physical theory does not add any genuinely physical consequences. We can "nominalize" physical theories, reformulating them without reference to mathematical entities. Mathematical sentences are then useful tools, not truths. The truthmaker theorist who accepts fictionalism simply denies that mathematical claims have truthmakers — they are, strictly, false, and have no worldly ground.

*(b) Structuralism (Resnik, Shapiro)*: Mathematical truths are about possible structures, not abstract objects. "7 is prime" is true because in any ω-sequence (any sequence with the structure of the natural numbers), the seventh element has the property of being divisible only by itself and unity. The truthmakers are possible structures, not abstract numbers. Shapiro's *ante rem* structuralism holds that structures exist independently of whether they are instantiated — the natural number structure exists as an abstract entity, and its internal relations (7 being prime) are real features of the structure.

*(c) Modal nominalism (Hellman)*: Mathematical truths are made true by the possibility of certain structures, grounding them in modal facts about the actual world. "7 is prime" is true iff necessarily, in any ω-sequence, the seventh element is prime. The truthmaker is the modal fact — the necessity — not an abstract object.

**Indispensability and truthmakers**

Indispensability arguments (Quine, Putnam) push back: if mathematics is indispensable to our best physical theories, and those theories are true, then mathematics is true, and there must be mathematical truthmakers — abstract objects — in our ontology. The argument:

- P1: We ought to believe in the existence of any entity indispensable to our best confirmed scientific theories.
- P2: Mathematical entities are indispensable to our best confirmed scientific theories.
- C: We ought to believe in mathematical entities.

If the conclusion stands, mathematical entities are genuine truthmakers: abstract objects that exist and whose properties (7's primeness, the infinity of primes) are made true by facts about those objects.

Consider "there are infinitely many primes" under each theory. Under Platonism: the truthmaker is the abstract mathematical fact of there being infinitely many prime numbers — a fact about the structure of the natural numbers. Under structuralism: the truthmaker is the internal property of the natural number structure: in any ω-sequence, infinitely many positions have the primeness property. Under fictionalism: there is no truthmaker; the sentence is "true in the mathematical fiction" but not true simpliciter. Under modal nominalism: the truthmaker is the modal fact that any possible ω-sequence would contain infinitely many "prime" positions.

The debate is not merely technical. It bears on ontology, epistemology, and the philosophy of science: what kinds of entities we must posit to make sense of our most reliable knowledge practices, and whether truth in the mathematical domain is the same kind of thing as truth in the empirical domain. The truthmaker question — what makes mathematical truths true — forces us to choose among positions each of which comes with a distinctive cost: the Platonist pays in epistemic inaccessibility, the fictionalist pays in the counterintuitive conclusion that mathematics is systematically false, the structuralist faces questions about the nature of abstract structures, and the modal nominalist inherits the full weight of a philosophical account of modality.
