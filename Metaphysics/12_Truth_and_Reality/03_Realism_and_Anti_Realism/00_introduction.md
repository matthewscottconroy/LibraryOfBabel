# Realism and Anti-Realism

The realism/anti-realism debate is one of the most fundamental and persistent in all of philosophy. At its core is a deceptively simple question: is there a world independent of our minds, our theories, and our practices, and do our most successful beliefs and theories succeed in accurately describing it? Realism says yes; anti-realism says no — or at least no in the robust sense the realist has in mind.

Realism, as Michael Dummett characterized it, holds that statements in a given domain are true or false independently of whether we can verify them, and that their truth consists in their correspondence to an independently existing reality. The anti-realist denies one or both of these claims: either truth does not transcend our evidence (semantic anti-realism), or reality is in some sense constituted by or mind-dependent on our cognitive activities (metaphysical anti-realism).

Idealism is the most thoroughgoing form of metaphysical anti-realism. British idealism (Bradley, McTaggart, Green) held that reality is fundamentally mental — that the apparent independence of the physical world is an appearance generated by finite minds inadequately grasping a reality that is ultimately spiritual. Kantian idealism is more subtle: the world as it appears to us is structured by the forms of our intuition and the categories of our understanding; the world as it is "in itself," independent of our cognitive contribution, is inaccessible to us. Contemporary neo-idealism explores whether these positions can be developed in ways that respect the achievements of modern science.

Pragmatism, associated with Peirce, James, and Dewey — and revived by Rorty and Putnam in different ways — holds that the realism/anti-realism debate is poorly posed. Truth should be understood in terms of what works, what serves our purposes, what inquiry converges on in the long run — not in terms of a mystical correspondence to a mind-independent realm. Putnam's internal realism and his later commonsense realism both represent attempts to find a position that respects both the achievements of science and the insights of the pragmatist tradition.

This chapter develops the full range of positions, the best arguments for each, and the genuine difficulty of finding a satisfying resolution.
