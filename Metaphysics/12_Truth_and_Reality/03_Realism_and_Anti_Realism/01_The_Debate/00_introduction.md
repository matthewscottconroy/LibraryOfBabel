# The Realism-Anti-Realism Debate

Is there a world independent of our minds, our concepts, and our theories? Do the things we talk about exist prior to and independently of our talking about them? Is truth a matter of correspondence to this mind-independent world? These are the questions at the heart of the realism-anti-realism debate — one of the most fundamental and most contested debates in all of philosophy, touching every area from metaphysics to science to ethics.

The realist answers each question affirmatively. There is a mind-independent world; electrons, mountains, and moral facts exist whether or not we think about them; the truths about the world are fixed by how the world is, not by how we represent it. Scientific investigation is the best method for discovering how this mind-independent world actually is; and the progress of science is genuine progress toward truth, not merely toward theories that satisfy human interests and purposes.

The anti-realist denies one or more of these claims. Idealism is the most radical form: there is no mind-independent world; what exists depends on mind or experience. More moderate anti-realisms accept that there is something "external" to any individual mind but hold that what exists or what is true is constrained or constituted by the practices and standards of a community. Dummett's anti-realism holds that truth cannot outrun our capacity for verification: it is not possible for there to be truths that are in principle unknowable.

The debate between realism and anti-realism cuts across every domain: is there objective mathematical truth independent of mathematical practice? Are there moral facts independent of human attitudes? Are there theoretical entities (electrons, fields) independent of scientific theories? Each domain generates its own specific version of the question, and the answer in one domain does not automatically settle the question in another.
