# What Is Realism?

There is a fact about how many hairs Julius Caesar had on his head the day he was born. It is either even or odd; it will never be verified. The realist says: all the same, one of those options is true. The anti-realist says: what could it mean for something to be true that is in principle inaccessible to any possible knower? This is where the realism debate starts — not in abstract metaphysics but in the question of what we are committed to when we use the word "true."

Realism is the family of positions according to which there is a mind-independent reality that our beliefs and statements purport to describe, and that those descriptions can be more or less accurate. The core commitments are typically two: an *ontological* thesis (some domain of entities exists independently of minds and conceptual schemes) and a *semantic* thesis (statements about that domain have determinate truth values — they are genuinely true or false — independently of our ability to verify them).

These two commitments are logically separable, and their separation defines the main fault lines in the debate. One might hold that physical objects exist independently of minds (ontological realism) while denying that every physical statement has a determinate truth value (semantic anti-realism). Conversely, one might hold that moral statements are true or false while denying that there are mind-independent moral facts (moral constructivism). The argument for realism about domain *D* has the structure:

- P1 (Ontological thesis): The entities in domain *D* exist and have their properties independently of any mind's representing them as such.
- P2 (Semantic thesis): Statements about *D* have determinate truth values — they are true or false — independently of our ability to verify which they are.
- P3 (Independence): The truth value of a statement about *D* is determined by how *D* actually is, not by our epistemic situation, practices, or conceptual scheme.
- C: Realism about *D* is the conjunction of P1, P2, and P3 for the relevant domain.

**Recognition-transcendent truth**

The semantic thesis is often glossed as the thesis that truth is "recognition-transcendent": a statement can be true even if no one could in principle recognize it as true. This generates the central tension that Dummett identified: a realist account of truth "outruns" our epistemic access, raising the question of what grounds such recognition-transcendent truths. If there is no possible evidence that settles whether *p*, what makes it determinate that *p* is true or false? The realist's answer: reality itself determines the truth value, independently of us. This answer is metaphysically serious but epistemologically challenging.

Realism is defined in opposition to a range of rival views. *Idealism* denies the ontological commitment: reality is constituted by mind or experience. *Instrumentalism* accepts the ontological commitment for ordinary objects but denies it for theoretical entities in science: electrons and quarks are useful fictions, not real entities. *Anti-realism* in the semantic sense denies that statements have verification-transcendent truth values. The contemporary debate about realism was substantially shaped by Michael Dummett, who argued that the key question separating realists from anti-realists is not ontological but semantic: realists hold that truth is recognition-transcendent, while anti-realists constrain truth by some epistemic notion like warranted assertibility or provability.

**Scientific realism**

Scientific realism — realism about the entities and properties posited by our best scientific theories — is a major battlefield. The realist holds that electrons, quarks, and dark matter exist and have the properties our theories attribute to them, independently of our theorizing about them. The *no-miracles argument* (Putnam, Boyd): the predictive and technological success of science would be a miracle if our theories were not at least approximately true. Scientific success is best explained by the hypothesis that science tracks mind-independent reality.

The anti-realist response is the *pessimistic meta-induction* (Laudan): scientific theories that were empirically successful in the past — Newtonian mechanics, the caloric theory of heat, the luminiferous ether — have later been abandoned as false. Predictive success does not guarantee truth. Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism draws the lesson: we should only commit to the empirical adequacy of our theories (they get the observables right), not to the literal truth of their claims about unobservables.

**Domain-specific realisms and Wright's marks of objectivity**

Realism also has domain-specific forms: moral realism, mathematical realism, scientific realism, aesthetic realism. Each faces domain-specific objections as well as the general ones.

Crispin Wright's *Truth and Objectivity* (1992) proposes that the realism debate is best approached not by asking a single binary question but by identifying several "marks of cognitive command": whether a discourse exhibits objectivity with respect to truth, whether its true statements have Wide Cosmological Role (they can figure in the explanation of observable phenomena), and whether its norms are mind-independently determined. Different discourses score differently on these marks, yielding a fine-grained pluralism.

**Putnam's model-theoretic argument**

Hilary Putnam's target in his anti-realist period was "metaphysical realism" — the conjunction of: (i) the world consists of a fixed totality of mind-independent objects; (ii) there is exactly one true and complete description of the world; (iii) truth involves correspondence between sentences and external things. Putnam's model-theoretic argument: any sufficiently rich theory has multiple models — structures that satisfy all its sentences — and these models may disagree about what entities the theory is about. There is no privileged "intended model" that uniquely determines reference and thereby fixes the truth conditions of our sentences. The metaphysical realist's claim that there is a unique "real" correspondence between our words and the world is underdetermined by any possible theory.

This argument does not establish anti-realism; it challenges a specific form of realism. Realists can respond by rejecting the correspondence theory while maintaining ontological realism: entities exist mind-independently, but the relation between our discourse and those entities is constituted by our practices of reference and usage, not by a model-theoretic correspondence.

**The Euthyphro contrast**

Wright proposes using a "Euthyphro contrast" to characterize the realism debate in each domain. For a given predicate *F*, the question is:

(R) *x* is *F* because we judge *x* to be *F*?

or

(A) we judge *x* to be *F* because *x* is *F*?

For color (on a naive anti-realist view): things are red because we judge them to be red — our responses constitute the color facts. For shape (realist): we judge things to be circular because they are circular — the shape facts are prior to our responses and ground them. For ethics: either actions are wrong because they have certain features (realist), or they are wrong because we disapprove of them (anti-realist). The Euthyphro contrast provides a diagnostic for domain-specific realism and anti-realism without forcing a global verdict.

Realism typically generates a commitment to bivalence: every meaningful statement in the domain is either true or false. This follows from the independence thesis: if the truth value of a statement is determined by how things are, and things are definitely one way or another, then every statement is definitely true or definitely false. The anti-realist, who ties truth to epistemic access, will allow truth-value gaps for statements that transcend epistemic access — statements that are not provable, verifiable, or decidable. Classical logic assumes bivalence; intuitionist logic (favored by Dummett's anti-realism) does not assume excluded middle (p ∨ ¬p), allowing for statements that are neither true nor false. The dispute over logic is thus downstream of the metaphysical dispute over realism — and it is one of the clearest illustrations of how metaphysics has consequences for formal inquiry.
