# Forms of Anti-Realism

Anti-realism is not a single view but a family of positions united by their rejection of one or both of realism's core commitments. Understanding the varieties of anti-realism is essential for navigating the realism debate, because different anti-realisms target different features of the realist picture and generate different alternatives. The natural first move is to ask: which part of the realist package are we disputing?

**Idealism**

The oldest and most radical form of anti-realism denies ontological realism outright: reality is mind-dependent, constituted by experience, thought, or conceptual scheme. Berkeley's subjective idealism holds that to be is to be perceived (*esse est percipi*). Physical objects are collections of ideas in minds; there is no matter beyond experience. Kant's transcendental idealism holds that the world as we know it is shaped by the forms of intuition and categories of the understanding; the "thing in itself" is unknowable. British Absolute Idealism holds that reality is a rational, spiritual whole — the Absolute.

Berkeley was responding to Locke's distinction between primary qualities (mind-independent: shape, size, number) and secondary qualities (mind-dependent: color, taste, smell). Berkeley argued that primary qualities are equally mind-dependent — there is no coherent notion of shape without the perceiving mind — and concluded that all of reality is mind-constituted. Hume accepted the phenomenal restriction of knowledge but rejected Berkeley's theistic conclusion. Kant distinguished the empirical and transcendental standpoints, preserving empirical realism (objects are real for us) while maintaining transcendental idealism (the nature of those objects is partly mind-constituted).

**Instrumentalism**

Applied especially to science, instrumentalism holds that theoretical entities (electrons, quarks, curved spacetime) are not real but are useful instruments for organizing and predicting observable phenomena. The truth of scientific theories is their empirical adequacy — their getting the observables right — not their accurate description of an unobservable realm. Bas van Fraassen's constructive empiricism (*The Scientific Image*, 1980) is the sophisticated contemporary version: science aims at empirical adequacy, and the acceptance of a theory involves only the belief that it is empirically adequate.

The argument:

- P1: Scientific theories posit unobservable entities (electrons, quarks, fields).
- P2: We have no direct epistemic access to unobservable entities; we can only check the observable predictions of theories.
- P3: Acceptance of a theory is epistemically warranted only by its empirical adequacy.
- C: We should believe scientific theories to be empirically adequate but should remain agnostic about the literal existence of unobservables.

Van Fraassen's "voluntarist" epistemology: it is epistemically permissible — not required — to accept the existence of unobservables. We go beyond the evidence when we believe in electrons; the instrumentalist stays within the evidence by believing only in what is observed.

**Constructivism**

Reality (or some domain of it) is socially or conceptually constructed — it is partly constituted by our practices, agreements, or conceptual schemes. Hilary Putnam's "internal realism" held that what objects exist depends on the conceptual scheme we employ; there is no "view from nowhere" that gives us objects as they are scheme-independently. Different conceptual schemes may be equally legitimate and yet "carve" the world differently.

Social constructivism (Berger and Luckmann, Hacking): social categories — money, gender, institutions, mental illness — are constituted by our social practices and conceptual frameworks. "Money" is not a natural kind; it exists as money because we treat it as such. The anti-realist dimension: if what objects there are depends on the conceptual scheme, then there is no scheme-independent answer to ontological questions.

**Eliminativism**

Applied to particular domains (morality, folk psychology, mathematics), eliminativism holds that the domain's characteristic entities simply do not exist. The discourse is systematically false or in need of replacement. Mackie's moral error theory: moral discourse purports to describe objective moral properties that do not exist — so all positive moral claims are false. Paul and Patricia Churchland's eliminative materialism: folk psychology posits beliefs, desires, and intentions, but a completed neuroscience will replace these with a better taxonomy of neural states. Hartry Field's mathematical fictionalism: mathematical claims are, strictly speaking, false.

Eliminativism is more radical than other anti-realisms. The idealist denies mind-independence while preserving the domain; the constructivist reconstitutes the domain in terms of practices; the eliminativist denies that the domain's characteristic entities exist at all.

**Semantic anti-realism (Dummett)**

Dummett's semantic anti-realism holds that truth must be constrained by what we can in principle recognize or verify. A statement is true only if there is a possible proof or verification procedure. Dummett's manifestation argument: understanding the meaning of a sentence must be manifested in recognizable behavior. If the meaning of a sentence were given by truth conditions that could in principle never be recognized as obtaining, understanding could not be manifested. Therefore, the meaning of a sentence is given by its verification conditions, not by recognition-transcendent truth conditions.

This generates anti-realism about: past truths (we can no longer verify what happened), distant truths (we cannot observe distant regions), counter-factual truths (we cannot actualize the antecedent), mathematical truths beyond what is constructively provable. Anti-realism in the Dummettian sense is thus a sweeping conclusion with revisionary consequences for logic (excluded middle fails) and mathematics (classical logic is invalid).

**The varieties and their formal structure**

The different anti-realisms can be characterized by what they deny:

- *Idealism*: denies the ontological thesis. Reality is constituted by mind.
- *Instrumentalism/Constructive empiricism*: accepts ontological realism for observables; denies it for unobservables. Accepts only "empirical adequacy" as the aim of science.
- *Constructivism*: denies the scheme-independence of ontology. Objects exist, but what objects there are depends on the conceptual scheme employed.
- *Eliminativism*: denies the existence of the domain's characteristic entities. The discourse is systematically false.
- *Semantic anti-realism (Dummett)*: accepts that entities exist but denies that statements about them have verification-transcendent truth values. Truth is epistemically constrained.

Rather than a binary realism/anti-realism distinction, contemporary philosophy recognizes a spectrum. *Strong realism* holds mind-independent entities, bivalent and verification-transcendent truth, and robust correspondence. *Minimal realism* holds mind-independent entities and bivalent truth, but no "substantive" correspondence — merely Tarskian truth. *Internal realism* (Putnam's middle period) holds object-existence as scheme-relative and truth as idealized rational acceptability within a scheme. *Superassertibilism* (Wright) holds that truth in a domain is what would be assertible under idealized conditions and would remain assertible no matter how those conditions were extended. *Full anti-realism* denies both ontological and semantic theses; the domain's discourse is either non-cognitive (expressivism), systematically false (error theory), or in need of replacement (eliminativism).

The history of anti-realism runs from Berkeley's refutation of material substance, through Kant's transcendental restriction of knowledge, through the Vienna Circle's verificationism (meaning is method of verification), to Putnam's internal realism, Dummett's semantic anti-realism, van Fraassen's constructive empiricism, and contemporary constructivist and quasi-realist positions. The common thread is skepticism about our ability to transcend our epistemic situation to access an independent reality, and the consequent tendency to "internalize" truth or reality into the practices and capacities of the knowing subject.

In the philosophy of science, the realism-anti-realism debate centers on unobservable entities. Let T be a scientific theory and O(T) its observational consequences:

- Realist: T is true → T is empirically adequate, and additionally, the unobservable entities T posits exist.
- Instrumentalist: T is acceptable iff T is empirically adequate (O(T) are all true). No commitment to unobservables.

The no-miracles argument for realism: if the unobservable entities T posits did not exist, the predictive success of T would be miraculous. This is an inference to the best explanation applied to the success of science itself. The pessimistic meta-induction counters: predictive success in the past has not guaranteed truth. The exchange between these arguments remains unresolved.
