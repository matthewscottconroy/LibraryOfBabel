# Dummett on Realism

The traditional form of the realism debate asks: do physical objects, moral facts, mathematical entities, exist independently of minds? Michael Dummett's contribution was to reframe the question entirely. What distinguishes realists from anti-realists, he argued, is not their answer to an ontological question but their theory of meaning and truth — specifically, their answer to the question of whether truth is "recognition-transcendent."

**The semantic reframing**

For Dummett, the realist holds a *truth-conditional* theory of meaning: the meaning of a statement is given by its truth conditions, which may obtain independently of any possibility of our recognizing them. This generates recognition-transcendent truth: a statement can be true or false even if there is no possible epistemic situation in which anyone could verify it. "The total number of hairs on Julius Caesar's head on the day he was born was even" is either true or false on the realist view, even though we can never verify which.

The anti-realist, by contrast, holds an *assertibility-conditions* theory of meaning: the meaning of a statement is given by the conditions under which we are warranted in asserting it. Truth cannot outrun warranted assertibility. A statement is true only if there is a possible procedure by which we could in principle come to know it as true. This constraint, Dummett argues, follows from the observation that meaning must be manifested in practice: if the understanding of a statement could not in principle be manifested in any recognizable behavior, the statement has no determinate content.

**The manifestation argument**

Dummett's central argument:

- P1: Understanding the meaning of a sentence must be manifested in linguistic behavior and epistemic responses.
- P2: If the meaning of a sentence were given by recognition-transcendent truth conditions, an ideal speaker could have full understanding without any manifestable difference from a speaker with a different understanding.
- P3: But a difference in meaning must be a difference that can in principle show up in practice.
- C: Meaning cannot be given by recognition-transcendent truth conditions — it must be given by conditions that can be manifested in practice (verification or assertibility conditions).

This argument has a Wittgensteinian flavor: the "private" content of recognition-transcendent truth conditions, which nothing in our practice could manifest, resembles the "private language" that Wittgenstein argued is unintelligible. Meaning must be public; truth conditions that are in principle beyond recognition are not publicly manifested and hence not genuinely grasped.

Dummett's arguments are developed in "Truth" (1959), "The Philosophical Basis of Intuitionistic Logic" (1975), *Frege: Philosophy of Language* (1973), and *The Logical Basis of Metaphysics* (1991). The systematic scope is remarkable: a single argument from meaning theory, if sound, settles a wide class of metaphysical disputes in favor of anti-realism.

**The connection to logic**

Dummett draws the connection to intuitionist logic: if truth must be knowable in principle, then the classical law of excluded middle (every statement is either true or false) fails — some statements are neither true nor false because neither they nor their negations are provably assertible. The anti-realist truth constraint:

T(⌜p⌝) → ◇K(p)

where ◇K(p) means "it is possible in principle for a knower to know that p." Truth cannot outrun possible knowledge. Equivalently: ¬◇K(p) → ¬T(⌜p⌝). This generates a truth-value gap: propositions for which neither *p* nor ¬*p* is knowable in principle are neither true nor false. The excluded middle P ∨ ¬P fails for such propositions, requiring intuitionist logic.

The realist accepts no such constraint: T(⌜p⌝) is independent of ◇K(p). Truth is recognition-transcendent. The revisionary consequence for realism: Dummett's anti-realism requires revising classical logic, replacing the law of excluded middle with a constructively validated principle. This affects not just mathematics but any domain where verification-transcendent statements occur — past history, distant physics, counterfactuals.

Consider "Goldbach's conjecture is true or Goldbach's conjecture is false." Classically this is a tautology. Intuitionistically, it requires a constructive proof: we must be able to determine which disjunct holds. Since no such proof exists, the disjunction is not intuitionistically assertible. This is not an isolated revision; it has consequences throughout mathematics and, on Dummett's view, throughout any domain governed by an assertibility-conditions semantics.

**Responses and Wright's refinement**

Critics press several objections. First, the manifestation argument may prove too much. Many truths about the past, about distant regions, about counterfactuals, seem perfectly meaningful — we know what would count as verifying them, even if actual verification is impossible. What determines the scope of "in principle"? Second, the constraint from manifestation may be question-begging. The realist can say: understanding a truth-conditional meaning is manifested in the dispositions to apply a sentence correctly to situations in which it is true — dispositions that could in principle extend to situations we cannot actually access. The anti-realist's claim that such dispositions are not genuinely manifested rests on a prior commitment to a restrictive account of what counts as manifestation.

Complementing the manifestation argument is Dummett's "acquisition argument": we could not acquire a concept of truth that exceeds all possible evidence, because our mastery of language is acquired through training in the conditions of assertibility. A child learns when it is appropriate to assert "it is raining" by learning what counts as evidence for rain. If the meaning of the sentence exceeded what the child could be trained to recognize, the child could never acquire the relevant meaning. The realist's response: we acquire concepts by understanding truth conditions, not verification conditions. When a child learns "the dinosaurs became extinct 65 million years ago," the child learns a truth condition (what the world would have to be like for the claim to be true), not a verification condition.

Crispin Wright refined and partly challenged Dummett's framework in *Realism, Meaning and Truth* (1986) and *Truth and Objectivity* (1992). Wright argued that the realism-anti-realism debate cannot be settled by a single argument from meaning theory. Instead, different discourses exhibit different "marks" of objectivity: whether they satisfy Cognitive Command (all disagreements are in principle resolvable by better inquiry), whether their truths have a Wide Cosmological Role, and whether their truth is "robust" (not merely constituted by ideal assertibility). For Wright, Dummett correctly identifies that semantic notions are central to the realism debate, but incorrectly assumes that a single anti-realist semantics applies across all domains.

Both Dummett and Davidson are influenced by Frege's philosophy of language but draw opposite conclusions. Davidson embraces truth-conditional semantics: the meaning of a sentence is given by its truth conditions, specified by a Tarskian truth theory. Understanding a sentence is knowing its truth conditions. This is Dummett's target. Dummett's anti-realist semantics: meaning is given by verification conditions. The debate between Davidson and Dummett over the foundations of semantics is thus a debate about the metaphysics of truth: whether truth is recognition-transcendent or epistemically constrained. It remains unresolved.

Dummett's framework is influential partly because it clarifies the terms of the debate and partly because it generates a principled, systematic anti-realism rather than a piecemeal rejection of particular entities. By making the dispute semantic rather than ontological, Dummett shows that the metaphysics of truth has direct implications for logic, mathematics, and the philosophy of science. Even philosophers who ultimately reject his anti-realism acknowledge that his framework has permanently shaped the terms in which realism is discussed.

Under Dummett's anti-realism, "Caesar had an even number of hairs on his head at birth" is neither true nor false. We cannot in principle verify whether Caesar had an even or odd number of hairs; there is no possible epistemic situation in which this is determined. By the anti-realist truth constraint, neither the statement nor its negation is true. The law of excluded middle fails here. The realist has strong intuitions on his side: surely either Caesar had an even number of hairs or he did not — surely there was a matter of fact? Dummett's response: this "surely" reflects a misleading metaphor of an independent world already fixed in every detail. The concept of "how things were" is itself constructed from the conditions of possible experience and knowledge; there is no fact "already there" independent of the possibility of evidence.
