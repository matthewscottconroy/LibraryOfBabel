# Contemporary Idealism

Idealism had largely disappeared from anglophone philosophy by the mid-twentieth century, displaced by the analytic revolt against Hegelianism and the subsequent dominance of naturalism and realism. But idealist themes have returned in several contemporary forms, often under different names and motivated by new problems.

**Putnam's internal realism**

Putnam (in his "middle period," roughly 1975–1990) argued for a position he called "internal realism": there is no view from nowhere, no perspective outside all conceptual schemes from which to assess whether our concepts "match" mind-independent reality. What objects exist depends on the conceptual scheme employed. Different schemes may be equally legitimate and equally "true to reality" without there being a single framework-independent answer to what exists. This is not Berkeley's subjective idealism — objects are real — but it is idealism in the sense that objecthood is scheme-dependent.

Putnam's argument from conceptual relativity: How many objects are in a room containing a coffee mug, a pen, and a piece of paper? The commonsense scheme gives 3 objects; the mereological scheme gives at least 7 — the 3 simple objects plus their 4 possible mereological sums. Both answers are internally correct given their respective schemes. The formal structure: let Q be the question "how many Fs are there?" Q has a determinate answer relative to a conceptual scheme S (with its criteria for what counts as an F and when Fs are identical) but no answer "in itself."

|S₁-objects in room| = 3 (commonsense: mug, pen, paper)

|S₂-objects in room| = 7 (mereological: mug, pen, paper, mug+pen, mug+paper, pen+paper, mug+pen+paper)

This is not relativism — both answers are correct — but conceptual relativity: the question has determinate answers only relative to schemes. The implication for truth: "the room contains 3 objects" is true-in-S₁, false-in-S₂. There is no scheme-independent truth of the matter.

Putnam later retreated from internal realism toward "pragmatic realism" and "natural realism," arguing that direct perceptual experience gives us access to the world without an intervening "interface" of representations. His later view is closer to realism, preserving the Kantian insight that the mind is actively involved in experience without generating scheme-relativity of objects. Putnam's claim that "the mind and the world jointly make up the mind and the world" (the opening line of *Reason, Truth, and History*, 1981) captures the idealist-compatible position: truth is not fixed by a mind-independent world but by the interaction of mind and world within a shared form of life.

**McDowell's naturalized Platonism**

McDowell's *Mind and World* (1994) argues that the space of reasons — concepts, meanings, norms — is not a "rampant Platonism" of abstract entities outside nature, but nor is it fully naturalizable into the causal-bald-naturalism of Quine. Second nature, acquired through enculturation and education, opens us to the conceptual content of experience. We do not first receive non-conceptual sensory inputs and then apply concepts; experience is from the outset conceptually structured.

McDowell's view has idealist resonances in its insistence that experience is always already conceptual, that the world we perceive is "laden" with conceptual content. But McDowell is emphatic that this does not make the world mind-dependent: the conceptual content of experience is not something we impose on a neutral world; it is the world presenting itself to us in its genuine character. The label "naturalized Platonism" reflects this: the Platonic space of reasons is not a separate realm but is immanent in the natural world, available to creatures with the right second nature.

**Idealist metaphysics of mind and the hard problem**

Some contemporary metaphysicians have revived idealism as a systematic position in response to the hard problem of consciousness. The hard problem (Chalmers, 1995): explaining why physical processes are accompanied by subjective experience — why there is "something it is like" to see red — seems to require something beyond what physical science provides. Idealism dissolves the hard problem by making mind ontologically fundamental: there is no gap between physical processes and experience because physical processes are themselves constituted by mind.

Bernardo Kastrup defends analytic idealism: mind is ontologically fundamental; what we call "matter" is the extrinsic appearance of mental processes. The hard problem dissolves because experience is primary and physical reality is its expression. Yujin Nagasawa and others explore panpsychism — a close neighbor of idealism in which mind (or proto-mental properties) pervade nature — as a response to the hard problem.

These positions argue that the hard problem is better dissolved by idealism than by physicalism. The truthmaking connection: if idealism is true, the truthmakers for physical truths are mental entities or processes. "The electron has charge −1e" is made true by the structure of mental reality that we represent as physical. This is a radical departure from standard scientific realism.

**Absolute Idealism revisited**

Timothy Sprigge defended a form of Absolute Idealism through the late twentieth century in *The Vindication of Absolute Idealism* (1983) and *The God of Metaphysics* (2006). Sprigge argued that reality is a system of centers of experience — all experience is real, and the physical world is the "noumenon" of which experience is the "phenomenon." The Absolute is not an impersonal structure but an infinite experiential whole.

More recently, German Idealism has attracted renewed scholarly and philosophical interest. Gabriel's *I Am Not a Brain* (2015), Houlgate's work on Hegel's logic, and Pippin's neo-Hegelianism attempt to recover systematic idealist philosophy for contemporary analytic discussion. These projects argue that Hegel's logic — the attempt to derive the categories of thought and reality from the self-unfolding of pure reason — addresses problems in metaphysics and epistemology that analytic philosophy has not adequately solved.

**Rorty and the dissolution**

Rorty, drawing on Davidson and Dewey, concluded that the realism-idealism debate should be dissolved rather than settled: once we give up the "mirror of nature" picture — the idea that truth is a matter of accurately representing a mind-independent world — neither realism nor idealism is the right framework. Inquiry is a social practice; truth is what the practice yields at its best; and the metaphysics of mind-dependence or mind-independence is an idle wheel that the practice does not turn.

Some contemporary idealists connect the hard problem of consciousness to the philosophy of time. If only the present moment is real (presentism), and if experience is always present experience, then the present's reality may be constituted by present experience. Julian Barbour's "Nows" in quantum gravity — discrete configurations of the universe that exist timelessly — have been interpreted idealistically: what we experience as "the present moment" is a timeless configuration, and the appearance of temporal flow is constructed by experience. The truth-theoretic implication: if time is constituted by experience, then temporal truths — truths about what happened in the past, what will happen in the future — are truths about the structure of experience rather than truths about a mind-independent temporal order. This generates a distinctive approach to the truthmakers for tensed propositions.

Contemporary idealism bears directly on truth theory. If reality is mind-constituted, then truth is correspondence with a mind-constituted reality, which is a weaker notion than the realist's mind-independent truth. This intersection between idealist metaphysics, the hard problem of consciousness, and the theory of truthmakers represents one of the most active frontiers in contemporary philosophy.
