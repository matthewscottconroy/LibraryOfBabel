# Pragmatism and Truth

Pragmatism began as a distinctively American philosophical movement — associated with Peirce, James, and Dewey — and offered a radical reconception of truth and knowledge. For the pragmatist, the meaning of any idea is exhausted by its practical consequences; truth is not correspondence to a mind-independent world but whatever belief works in practice, guides successful action, and proves fruitful in the long run. Philosophy should serve life, not abstract metaphysical speculation.

Peirce's version of pragmatism is the most epistemically respectable: truth is what the community of inquirers would converge on if inquiry were pursued indefinitely under ideal conditions. This is not a purely pragmatic criterion — it is not what works for any particular individual — but an idealized notion of truth as the endpoint of rational inquiry. Peirce's pragmatism is compatible with realism about truth: the truth is fixed (by the idealized endpoint of inquiry), not merely by practical success.

James's version is more radical. Truth is what is good to believe; what is "good" is determined by the practical consequences of belief in a broad sense — what the belief leads to, how it satisfies our needs and purposes, how it fits with the rest of our beliefs. This instrumentalism about truth makes it relative to human purposes in a way that many philosophers find unacceptable: does it make religious beliefs true if believing them makes people happier? James's answer, stretched beyond his intent, seems to say yes.

Contemporary neo-pragmatism — associated above all with Richard Rorty — holds that the question "Does our language correspond to reality?" is a bad question, grounded in a discredited Cartesian picture of the mind as a mirror of nature. We should abandon the realism-anti-realism debate and think of knowledge as a social practice, truth as a compliment we pay to successful beliefs, and philosophy as continuous with literature and cultural criticism.

This section examines pragmatism about truth — its historical forms, contemporary neo-pragmatism, and the objections that have been raised to the pragmatist reconception of truth.
