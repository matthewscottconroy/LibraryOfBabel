# Peirce and James on Truth

What would it mean for a belief to be true if we could never, even in principle, verify it? The pragmatist tradition answers: nothing coherent. Truth must be tied to practice, to inquiry, to what a difference a belief makes in our experience and action. Charles Sanders Peirce and William James are the founding figures of this tradition, and they offer significantly different accounts of truth that reveal tensions within pragmatism from the very beginning.

**Peirce's account**

Peirce defined truth as the opinion which is "fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate" at the ideal limit of inquiry. Truth is not correspondence to a pre-given reality but the outcome of an indefinitely extended process of inquiry conducted by the community of rational investigators. This is not relativism — the ideal limit is unique, not community-relative — but it is anti-realist in the sense that truth is essentially tied to a process of inquiry rather than to mind-independent fact.

Peirce's argument:

- P1: We can have no conception of an object except in terms of its practical effects and its manifestations in possible experience.
- P2: The "truth" of a proposition can have no meaning beyond its being the settled, final result of extended inquiry.
- P3: The community of inquirers, following the methods of science, converges over time to a stable body of beliefs.
- C: Truth is whatever this ideal convergence yields — the "fated" final opinion.

Peirce's account has attractive features: it connects truth to scientific method, it explains why truth is accessible (by inquiry), and it generates a kind of realism about the results of ideal inquiry — the ideal opinion is not merely a social construction but is genuinely responsive to reality. Peirce sometimes wrote as if the external world "forces" itself on inquiry — the real is that which causes the final settled opinion. This gives Peirce's pragmatism a realist dimension missing from James's. Peirce's "The Fixation of Belief" (1877) identifies four methods by which doubt is resolved and belief fixed; only the scientific method is truth-directed because only it is governed by the real, which is independent of what any individual or community thinks.

The account's main challenge is making sense of the "ideal limit" — what is it, and why think all inquiry converges to a unique outcome? Quantum mechanics and relativity seem to suggest that some questions may not have determinate answers even at the limit of scientific inquiry. And some truths about the past (what Caesar ate for breakfast on the Ides of March) seem to have a determinate answer that inquiry may never reach.

**James's account**

James's account in *Pragmatism* (1907) is more radical: truth is what it is "useful to believe," what "works" in practice. A belief is true if acting on it leads to satisfying results — if it "pays." This is explicitly anti-correspondence: truth is not a property of a belief in virtue of its relation to mind-independent fact but in virtue of its role in the believer's practical life. "True ideas are those that we can assimilate, validate, corroborate, and verify. False ideas are those that we cannot."

James's formulation was widely criticized for making truth too subjective and too tied to individual consequences. If "God exists" is true for me because believing it brings me comfort, does that mean God exists? Russell and Moore objected strenuously that James confused truth with utility. James responded that the distinction is less sharp than critics suppose: the "useful" is not merely pleasant but includes predictive success, explanatory power, and coherence with other beliefs. The cash value of truth is not individual satisfaction but the whole package of consequences for the life of the believer and her community.

**The Peirce-James divergence**

The divergence between Peirce and James reflects a fundamental tension in pragmatism. Peirce's account is community-based and regulative — truth is the ideal limit of communal inquiry, which grounds a form of realism. James's account is individual and instrumental — truth is whatever works for the individual believer in practice.

Formally:
- Peirce: T(p) iff ∃ ideal inquiry process I such that the result of I is universal convergence on *p*.
- James: T(p) iff believing *p* "works" for the believer — it satisfies, validates, leads to successful action.

Peirce was dismayed by James's account and felt it misrepresented pragmatism. He renamed his own position "pragmaticism" to distance it from James's. The key difference: for Peirce, truth is a regulative ideal of inquiry, independent of any individual's satisfaction. For James, truth is constituted by individual satisfaction and success.

The most powerful objection to both versions: the truth of past events seems independent of any inquiry process. "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" was true before any inquiry into it; it will be true even if all inquirers cease to exist. Truth seems to precede and ground inquiry rather than being constituted by it.

Peirce's response: our concept of truth is inseparable from its role in inquiry. To say "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" would, if inquiry were ideally conducted, be confirmed — this is what we mean when we say the proposition is true. We cannot step outside our epistemic situation to a perspective from which truth is independent of all possible inquiry.

James's response: the truth about Caesar's crossing is cashed out in its evidential consequences — the truth "works" because it enables us to make historical sense of the Gallic Wars, the political history of Rome, and the subsequent development of Western civilization. The practical consequences of the belief extend far beyond individual comfort.

Comparing the three accounts — Peirce, James, and Davidson — helps locate the options precisely:

- Peirce: T(p) iff Limit_{ideal inquiry}(p) = agreement. Truth is the output of an idealized social inquiry process.
- James: T(p) iff believing *p* "pays" — leads to experiential satisfaction, predictive success, and coherent integration with other truths. Truth is a dynamic property of the belief-in-practice.
- Davidson: T(p) iff *p* is assigned "true" by the correct truth theory for the speaker's language, as determined by radical interpretation. Truth is characterized by the formal theory, not by any epistemic or practical process.

Davidson is closest to realism; Peirce is a form of anti-realism with realist aspirations; James is the most thoroughly anti-realist of the three, grounding truth entirely in practical consequences.

Peirce's account of truth is embedded in his broader semiotic theory. Signs (representations) stand for objects (what is represented) in the minds of interpretants (further signs or thoughts). Truth is a property of signs: a sign is true when the inquiring process it initiates would, at the ideal limit, produce a sign that corresponds to its object. The pragmatic maxim (Peirce, 1878): "Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then our conception of those effects is the whole of our conception of the object." Applied to truth: the conception of truth is the conception of all the practical effects of the inquiry process — what the ideal limit of inquiry would produce. Truth is not a thing but a regulative ideal of a process. The contemporary relevance: Peirce's account anticipates the Peircean realism defended by Putnam in his later pragmatist period, while James's account foreshadows Rorty's radical neo-pragmatism and the critique of representationalism.
