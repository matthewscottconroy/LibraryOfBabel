# Dewey and Instrumentalism

John Dewey's version of pragmatism — which he preferred to call "instrumentalism" — developed the pragmatist account of truth and knowledge in a biological and social direction. For Dewey, knowing is not a passive contemplation of mind-independent reality but an active, problem-solving engagement with the environment. Organisms face problems; inquiry is the process by which they resolve them. Knowledge is the outcome of successful inquiry, not a correspondence between propositions and facts.

**The critique of the spectator theory**

Dewey rejected the traditional epistemological framework, which he called the "spectator theory of knowledge": the picture of a mind standing over against the world, inspecting it, and forming representations that may or may not match. On this picture, truth is the matching relation. Dewey argued that this picture generates insoluble puzzles — how does the mind "reach" the world to compare itself with it? — and should be replaced by a naturalistic account in which knowing is continuous with doing.

The spectator theory's problems:

- P1: Knowledge is supposedly a relation between a mind and an independent reality.
- P2: But the mind can only access what it is given in experience.
- P3: Experience is shaped by the cognitive and practical activities of the knower.
- C: The spectator theory posits a relation that cannot in principle be established. The framework is incoherent.

Dewey's alternative: inquiry is a transaction between organism and environment. Organisms encounter "problematic situations" — situations that are indeterminate, uncertain, confused. Inquiry is the process of transforming a problematic situation into a "resolved" one — one that is stable, coherent, and allows for further action. The "true" belief is the one that resolves the problematic situation; "knowledge" is the outcome of successful inquiry.

**Warranted assertibility**

For Dewey, "warranted assertibility" is a better concept than "truth" for what inquiry aims at. A claim is warranted when the inquiry that produced it followed proper methods and would withstand further inquiry. This is weaker than truth in the correspondence sense: it is possible for warranted claims to be revised by further inquiry, and there is no "final truth" beyond the ongoing process.

The formal representation: WA(p) iff *p* is the outcome of an inquiry process that (i) began with a genuine problematic situation, (ii) followed appropriate methods of investigation, and (iii) resolved the problematic situation in a stable, further-inquiry-surviving way.

Truth-talk for Dewey is a way of endorsing a claim as warranted: to say "*p* is true" is to commend *p* as having emerged from proper inquiry. The T-schema T(⌜p⌝) ↔ p is acceptable but trivial; what matters is not the semantic role of "true" but the conditions of warranted assertibility.

Dewey's *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (1938) is his most systematic work. Dewey distinguishes between the *antecedent* and *consequent* of inquiry: the antecedent is the problematic situation — indeterminate, unsettled, requiring resolution; the consequent is the unified, resolved situation — the outcome of successful inquiry. Truth (warranted assertibility) characterizes propositions as they function within the inquiry process, not as static correspondences between mind and world.

**The biological and social dimensions**

Dewey's naturalism gives knowing a biological basis. Inquiry emerges from the same source as other adaptive behaviors: organisms that can transform problematic situations through symbolic thought and communication have a survival advantage. The categories of inquiry — problem identification, hypothesis formation, testing, resolution — are biological functions elevated to reflective, social practice in human beings.

The social dimension: Dewey argued that inquiry is inherently social. Knowledge is not the private possession of a solitary knower but a social achievement. The community of inquiry — scientists, citizens, scholars — is the proper locus of knowledge. Dewey's implications for education and democracy were direct: education should cultivate the skills of inquiry, not transmit fixed bodies of knowledge; democracy is the form of social life that most fully embodies the cooperative, self-correcting character of inquiry.

**Dewey's philosophy of science**

Dewey's philosophy of science is resolutely anti-foundationalist: there are no bedrock certainties, no given data immune to revision. Science is a self-correcting process, and its results at any stage are provisional. The "instrumentalism" of the view lies in treating scientific theories as instruments — tools for solving practical and theoretical problems — rather than as descriptions of a theory-independent reality.

This contrasts sharply with scientific realism: the realist holds that successful scientific theories are at least approximately true descriptions of an independent reality; Dewey holds that they are tools that "work" in solving problems and making predictions. The question "is the electron real?" receives an instrumentalist answer: the electron concept is a highly useful tool for organizing and predicting electrical phenomena; whether it corresponds to a mind-independent entity is a question that dissolves once we adopt the instrumentalist framework.

Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism (*The Scientific Image*, 1980) shares Dewey's skepticism about unobservable entities, though van Fraassen grounds it differently. For van Fraassen, the aim of science is empirical adequacy — saving the phenomena — not truth. A theory is empirically adequate iff it correctly describes what is observable. Van Fraassen's voluntarism differs from Dewey's naturalism: van Fraassen grounds the restriction on epistemic grounds (we have no evidence for unobservables beyond what is required for empirical adequacy), while Dewey grounds it on the pragmatist account of meaning (claims about unobservables have meaning only insofar as they have observable consequences). Despite this difference, both positions converge on the anti-realist conclusion that science does not require commitment to mind-independent unobservable entities.

**The relationship to Sellars**

Dewey's anti-foundationalism anticipates Sellars's "Myth of the Given" (1956). Both argue against the idea that knowledge rests on a "given" stratum of bare, unconceptualized data. For Dewey, all experience is already transactional — already shaped by the organism's active engagement with the environment. For Sellars, all epistemic justification is within the "space of reasons" — the inferential structure of conceptual thought. Neither thinker allows for a non-conceptual, epistemically basic stratum that grounds the rest of knowledge. Both Dewey and Sellars accept a form of coherentism about justification (beliefs justify other beliefs within a normative-inferential structure) while denying that this makes justification "circular" in a vicious sense.

**Legacy**

Dewey's influence extends beyond philosophy of science to education (Dewey's progressive education movement), political theory (his defense of democratic deliberation), and social philosophy. In philosophy proper, his critique of the spectator theory of knowledge anticipated later critiques of "the myth of the given" (Sellars), representationalism (Rorty), and the view from nowhere (Nagel, Putnam). The neo-pragmatists — Rorty, Brandom, Putnam in his later phases — all engage seriously with Dewey, drawing different lessons from his pragmatist inheritance.

Consider "The theory of evolution is true." Under Dewey's instrumentalism: the theory of evolution successfully resolves the problematic situation constituted by the diversity of life, the fossil record, anatomical homologies, biogeographical distributions, and molecular genetics. The theory "works" as a guide to biological inquiry: it generates testable predictions, integrates disparate phenomena, and enables successful interventions. Its "truth" is its warranted assertibility — the outcome of a sustained, methodologically rigorous inquiry process. Under scientific realism: "the theory of evolution is true" means the historical process of evolution by natural selection actually occurred — there was genuine common descent, actual speciation events, real selective pressures. The theory is true because it corresponds to the historical facts of life's development on Earth, facts that existed before Darwin described them and would exist even if Darwin had never been born. The pragmatist and realist accounts are not obviously incompatible — they may be descriptions of the same phenomenon from different angles — but they generate different implications for what truth in science requires.
