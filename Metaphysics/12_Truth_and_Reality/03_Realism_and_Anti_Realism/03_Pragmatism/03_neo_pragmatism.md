# Neo-Pragmatism

Neo-pragmatism — the revival and transformation of classical pragmatist themes in contemporary analytic philosophy — is associated primarily with Richard Rorty, Hilary Putnam, and Robert Brandom, though the three differ significantly in their conclusions. Each engages with the classical tradition (Peirce, James, Dewey) while responding to the analytic philosophy of Quine, Davidson, and Sellars.

**Rorty's eliminativism**

Rorty drew radical conclusions from pragmatist premises. He argued that "truth" and "reality" are terms that do more harm than good in philosophy: they generate unanswerable questions — what is the ultimate nature of truth? how does mind reach the world? — that should simply be dissolved. Philosophy should abandon the aspiration to be a "mirror of nature" and instead see knowledge as a form of social practice — coping, conversing, problem-solving — with no special relationship to mind-independent reality. Rorty's neo-pragmatism is thus anti-metaphysical: he does not try to give a better account of truth; he tries to show that we do not need one.

Rorty's argument (*Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*, 1979; *Consequences of Pragmatism*, 1982):

- P1: The traditional image of mind as a "mirror of nature" — a representational faculty that accurately depicts an independent world — generates the problems of modern epistemology (external world skepticism, the problem of other minds, the problem of induction).
- P2: Quine's dissolution of the analytic-synthetic distinction, Sellars's critique of the myth of the given, and Davidson's anomalous monism jointly undermine the mirror-of-nature picture without replacing it with a better representationalist account.
- P3: The right response is not a better representationalism but the abandonment of representationalism altogether.
- C: "Truth" should be deflated to a commendation of our beliefs and practices, not understood as correspondence to mind-independent reality.

Rorty's "neo-pragmatist" account of truth: "'true' is a compliment we pay to claims that we find useful, that survive our critical scrutiny, that fit into our ongoing conversation." This is not relativism in the sense that anything goes: some claims survive scrutiny better than others, and the standards of scrutiny are not arbitrary. But there is no meta-level perspective from which to validate the standards themselves as tracking mind-independent reality.

The Davidson connection: Davidson's argument that there is no coherent notion of "conceptual scheme" (alternative conceptual frameworks are possible only given a shared "scheme-content" distinction that itself cannot be coherently stated) is read by Rorty as supporting the abandonment of the scheme-independent reality that realists appeal to. Davidson and Rorty disagreed about this reading; Davidson insisted he was a realist.

**Putnam's pragmatism**

Putnam moved through several positions but retained pragmatist commitments throughout. His "internal realism" (1975–1990) was criticized as a form of idealism that made truth scheme-relative. His later "pragmatic realism" or "natural realism" (from roughly 1990 onward) rejected both internal realism and Rorty's eliminativism.

Putnam's natural realism: our perceptual experiences directly acquaint us with the world — there is no Cartesian "veil of ideas" between mind and world. We perceive tables, chairs, colors, and people directly; these are the real objects of knowledge. This is "realism with a human face": objective, mind-independent truth about the empirical world, but without the metaphysical inflation of representationalism.

Putnam's critique of Rorty: Rorty's dismissal of truth as mere commendation cannot make sense of the *norm* of inquiry — the distinction between what we believe and what we should believe, between what is accepted and what is correct. A genuine account of inquiry must preserve the regulative ideal of truth, even if it is "truth as internal to our practices."

**Brandom's inferentialism**

Brandom's *Making It Explicit* (1994) and *Articulating Reasons* (2000) develop a sophisticated neo-pragmatist philosophy of language in which the meaning of concepts is constituted by their role in inferential practice — what follows from what, what counts as a reason for what. This is the "inferentialist" account of content: concepts are understood not by their representational relation to a mind-independent world, but by their place in the web of inferential commitments and entitlements.

"True" functions as a device for endorsing propositions, in the disquotationalist spirit, but within a richer account of rational discourse. Brandom allies this with a "de-ontologized" Hegelianism in which norms of inquiry are socially instituted rather than discovered in an independent realm. The categories of thought are not Kantian a priori forms but socially developed and historically evolving frameworks that are nonetheless genuinely normative.

Brandom's response to the normativity objection to deflationism: the normativity of truth derives from the normativity of inferential commitments. A speaker who asserts *p* undertakes a commitment to defend *p*, to license others to infer from *p*, and to be criticized if *p* turns out to be false. These inferential obligations constitute the normative significance of truth-talk without requiring a substantive, mind-independent truth property. "True" functions as a "semantic anaphora" device: "That's true" inherits its semantic content from the proposition it endorses, enabling a speaker to endorse claims without repeating them.

**Shared features and divergences**

These neo-pragmatisms share the classical pragmatist skepticism about mind-independent "Truth with a capital T" while differing in how much of ordinary realist talk they are willing to recover. Rorty rejects most realist talk as philosophically harmful; Putnam recovers a robust commonsense realism while rejecting metaphysical realism; Brandom recovers the normative dimension of truth through inferential practice.

A key formal difference:
- Rorty: T(p) ≈ "I commend *p*" — truth is a speech act of endorsement, not a property.
- Putnam: T(p) ↔ *p* holds for our language, and "*p*" picks out real features of the world — but there is no further metaphysical story about correspondence.
- Brandom: T(p) is a device for "anaphoric inheritance" — it inherits the inferential commitments associated with *p*, enabling generalization and endorsement within a network of social inferential practice.

A central Rortyan argument: the history of philosophy is the history of attempts to identify "privileged representations" — mental states, sense-data, clear and distinct ideas, protocol sentences — that are directly in contact with reality and can serve as foundations for knowledge. All such attempts have failed. There are no privileged representations; all knowing is inferential, all knowledge is within a web of beliefs. The "mirror of nature" metaphor should be abandoned, and with it the correspondence theory of truth. Justification is a matter of what our community accepts as good reasons. "True" is what it is good to say given our current standards of inquiry.

The debate among neo-pragmatists about how much of realism to salvage reflects the broader tension in the tradition between the anti-representationalist impulse (deny the mind-world correspondence picture) and the normative impulse (preserve the distinction between truth and mere acceptance). Consider "Atoms exist" across the three neo-pragmatist positions:

Under Rorty's neo-pragmatism: "Atoms exist" is a well-entrenched part of our current scientific vocabulary; saying it "is true" is just commending it as part of our established practice of scientific inquiry. There is no further fact about atoms "existing mind-independently" that the statement tracks.

Under Brandom's inferentialism: "Atoms exist" generates a dense web of inferential commitments — about the predictions of quantum mechanics, the behavior of spectroscopy, the outcomes of particle collider experiments, the validity of atomic theory in chemistry. The "truth" of "atoms exist" is its being robustly embedded in this web — sustaining coherent inferential practice across physics, chemistry, and technology.

Under Putnam's later pragmatic realism: "Atoms exist" is simply true — atoms are real. The pragmatist insights about the role of practice in knowledge are compatible with atoms being genuinely there, independent of our theorizing about them. Pragmatism about method does not entail anti-realism about atoms.

This tension is not resolved in contemporary neo-pragmatism; it remains a productive fault line in the debate about truth.
