# Review Questions — Realism and Anti-Realism

1. The chapter distinguishes two separable realist commitments: an ontological thesis (mind-independent existence) and a semantic thesis (recognition-transcendent truth). Dummett argues that the real locus of the realism debate is the semantic thesis, not the ontological one. Is Dummett right that the semantic question is more fundamental? Could one be an ontological realist while accepting the anti-realist's epistemic constraint on truth?

2. Dummett's manifestation argument concludes that meaning cannot be given by recognition-transcendent truth conditions because such conditions could not be manifested in any recognizable behavior. Reconstruct the argument precisely. Then identify the premise the realist is most likely to challenge, and evaluate whether that challenge succeeds or merely restates the original dispute.

3. Bradley's Absolute Idealism holds that ordinary things and relations — including space, time, and causal relations — are "appearances" rather than ultimately real, because they involve internal contradictions. Russell and Moore rejected this. Assess whose critique of Bradley's regress argument is stronger: Russell's claim that facts are primitive, non-relational unities, or Moore's distinction between act of perception and its object. Does either critique fully dissolve the problem Bradley identifies?

4. Kant's transcendental idealism preserves what he calls "empirical realism" while maintaining "transcendental idealism." The two-aspect interpretation holds that phenomena and noumena are the same reality considered from two standpoints, while the two-object interpretation treats them as genuinely distinct domains. Which interpretation is more philosophically coherent? What turns on getting this right — for Kant's account of causality, his antinomies, or his epistemology?

5. Peirce defines truth as the outcome of ideal inquiry converging at the limit, while James defines it as what "works" in practice. The chapter notes that Peirce regarded James's account as a distortion of pragmatism. Articulate the sharpest version of Peirce's objection to James. Then consider whether Peirce's own account avoids the realist objection that truth precedes and is independent of any inquiry process.

6. Putnam's model-theoretic argument is supposed to undermine "metaphysical realism" by showing that no theory has a uniquely intended model. But Putnam later retreated from internal realism. Explain why the model-theoretic argument seems to generate conceptual relativity — different schemes yielding different counts of the same domain — and assess whether Putnam's later "pragmatic realism" is a stable resting point or itself vulnerable to similar problems.

7. Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism accepts realism about observables but counsels agnosticism about unobservables. The no-miracles argument contends that the predictive success of theories positing unobservables would be miraculous without approximate truth. The pessimistic meta-induction responds that past predictive success has not guaranteed truth. Evaluate which argument is stronger, and consider whether the distinction between "observable" and "unobservable" is principled enough to bear the weight van Fraassen places on it.

8. Rorty, Putnam, and Brandom all count as neo-pragmatists but reach strikingly different conclusions about how much realist vocabulary to retain. Rorty deflates truth to a term of commendation; Putnam preserves direct perceptual access to the world; Brandom grounds normativity in inferential practice. Which neo-pragmatist position best preserves what matters in the classical pragmatist tradition while remaining defensible against the realist charge that it cannot account for the normative distinction between what we believe and what we should believe?
