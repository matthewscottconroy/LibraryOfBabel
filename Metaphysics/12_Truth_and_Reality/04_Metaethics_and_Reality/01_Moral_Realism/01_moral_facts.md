# Moral Facts

Most of us are prepared to say that slavery is wrong — not just wrong for us, not just wrong by the standards of our culture, but wrong full stop. When we say this, we seem to mean that there is a fact about it. But what kind of fact? Where does it live in the world? This is the central question of moral metaethics, and it is also — as we have seen — a question in the theory of truthmakers.

Moral realism is the position that there are objective moral facts — facts about what is right and wrong, good and bad — that are not merely matters of opinion, cultural convention, or subjective response. When we say "torturing children for fun is wrong," we are not merely expressing an attitude or reporting a social norm; we are making a claim that is true independently of what anyone thinks or feels.

The case for moral facts rests on several observations. First, moral discourse has the surface grammar of fact-stating: we say "it is true that slavery is wrong" in the same form as "it is true that the Earth is spherical." Second, we disagree about moral questions in ways that presuppose there is a fact to be right about: debates about the ethics of capital punishment or euthanasia are not merely expressions of different tastes. Third, moral progress seems real: the abolition of slavery, the recognition of women's rights, and the decline of torture as legal punishment look like moral improvements, not merely moral changes. If moral facts did not exist, it is unclear in what sense these changes would constitute progress rather than mere change.

The formal argument:

- P1: Some moral claims are true ("slavery is wrong," "gratuitous cruelty is bad").
- P2: If a claim is true, there must be something in the world in virtue of which it is true — a fact.
- P3: The facts in virtue of which moral claims are true are moral facts.
- C: Moral facts exist — there is a domain of moral reality.

This argument mirrors the standard truthmaker argument. The anti-realist challenges P1 (expressivism, error theory) or P2 (quasi-realism, deflationism about moral truth) or P3 (naturalism, which reduces moral facts to natural facts, or constructivism, which makes "moral facts" into facts about rational agreement).

**The supervenience constraint**

A key constraint on moral realism is moral supervenience: moral facts cannot vary without corresponding variation in natural facts. If two situations are identical in every natural respect, they are identical in every moral respect. Formally:

□ ∀x ∀y ((∀φ ∈ N: φ(x) ↔ φ(y)) → (∀μ ∈ M: μ(x) ↔ μ(y)))

where N is the set of natural properties and M is the set of moral properties. This constraint is almost universally accepted: you cannot make a situation morally worse without making some natural change (adding more suffering, removing consent, etc.).

Supervenience is important for moral realism because it shows that moral facts are not "free-floating" but are tied to natural facts. But it does not show that moral facts are *identical* to natural facts (this would be naturalism) or that they are *reducible* to natural facts. Non-naturalists can accept supervenience while maintaining that the moral supervenes on the natural via a necessary connection that is itself non-natural.

**What kind of entity is a moral fact?**

Here moral realists diverge. *Naturalist realists* hold that moral facts are natural facts — facts about wellbeing, preference satisfaction, flourishing, or some other naturalistically describable state. *Non-naturalist realists* hold that moral facts are sui generis — irreducible to natural facts. G. E. Moore's "open question argument" was the classical case for this: for any natural property N, it is always an open, non-trivial question whether things that are N are good. This suggests that "good" does not mean the same as any natural predicate, and that moral properties are not natural properties.

*Cornell realists* (Peter Railton, Nicholas Sturgeon, David Brink) occupy a sophisticated middle ground: moral properties are natural but "higher-order" — constituted by clusters of natural properties without being identical to any single one. Sturgeon's "explanatory objection" to Mackie's error theory: if moral facts were illusory, we could not explain why we have the moral beliefs we have. But the explanation of moral beliefs typically invokes moral facts — the wrongness of slavery explains why abolitionists believed slavery was wrong. This explanatory role is evidence that moral facts are genuine.

Mackie's "argument from queerness" (*Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, 1977): if there were objective moral values, they would have to be "queer" — unlike anything else in the universe. They would have an intrinsic "to-be-pursuedness" built into them, which nothing in our natural ontology resembles. The epistemological corollary: if there were moral facts, we would need a special faculty — "moral sense" or moral intuition — to detect them, which is an additional and unexplained epistemological posit.

The realist's standard response: the queerness of moral facts is no greater than the "queerness" of mathematical facts or modal facts. If we accept that "necessarily, 7 is prime" is true and that we know it by reason rather than sensory perception, we have already accepted that some facts are accessible by a non-sensory faculty. Why should moral knowledge be more problematic than mathematical knowledge?

**Moral knowledge and moral progress**

The epistemology of moral realism faces a particular challenge: given that moral beliefs vary widely across cultures and history, how can we identify the reliable route to moral truth? The realist can point to mechanisms of moral progress that parallel the mechanisms of scientific progress: the expansion of the class of beings whose interests are taken seriously, the use of reason to achieve consistency in the application of moral principles, the appeal to considered intuitions as data for moral theory. Moral epistemology, like scientific epistemology, is fallible but not arbitrary.

Nicholas Sturgeon ("Moral Explanations," 1985) argued that moral facts play an indispensable explanatory role:

- P1: If moral facts exist and are causally efficacious, they should play a role in explaining why people hold the moral beliefs they do.
- P2: The wrongness of slavery explains (in part) why abolitionists were motivated to oppose it and why the abolitionist argument was persuasive.
- P3: The best explanation of the abolitionist movement invokes moral facts, not merely social or economic facts.
- C: Moral facts are real and causally efficacious — which is evidence for moral realism.

Mackie's counter: the wrongness of slavery, if it exists, is a non-natural, causally inert property. It cannot literally cause beliefs. The abolitionists' beliefs are fully explained by their natural psychological responses to the observable suffering of slaves — no moral fact is needed. Sturgeon responds: the suffering of slaves is constitutive of (or at least strongly connected to) the wrongness of slavery; so in citing the suffering, we are (on a naturalist reading) citing the moral fact, just described in natural terms.

If moral realism is true, the moral truth predicate satisfies the T-schema:

T_moral("torturing children for fun is wrong") ↔ torturing children for fun is wrong

And the right-hand side picks out a genuine moral fact — the wrongness of gratuitous child torture. This fact is mind-independent: it would hold even if no one believed it, even if different cultures had different norms about it, even if human psychology were different. The T-schema alone is acceptable to deflationists; the realist adds the substantive claim that the right-hand side picks out a genuine, mind-independent moral fact.
