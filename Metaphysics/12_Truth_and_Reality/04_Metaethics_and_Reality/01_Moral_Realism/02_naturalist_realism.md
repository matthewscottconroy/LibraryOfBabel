# Naturalist Moral Realism

The naturalist moral realist holds that moral properties are natural properties — or at least that moral facts supervene on and are constituted by natural facts — in a way that makes moral knowledge continuous with empirical knowledge. The view is realist (moral claims are true or false, and some are genuinely true) and naturalist (moral truth is grounded in the natural world rather than in a separate normative realm).

The tradition goes back to Aristotle's naturalistic account of the good as the flourishing of human nature (*eudaimonia*). Aristotle argued that "the good" for a human being is the activity of the soul in accordance with its essential function, which is rationality (*Nicomachean Ethics* I.7). This is a naturalistic claim: what is good is determined by what human nature essentially is. The good is not a non-natural property but a natural one — the fulfillment of the human form of life.

**Cornell realism**

Peter Railton, Nicholas Sturgeon, and David Brink developed the most influential contemporary versions, on which moral properties — like goodness, rightness, justice — are natural properties, though not necessarily identical to any simple natural description. Railton's "sophisticated consequentialism" identifies what is good for an agent with what that agent would want if fully informed and rational about herself and the world around her.

Railton's argument:

- P1: The good for an agent consists in what she would want if she were fully informed about herself, the world, and the relations between her desires.
- P2: Being fully informed is a natural condition — it is the psychological state of having no false beliefs and lacking no relevant information.
- P3: What a fully informed, rational agent would want is determined by her actual psychological constitution and circumstances.
- C: Goodness for an agent is a natural property — constituted by her informed, rational preferences, which are psychological-natural facts.

Sturgeon's contribution (*Moral Explanations*, 1985): the best explanation of certain historical phenomena — the abolitionist movement, the Holocaust, moral progress — invokes moral facts. If moral facts were not real, these explanations would fail. The realist can point to moral facts doing genuine explanatory work, just as physical facts do explanatory work in science. This is an abductive argument for moral realism: moral facts figure in our best explanations, so we are epistemically justified in believing in them.

Some naturalists treat moral properties functionally: "wrongness" is the property that plays a certain functional role in human moral reasoning, motivation, and practice. What plays this role varies with natural facts about human psychology and social cooperation. Lewis's functionalism about mind provides the model: mental properties are functional properties — the properties that play a certain functional role in the causal structure of a system. Just as pain is the property that plays the pain-role (causing distress, motivating avoidance behavior), wrongness is the property that plays the wrongness-role (motivating disapproval, warranting criticism).

**The open question argument and its answers**

The main objection to naturalist realism is G. E. Moore's open question argument. For any natural property N, it is always an open question whether something that is N is good. "Causing pleasure is good" does not seem analytically true — it can be doubted without contradiction. This suggests that moral predicates are not synonymous with natural ones.

Naturalists offer several responses. First, deny that synonymy is required for naturalistic reduction. Reduction can be empirical rather than conceptual. "Water" is identical to "H₂O" even though the proposition "water is H₂O" is not analytic — it was discovered empirically. Similarly, "goodness" might be identical to "flourishing" or "preference satisfaction" even if this identity is not analytic. The "open question" intuition reflects only that the identity is not analytic, not that it is false. (Boyd, Railton.) Second, accept the open question but deny its metaethical significance. The openness of "Is N good?" reflects the richness of the concept of goodness — it is not a simple natural concept, and it is appropriate that questions about its natural realization remain open for inquiry. Third, the functionalist reply: the open question arises because the functional role of goodness is not obviously filled by any single natural property. But this is compatible with goodness being constituted by whichever natural property fills the role in particular contexts.

**Moral epistemology for naturalists**

The naturalist has an advantage over the non-naturalist in moral epistemology: moral knowledge is continuous with ordinary empirical knowledge. We learn what is good for human beings by empirical investigation of human psychology, history, and social life — just as we learn about any other natural phenomena. Moral knowledge is fallible, revisable, and subject to the same norms as empirical knowledge generally.

The empirical method in ethics: moral inquiry begins with considered moral judgments (analogues of experimental data), constructs principles that organize and explain those judgments (analogues of theories), and then tests the principles against further considered judgments and empirical facts about human nature. This "reflective equilibrium" method (Rawls, 1971) is compatible with naturalistic moral realism.

The challenge for naturalists: moral intuitions that are widely shared may reflect evolutionary pressures rather than moral truth. If our moral intuitions are explained by their contribution to reproductive fitness rather than by their tracking moral facts, they provide no evidence for moral realism. Joyce (*The Evolution of Morality*, 2006) uses this "evolutionary debunking argument" against moral realism. Naturalists respond that evolved capacities can be truth-tracking — our perceptual capacities evolved but are nonetheless reliable — and that the debunking argument proves too much if it undermines all intuition-based evidence.

**The analogy with scientific realism**

Naturalist moral realism draws an explicit parallel with scientific realism. Just as we come to know physical facts by empirical inquiry, we come to know moral facts by moral inquiry. The epistemic methods are different in detail but the general structure is the same: we form hypotheses, test them against evidence, revise in the light of challenges, and converge on stable results. Railton draws the comparison explicitly: moral inquiry is like social science: we study human flourishing, suffering, preference satisfaction, and social cooperation empirically. Our moral knowledge grows as our empirical understanding of human psychology and social dynamics improves.

**Moral Twin Earth**

Horgan and Timmons's "moral twin earth" argument targets naturalistic moral realism specifically. Suppose there is a planet exactly like ours in all natural respects, but where the term "good" is connected to a different natural property (say, evolutionary fitness rather than flourishing). When inhabitants of moral twin earth say "X is good," are they saying the same thing we say when we say "X is good"? Intuition: no, they mean something different, even though the natural base is the same. This suggests that the reference of "good" is not fixed by natural properties alone — moral terms have a distinctive normative reference that goes beyond the natural.

Naturalist responses: the reference of natural kind terms is determined by causal-historical chains and the underlying nature of what is referred to; "good" refers to whatever natural property plays the goodness-role in our actual context. Moral twin earth inhabitants use "good" to refer to a different natural property, not the same property under a different description. The divergence in reference reflects a difference in the causal histories of the two communities' uses of "good."

Consider "Female genital mutilation is wrong." Under naturalistic moral realism (Railton's account): this is true because the practice causes severe suffering, removes sexual pleasure, involves the non-consensual permanent alteration of a person's body, and is not required for the flourishing of the people it is performed on. These are natural facts about suffering, bodily autonomy, and flourishing. The wrongness of the practice consists in these natural facts — it is constituted by the harm caused and the violation of bodily autonomy. Under the open question challenge: "Female genital mutilation causes severe suffering and removes autonomy — but is it wrong?" seems like an open question. The naturalist says: the openness reflects our uncertainty about the correct naturalistic theory of wrongness, not the existence of a further non-natural moral fact. Once we have the correct theory (wrongness consists in causing harm without proportionate benefit and without consent), the open question closes. Under non-naturalism: the wrongness of FGM is a non-natural moral fact that supervenes on the natural facts about suffering and autonomy. The natural facts are the supervenience base; the moral fact is irreducibly normative. The open question remains open because natural facts, however complete, do not analytically entail moral facts — the normative is irreducible to the descriptive.
