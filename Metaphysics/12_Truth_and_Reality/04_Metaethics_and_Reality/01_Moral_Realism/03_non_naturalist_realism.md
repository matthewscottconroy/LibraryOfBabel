# Non-Naturalist Moral Realism

Non-naturalist moral realism holds that moral properties — goodness, rightness, obligation — are real features of the world but are irreducible to natural properties. They occupy a distinct normative domain that is not straightforwardly physical, biological, or psychological. This position accepts the full reality of moral facts while denying that they can be explained in terms that a naturalistic science would recognize.

**Moore's open question argument**

The classic statement is G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903). Moore argued that "good" is a simple, unanalyzable property, not identical to any natural property. His "open question argument": for any natural property N, it is always a non-trivial, open question whether N things are good. If "good" meant "produces pleasure," then "does pleasure-producing X produce good?" would be trivially true by definition — but it is not trivially true; it is a substantive question. Therefore "good" does not mean "produces pleasure," and by similar reasoning, it does not mean any natural property. Goodness is a non-natural, sui generis property, knowable by a special faculty of moral intuition.

Formalized:

- P1: For any natural property N, the question "Is everything that is N good?" is an open question — it can be coherently denied without self-contradiction.
- P2: If "good" were identical to N, then "Is everything that is N good?" would be a trivial tautology, not an open question.
- P3: Therefore, "good" is not identical to N, for any natural property N.
- C: Goodness is a non-natural property.

Moore's naturalistic fallacy: the mistake of identifying goodness with a natural property. The fallacy is not merely a moral error but a logical one — confusing a normative predicate with a descriptive one.

**Contemporary non-naturalists**

Contemporary non-naturalists include Russ Shafer-Landau, Derek Parfit, and T. M. Scanlon. Parfit's *On What Matters* (2011) argues that normative truths are objective but not naturalistic — they are irreducibly normative. Parfit's "Triple Theory" identifies an overlapping consensus among consequentialism, Kantian deontology, and contractualism: where these theories agree, they converge on genuinely objective normative truths. The convergence is evidence that these truths are being tracked by moral reasoning, not merely constructed by it.

Shafer-Landau (*Moral Realism: A Defence*, 2003) argues that non-naturalism is the most defensible form of moral realism. Moral properties are sui generis — real but irreducible — and moral knowledge is possible through a form of rational intuition that, while fallible, is analogous to the non-empirical knowledge we have in logic and mathematics.

Scanlon's contractualism (*What We Owe to Each Other*, 1998) grounds moral facts in facts about what principles no one could reasonably reject. This is a form of constructivism — moral facts are constructed from facts about reasons — but Scanlon treats facts about reasons as irreducibly normative, making his view closer to non-naturalism than to naturalism.

**The epistemological challenge**

The main challenges for non-naturalism are epistemic and metaphysical. Epistemically: how do we know non-natural moral facts? If moral properties are not causally efficacious, they cannot cause our moral beliefs in the usual way. Moore posited "moral intuition" as the faculty of moral knowledge, but intuitions are unreliable and culturally variable. Metaphysically: non-natural properties seem to "float free" of the natural world in an explanatorily unsatisfying way.

The reliability objection (Mackie, Harman): our moral beliefs can be explained without invoking moral facts. The psychological, sociological, and evolutionary explanations of why we have the moral beliefs we have do not invoke mind-independent moral properties. If moral beliefs can be fully explained without moral facts, we have no good reason to posit moral facts.

Non-naturalist responses:

*(a) Companions in guilt:* mathematical beliefs face the same epistemological challenge. We cannot perceive mathematical objects; our mathematical beliefs are formed by processes not causally connected to abstract mathematical entities. Yet most philosophers accept that mathematical knowledge is possible. If mathematical non-naturalism is acceptable, moral non-naturalism is not uniquely problematic.

*(b) Reflective equilibrium and intuitions as data:* moral intuitions — strong, widely shared, considered reactions to moral cases — are evidence for moral truths even if they are not infallible. The method of reflective equilibrium treats considered intuitions as data and moral principles as theories that must account for the data.

*(c) Explaining the supervenience:* the supervenience of the moral on the natural needs explaining even on naturalism. The non-naturalist can say: the supervenience is a necessary brute connection — the natural facts necessitate the moral facts, but not because the moral facts reduce to the natural. The connection is a fundamental normative necessity, not a reduction.

**The metaphysical challenge: supervenience without reduction**

If moral properties are irreducibly non-natural but necessarily supervene on natural properties, there must be a necessary connection between the moral and the natural that is itself unexplained by reduction. This "superdupervenience" (Blackburn's term, deployed critically) seems to be a brute modal fact — a necessary but unanalyzable connection. Non-naturalists respond: many fundamental modal connections are brute — the necessity of arithmetic truths, the impossibility of something being both red and green all over at the same time. If we accept brute modal necessities elsewhere, the supervenience of the moral on the natural need not be more problematic.

**Intuitionism and the role of moral perception**

Moore's intuitionism held that we perceive basic moral truths by a direct, non-inferential faculty. W. D. Ross refined this in *The Right and the Good* (1930): we have *prima facie* duties (to keep promises, to help others, to avoid harm) that are self-evident to any reflective person. These duties are real moral truths, known by intuition, irreducible to consequences or natural facts.

Ross's argument for non-naturalism:

- P1: The duty to keep a promise is not derived from any facts about consequences — we are obligated to keep promises even when doing so produces slightly less good than breaking them.
- P2: If duties were reducible to consequential facts, the duty to keep a promise would be overridden whenever breaking it produced marginally more good.
- P3: But our strongest moral intuitions hold that promises generate genuine, though defeasible, obligations not reducible to consequences.
- C: Moral properties (duties, obligations) are not reducible to natural consequential facts.

Contemporary intuitionism (Audi, Huemer) defends a similar position: we have a fallible but genuine faculty of moral perception that gives us direct access to basic moral truths. This perception is not infallible — cultural conditioning, self-interest, and cognitive bias can distort it — but it is reliable enough to generate genuine moral knowledge when exercised under reflective conditions.

**Non-naturalism and truth**

For the non-naturalist, moral truths are as robustly true as any other truths. The T-schema applies: T("torturing children is wrong") ↔ torturing children is wrong. And the right-hand side picks out a genuine, mind-independent, non-natural moral fact. Moral truth is recognition-transcendent in Dummett's sense: "torturing children is wrong" was true before any human being recognized it, and would remain true even if cultural evolution had generated a different moral consensus. This gives non-naturalism a clear advantage over constructivism and quasi-realism: the truth of moral claims is not constituted by what rational agents would endorse or by what attitudes pass reflective scrutiny. It is constituted by the moral facts themselves — the irreducibly normative properties of actions, persons, and states of affairs.

Consider "The Holocaust was morally wrong." Non-naturalist analysis: the Holocaust was morally wrong not because of its consequences (though the consequences were horrific) and not because it violates principles that rational agents would construct — but because the actions themselves had the non-natural property of wrongness. The wrongness supervenes on the natural facts (the mass murder of millions of people, the systematic dehumanization, the use of state power to eliminate populations) but is not identical to those natural facts. The moral verdict is objective, mind-independent, and necessary given the supervenience base. Under this analysis, moral relativism is false: even if some culture or society had endorsed the Holocaust, it would still have been wrong — the social endorsement cannot create or destroy moral facts.
