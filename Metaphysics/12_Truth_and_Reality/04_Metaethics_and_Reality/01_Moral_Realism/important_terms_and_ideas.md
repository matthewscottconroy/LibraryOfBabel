# Important Terms and Ideas: Moral Realism

**Moral Realism**
The view that there are objective moral facts or truths—facts that are mind-independent, stance-independent, and not merely projections of human attitudes. Moral realism holds that sincere moral assertions can be true or false and that some are true.

**Non-Naturalism**
The form of moral realism holding that moral properties are sui generis—neither identical to nor reducible to natural (physical, biological, psychological) properties. Goodness is a distinctive kind of property, not just a natural kind. Moore is the canonical non-naturalist.

**Naturalist Moral Realism**
The form of moral realism that identifies moral properties with natural properties: goodness is identical to (or reducible to) some natural property like welfare-promotion, evolutionary fitness, or rational approval under ideal conditions.

**The Naturalistic Fallacy**
Moore's charge: any attempt to define good in natural terms commits a fallacy by conflating an evaluative concept (good) with a descriptive one. The open question argument: for any proposed natural definition of "good" (e.g., "good = pleasurable"), it is always an open question whether pleasurable things are really good.

**Stance-Independence**
A criterion for moral realism: moral facts are stance-independent if their truth does not depend on what any actual or hypothetical agent believes, desires, or approves. Realists hold that "torture is wrong" is true regardless of whether anyone believes or approves of this.

**Companions in Guilt**
An argument for moral realism by analogy: mathematical and modal truths are also non-natural, mind-independent truths that seem epistemically accessible without observation. If we accept mathematical realism, we should accept moral realism for analogous reasons. Anti-realists must either accept all or reject all.
