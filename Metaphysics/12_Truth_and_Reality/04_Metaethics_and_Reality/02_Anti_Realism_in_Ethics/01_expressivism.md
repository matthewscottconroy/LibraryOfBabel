# Expressivism

When I say "torture is wrong," am I reporting a fact about torture, or am I doing something else — expressing an attitude, endorsing a standard, projecting a commitment onto the world? Expressivism is the view that moral utterances do not express beliefs or make truth-apt claims about moral facts. Instead, they express the speaker's non-cognitive mental states: attitudes, emotions, commitments, or endorsements. On a simple emotivist version (Ayer, Stevenson), "slavery is wrong" means something like "slavery — boo!" It expresses disapproval, not a belief.

**The motivation**

The motivation for expressivism comes from Hume's "is-ought gap" and the perceived queerness of moral facts. If moral claims were factual, they would describe features of the world. But what features? Non-natural moral properties are ontologically puzzling and epistemically inaccessible. Natural properties cannot capture the normative "to-be-pursuedness" that moral properties are supposed to have. Better to say that moral discourse is not in the business of describing anything — it expresses and communicates attitudes.

Hume's insight: "In every system of morality which I have hitherto met with... the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning... when of a sudden I am surprised to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought" (*Treatise*, III.i.1). From "is" alone, "ought" cannot be derived; therefore "ought" must express something beyond what "is" statements describe.

A. J. Ayer's *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) gave the bluntest version: moral sentences are "ejaculations," neither true nor false. "Stealing is wrong" expresses the speaker's disapproval of stealing and is designed to evoke similar disapproval in hearers. C. L. Stevenson refined this in *Ethics and Language* (1944): moral sentences have both emotive meaning (expressing attitudes) and descriptive meaning (making ordinary empirical claims), and moral argument proceeds by trying to change people's attitudes through rational persuasion or non-rational techniques.

The argument:

- P1: Meaningful, factual statements are either analytically true or verifiable by experience (the logical positivist's verification criterion of meaning).
- P2: Moral statements are neither analytically true nor empirically verifiable.
- C1: Moral statements are not factual; they are not truth-apt.
- C2: The function of moral statements is expressive: they express emotional attitudes, not cognitive beliefs.

Even without logical positivism, the expressivist can argue:

- P1: If moral statements were truth-apt fact-stating claims, there would be mind-independent moral facts making them true.
- P2: The existence of mind-independent moral facts is metaphysically implausible (queerness) and epistemically inaccessible (no causal connection to moral knowers).
- C: Moral statements are not truth-apt fact-stating claims; they express attitudes.

**The Frege-Geach problem**

Simple emotivism faces the "Frege-Geach problem" (or embedding problem), identified by Peter Geach (1965). Moral predicates in unasserted contexts — "if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong" — cannot be expressions of disapproval, since nothing is being asserted. But the argument is valid, and validity requires that the moral predicate mean the same thing in the consequent as in the antecedent. The modus ponens:

- P1: If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong.
- P2: Lying is wrong.
- C: Getting your brother to lie is wrong.

This argument is valid. For it to be valid, "lying is wrong" must have the same content in P1 (embedded in a conditional), P2 (asserted), and C (derived). But if "lying is wrong" in P2 expresses disapproval — an emotive ejaculation — what does "lying is wrong" in P1 express? The conditional as a whole cannot be read as "if [boo!-lying], then [boo!-getting your brother to lie]" — this is not a coherent proposition with a truth value.

**Sophisticated expressivism: Blackburn and Gibbard**

Blackburn's quasi-realism and Gibbard's norm-expressivism are sophisticated responses to Frege-Geach. They develop richer expressive theories that can account for the logical behavior of moral predicates while retaining the anti-realist core.

Blackburn's approach (*Spreading the Word*, 1984; *Ruling Passions*, 1998): moral attitudes are not just first-order emotional reactions but complex states with internal logical structure. The conditional "if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong" expresses a higher-order commitment: a commitment not to hold a combination of attitudes — approving of lying while disapproving of getting one's brother to lie. The attitude expressed by the conditional is a second-order attitude toward combinations of first-order attitudes: it is the commitment to coherence in one's moral outlook.

Gibbard (*Wise Choices, Apt Feelings*, 1990; *Thinking How to Live*, 2003): moral judgments express "normative plans" — the speaker plans to act in certain ways and endorses this planning for anyone in similar circumstances. "Lying is wrong" expresses the plan not to lie. The conditional "if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong" expresses the plan not to get one's brother to lie given that one plans not to lie oneself. The logical relations among moral statements are relations among plans, which can be assessed for consistency and coherence.

Critics (Hale, Dreier, Schroeder) press that the expressivist's account of moral logic is not merely analogous to propositional logic but must be literally the same logic — otherwise the validity of moral arguments cannot be explained. Whether the sophisticated expressivist has fully solved this problem remains disputed.

**Expressivism and the T-schema**

A central challenge for expressivism is that the T-schema seems to apply to moral claims: "torturing children is wrong" is true iff torturing children is wrong. If the expressivist denies that moral sentences are truth-apt, she must explain why the T-schema apparently applies. The sophisticated expressivist's response: the T-schema is a deflationary truth device, not a substantive claim about correspondence. Expressing disapproval of X is what it is to "say truly that X is wrong." The truth predicate in "it is true that X is wrong" is just an endorsement device. No mind-independent moral fact is invoked.

This connects expressivism to deflationism about truth: if truth is merely disquotational, then the truth of a moral claim is just the claim itself, and the expressivist can accept moral "truths" without accepting moral facts. Horwich's minimalism about truth is thus a natural ally of expressivism: the minimal theory of truth applies to moral sentences just as to empirical ones, without committing to any substantive moral property.

When faced with valid-seeming arguments to unacceptable moral conclusions — utilitarian arguments that justify torturing one innocent person to save five — many philosophers argue by "tollensing the ponens": instead of accepting the conclusion, they reject a premise. For the expressivist, this reasoning structure is available: the valid argument from premises to moral conclusion generates a derived attitude that conflicts with entrenched attitudes. The conflict is resolved by revising one of the premises. The validity of the argument is not the validity of a deductive inference from truths to truths, but a coherence requirement on a system of attitudes.

Consider "Lying is wrong in all circumstances." An expressivist analysis: asserting this expresses the commitment that I disapprove of lying in all circumstances and endorse this attitude for anyone in relevantly similar situations. When confronted with lying to a murderer to protect an innocent victim, the expressivist says: the challenge is not that the claim is "false" (corresponds to no moral fact) but that the commitment expressed is one I cannot coherently sustain when I also hold the commitment to protect innocent lives. The revision is a revision of my attitude, not a correction of a factual error. Under non-naturalism: "lying is wrong in all circumstances" is false — there is a moral fact that lying to protect innocent lives is permissible (or even obligatory) in the relevant cases. The contrast illuminates the expressivist's distinctive picture: moral discourse is not a system of beliefs tracking facts but a system of attitudes governed by norms of coherence, consistency, and universalizability.
