# Error Theory

Error theory, developed by J. L. Mackie in *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977), takes a distinctive position in the moral anti-realism debate. Unlike expressivists, who say moral utterances are non-cognitive — they express attitudes, not beliefs — the error theorist holds that moral utterances are genuine belief-expressing claims. They purport to describe objective moral facts. But those facts do not exist. All positive moral claims are false; moral discourse is systematically in error.

**The error theorist's diagnostic claim**

Mackie's key move is to accept the "objectivist" analysis of ordinary moral language: when people say "slavery is wrong," they mean to assert that slavery has a mind-independent property of wrongness — a property that would exist regardless of whether anyone believed it, that would give everyone reasons to avoid slavery regardless of their existing preferences. This is what moral language purports to do. But this purported property does not exist. Hence, moral claims are systematically false.

The argument:

- P1 (Semantic claim): Moral assertions purport to describe objective, mind-independent moral facts — facts that would give anyone categorical reasons regardless of their desires.
- P2 (Metaphysical claim): There are no objective, mind-independent moral facts of this kind.
- C: All positive moral assertions ("X is wrong," "X is good") are false — moral discourse is systematically in error.

This distinguishes Mackie's error theory from expressivism. The expressivist denies P1: moral assertions do not purport to describe moral facts — they express attitudes. Mackie accepts P1 and denies the facts: moral assertions do purport to describe moral facts, and they fail.

**The argument from queerness**

Mackie's argument has two parts. The *argument from queerness*: if there were objective moral values, they would have to be "queer" — unlike anything else in the universe. They would be non-natural entities, neither physical nor psychological, with an intrinsic "to-be-pursuedness" built into them. Nothing in our natural ontology resembles this; therefore, we have no reason to believe such entities exist.

- P1: Objective moral values (if they existed) would be entities with an intrinsic prescriptivity — a property that commands action from anyone regardless of their desires.
- P2: Nothing in our established ontology (physical objects, natural properties, psychological states) has intrinsic prescriptivity.
- P3: If an entity is unlike anything in our established ontology, we should not posit it without compelling reason.
- C: We should not posit objective moral values.

The *argument from relativity*: the substantial variation in moral beliefs across cultures and history is best explained by the hypothesis that moral beliefs are shaped by social conditioning rather than by tracking independent moral facts. If there were objective moral facts, we would expect more convergence over time (as we see in mathematics and science). The divergence is evidence against objective moral facts.

**Mackie's positive account**

Mackie does not conclude that we should stop making moral judgments. He advocates a "second-order" revision of our moral practice: we can continue using moral language while understanding that its assertions are, strictly speaking, false. Moral claims function as useful social instruments for coordinating behavior, managing cooperation, and expressing collective commitments — even though they do not describe moral facts.

This is an instrumentalist view of moral practice: moral claims are useful fictions. Richard Joyce (*The Myth of Morality*, 2001) develops this position further, arguing for an "abolitionist" view of moral language: we should eventually replace it with non-moral vocabulary that is honest about its expressive and social functions.

**Companions in guilt objection**

The most powerful objection to Mackie's error theory is the "companions in guilt" argument. Mathematical objects are equally "queer" by Mackie's standards: they are abstract, non-physical, causally inert, and epistemically inaccessible in the way that Mackie charges moral properties with being. Yet Mackie did not advocate mathematical error theory. If the queerness argument does not apply to mathematics, why should it apply to ethics?

Mackie's response: mathematics does not involve prescriptivity — mathematical facts do not command action. Mathematical facts are in this sense less queer than moral facts. The queerness of prescriptivity is a distinctive feature of moral facts, not shared by mathematical ones. Counter-response: the prescriptivity of moral facts is itself a substantive philosophical claim. If we understand moral claims as expressions of the standards of a community (constructivism) or as natural facts about flourishing (naturalism), the prescriptivity is not metaphysically queer.

**Joyce's evolutionary debunking argument**

Joyce adds an evolutionary debunking argument: our capacity for moral judgment evolved because it conferred reproductive fitness on our ancestors, not because it tracked moral facts. The selection pressure that produced moral psychology was fitness, not truth. Therefore, moral intuitions cannot be treated as evidence for moral facts — they were selected for non-epistemic reasons.

Realist responses (Street, Enoch, Parfit): the evolutionary debunking argument proves too much — it also undermines logical and mathematical intuitions, since these evolved cognitive capacities were not selected for their truth-tracking properties directly. Enoch (2011) argues that there may be a "striking coincidence" between the fitness-promoting and the morally true — natural selection may have tracked moral facts by tracking what promotes flourishing, which is itself a moral fact.

**The semantic thesis: cognitivism**

The error theorist's semantic thesis — that moral sentences are truth-apt, cognitive claims — is not universally shared. The expressivist denies this semantic thesis: moral sentences are not truth-apt because they express attitudes, not beliefs. The error theorist and the realist agree on the semantics (moral sentences make claims that purport to be true) but disagree on the metaphysics (are the claims actually true?).

Mackie's reasoning: the best semantic theory of moral language is cognitivist — moral sentences purport to express beliefs about objective features of the world. This is what moral language looks like on its face: its grammar, its embedding in conditionals and negations, its role in reasoning — all presuppose that moral sentences have truth conditions. Given this semantic thesis, the options are: (a) the beliefs are true (moral realism), (b) the beliefs are systematically false (error theory), or (c) the semantic thesis is wrong (expressivism). Mackie argues that (c) does not capture the phenomenology of moral discourse — we experience ourselves as making factual claims about the world — so (b) is the honest conclusion.

Some philosophers accept Mackie's error theory while drawing a different practical conclusion: engage in moral fictionalism — treating moral claims as useful fictions that we assert without believing. Just as we can usefully reason within the fictional framework of Sherlock Holmes without believing Holmes is real, we can reason within the moral framework without believing moral claims are literally true. Fictionalists divide into "hermeneutic fictionalists" (who hold that ordinary moral discourse is already a kind of fiction that speakers implicitly understand as such) and "revolutionary fictionalists" (who hold that once we understand the error theory, we should revise our moral discourse to make it explicitly fictional). The hermeneutic approach is difficult to sustain empirically: most moral speakers seem sincerely to believe that moral claims are literally true.

Jonas Olson (*Moral Error Theory*, 2014) extends Mackie's error theory beyond ethics to all normativity — including epistemic norms and prudential norms. If the error theory is correct about moral claims, it should apply equally to "you ought to believe what the evidence supports" (epistemic) and "you ought to pursue your long-term interests" (prudential). The self-undermining objection: if all normative claims are false, then "you ought to believe what the evidence supports" is false, and error theory itself has no normative authority. Olson's response: the error theory is true regardless of whether we ought to believe it; truth and rational obligation are independent.

Consider "Apartheid was morally wrong." Under error theory: "Apartheid was morally wrong" is a belief that purports to describe an objective moral property of the apartheid regime. But no such property exists — there are no objective moral properties. The claim is false, strictly speaking. The natural facts (systematic racial discrimination, deprivation of rights, violence, economic exploitation) are real; the claim that these facts constitute an objective wrongness is the false part. The error theorist does not advocate moral quietism; she advocates moral honesty: understanding what moral discourse actually is, rather than what it falsely purports to be.
