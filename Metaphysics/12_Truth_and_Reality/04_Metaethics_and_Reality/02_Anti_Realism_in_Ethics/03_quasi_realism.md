# Quasi-Realism

The expressivist says moral judgments express attitudes rather than beliefs. But we do not talk that way. We say moral claims are true or false, that we know some of them, that moral progress is real, that someone who endorses torture is not just differently inclined but genuinely wrong. Expressivism seems to force a wholesale revision of our moral practice — or, worse, to expose ordinary moral discourse as an elaborate confusion about its own nature. Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism is the project of showing that this revision is not needed. The expressivist does not have to apologize for moral discourse; she can earn the right to every piece of it. The question is whether that project can succeed without quietly reinstating the realism it set out to avoid.

**Earning the right: the five-stage progression**

Blackburn begins from the basic expressivist starting point: moral judgments express practical attitudes — commitments, endorsements, plans — rather than cognitive beliefs. "Torturing children is wrong" expresses the speaker's disapproval of child torture, her commitment not to torture, and her endorsement of similar attitudes in others. It does not report a moral fact.

This starting point threatens the architecture of ordinary moral discourse: if moral judgments are not truth-apt, moral reasoning cannot be valid in the familiar sense, "it is true that torture is wrong" cannot be literally true, moral knowledge becomes puzzling, and the very idea of moral progress seems incoherent. Blackburn's response is to trace a developmental story — five stages in the maturing of our expressive practice — at the end of which all this vocabulary can be recovered.

We begin with basic expressive attitudes: approval, disapproval, commitment. We develop sensibilities — structured, interrelated sets of attitudes that form a coherent moral outlook. We develop higher-order attitudes: attitudes about which combinations of first-order attitudes are permissible. I can disapprove of lying; but if I also approve of getting my brother to lie when it is convenient, my attitudes are not merely different — they are in practical incoherence, an analogue to logical inconsistency. We develop practical identities, stable characters that sustain our attitudes through time and give them the form of settled conviction rather than passing whim. And we arrive at something that functions like moral belief: a stable, reflectively endorsed, coherence-governing attitude expressed in the grammar of truth-apt assertion.

At this endpoint, to call something "morally true" is not to point toward a mind-independent moral fact. It is to express the claim that the relevant attitude is stable, coherence-satisfying, survives reflection and full information, and appropriately governs conduct:

T_moral(p) iff p represents a stable, reflectively endorsed, coherence-satisfying moral attitude — one that survives scrutiny and appropriately governs conduct in morally relevant situations.

The quasi-realist earns the right to say "it is true that slavery is wrong" not by discovering an objective moral property but by showing that this is what it is to speak of "moral truth" within a mature expressive practice.

**The Frege-Geach problem revisited**

The embedding problem returns here with some force. Blackburn offers a formal treatment in which moral predicates appearing in unasserted contexts express higher-order attitude commitments rather than first-order approvals or disapprovals. The conditional "if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong" is rendered as:

[Disapproves of lying] → [Disapproves of getting one's brother to lie]

This is not a conditional with a truth value in the ordinary sense; it expresses a higher-order commitment: not to combine the attitude of disapproving of lying with the attitude of approving of getting one's brother to lie. The commitment has logical structure — one can be consistent or inconsistent with it. The modus ponens then runs:

- P1: "If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong" expresses the higher-order commitment H: not to combine [disapproval of lying] with [approval of getting one's brother to lie].
- P2: "Lying is wrong" expresses [disapproval of lying].
- C: To be consistent with H and [disapproval of lying], one must have [disapproval of getting one's brother to lie] — that is, one must "infer" "getting your brother to lie is wrong."

The modus ponens structure is preserved, but in terms of consistency among attitudes rather than truth-preserving inference among beliefs. Critics (Hale, Schroeder) press that this is not literally the same logic — the consistency relation among attitudes is not obviously identical to the deductive validity of propositional inference — and that quasi-realism therefore cannot explain what the validity of moral arguments consists in. Whether Blackburn has fully answered this objection remains disputed.

**The too-much and too-little objection**

Critics press that quasi-realism faces a dilemma: it delivers either too much or too little. Too much: by the end of the quasi-realist's project, we seem to have genuine moral truths — truths about which attitudes are stable, coherent, and reflectively endorsed. At this point, the expressivist starting point appears irrelevant. We have, in effect, reinvented moral realism under an expressivist description. Dreier (2004) presses this as the "expressivism-equivalent" objection: if the quasi-realist earns every piece of realist vocabulary — moral truth, moral knowledge, moral objectivity — what distinguishes her position from moral realism?

Too little: the "truths" delivered are not mind-independent in the way the realist wants. "Torturing children is wrong" is quasi-true if the appropriate attitudes are stable and pass coherence tests. But these are contingent psychological-social facts: facts about which attitudes survive reflection given human psychological constitution and social history. The moral realist wants truth that is independent of the attitudes we happen to have — even idealized attitudes.

Blackburn accepts the first horn. Quasi-realism is designed to end up in a position that sounds like realism; that is the point. The project is not to avoid realism-sounding conclusions but to explain why we are entitled to them without positing queer non-natural moral entities. The quasi-realist's realism is earned rather than posited — it is the endpoint of a progressive justification from the expressivist base, not a brute ontological assumption. The difference from genuine moral realism is not in the vocabulary but in the underlying metaphysics: the expressivist still holds that there are no mind-independent moral facts, that the "truths" of the mature practice are grounded in attitudes rather than in facts about a normative realm.

Notice that this places quasi-realism in proximity to moral constructivism (Korsgaard, Street), which grounds moral facts in facts about what practically rational agents would endorse. The key difference is subtle: constructivists typically present their view as a form of moral realism — there are moral facts, but constructed ones, constituted by rational endorsement. Quasi-realists resist the "moral facts" framing: we have attitudes and practices that earn the vocabulary of facts, not facts themselves in any robust sense. Whether this is a genuine metaphysical distinction or a terminological one is itself a live question — and the answer may depend on what "genuine moral fact" means, which is, of course, the original question the entire debate is trying to settle.
