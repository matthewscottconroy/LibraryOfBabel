# Important Terms and Ideas: Anti-Realism in Ethics

**Moral Non-Cognitivism**
The view that moral utterances are not truth-apt—they do not express beliefs that can be true or false. They express attitudes (emotivism), prescriptions (prescriptivism), or endorsements of norms (expressivism). Since they have no truth value, there is nothing for moral realism to be right about.

**Emotivism**
Ayer and Stevenson's non-cognitivism: moral utterances express the speaker's emotional attitudes toward the action described. "Stealing is wrong" expresses disapproval of stealing; it does not describe a moral fact. Disagreement in ethics is disagreement in attitude, not belief.

**Error Theory (Mackie)**
The cognitivist but anti-realist view: moral judgments are truth-apt and purport to describe objective moral facts, but since no such facts exist, all positive moral claims are false. We are systematically in error when we make moral claims.

**The Argument from Queerness**
Mackie's argument: if there were objective moral properties, they would be "queer" entities—unlike anything else in the universe, motivating action purely by being known. This metaphysical and epistemological queerness makes objective moral facts implausible.

**Quasi-Realism**
Blackburn's project: starting from an expressivist position, earn the right to use moral realist language—including talk of moral truth, moral knowledge, and moral facts—by showing that such talk is legitimated by the normative functions of moral discourse, without positing objective moral facts.

**Moral Fictionalism**
Joyce's view: moral discourse is a useful fiction—we act as if moral claims are objectively true because this is pragmatically valuable, while knowing that moral realism is false. We should use moral language without believing it is literally true.
