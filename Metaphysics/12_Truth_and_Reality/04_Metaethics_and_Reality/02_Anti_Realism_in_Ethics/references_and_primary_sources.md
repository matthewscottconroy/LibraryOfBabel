# References and Primary Sources: Anti-Realism in Ethics

## Foundational Texts

- Ayer, A.J., *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936), Chapter 6. The emotivist non-cognitivism: moral utterances are not truth-apt; they express the speaker's emotional attitudes rather than describing moral facts. "Stealing is wrong" expresses disapproval, not a fact.

- Mackie, J.L., *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977). The error theory: moral judgments purport to describe objective, intrinsically prescriptive facts, but no such facts exist. All positive moral claims are false; moral properties would be "queer" entities unlike anything in the natural world.

- Blackburn, Simon, *Spreading the Word* (1984). Quasi-realism: we can earn the right to moral realist talk while maintaining an expressivist base. Moral attitudes can be "projected" onto the world and subjected to norms of rationality without positing objective moral facts.

## Contemporary Works

- Gibbard, Allan, *Wise Choices, Apt Feelings* (1990). Norm-expressivism: moral judgments express endorsement of norms of conduct; rationality consists in having norms that are collectively acceptable.

- Joyce, Richard, *The Myth of Morality* (2001). Defends the error theory; argues that the fiction of objective moral requirements is evolutionarily explicable but false. We should engage in moral fictionalism—using moral discourse while knowing it is not literally true.
