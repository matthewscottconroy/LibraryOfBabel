# Aesthetics and Reality

Is there objective beauty in the world? When we say that Beethoven's Ninth is more beautiful than elevator music, are we reporting a fact about the musical works themselves, or merely expressing our subjective preferences? Aesthetic realism holds that there are genuine aesthetic properties — beauty, sublimity, elegance, tragedy — that objects have independently of any observer's reaction to them. Aesthetic anti-realism holds that aesthetic properties are in some sense response-dependent: they are constituted by or essentially connected to the responses of suitably qualified observers.

The debate between aesthetic realism and anti-realism is complicated by the distinctive features of aesthetic experience. Aesthetic responses are not arbitrary — there are standards of taste, and judgments can be more or less appropriate, more or less informed. Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" argued that while aesthetic judgments are expressions of sentiment rather than descriptions of fact, they are nonetheless criticizable: some sentiments reflect greater sensitivity, cultivation, and experience than others, and those sentiments are the standard against which aesthetic judgments are measured.

Kant's treatment of aesthetic judgment in the Critique of Judgment is more nuanced. Kant distinguished pure aesthetic judgments of taste ("This is beautiful") from mere expressions of subjective pleasure. Pure aesthetic judgments claim universal validity — we expect others to agree — and are not merely about our subjective states. But they are not objective in the sense of scientific judgments; the ground of the judgment is a free play of imagination and understanding that is peculiar to aesthetic experience.

Contemporary debates in aesthetics have focused on whether aesthetic properties are "natural" — supervening on non-aesthetic, physical properties — or "non-natural" — sui generis evaluative properties. The supervenience of aesthetic properties on physical properties seems clear (two artworks that are physically indistinguishable would be aesthetically indistinguishable), but whether aesthetic properties are reducible to physical or functional properties is contested.

This section examines aesthetic realism and anti-realism, the supervenience of aesthetic properties, and the relationship between aesthetic value and other forms of value.
