# Aesthetic Realism

When two people argue about whether Beethoven's Fifth is more beautiful than a pop song, something about the exchange looks like genuine disagreement — not like two people reporting different preferences, but like two people trying to get something right. We correct aesthetic judgments; we defer to critics with trained perception; we speak of aesthetic education as the development of an ability to see what is there. None of this looks like the mere cultivation of taste. It looks like the acquisition of perceptual accuracy. Aesthetic realism is the attempt to take this appearance seriously: aesthetic properties — beauty, sublimity, elegance, profundity — are real features of objects, existing independently of any particular response to them, just as physical properties do.

**The case for objective aesthetic properties**

The case for aesthetic realism runs parallel to the case for moral realism, and its argumentative structure is similar. Aesthetic disputes have the form of genuine disagreements: when one person says that Rembrandt's late self-portraits have a depth that a competent contemporary artist lacks, and another denies it, they seem to be saying incompatible things, not reporting incompatible preferences. Genuine disagreements presuppose a fact of the matter — something that makes one party right and the other wrong. The fact of the matter in aesthetic disputes is whether the objects in question possess the relevant aesthetic properties.

We can put this as an argument:

- P1: Aesthetic disputes ("Beethoven is beautiful," "Beethoven is not beautiful") are genuine disagreements, not mere reports of different preferences.
- P2: Genuine disagreements presuppose that there is a fact of the matter — something that makes one party right and the other wrong.
- P3: The fact of the matter in aesthetic disputes consists in whether the objects assessed have the relevant aesthetic properties.
- C: Aesthetic properties are objective features of objects — aesthetic realism is true.

A separate but reinforcing argument comes from the normativity of aesthetic education. We teach people to hear music, to read poetry, to see painting; we distinguish critics with trained perceptual capacity from those who lack it; we treat some aesthetic judgments as more authoritative than others. This normative structure presupposes that there is something to be right or wrong about — something beyond mere preference. The music teacher who corrects a student's assessment of a Beethoven sonata is not merely transmitting different tastes; she is claiming the student is missing something that is there to be heard.

**Zangwill's realism and aesthetic supervenience**

Nick Zangwill offers the most sustained contemporary defense of aesthetic realism in *The Metaphysics of Beauty* (2001). He draws a distinction between aesthetic properties (beauty, elegance, sublimity, gracefulness) and non-aesthetic base properties (colors, shapes, textures, tonal intervals) and argues that aesthetic properties are genuine properties that supervene on non-aesthetic ones without being reducible to them. The beauty of a melody is grounded in its sonic properties — its intervals, rhythms, dynamics — but is not identical to any particular combination of sonic properties. The aesthetic transcends the physical base while necessarily following from it.

The formal representation of aesthetic supervenience:

□ ∀x ∀y ((∀φ ∈ Base: φ(x) ↔ φ(y)) → (∀α ∈ Aes: α(x) ↔ α(y)))

where Base is the set of non-aesthetic perceptual properties and Aes is the set of aesthetic properties. Two objects indistinguishable in all their non-aesthetic perceptual properties cannot differ aesthetically. This constraint is nearly universally accepted even by anti-realists: you cannot change an object's aesthetic properties without changing something in its perceptual-physical base. The constraint does not show that aesthetic properties reduce to base properties — that would be aesthetic naturalism — but it does show that aesthetic facts are tied to the world in a non-arbitrary way.

Zangwill's position on the nature of aesthetic properties is closest to option (c) of the three available: beauty is a supervenient property, real and grounded in the perceptual base, but not identical to any natural combination of base properties. This is the aesthetic analogue of non-naturalist moral realism, and it faces the same epistemological challenge: if aesthetic properties are not identical to natural properties, how do we perceive or detect them?

Frank Sibley's "Aesthetic Concepts" (1959) bears on this question directly. Sibley distinguished aesthetic terms (beautiful, elegant, graceful, balanced) from non-aesthetic terms (red, square, loud, smooth) and argued that aesthetic terms are not condition-governed in the way non-aesthetic terms are. No finite set of non-aesthetic descriptions is sufficient for the correct application of an aesthetic predicate. Whether a painting is balanced cannot be determined by listing its colors and shapes; it requires an act of aesthetic perception. This non-condition-governed character of aesthetic judgment supports realism: aesthetic properties are genuinely there to be perceived, but perception of them requires capacities that go beyond mechanical processing of the base properties.

**Hume, the standard of taste, and the epistemological challenge**

Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757) occupies a position that sharpens the epistemological question. Hume acknowledged the apparent subjectivity of taste — "beauty is no quality in things themselves: it exists merely in the mind which contemplates them" — but also argued that some aesthetic judgments are better than others, and that the judgment of the "true judge" (one with delicacy of taste, extensive practice, careful comparison, and freedom from prejudice) is authoritative. The existence of a standard of taste, for Hume, does not depend on the existence of mind-independent aesthetic properties; it depends on facts about the conditions under which responses are formed.

The realist can read Hume differently. The trained judge whose responses are authoritative is authoritative precisely because her responses reliably track objective aesthetic properties of objects. On this reading, Hume's standard of taste is not a substitute for objectivity but a specification of the epistemic route to it: we come to know objective aesthetic facts by developing the perceptual capacities of the true judge.

The main challenges to this picture are the argument from disagreement and the argument from the felt, subjective character of aesthetic experience. Persistent disagreement among qualified observers is hard to explain if aesthetic properties are objective: equally trained perceivers should converge on the same properties. The realist's response is to note that similar disagreement occurs for color — color experience admits of genuine variation — without our concluding that colors are not objective. Aesthetic experience, moreover, has a characteristic felt quality — the delight or pleasure associated with beauty — that seems to make aesthetic judgment more like a response than a detection. Realists counter that felt quality does not entail response-dependence: pain feels a certain way, but pain is a brain state with objective features, and the felt quality is the way we access it, not a sign that the underlying property is mind-dependent.

Consider "Beethoven's Ninth Symphony is sublime." Under aesthetic realism, this attributes a genuine property — sublimity — to the symphony. The property is grounded in the symphony's structural features (the dramatic dynamic contrasts, the monumental scale of the finale, the transition from the dark opening to the choral affirmation) but is not identical to them. Sublimity is a genuine property that appropriately equipped listeners can detect, existing independently of whether any particular listener happens to be moved. Under aesthetic anti-realism, there is no such property in the symphony; the sublimity is in the response, not the object. The realist's advantage is that she can explain why the response is the appropriate way to form the judgment — because the response tracks the property — without making the property constitutively response-dependent. The anti-realist's advantage is that she avoids the epistemological posit of a sui generis aesthetic faculty. This tension sets up the response-dependence framework developed in the final file of this module.
