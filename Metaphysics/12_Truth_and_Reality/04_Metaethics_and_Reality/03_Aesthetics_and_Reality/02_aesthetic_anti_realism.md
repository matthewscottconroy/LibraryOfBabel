# Aesthetic Anti-Realism

Beauty feels like it is in the object — in the sweep of the melody, the proportions of the building, the pacing of the sentence. But consider how differently people respond to the same work, how what one culture regards as a supreme achievement leaves another cold, how tastes shift across generations in ways that cannot all be tracked to improvements in perceptual competence. The aesthetic realist holds that beneath this variation there are objective properties being tracked with varying degrees of success. The aesthetic anti-realist thinks the variation is the point: what we call aesthetic properties are constituted by, or essentially dependent on, the responses of observers, not features of objects that exist independently of any response. The core of anti-realism is the thought that "beautiful" belongs to the eye of the beholder — though the different anti-realist views disagree sharply about whose eye, under what conditions, and whether "beautiful" really attributes anything at all.

**Subjectivism, relativism, and their limits**

The simplest version — simple subjectivism — reads "X is beautiful" as meaning "I find X pleasing." This captures the intimate connection between aesthetic judgment and aesthetic response: we call things beautiful when we experience them as beautiful. But it immediately generates the objection that it trivializes aesthetic disagreement. If "beautiful" means "pleasing to me," then when I say "Beethoven is beautiful" and you say "Beethoven is not beautiful," we are not disagreeing at all: I am reporting that Beethoven pleases me, you are reporting that Beethoven does not please you. These are compatible claims. But our ordinary understanding of aesthetic dispute is that we are genuinely disagreeing — that there is something we are both trying to get right.

The subjectivist can appeal to Stevenson's notion of "disagreement in attitude": aesthetic discourse expresses and attempts to influence attitudes, not report beliefs. But this still leaves ordinary aesthetic practice looking like a confused enterprise — speakers who think they are disagreeing are merely expressing different preferences at each other. This parallel with emotivism about ethics is instructive: the subjectivist account of aesthetics is as uncomfortable to maintain as the emotivist account of morality.

Relativism offers a more sophisticated position: "X is beautiful" means "X is beautiful relative to the standards of my community." Aesthetic truth becomes relative: Beethoven is beautiful relative to classical music standards; a performance style that seems coarse by those standards may be excellent by the standards of a different tradition. This preserves the idea that aesthetic judgments can be correct or incorrect — relative to a framework. But it implies that aesthetic disagreements across communities are merely verbal: each party is right relative to their own standards, and there is no further question about which standards are correct. This undercuts the normativity that aesthetic practice seems to require — we want to say not just that Beethoven is beautiful by our standards but that he is simply beautiful, or at least that trained judgment by better standards gives a more accurate assessment.

**Projectivism and irrealism**

Following Hume, the projectivist holds that we project aesthetic qualities onto objects: beauty is not in the objects but in the mind that perceives them, attributed outward. "Beautiful" seems to denote a property of the object, but this is a grammatical illusion — the "property" is really our disposition to respond in certain ways, cast as if it were an objective feature. Hume makes the point directly in "Of the Standard of Taste" (1757): "the mind spreads its own sentiments over external objects, making what is a quality merely of the response seem like a quality of the thing." This is analogous to the projection of secondary qualities — redness seems to be a property of the tomato, but on the Humean account it is really a quality of our visual experience, projected onto the object.

Some philosophers — Walton, Scruton — press further into irrealism: aesthetic terms do not pick out properties at all, in any robust sense. Scruton's irrealism (*The Aesthetics of Architecture*, 1979; *Beauty*, 2009) holds that aesthetic experience is a mode of understanding, a way of relating to objects that is not reducible to either perceptual detection or emotional response. The concept of beauty is not empirical but interpretive: it belongs to the hermeneutic dimension of human life, the dimension in which we create and sustain meaning rather than merely register facts. "Beethoven's Fifth is beautiful" does not describe the symphony; it invites others into a particular mode of listening, expresses and sustains a shared form of life around great music, articulates a value the speaker recommends. Aesthetic discourse on this view has genuine cultural and social functions without needing to attribute real aesthetic properties.

**The normativity objection and the ideal observer response**

The recurring problem for all anti-realist views is accounting for the apparent normativity of aesthetic judgment. We think some aesthetic judgments are better than others. Aesthetic education involves learning to see more accurately, not merely to have different preferences. The music teacher who corrects a student's assessment of a sonata is not merely expressing a different preference; she is claiming the student is missing something — some feature of the music that is there to be heard.

We can put this more formally:

- P1: Aesthetic education is possible: people can be taught to hear, see, or read more accurately in aesthetic terms.
- P2: If aesthetic judgments were merely expressions of preference (subjectivism) or merely relative to standards (relativism), aesthetic education would be mere preference-shaping or standard-transmission — not the development of perceptual accuracy.
- P3: But aesthetic education seems to develop perceptual accuracy — the ability to detect features of objects that are really there.
- C: Aesthetic judgments are not merely expressions of preference or reports of relative standards; they have objective content.

The sophisticated anti-realist response is the ideal observer theory. "X is beautiful" is true iff an ideal observer O*, in ideal conditions C*, would respond to X with aesthetic pleasure:

- P1: "X is beautiful" is true iff an ideal observer O*, in ideal conditions C*, would respond to X with aesthetic pleasure.
- P2: Ideal conditions include unimpeded perception, adequate knowledge of the art form, freedom from distraction, sufficient reflection.
- P3: These conditions are specifiable in non-circular terms.
- C: Aesthetic truth is grounded in the responses of ideal observers under ideal conditions.

This preserves genuine truth-aptness: there is a fact about what the ideal observer would feel. It grounds the normativity of aesthetic education: to improve one's aesthetic judgment is to approximate the ideal conditions. And it explains disagreement: most actual aesthetic disagreement involves failures to approximate those conditions, not genuine conflict among fully qualified judges. The challenge — the circularity problem — is to specify what makes an observer or conditions "ideal" without implicitly relying on the concept of correct aesthetic judgment. If the ideal observer is defined as the one who reliably makes correct aesthetic judgments, the account says: x is beautiful iff it would be judged beautiful by someone who judges beautiful things correctly — which is circular. Hume's "true judge" in "Of the Standard of Taste" is characterized by delicacy of imagination, practice in aesthetic comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense — conditions arguably specifiable without invoking aesthetic correctness directly, and which anticipate the response-dependence framework developed in the next file.

**Aesthetic anti-realism and truth**

Under aesthetic anti-realism, the T-schema for aesthetic claims:

T("the Parthenon is beautiful") ↔ the Parthenon is beautiful

can still be accepted, but the right-hand side is understood anti-realistically. "The Parthenon is beautiful" does not pick out a mind-independent aesthetic property of the Parthenon; it expresses (subjectivism), is relativized to standards (relativism), or projects our response onto the object (projectivism). The truth of the aesthetic claim is not recognition-transcendent; it is constituted by or depends essentially on our responses. This connects to Dummett's anti-realism more broadly: if aesthetic truth is not recognition-transcendent, then excluded middle may fail for aesthetic claims — "the Parthenon is beautiful or the Parthenon is not beautiful" may not be determinately true for claims that exceed any possible basis in response. The Dummettian anti-realist should endorse intuitionist logic for aesthetic discourse, just as she endorses it for any domain where truth is epistemically constrained. Consider "Jackson Pollock's *Number 31* is a great work of art." Under subjectivism: this reports the speaker's aesthetic experience of the painting, and someone who finds it visually chaotic and unrewarding is not disagreeing but reporting a different experience. Under ideal observer theory: *Number 31* is great iff an ideal observer — one with extensive experience of abstract expressionism, knowledge of Pollock's technique and aims, freedom from cultural prejudice, and adequate time for careful attention — would judge it great. Under projectivism: we project "greatness" onto the painting on the basis of our experience of it; the property is in the response, expressed as if it were in the object. The contrast with aesthetic realism is sharp: the realist says the properties are there to be detected; the anti-realist says the properties are in the detecting. The response-dependence framework tries to articulate a third position.
