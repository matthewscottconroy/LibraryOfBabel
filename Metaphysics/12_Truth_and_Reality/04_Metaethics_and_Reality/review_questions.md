# Review Questions — Metaethics and Reality

1. Moral supervenience — no moral difference without a natural difference — is accepted by nearly every party in the metaethical debate. Yet it is compatible with naturalism, non-naturalism, and expressivism alike. Explain precisely what supervenience does and does not establish. In particular, why does accepting supervenience not settle whether moral properties reduce to natural properties?

2. Moore's open question argument concludes that "good" is not identical to any natural property because for any such property N, "Is N good?" remains a substantive, open question. Cornell realists respond that reduction need not be conceptual — "water is H₂O" is not analytic either. Assess this analogy. Is the disanalogy that Horgan and Timmons's "moral twin earth" argument reveals — a divergence in reference that the natural-kind model does not capture — a decisive objection to naturalist realism?

3. Mackie's error theory accepts that moral language makes genuine, truth-apt claims but holds those claims are systematically false because no objective moral properties exist. Expressivism denies that moral language makes truth-apt claims at all. Both positions end up with a form of moral anti-realism. Compare their costs: which position has a harder time accounting for the surface grammar of moral discourse, the phenomenology of moral commitment, and the apparent validity of moral arguments?

4. The Frege-Geach problem challenges simple expressivism by pointing out that moral predicates must have the same semantic content in asserted and unasserted contexts for moral arguments to be valid. Blackburn's quasi-realism responds by interpreting the conditional "if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong" as expressing a higher-order commitment to consistency among attitudes. Does this response genuinely preserve the validity of moral arguments, or does it merely describe an analogue of validity that is not validity in the strict logical sense?

5. Blackburn argues that quasi-realism can "earn the right" to every piece of realist moral vocabulary — truth, knowledge, objectivity — without positing mind-independent moral facts. Critics reply that by the end of this project, quasi-realism is indistinguishable from moral realism. Assess this objection. Is there a genuine, philosophically substantive difference between quasi-realist moral "truth" and what a moral constructivist or a minimal moral realist would call moral truth?

6. The "companions in guilt" response is used both in metaethics (against Mackie's queerness argument) and in philosophy of mathematics (against analogous skepticism about abstract objects). In each case, the response says: your objection applies equally to a domain you are prepared to accept, so either retract the objection or extend the skepticism. Evaluate the force of this maneuver in the metaethical context. Does pointing to the parallel with mathematics genuinely defuse the queerness concern, or does it rather suggest we should be more skeptical about mathematics than we usually are?

7. Aesthetic realism and moral realism share the same general argumentative structure — genuine disagreement implies a fact of the matter, aesthetic or moral education implies there is something to be accurate about — yet aesthetic anti-realism is regarded by many as more plausible than moral anti-realism. What accounts for this asymmetry of intuitive plausibility? Is the asymmetry philosophically principled, or does it reflect a bias toward taking moral discourse more seriously than aesthetic discourse?

8. The response-dependence framework proposes that aesthetic (and moral) properties are neither fully mind-independent nor merely subjective: they are dispositions in objects to elicit appropriate responses in idealized observers. The circularity problem threatens any non-trivial specification of "ideal observer." Evaluate the three strategies for avoiding circularity identified in the chapter — specification by cognitive conditions, by training and expertise, and by reflective equilibrium — and consider whether any of them gives response-dependence a genuine advantage over either realism or anti-realism, or whether it simply occupies an unstable middle ground.
