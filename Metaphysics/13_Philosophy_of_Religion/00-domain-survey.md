# Domain Survey: Philosophy of Religion

## Overview

Philosophy of religion applies the tools of analytic metaphysics to questions about the existence, nature, and knowability of God, as well as to broader questions about religious experience, miracles, providence, and the afterlife. Unlike theology (which operates within a faith tradition) or the sociology of religion (which studies religious practice descriptively), philosophy of religion subjects religious claims to the same logical and evidential scrutiny applied to any metaphysical or empirical claim. Its central question — whether there is a being that is omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good, and the creator of everything — is among the most contested questions in philosophy, generating positions ranging from confident theism to confident atheism, with many gradations between.

The **theistic arguments** — the ontological argument, the cosmological argument, the teleological argument, and arguments from religious experience — have been developed and refined over centuries. Anselm's ontological argument attempts to derive God's existence from the very concept of God as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived." Leibniz, Clarke, and Kalam versions of the cosmological argument infer a first cause or sufficient reason from the existence of contingent beings. The design argument infers an intelligent designer from the apparent fine-tuning of physical constants and the complexity of biological organisms. Each argument has been subjected to sustained philosophical criticism, and the contemporary versions represent significant refinements over their classical formulations.

The **problem of evil** — the inference from the existence, quantity, or distribution of suffering in the world to the non-existence or defectiveness of a perfectly good and omnipotent God — is the central objection to theism in the analytic tradition. In its logical form (Mackie 1955), it claims that the existence of an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good God is *logically inconsistent* with the existence of evil. In its evidential form (Rowe 1979), it claims only that the amount and distribution of evil provides significant evidence against theism. The free will defense (Plantinga), the soul-making theodicy (Hick), and skeptical theism (Wykstra, van Inwagen) are the main responses.

**Divine attributes** — omniscience (including foreknowledge of free actions), omnipotence (whether God can do anything, or only what is coherently doable), perfect goodness, simplicity, immutability, eternity, and aseity — generate their own philosophical puzzles. God's omniscience seems to threaten free will: if God knew yesterday what you would do today, you cannot do otherwise without God having been wrong — but God cannot be wrong. Boethius's "eternal now" solution (God knows all of time from a timeless perspective), Molinism (God knows counterfactuals of creaturely freedom), and Open Theism (God does not have exhaustive foreknowledge of future contingents) are the main responses. Omnipotence generates puzzles about whether God can create a stone too heavy for him to lift, or whether God can change the past.

## Major Positions and Their Logical Relations

### Varieties of Religious Belief and Disbelief

**Classical theism**: God exists; is omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good, eternal (timeless), simple (no composition of parts), and the creator *ex nihilo* of all contingent beings. Defended by Aquinas, Leibniz, and contemporary figures like Eleonore Stump and Brian Leftow.

**Open theism**: God exists but does not have exhaustive foreknowledge of future free actions; God's knowledge "opens up" as events unfold. This preserves divine goodness and human freedom at the cost of limiting divine omniscience. (Sanders 1998, Hasker 1989.)

**Process theism**: God exists but is not omnipotent in the classical sense; God "lures" creatures toward good without coercing. God is not the creator *ex nihilo* but is one entity among others. (Whitehead, Griffin.)

**Agnosticism**: Insufficient evidence to determine whether God exists; theism and atheism are both epistemically unjustified.

**Atheism**: God does not exist; the balance of argument and evidence favors non-existence.

**Religious non-realism** (Braithwaite, Cupitt): Religious language is not descriptive of a metaphysically real entity but expressive of attitudes, commitments, or social practices. "God" does not refer; religious claims are not literally true or false.

### The Theistic Arguments

**The Ontological Argument** (Anselm, *Proslogion* ch. 2–3; Descartes; Plantinga 1974):

*Classical form* (Anselm): God is "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" (id quo maius cogitari non potest). If God existed only in the intellect and not in reality, we could conceive of something greater (a God that exists in reality). But then "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" would not be that — contradiction. Therefore, God exists in reality.

*Modal form* (Plantinga): God is a *maximally great being* — one that has maximal greatness in every possible world. Maximal greatness entails maximal excellence (omnipotence, omniscience, and moral perfection) in every world. If it is *possible* that a maximally great being exists (if the concept is coherent), then by the logic of S5 modal logic, a maximally great being exists in every possible world, including the actual world.
- *Gaunilo's objection* (to Anselm): By parallel reasoning, we could prove the existence of a perfect island. The argument form is too permissive.
- *Kant's objection*: "Existence is not a predicate" — it does not add to the concept of a thing but only to our concept of where it is placed ontologically. The ontological argument illicitly treats existence as a property.
- *Response to Kant* (Plantinga): The modal argument does not treat bare existence as a perfection; it treats necessary existence — existing in all possible worlds — as a perfection. Necessary existence is a coherent modal property.

**The Cosmological Argument**:

*Leibniz's version* (principle of sufficient reason): Everything has a sufficient reason for its existence. The universe exists contingently. There must be a sufficient reason for the universe's existence outside the universe — a necessary being. That necessary being is God.

*Kalam version* (al-Ghazali, Craig 1979): Everything that begins to exist has a cause. The universe began to exist. Therefore, the universe has a cause. That cause must be outside time and space, uncaused, immensely powerful, and personal — that is, God.
- *Objection to Kalam*: The universe may not have "begun" in any causally relevant sense (Hawking's "no boundary" proposal; eternal inflation). The first premise applies to things within the universe but may not apply to the universe as a whole.
- *Objection to Leibniz*: The Principle of Sufficient Reason may be too strong — it would seem to require a sufficient reason for God's own existence, generating a regress, or to imply that the universe necessarily exists.

**The Teleological Argument** (Paley 1802; fine-tuning version, Collins 2009):

*Paley's design argument*: The complexity and apparent purposiveness of biological organisms (the eye, the hand) require an intelligent designer, just as a watch found on the ground implies a watchmaker.
- *Hume's objection*: The universe is unlike a watch; we have no independent knowledge of universes being designed; the analogy is too weak.
- *Darwin's objection*: Natural selection produces the appearance of design without an actual designer; the inference from apparent design to intelligence is defeated by the mechanism of evolution.

*Fine-tuning argument* (contemporary): The physical constants of the universe (the cosmological constant, the mass of the electron, the strong nuclear force) are extraordinarily finely tuned for the existence of complex structures (stars, chemistry, life). The probability of their falling in the life-permitting range by chance is extremely small. The best explanation is design.
- *Objection*: the **multiverse** response — if there are infinitely many universes with different physical constants, it is unsurprising that we observe one with life-permitting constants (observer selection). Fine-tuning does not require a single tuner.
- *Objection*: the **single sample problem** — we have only one universe; we cannot assess the prior probability that its constants are fine-tuned without a distribution over possible universes.

**The Argument from Religious Experience** (Swinburne 1979): Many people, across cultures and times, have had experiences they describe as experiences of God. The principle of credulity (if it seems to one that x is present, one has a prima facie reason to believe x is present) suggests that such experiences provide evidence for God's existence.
- *Objection*: religious experiences can be explained naturalistically (psychology, neuroscience); their diversity (different religions report different experiences) suggests they do not all track the same reality.

### The Problem of Evil

**Logical problem of evil** (Mackie 1955, Flew): The following set is logically inconsistent:
(1) God is omnipotent.
(2) God is omniscient.
(3) God is perfectly good.
(4) Evil exists.

Plantinga's response: (4) does not follow from (1)–(3) because it is *possible* that God could not have created a world with free creatures who always do right. If libertarian free will is possible and morally significant, God's permission of evil is consistent with his goodness and power. The free will defense shows that (1)–(4) is *not* inconsistent.

**Evidential problem of evil** (Rowe 1979): There exist instances of intense suffering (the fawn burning in a forest fire, the abuse of children) that serve no apparent moral purpose and that an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good God could have prevented without thereby losing a greater good. Therefore, probably, God does not exist.

*Skeptical theism* (Wykstra, van Inwagen, Bergmann): We should not expect to be able to see the reasons that would justify God in permitting evil; given the vastness of God's knowledge and the complexity of the reasons that might justify permission of suffering, our failure to see justifying reasons is not evidence that there are none. Our "cognitive limitations" give us no reason to expect transparency into divine reasons.
- *Objection to skeptical theism* (Dougherty, Trakakis): Skeptical theism is too permissive — by the same logic, we could not draw inferences about human evil either; skeptical theism seems to undermine any moral reasoning from appearances.

*Theodicies*: attempts to identify the goods that justify God's permitting evil:
- *Soul-making* (Hick 1966): The world is a vale of soul-making; suffering is necessary for the development of virtuous character. An "Irenaean" theodicy in which creatures develop toward God rather than falling from a perfect state.
- *Natural evil and natural law* (Plantinga, Swinburne): Regular natural laws are necessary for a world in which agents can make predictions and form effective intentions; but regular natural laws generate natural disasters and disease as by-products.

## Key Arguments and Counterarguments

### Plantinga's Modal Ontological Argument

Let *MG(x)* mean *x* is maximally great — has maximal excellence (omnipotence, omniscience, moral perfection) in every possible world.

**P1.** Possibly, there exists a maximally great being: ◇∃x MG(x).
**P2.** Necessarily, if a maximally great being exists, it exists in every possible world: □(∃x MG(x) → □∃x MG(x)).
**C.** By S5: ∃x MG(x). A maximally great being exists.

*The key step*: P1 asserts mere possibility; S5 axiom (◇□p → □p) converts possibility of necessary existence to actual necessary existence. If P1 is granted, the conclusion follows in S5.

*The parallel objection*: Replace *MG* with *MNG* (maximally great non-existence — a being whose non-existence is necessary in every world). Possibly there is a MNG? Then necessarily there is no maximally great being. The argument form allows one to get whatever one wants by adjusting P1.

*Plantinga's response*: P1 is not question-begging if MG is a coherent concept; MNG is incoherent (a "being" whose non-existence is necessary is no being at all). The burden is on the objector to show that MG is incoherent.

### The Foreknowledge-Freedom Argument

**P1.** God infallibly believed yesterday that you will do A tomorrow.
**P2.** It is not in your power to make it the case that God held a false belief yesterday.
**P3.** Therefore, it is not in your power to refrain from doing A tomorrow.
**C.** You are not free with respect to A.

*Boethius/Aquinas's timeless eternity response*: God is outside time; God does not "believe yesterday" anything; God's knowledge is an eternal present. From outside time, God observes all events without any earlier-than relation between God's knowledge and the event. So P1 is false as stated.

*Molinist response* (Molina 1588): God's "middle knowledge" — knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (what any free creature would do in any possible circumstance) — grounds God's comprehensive foreknowledge while preserving libertarian freedom. *Objection* (the grounding problem): what grounds counterfactuals of creaturely freedom before God creates the relevant creatures? Their truth seems to depend on the creatures' choices, but the choices haven't been made yet.

*Open theist response*: Deny that God infallibly foreknows free future actions; a perfectly good God would not acquire knowledge that constrains creaturely freedom. Open theists accept a limited omniscience.

## Key Papers and Books

1. **Plantinga, Alvin, *The Nature of Necessity* (1974)** — Develops the modal ontological argument and the free will defense; the most technically rigorous theistic philosophy.

2. **Mackie, J. L., "Evil and Omnipotence" (1955)** — The definitive statement of the logical problem of evil; the target for Plantinga's free will defense.

3. **Rowe, William, "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism" (1979)** — Introduces the evidential form of the problem of evil; the fawn in the forest fire.

4. **Swinburne, Richard, *The Existence of God* (1979, 2nd ed. 2004)** — Systematic Bayesian argument for theism; covers all the major theistic arguments and the problem of evil; the most comprehensive contemporary theistic apologetic.

5. **Hick, John, *Evil and the God of Love* (1966)** — The soul-making theodicy; the Irenaean model of creation as a vale of development.

6. **Craig, William Lane, *The Kalam Cosmological Argument* (1979)** — Systematic defense of the Kalam argument; reviews cosmological and philosophical support.

7. **van Inwagen, Peter, *The Problem of Evil* (2006)** — Develops both skeptical theism and a theodicy; argues that the evidential problem of evil is less compelling than it appears.

8. **Stump, Eleonore, *Wandering in Darkness* (2010)** — Develops an Aquinian theodicy using narrative methodology; argues that the problem of evil has an answer that can only be appreciated through first-person engagement with stories of suffering.

## Live Debates and Open Questions

1. **The coherence of divine omniscience and free will**: The debate between Boethianism, Molinism, Ockhamism (simple foreknowledge), and Open Theism continues without resolution. The grounding problem for Molinism and the loss of divine omniscience in Open Theism both remain significant objections.

2. **Divine simplicity**: The traditional doctrine that God is absolutely simple — without composition of any kind, identical to each of his attributes — is increasingly questioned. Alvin Plantinga has argued that divine simplicity is incoherent; others (Stump, Brower) defend it. The debate has implications for divine aseity and timelessness.

3. **Natural theology and scientific cosmology**: The fine-tuning argument depends on cosmological parameters whose probability distribution we cannot assess independently. The multiverse response requires understanding of inflationary cosmology and quantum mechanics; the theological response must engage these scientific developments.

4. **Hiddenness of God** (Schellenberg 1993): If God is perfectly loving, God would ensure that no sincere seeker of God fails to find Him. Many sincere seekers fail to find God; hence, probably, God does not exist. Theist responses include: people may resist relationship without recognizing it; non-belief may serve soul-making purposes; the argument misunderstands what perfect love requires.

5. **Religious epistemology**: Alvin Plantinga's reformed epistemology holds that belief in God can be "properly basic" — rational without requiring argument or evidence, just as perceptual and memory beliefs are basic. William Alston's doxastic practice approach holds that religious experience grounds religious belief with the same rationality as sense experience. Debunking arguments (Evolutionary debunking of religious belief) challenge whether these beliefs are reliably formed.

## Connections to Other Domains

**Philosophy of Religion and Modality (Domain 05)**: The modal ontological argument is the most direct application of possible worlds metaphysics to religion. God as a necessarily existent being is a position in modal ontology. Plantinga's work on modal logic and theism crosses both domains continuously.

**Philosophy of Religion and Free Will (Domain 08)**: Divine foreknowledge and human free will is the defining intersection. The Kalam argument's appeal to the beginning of the universe connects to the cosmological determinism debate. The free will defense of God's permission of evil requires libertarian free will.

**Philosophy of Religion and Truth/Reality (Domain 12)**: Divine omniscience requires that God knows all truths. The extent of divine knowledge depends on what truths there are — including modal truths, counterfactuals, and truths about the future. Truthmaker theory connects here: if God knows all truths, what entities make the truths God knows true?

**Philosophy of Religion and Causation/Laws (Domain 04)**: The cosmological argument appeals to the Principle of Sufficient Reason (everything has a cause or ground). The concept of a first cause requires a distinction between caused and uncaused beings. Hume's argument against miracles invokes the uniformity of nature and the nature of testimony in a way that connects to the metaphysics of laws.

**Philosophy of Religion and Metaphysics of Science (Domain 10)**: Fine-tuning and intelligent design arguments engage cosmological physics, evolutionary biology, and the philosophy of probability. Scientific naturalism — the view that science exhausts what there is — is a direct challenge to religious ontology. The debate between methodological and metaphysical naturalism defines the interface.
