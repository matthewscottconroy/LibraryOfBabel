# Philosophy of Religion

The question of whether God exists is probably the most consequential metaphysical question a human being can confront. Billions of people have organized their lives around an affirmative answer, finding in it meaning, moral direction, and consolation. Many others have organized their lives around a negative answer, or around the view that the question cannot be settled with the intellectual resources available to us. Still others have noticed that the question itself is more complicated than it first appears: before asking whether God exists, we need to ask what "God" means, and whether any coherent description of a divine being is available.

Philosophy of religion approaches these questions with the tools of careful argument rather than faith or sentiment alone. It is neither theology — which typically begins from revealed premises — nor atheism — which begins from their denial. It is inquiry, and it takes seriously both the best arguments for the existence of God and the best arguments against it. This is not an artificial posture of neutrality but a genuine commitment to following the argument where it leads, even when it leads somewhere uncomfortable.

The arguments for the existence of God are among the most sophisticated and durable in the history of philosophy. The cosmological argument — in its various forms stretching from Aristotle through Aquinas and Leibniz to contemporary defenders — attempts to show that the existence of contingent things requires a necessary being that explains their existence. The teleological argument — from the appearance of design in the universe, or from the fine-tuning of physical constants, or from the elegance of mathematical structure — contends that the best explanation of certain features of the world is an intelligent creator. The ontological argument — in Anselm's medieval version, in Descartes's version, in Plantinga's modal version — attempts the audacious move of deriving God's existence from the very concept of a maximally great being. These arguments have been contested at every step, but they have also been defended at every step, and the debate remains genuinely open.

The arguments against the existence of God are equally serious. The problem of evil — the existence of suffering, both natural and moral, in quantities that seem incompatible with the existence of an omnipotent, omniscient, and wholly benevolent creator — is the most powerful challenge to theism. It comes in a logical form (suffering is logically incompatible with classical theism) and an evidential form (the amount and distribution of suffering constitutes evidence against theism even if it does not decisively refute it). The theist's responses — the free will defense, skeptical theism, theodicies that find redemptive value in suffering — are philosophically sophisticated but not universally persuasive.

This unit examines philosophy of religion across four chapters. We begin with the major **arguments for God's existence**: cosmological, teleological, and ontological. The goal is not to adjudicate the debate but to understand the arguments at their most careful and compelling, and to understand the best objections to them.

**Divine attributes** examines what classical theism takes God to be: omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent, eternal, simple, and immutable. Each attribute raises its own puzzles. Omnipotence: can God create a stone too heavy for God to lift? Omniscience: does God's foreknowledge of free acts undermine those acts' freedom? Eternity: is God timeless — outside time entirely — or everlasting, persisting through all times? Simplicity and immutability: does God have genuinely distinct properties, or is divine nature one simple unity? These are not word games; they determine whether the concept of God is internally coherent.

The **problem of evil** receives the sustained attention it deserves. Distinguishing natural from moral evil, and the logical from the evidential formulation of the problem, reveals the structure of the challenge. The chapter on suffering and meaning examines the hiddenness of God and the broader question of whether suffering can be meaningful.

**Religious epistemology** closes the unit by asking how, if at all, belief in God can be justified. Natural theology seeks rational arguments; reformed epistemology holds that belief in God can be rational without argument, as a properly basic belief; religious experience is scrutinized as a potential source of evidence. The chapter on pluralism and exclusivism asks how religious belief should respond to the diversity of religious traditions.

Philosophy of religion is, in the end, philosophy at its most consequential.
