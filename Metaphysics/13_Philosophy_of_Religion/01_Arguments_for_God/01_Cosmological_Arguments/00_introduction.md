# Cosmological Arguments for God's Existence

Why is there something rather than nothing? The question is ancient and the discomfort it generates is genuine. The universe exists; it might not have existed; and its existence seems to call for explanation. The cosmological argument is the family of arguments that attempt to explain the existence of the universe by appeal to a first cause — an uncaused cause, a necessary being, a self-existent ground of all contingent existence — which is identified with God.

The Kalam cosmological argument, associated with medieval Islamic philosophy and recently revived by Craig, begins with the premise that everything that begins to exist has a cause. The universe began to exist (as the Big Bang cosmology seems to confirm). Therefore, the universe has a cause. This cause must be itself uncaused — it cannot have begun to exist — and must be sufficiently powerful to bring the universe into existence. The argument concludes that this first cause is something like the God of classical theism.

Leibniz's cosmological argument takes a different form: each contingent thing — each thing that exists but might not have — requires an explanation of why it exists rather than not. The universe as a whole is contingent; therefore, it requires an explanation. This explanation cannot itself be contingent (which would merely push the question back); it must be a necessary being — one that exists necessarily, as a matter of its own nature. God is this necessary being: the ultimate explanation of why there is something rather than nothing.

Objections to cosmological arguments are varied. Hume argued that the demand for a cause of the universe may simply be a case of applying within-universe patterns of explanation beyond the universe, where they no longer apply. Russell argued that the universe is just a brute fact — an uncaused existent — and that positing God to explain it merely introduces a further entity in need of explanation. The question "What caused God?" generates an apparent regress.

This section examines the cosmological argument in its main forms, the philosophical objections, and the theist's responses.
