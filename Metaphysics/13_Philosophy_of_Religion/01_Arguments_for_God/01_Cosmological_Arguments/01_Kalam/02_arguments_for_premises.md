# Arguments for the Kalam's Premises

Each premise of the Kalam requires independent support, and William Lane Craig has developed detailed arguments for both. The two sets of arguments are philosophically different in character — one appeals to the limits of metaphysical possibility, the other to cosmological physics — and Craig regards them as mutually reinforcing rather than redundant.

## Supporting Premise 1

The first premise — "everything that begins to exist has a cause" — is supported primarily by the principle *ex nihilo nihil fit*: nothing comes from absolutely nothing. We can put this more sharply. In every instance of observation and scientific inquiry, things that come into being are produced by prior conditions. A skeptic might point to quantum mechanics, where events like radioactive decay or the spontaneous appearance of virtual particle-antiparticle pairs seem to occur without a deterministic prior cause. Craig's response is that these are not genuine cases of something arising from absolute nothingness — they occur within a causally structured quantum vacuum, which is itself a physical state with energy, structure, and causal potency. "Nothing" in the physicist's sense is not nothing in the philosopher's sense.

Formally:

- P1a: We have never observed anything that begins to exist without a cause.
- P1b: The best available theories in physics posit causes for all physical events.
- P1c: There is no coherent account of how something can arise from absolute nothingness.
- C: Therefore, it is highly probable that everything which begins to exist has a cause.

The argument is probabilistic, and Craig is content with that. His claim is that P1 is more plausible than its denial — and that anyone who denies it faces the uncomfortable consequence of allowing that things can simply pop into existence from nothing at all.

## Supporting Premise 2

Premise 2 — "the universe began to exist" — receives both philosophical and scientific support, and the two kinds of argument converge on the same conclusion.

The first philosophical argument draws on David Hilbert's work on infinity. Craig contends that an actually infinite number of past events would generate genuine metaphysical absurdities. Hilbert's Hotel is the standard illustration: an imaginary hotel with infinitely many occupied rooms can still accommodate infinitely many new guests by shifting each occupant to a room with twice their current number — and yet when the infinite guests in odd-numbered rooms check out, infinitely many guests remain, even though infinitely many have left. Craig argues that the arithmetic of actual infinities (where ∞ − ∞ can equal any value, or zero, or infinity) shows not merely that infinite sets are counterintuitive but that they cannot be instantiated in the concrete physical world.

Formally:

- P2a: An actually infinite collection of things cannot exist in reality (it leads to absurdity).
- P2b: A beginningless series of past events would constitute an actually infinite collection.
- C: Therefore, the series of past events is not beginningless — the universe had a beginning.

The second philosophical argument concerns the impossibility of reaching the present by traversing an infinite past. Even if actual infinities could exist, Craig argues they could not be *formed* by successive addition — by events following one upon another. No matter how many events have occurred, one more can always be added; there is no point at which infinitely many prior steps have been traversed. Consider: if the past were infinite, then getting to now would have required completing an infinite sequence of steps, each adding one more. But an infinite series cannot be completed by successive addition. The present moment would be unreachable.

Formally:

- P2c: The temporal series of past events has been formed by successive addition.
- P2d: A series formed by successive addition cannot be actually infinite.
- C: Therefore, the temporal series of past events is finite — the universe had a beginning.

The scientific argument appeals to Big Bang cosmology. The observed expansion of the universe (Hubble's observations), confirmed by cosmic microwave background radiation, implies that extrapolating backward in time, all matter, energy, space, and time converge on a singularity approximately 13.8 billion years ago. The Borde-Guth-Vilenkin (BGV) theorem (2003) strengthens this considerably: any universe that has been, on average, in a state of cosmic expansion cannot be infinite in its past — it must have a past boundary. Crucially, the BGV theorem applies to all inflationary models, including multiverse scenarios. Even a universe embedded in a broader inflationary spacetime would, on this theorem, require a beginning.

These arguments are not independent of objection. Many mathematicians and physicists argue that Cantorian set theory demonstrates the consistency of actual infinities, and that Craig's examples show only that infinite sets behave non-intuitively, not that they are metaphysically impossible. Graham Oppy presses this point: mathematical consistency is not the same as metaphysical impossibility. Craig replies that the question is not whether infinite sets can be manipulated in abstract mathematics but whether they can be instantiated in the concrete physical world — and here intuitions of absurdity carry real evidential weight.

On the scientific side, alternative cosmological models — loop quantum cosmology, the Hartle-Hawking no-boundary proposal, Penrose's conformal cyclic cosmology — suggest scenarios in which the Big Bang is not an absolute beginning. Craig responds that these models are speculative and that the BGV theorem constrains all of them insofar as they describe a universe that has been, on average, expanding.
