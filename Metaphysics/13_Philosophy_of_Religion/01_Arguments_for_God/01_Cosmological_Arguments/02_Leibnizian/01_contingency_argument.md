# The Leibnizian Contingency Argument

Suppose matter and energy have always existed — suppose the universe is eternal, without a temporal beginning. Does that settle the question of why anything exists at all? Leibniz thought not. Even an eternal universe might not have existed. The fact that it does exist, when it could have been otherwise, demands an explanation. And this demand — the question *why is there something rather than nothing?* — is what drives the contingency argument.

The Leibnizian cosmological argument differs from the Kalam precisely in not depending on a temporal beginning. It turns instead on the concept of *contingency*: the universe exists but might not have, and this modal fact cries out for explanation. The argument runs as follows:

- P1: Everything that exists has an explanation of its existence, either in the necessity of its own nature or in an external cause (the Principle of Sufficient Reason, or PSR).
- P2: The universe exists.
- P3: The universe does not exist by the necessity of its own nature — it is a contingent being that could have failed to exist.
- P4: Therefore, the universe has an external explanation of its existence.
- P5: The explanation must terminate in a necessary being — one whose non-existence is impossible — since any further contingent explanation merely relocates the puzzle rather than resolving it.
- C: A necessary being exists, and it is the ultimate explanation of the universe's existence.

The crucial move is P3 to P4. The universe's contingency means it does not explain its own existence. The explanation must come from outside. But then that external explanation either is itself contingent — in which case we face the same question again — or it is necessary. A beginningless series of contingent events is still a collection of contingent things, and the collection inherits the contingency of its members. Only something that *could not fail to exist* can stop the regress.

## Samuel Clarke and the Self-Existent Being

Samuel Clarke (1675–1729) developed an important eighteenth-century version of the argument in *A Demonstration of the Being and Attributes of God* (1705). Clarke distinguishes necessary from contingent beings and argues that a series of contingent beings, however long, cannot explain itself. A collection of contingent truths cannot generate a necessary one. There must therefore be a self-existent being — one that has the reason for its existence within its own nature — from which all contingent existence derives. Clarke derives further attributes of this being (eternity, infinity, omnipresence) from the concept of self-existence itself.

## Contemporary Developments: Pruss and Koons

The full PSR — "everything that exists has an explanation" — is philosophically controversial. It seems to imply that God's own existence must have an explanation, and it may rule out genuine brute facts in ways many find implausible. Alexander Pruss and Robert Koons have developed sophisticated contemporary versions of the argument that work with weaker principles.

Pruss proposes replacing the full PSR with a *Weak PSR*: every contingent fact has an explanation. This applies only to contingent facts, not to necessary truths, and does not require that necessary beings explain themselves in some further, higher sense. Koons argues for a "restricted PSR" that applies to "wholly contingent" states of affairs — those that could have failed to exist without any further contingent state of affairs explaining their absence. Even on these weaker formulations, the argument generates the conclusion that the cosmos as a whole, being wholly contingent, has a cause or explanation that must ultimately be a necessary being.

The question of what the necessary being is *like* remains open after the argument is completed. Theists argue it must be uncaused (it is its own sufficient reason), enormously powerful (it must account for the entire universe's existence), immaterial (if material, it would be part of the contingent universe it explains), and personal (it must be a being that can "choose" to bring contingent things into existence, rather than a natural necessity that necessitates them). The last point — that the necessary being must be personal — is contested. Critics argue that a necessary physical law, or a necessarily existing abstract structure, might terminate the explanatory regress without being personal in any theistically relevant sense.
