# Necessary Being

Consider why any explanation of the universe's existence must eventually terminate. If we explain the universe's contingent existence by appeal to some prior state, and that state is also contingent, we have not really explained anything — we have simply relocated the puzzle. The only thing that can genuinely stop the regress is something whose existence requires no further explanation: a being that exists not as a matter of contingent fact but because it *could not fail to exist*. This is the concept of a necessary being, and it does essential structural work in the Leibnizian cosmological argument.

We say a contingent being *exists but might not have*. You and I are contingent: we exist, but there are possible worlds where we don't. A necessary being, by contrast, exists in every possible world; its non-existence is impossible. There is no coherent way of describing a situation in which a necessary being fails to be.

## Two Readings

There are two main ways of understanding what it is for a being to be "necessary," and they are not the same.

The *logical reading* holds that a necessary being is one whose existence is logically necessary — its non-existence would be self-contradictory, like a married bachelor. On this reading, "God does not exist" would be a formal logical falsehood. Kant's objection is most forceful against this version: existence is not a predicate, and no description of a being can logically entail that being's existence. It is unclear that any conceivable description of God generates a formal logical contradiction on its denial.

The *metaphysical reading* holds that a necessary being exists in all possible worlds — its non-existence is metaphysically impossible, though perhaps not analytically self-contradictory. Just as "water is H₂O" is necessarily true but not a logical truth, "God exists" might be metaphysically necessary without being analytically necessary. This is the reading favored by most contemporary defenders of the Leibnizian argument, including Pruss and Koons. On this reading, necessary existence is a modal property, not a feature of concepts.

## Aquinas's Third Way

Thomas Aquinas's "Third Way" in the *Summa Theologiae* (I, q. 2, a. 3) is one of the most influential arguments in this tradition. Aquinas observes that things in the world come into being and pass away — they are "possible" beings that can exist or not exist. From this he argues:

- P1: Things that can fail to exist actually fail to exist at some time.
- P2: If everything were such a "possible" being, then at some point nothing would have existed.
- P3: But if nothing existed, nothing could have come to exist (ex nihilo nihil fit).
- P4: Therefore, not everything is merely possible — there must be something that is necessary.

Aquinas then asks whether necessary beings must themselves derive their necessity from something else, or whether some being is necessary through its own essence. The chain of necessary-but-derived beings must terminate in a being that is necessary by its own nature — what Aquinas identifies with God.

The argument is vulnerable at P1 and P2: even if every individual contingent thing fails to exist at *some* time, it does not follow that there was a *single* time at which all contingent things simultaneously failed to exist. This is a scope fallacy: from "each thing possibly fails to exist" it does not follow that "possibly, all things fail to exist at once." Contemporary defenders of Aquinas acknowledge this difficulty and have proposed amended versions.

## Abstract Objects and the Necessity Problem

Some philosophers argue that abstract objects — numbers, sets, propositions — are necessary beings. The number 7 exists necessarily in the sense that there is no possible world in which it fails to exist. If so, the regress of contingent explanation might terminate in abstract objects rather than in God.

Theists respond in several ways. Some, following Plantinga, hold that God is the source of abstract objects — numbers and propositions exist as ideas in the divine mind. Brian Leftow argues in *God and Necessity* (2012) that abstract objects are ontologically dependent on God. Others argue that abstract objects, even if necessary, are causally inert: they cannot explain the existence of concrete contingent things, because they stand in no causal relations to anything. What terminates the cosmological regress must be not merely a necessary being but a *causally active* necessary being — and that is a further constraint that abstract objects cannot satisfy.

## Contemporary Defenders

Brian Leftow argues that God is the unique concrete necessary being and that all possible worlds are grounded in God's modal nature. Robert Koons argues that the PSR-based argument succeeds in establishing a concrete, self-existent being that natural theology then identifies with God. Alexander Pruss defends the PSR and the argument from contingency in *The Principle of Sufficient Reason* (2006) and *Infinity, Causation, and Paradox* (2018), carefully addressing the objections from modal logic and from the metaphysics of abstract objects.
