# Objections to the Leibnizian Argument

The Leibnizian cosmological argument has attracted sustained criticism on multiple fronts. The objections target the Principle of Sufficient Reason, the coherence of necessary existence, and the inference from "necessary being" to "God." We can work through the main lines of attack in turn.

## The Problem of the PSR

The argument's engine is the Principle of Sufficient Reason: everything that exists has an explanation. Hume and Russell resisted this from the outset. Demanding an explanation for the universe's existence may simply be a category error — a projection onto the universe of a demand that only makes sense for things *within* the universe. Russell's reply in the 1948 BBC debate with Frederick Copleston is blunt: "The universe is just there, and that's all."

The theist faces a dilemma. If the PSR is unrestricted — everything has an explanation — then it applies to God too, and God's existence demands an explanation. The theist's response that God is "self-explanatory" (God's essence is existence) looks question-begging: it assumes the conclusion to support the premise. If the PSR is restricted to contingent things only, then the question becomes why the universe counts as contingent and therefore subject to the PSR. The argument's grip loosens if the universe might itself be a necessary being.

Defenders like Pruss and Koons have worked to show that even weaker principles — a PSR restricted to contingent or "wholly contingent" states — are sufficient to generate the conclusion. The debate turns on whether these restricted principles can be independently motivated.

## Could the Universe Be Necessary?

Some philosophers have argued that the fundamental physical state of the universe, or the laws of physics, might be metaphysically necessary. If the laws of nature hold in every possible world — if there is no coherent possibility of different fundamental physics — then the universe (or its physical ground) would be necessary, and no external explanation would be required.

The theist's standard response is that this is ad hoc. We can coherently conceive of worlds with different fundamental physics, and this conceivability is at least prima facie evidence of metaphysical possibility. God's necessity, by contrast, is supposed to be grounded in the concept of a being whose essence is existence — a principled rather than a merely stipulated necessity. Critics reply that the appeal to "essence entailing existence" is itself ad hoc unless the concept of such an essence can be independently elucidated. The debate connects directly to questions about the modal ontological argument (see section 03).

## The Fallacy of Composition

Hume argued in the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (Part IX) that it is a fallacy to infer that a collection has a property simply because each member has it. Even if each brick in a house was made by someone, it does not follow that the house as a whole was made by one person. Similarly, even if each contingent thing has an explanation, it does not follow that the collection of all contingent things has a single unified external explanation.

Russell pressed the same point against Copleston. The "universe" as a totality may not be the kind of entity that can have an explanation — it may be a logical construction we impose on the sum of contingent things rather than a genuine unified object with its own causal requirements.

The Leibnizian response, associated with Pruss and others, is to notice that the composition objection has limited application here. The relevant question is not whether the universe as a whole inherits properties from its parts — that would be the fallacy Hume identifies — but why there should be *any* contingent beings at all rather than none. That question cannot be answered by citing another contingent being within the collection. It demands something outside the collection entirely.

## The Unexplained Explainer

Even if the argument succeeds, God's existence seems to demand its own explanation. The theist answers that God's essence entails existence — God is *id quo maius cogitari non potest*, and such a being cannot fail to exist. But this looks like it borrows the central thesis of the ontological argument, which is itself highly contested. The Leibnizian argument appears to require the ontological argument to complete it — which means its success is conditional on the success of a second, independently problematic argument.

## Quentin Smith's Reversal

Quentin Smith has argued that the cosmological argument's conclusion is actually atheistic: if everything that exists has a cause, and God caused the universe, then God's existence also requires a cause. The only escape is to accept that something — either God or the universe — is uncaused. But if we must accept an uncaused existent, Smith argues it is more parsimonious to accept the universe itself as uncaused rather than adding God as an extra layer of explanation.

## The PSR and Divine Freedom

A further internal difficulty: if the PSR is true, what is God's sufficient reason for creating *this* universe rather than a different one? If God had a sufficient reason for creating this particular world, then God's choice was in some sense determined — which threatens divine freedom. If God had no sufficient reason, the PSR is violated. This Buridan's-ass style objection suggests that the PSR generates serious difficulties for theism itself, not only for naturalism.

## Evaluation

The contemporary debate has been substantially refined by Pruss, Koons, and Timothy O'Connor, who have worked hard to show that weaker versions of the PSR can generate the relevant conclusion while avoiding the sharpest objections. The contested issues remain: whether any version of the PSR is independently plausible, whether the universe might be its own necessary being, and whether the regress of contingent explanations can genuinely be terminated by cosmological reasoning alone without borrowing from the ontological argument. The Leibnizian argument is perhaps philosophically more robust than often credited — but its success is sensitive to contested prior commitments in modal metaphysics.
