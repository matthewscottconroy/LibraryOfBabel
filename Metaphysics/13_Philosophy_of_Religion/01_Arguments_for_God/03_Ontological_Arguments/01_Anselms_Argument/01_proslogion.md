# Anselm's Proslogion Argument

Consider the atheist who says there is no God. To deny God's existence, the atheist must understand the claim being denied — must have in mind the concept of God. But Anselm of Canterbury (c. 1078) thought this much was already too much. If the atheist has genuinely understood what "God" means, then, Anselm argues, the atheist cannot coherently deny that God exists. Understanding the concept, he claims, already commits us to acknowledging the reality.

This is an extraordinary ambition: to derive existence from concept alone, without any appeal to empirical evidence. The ontological argument, as it has come to be known, is entirely a priori. If it works, it shows that atheism is not merely false but necessarily false — impossible in the same way that denying a mathematical truth is impossible.

Anselm's target is "the fool" of Psalm 14:1, who says in his heart that there is no God. Even the fool, in understanding the denial, understands what is denied. And Anselm's God is identified by a characterization he thinks any rational person — including the atheist — must accept: God is "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" (*id quo maius cogitari non potest*).

## The Argument in Two Stages

**Chapter II: God exists in reality.**

- P1: God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived (by definition, accepted by everyone including the atheist).
- P2: The atheist understands this definition — so God exists "in the understanding" (*in intellectu*).
- P3: Something that exists both in the understanding and in reality is greater than something that exists in the understanding alone.
- P4: If God existed only in the understanding, then we could conceive of something greater — namely, a God that also exists in reality.
- P5: But then that greater thing would be greater than God — which contradicts the definition of God as that than which nothing greater can be conceived.
- C: Therefore, God must exist not only in the understanding but in reality.

**Chapter III: God's existence is necessary.**

Anselm develops a stronger version in the next chapter. Not only does God exist in reality, but God cannot be *conceived* not to exist. A being whose non-existence is inconceivable is greater than a being whose non-existence is conceivable. Therefore God — as the greatest conceivable being — must be a being whose non-existence is inconceivable: a necessarily existing being. This Chapter III argument is the ancestor of what contemporary philosophers call the modal ontological argument (see file 03), and Anselm's reply to Gaunilo (see file 02) rests primarily on it.

## Methodological Character and Theological Significance

The argument is unusual in the history of philosophy: it attempts to derive an existential conclusion from conceptual analysis alone. If successful, it shows that God's existence is not a contingent matter — something that might or might not be the case — but a necessary truth, as certain as mathematical truths. Atheism would then be not merely false but necessarily false, like the claim that there is a largest prime number.

The *Proslogion* is written as a prayer, and Anselm presents his discovery of the argument as a spiritual event rather than merely an intellectual one. He is engaged in *fides quaerens intellectum* — "faith seeking understanding" — not in neutral philosophical demonstration. This context matters: Anselm is not trying to persuade unbelievers with a cold proof but to articulate, from within faith, why God's existence is not a contingent accident.

Moreover, if the argument succeeds, it places God in a different ontological category from all other beings. God is the *ens necessarium* — the necessary being — whose existence cannot be denied without contradiction. This resonates with the classical theological tradition shared by Aquinas and Maimonides: God's being is radically unlike the being of creatures.

## Historical Reception

The argument was challenged immediately by Gaunilo and subsequently by Aquinas, who rejected it on the grounds that we do not have a clear enough grasp of God's essence to derive God's existence from it. Aquinas held that God's existence is self-evident *in itself* but not self-evident *to us*, because our intellect cannot grasp the divine essence directly. Descartes revived the argument in *Meditations* V, arguing that the concept of God necessarily includes existence as a perfection, just as the concept of a triangle necessarily includes having angles summing to 180°. Kant's critique in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) — that existence is not a predicate — set the terms for subsequent debate. The twentieth and twenty-first centuries have seen significant formal developments through Hartshorne's and Plantinga's modal versions, which use possible worlds semantics to reformulate the argument in a way that aims to evade Kant's objection.
