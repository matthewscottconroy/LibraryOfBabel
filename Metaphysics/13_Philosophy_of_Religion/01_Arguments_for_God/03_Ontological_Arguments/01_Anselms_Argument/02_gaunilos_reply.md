# Gaunilo's Reply and Kant's Objection

Two of the most influential responses to the ontological argument — one from a medieval monk, one from an Enlightenment philosopher — have shaped nearly all subsequent discussion. Gaunilo's parody targets the form of the argument; Kant's objection targets its key logical move. Both remain live in the contemporary literature.

## Gaunilo's Perfect Island

A contemporary of Anselm, the monk Gaunilo of Marmoutiers wrote "On Behalf of the Fool" (*Pro Insipiente*, c. 1078), arguing that Anselm's reasoning proves too much. Consider the "island than which no greater island can be conceived" — the perfect island, with the finest beaches, the most abundant food, the most beautiful scenery. By Anselm's logic:

- This perfect island is understood — it exists in the understanding.
- But an island that existed in reality would be greater than one existing only in understanding.
- Therefore, by the same reasoning Anselm employs, the perfect island must exist in reality.

The conclusion is absurd — concepts do not conjure islands. If Anselm's argument is valid, it generates parody proofs for the existence of any perfect thing: a perfect city, a perfect philosopher, a perfect pirate. Since these are clearly unsound, something must have gone wrong in the original argument.

Anselm replied that the argument applies only to a being of a very special kind: one whose existence is necessary — such that its non-existence is inconceivable. Islands and other contingent things can always be conceived not to exist; even the most perfect conceivable island might never have existed and might be destroyed tomorrow. There is no contradiction in conceiving the perfect island as non-existent. God, by contrast, is a being whose non-existence is supposed to be inconceivable. The argument applies uniquely to *this* kind of being — the greatest conceivable being, whose greatness includes necessary existence — and not to contingent things, however perfect.

This reply is significant: it shifts the ontological argument decisively toward the claim that God *necessarily* exists, anticipating the modal versions. But it raises a further question. How do we know that the concept of "greatest conceivable being" is instantiated with genuine necessary existence rather than being an incoherent concept? Plantinga argues in *The Nature of Necessity* (1974) that Gaunilo's parody fails because "the greatest conceivable island" is incoherent — islands can always be made greater, so there is no maximum. A being that is maximally excellent in all great-making properties might genuinely be a coherent maximum. Critics respond that this concedes too much: if some concepts admit a maximum and some do not, we need a principled account of which ones the ontological argument applies to, and the argument itself provides none. David Lewis and Graham Oppy have pressed a structural version of the parody: if we can coherently conceive of a *maximally evil* being, the same argument would prove its existence — which contradicts the existence of the maximally good being.

## Kant's Objection: Existence Is Not a Predicate

Kant's critique in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781, A592–A602/B620–B630) is the most cited objection to the ontological argument.

The argument depends on existence functioning as a "great-making property" — as something that can make God greater if possessed than if not. Kant disputes this. When I say "God exists," I am not adding a new property to the concept of God; I am asserting that the concept has an instance in the world. Existence is not a "real predicate" — it does not add to the conceptual content of a subject. To say that a hundred real dollars and a hundred imaginary dollars are different in some way that "existence" names as a property of the real dollars is a mistake: as concepts, they are identical; the difference is in whether anything in the world falls under the concept.

More formally:

- P1: "Existence" is not a real predicate — it does not add to the conceptual content of a subject.
- P2: The ontological argument treats existence as a great-making predicate that makes God "greater" if instantiated.
- P3: But if existence is not a predicate, it cannot be a great-making property.
- C: The inference from "God is the greatest conceivable being" to "God exists" is invalid.

Comparing "a God that exists" with "a God that doesn't exist" is comparing two descriptions, not two beings — and two descriptions that are identical in conceptual content. The difference, if there is one, is in the world, not in the concept.

Several replies to Kant have been developed. The modal reply distinguishes bare existence from *necessary* existence. Even if Kant's objection defeats the claim that contingent existence is a great-making property, necessary existence — the property of being unable to fail to exist, of owing existence to nothing outside oneself — is a substantive modal property, and it is not obvious that Kant's argument applies to it. Hartshorne, Malcolm, and Plantinga all press this line (see file 03). The Anselmian reply notes that Anselm's argument does not simply say God is greater *because* God exists; it says that God's nature as the greatest conceivable being requires that God cannot be conceived not to exist. The argument concerns necessary existence, not the bare addition of existence to a description.

Frege's logical reform adds a further complication. Frege distinguished existential claims from predication: "x exists" is not a first-order predicate of individuals but a second-order predicate of concepts — it says the concept "is God" has at least one instance. Some philosophers of religion see this as supporting Kant (existence is not a property of individuals) while others see it as merely reformulating the issue.

## Legacy

Gaunilo's parody has generated a rich literature on which concepts admit of ontological proof — and whether any parody argument is fully successful against Anselm's intended target. Kant's objection set the agenda for modern ontological argument scholarship: defenders must show either that existence can function as a predicate or that necessary existence is a special modal property that evades the Kantian critique. The modal ontological argument (see file 03) is the most sustained contemporary attempt to do this.
