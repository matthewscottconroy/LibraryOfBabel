# Important Terms and Ideas: Ontological Arguments

**Ontological Argument**
An argument for God's existence that proceeds from the concept of God alone, without appealing to contingent facts about the world. The general structure: God is defined as a being of maximal greatness or perfection; such a being must exist by virtue of its very concept.

**That Than Which Nothing Greater Can Be Conceived**
Anselm's definition of God: God is by definition the greatest conceivable being. If God existed only in the understanding but not in reality, we could conceive of something greater (the same being existing in reality). Therefore God must exist in reality.

**The Fool's Objection (Gaunilo's Reply)**
Gaunilo's counterexample: by the same logic, we could define a perfect island—the greatest conceivable island—and argue it must exist. But clearly this does not follow. The argument form, if valid, proves too much. Anselm responded that greatness applies only to necessary beings, not contingent islands.

**Kant's Objection: Existence Is Not a Predicate**
Kant's critique: "existence" is not a real predicate—it does not add to the concept of a thing; it merely posits a thing as instantiating a concept. Adding "existing" to the concept of God does not generate a being richer than the concept alone. Existence cannot be derived from a concept.

**Modal Ontological Argument**
Plantinga's version using possible-worlds semantics and S5 modal logic: (1) A maximally great being is possible (there is a possible world where it exists); (2) If a maximally great being exists, it exists in every possible world (by the definition of maximal greatness); (3) Therefore it exists in the actual world.

**Possible Worlds Formulation**
The S5 modal logic framework for the ontological argument: possibility (◇□P) implies necessity (□P) in S5. If God possibly necessarily exists, then God necessarily exists. The argument is logically valid; the debate centers on whether the key premise (God's existence is possible) is true.
