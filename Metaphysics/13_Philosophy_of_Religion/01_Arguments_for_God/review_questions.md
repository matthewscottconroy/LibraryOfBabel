# Review Questions — Arguments for God

1. The Kalam and Leibnizian cosmological arguments both conclude that God exists, but they operate on entirely different logical foundations. What does each argument actually require of its key premise, and why does the Kalam's conclusion not depend on the universe being contingent while the Leibnizian argument's does? What can each establish that the other cannot?

2. Craig supports Premise 2 of the Kalam with two distinct kinds of argument — philosophical arguments against actual infinities and the Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem. Evaluate the force of each independently: does the philosophical case from Hilbert's Hotel show that an actual infinite is *metaphysically* impossible, or only that it behaves counter-intuitively? And what does the BGV theorem actually establish about alternative cosmological models such as loop quantum cosmology and the Hartle-Hawking no-boundary proposal?

3. The Leibnizian argument's key move is that a series of contingent beings, however long, cannot explain its own existence. Hume responds that this inference commits the fallacy of composition, while Pruss insists the real question is why there are any contingent beings at all rather than none. Who has the stronger position, and what does settling this dispute require about the scope and justification of the Principle of Sufficient Reason?

4. Craig's supplementary argument concludes that the cause of the universe must be *personal*, on the grounds that only a being with will can explain why a timeless cause produced a temporal effect at one "moment" rather than another or never. Mackie and Oppy charge that this reasoning illicitly applies temporal concepts to a being with no temporal location. How serious is this objection, and what would a satisfying account of "timeless personal agency" need to look like?

5. Paley's watchmaker argument and the fine-tuning argument both infer design from a similar observation — complexity that serves a purpose. But Darwin displaced Paley's version while the fine-tuning argument survived. What exactly did Darwin show, and why does it refute Paley's biological argument while leaving the fine-tuning argument intact? Does this difference vindicate Swinburne's claim that the design argument's proper target is physics, not biology?

6. The multiverse response to fine-tuning and the Leibnizian argument against necessary being share a structural resemblance: both allow an unexplained brute plurality to terminate the explanatory regress. Defenders of fine-tuning argue that the multiverse itself requires explanation — its law-generating mechanism may itself be fine-tuned — while defenders of the Leibnizian argument argue the universe cannot simply be declared necessary. Are these two "brute plurality" moves equally ad hoc, or is there a principled asymmetry between them?

7. Gaunilo's perfect-island parody and the modal ontological argument's "maximally evil being" symmetry problem both challenge the ontological argument by running parallel reasoning to absurd or contradictory conclusions. Plantinga responds differently to each: he says the "greatest island" concept has no coherent maximum, and that a maximally evil being is internally incoherent. Are these responses principled and independent, or does each response borrow assumptions that could be turned against the original argument?

8. The modal ontological argument is logically valid in S5 but hinges entirely on whether it is possible that a maximally great being exists. Plantinga concedes that one can rationally deny this premise. How does the argument's dependence on S5 and on the coherence of "necessary existence" as a genuine modal property connect to the Leibnizian argument's requirement that God be a necessary being in the metaphysical rather than merely logical sense? Could someone accept the Leibnizian conclusion (a necessary being exists) while rationally denying the modal ontological argument's key premise?
