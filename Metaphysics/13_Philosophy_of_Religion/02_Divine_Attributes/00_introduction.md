# Divine Attributes

Classical theism — the conception of God developed by Anselm, Aquinas, and the mainstream of medieval philosophy — characterizes God in terms of a cluster of attributes: omnipotence (all-powerfulness), omniscience (all-knowingness), omnibenevolence (perfect goodness), necessary existence, simplicity, immutability, and eternity. These attributes are supposed to flow from the fundamental conception of God as the greatest possible being — the being than which nothing greater can be conceived. If God is maximal greatness, God must have all perfections; omnipotence, omniscience, and perfect goodness are perfections; therefore, God has them.

But the individual attributes, when examined closely, generate puzzles that can seem to undermine the coherence of classical theism. Can omnipotence be coherent? The stone paradox — can God create a stone too heavy for God to lift? — suggests that omnipotence might be self-undermining. Standard responses distinguish omnipotence from the ability to do the logically impossible and note that a being who cannot do the logically impossible may be more rather than less perfect than one that is subject to no limits.

Omniscience raises harder problems. If God knows in advance what free agents will do, do those agents have genuine freedom? The foreknowledge-freedom problem has generated an enormous literature. Compatibilist responses argue that divine foreknowledge is compatible with human freedom; Boethian responses appeal to divine timelessness (God sees all of history "at once" from outside time); open theist responses deny that God foreknows future free actions, revising the conception of omniscience to exclude knowledge of what no one has yet made true.

Divine eternity — whether God is timeless or everlasting — and divine simplicity — whether God has genuinely distinct properties or is absolutely simple in nature — raise further metaphysical questions that this chapter addresses systematically. The attributes of God are not theological decorations; they are the core of what theism claims, and understanding them is essential to evaluating whether theism is coherent.
