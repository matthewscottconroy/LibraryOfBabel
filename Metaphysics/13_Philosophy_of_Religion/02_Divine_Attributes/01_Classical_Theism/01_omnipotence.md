# Omnipotence

"God can do anything." This seems like a natural articulation of unlimited power, but it generates immediate paradoxes that reveal the difficulty of making the concept precise. Can God create a stone so heavy that God cannot lift it? If yes, there is something God cannot do (lift it). If no, there is something God cannot do (create it). The paradox is not merely wordplay — it forces us to think carefully about what omnipotence really means.

The standard response is to refine the concept. A common formulation: God can do anything that is *logically possible* and *consistent with God's nature*. Creating a stone too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift is not a coherent task — it is not something that God fails to accomplish; it is simply not a possible task at all. Just as "drawing a round square" is no task at all, "making a stone that an omnipotent being cannot lift" describes nothing that could exist. And where there is no coherent task, there is no limit on power.

## Definitions of Omnipotence

Several formal definitions have been proposed, each capturing something important:

**Omnipotence-1 (naive)**: God can do everything. Problem: this includes impossible tasks (make a round square), which are not genuine tasks.

**Omnipotence-2 (Descartes' version)**: God can do everything, including logical impossibilities. Descartes held that God could violate logical laws if God chose, grounding modal truths in God's will. Problem: this makes God's nature radically unknowable and many find it incoherent — logic itself would be undermined.

**Omnipotence-3 (standard modal version)**: God can bring about any state of affairs that is intrinsically possible — not self-contradictory. This excludes round squares and stones too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift, while preserving maximal power over genuinely possible states. This is the most common contemporary formulation.

**Omnipotence-4 (Flint-Freddoso)**: God is able to actualize any state of affairs that is intrinsically possible for God to actualize — excluding not only logically impossible states but also states that cannot be actualized by any being with God's essential properties.

## Moral Limitations

A different kind of puzzle arises from God's essential goodness. Can God sin? Can God lie? Traditional theists say no — God's essential goodness prevents sinful action. But doesn't this mean there are things God cannot do?

The standard response is that the inability to sin is a perfection, not a limitation. The "power" to sin is not a power in the morally relevant sense but a defect — a capacity for failure. Just as it would be no intellectual perfection for a mathematician to be able to believe that 2 + 2 = 5, it is no perfection for God to be able to do what is morally evil. Omnipotence concerns power over states of affairs in the world, not over God's own essential nature.

## The Paradox of the Weak and Questions of Self-Limitation

Can God create a being whose strength exceeds God's? If omnipotence includes the power to create any possible kind of being, then apparently yes. But then there would be something stronger than an omnipotent being. The standard response: omnipotence is not about being the strongest being that could exist but about being able to bring about any possible state of affairs. God creating a being stronger in some dimension does not diminish God's power, and in any case an omnipotent God could simply remove that being.

A more theologically interesting question concerns *kenosis* — voluntary self-limitation. The Philippians 2:7 passage describes Christ "emptying himself" in the incarnation, which has generated kenotic Christology: the second person of the Trinity voluntarily limited divine attributes. Can an omnipotent being genuinely limit its own power? If so, does the self-limited being remain God? And can such limitation be genuine — does God's power persist regardless of intentions? The conceptual issues here connect to debates in Christology and to questions about what features of divinity are essential.

One further puzzle: can God change the past? Some argue that omnipotence extends over all of time. Most philosophers hold that changing the past is logically impossible even for God — what has happened has happened, and "changing" it would involve contradicting the fact that it occurred. This is not a limit on God's power but a feature of the logic of time.

## Omnipotence and Foreknowledge

A less-discussed interaction: if God is both omnipotent and omniscient, and God knows what God will do in the future, and God's beliefs are infallible, then God seems unable to act other than as foreknown. God's future actions appear constrained by God's own past knowledge-states. This creates an unexpected complication about divine freedom that runs parallel to the problem of human freedom under divine foreknowledge — God's own future choices, it seems, would need to be unconstrained by anything, including God's own prior self-knowledge.
