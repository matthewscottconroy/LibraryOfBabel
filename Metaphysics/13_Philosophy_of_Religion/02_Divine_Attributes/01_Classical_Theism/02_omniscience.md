# Omniscience

God knows everything that is the case — every fact about the past, present, and future; every mathematical truth; every moral truth; every fact about every possible world. Omniscience is typically held to be a great-making property: a perfectly rational and powerful being would, all else equal, know more rather than less, and a maximally great being would know everything. This is clean in the abstract. It generates serious puzzles in the particular.

## What Does Omniscience Include?

The scope of omniscience is not entirely straightforward, and careful thinking reveals several contested dimensions.

Does God know future contingent events? If tomorrow's free actions are not yet determined, is there a fact now about what they will be? Classical theists say yes — God's eternal knowledge encompasses all of history. Open theists say no — future free actions are not yet determinate and so cannot be known even by an omniscient being, and this limitation is no imperfection in God.

Does God know *counterfactuals of creaturely freedom* — what any free creature *would* do in any possible circumstance? Molinism holds that God has such "middle knowledge." Critics (Robert Adams, William Hasker) argue there may be no truths of this kind to be known, in which case middle knowledge is an illusion rather than a form of omniscience.

Does God know indexical truths — "what time it is now"? Some philosophers argue that God, if eternal and timeless, cannot know tensed truths such as "the battle of Waterloo is in the past," because tensed truths are perspectival and change truth-value over time. A timeless God's knowledge, being eternal, cannot change — which may mean God lacks access to a whole class of genuinely informative truths.

## The Foreknowledge Problem

Omniscience generates its most significant puzzle when combined with human freedom. The argument can be made precise. Suppose God knew yesterday, with infallible certainty, that you would eat cereal for breakfast today. Call this God's past belief B. Then:

1. □(B → A at t₂) — God's beliefs are infallible: necessarily, if God believed it, it occurs.
2. B is a fact about t₁ — it is in the fixed, unalterable past at t₂.
3. □(Alterable(A at t₂) → ~B at t₁) — if you could do otherwise at t₂, God's belief at t₁ was false.
4. ~Alterable(past facts) — the past is fixed.
5. Therefore, ~Alterable(A at t₂) — you cannot do otherwise.
6. If you cannot do otherwise, you do not freely do A (libertarian freedom requires the ability to do otherwise).
7. Therefore, you do not freely do A.

This argument, given its sharpest contemporary formulation by Nelson Pike in "Divine Omniscience and Voluntary Action" (1965), has generated an enormous literature. The natural first response is compatibilism: freedom requires acting according to one's own desires without external compulsion, not the ability to do otherwise. God's foreknowledge doesn't compel you; it merely tracks what you freely do. This response accepts the incompatibility of foreknowledge with *libertarian* freedom while denying that libertarian freedom is required.

This drives us toward Molinism if we want to preserve libertarian freedom. Luis de Molina (1535–1600) proposed that God knows not what you will do simpliciter, but what you *would* freely do in any possible circumstance — "counterfactuals of creaturely freedom" (CCFs). God's "middle knowledge" stands between knowledge of all possible worlds and knowledge of the actual world. You genuinely could have done otherwise; God simply knew you wouldn't. William Lane Craig and Plantinga defend this view. The objection from Adams and Hasker is that CCFs may have no truth value if the free actions they describe are genuinely undetermined — in which case there is nothing for middle knowledge to be knowledge of.

Open theism takes a more radical route: God voluntarily limits foreknowledge of future free actions. God does not know what you will freely do until you do it. On this view, omniscience is properly defined as knowledge of all *knowable* truths, and future free actions may not yet be knowable. Clark Pinnock, John Sanders, and Gregory Boyd defend this position. Critics argue that it makes God's providential guidance of history contingent on what creatures happen to do, and raises questions about the reliability of divine promises.

The timelessness solution, defended by Boethius and elaborated by Eleonore Stump and Norman Kretzmann, holds that God's knowledge is not temporally prior to your action at all. God "sees" all of history in an eternal present, simultaneously with every temporal moment rather than before them. Boethius's image: a spectator on a high tower watching a procession sees all of it at once, without determining how it unfolds. Since God's knowledge has no temporal location, the argument's premise that God's belief is a fixed *past* fact does not apply.

Finally, the Ockhamist solution (Plantinga, Zagzebski) distinguishes *hard* past facts — genuinely about the past, independent of the future — from *soft* past facts — about the past but constitutively dependent on what happens in the future. God's past belief about your future action is a "soft" past fact: its content depends on what you actually do. If soft past facts are not fixed in the relevant sense, the argument's premise that the past is immovable applies only to hard past facts, and God's past beliefs fall on the soft side of the distinction. Critics argue the hard/soft distinction is unprincipled, or that soft past facts cannot be altered even by future actions.

## Liar-Style Paradoxes

A further technical puzzle: can God know propositions that are self-referentially paradoxical? Consider "This proposition is not known by God." If God knows it, it is false; if God doesn't know it, there seems to be a truth God doesn't know. These paradoxes parallel the Liar and suggest that omniscience cannot include an unqualified domain of all propositions.
