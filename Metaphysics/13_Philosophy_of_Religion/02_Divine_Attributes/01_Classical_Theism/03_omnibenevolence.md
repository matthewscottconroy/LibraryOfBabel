# Omnibenevolence

In classical theism, God's goodness is not an additional feature alongside power and knowledge, something God happens to have in addition to everything else. Goodness is what God *is*. God doesn't just happen to be good; the divine nature is not merely characterized by goodness but is constitutive of it. This is a striking claim — and it generates immediate questions about what "good" means when predicated of God, and whether God is subject to any moral standard at all.

## What Does Omnibenevolence Require?

Three main interpretations of divine goodness are at stake here.

The *moral goodness* reading holds that God always acts in accordance with the highest moral principles — God never sins, never acts unjustly, always promotes the good of creatures. On this reading, omnibenevolence is an evaluative property, and there is a moral standard against which God's actions can in principle be assessed.

The *ontological perfection* reading follows the Platonic and Neoplatonic tradition in which "good" (*bonum*) is convertible with "being" (*esse*) — what is real is good to the degree it participates in being. God, as the fullness of being, is the supreme Good, which is the source and standard of all created goodness. Divine goodness on this reading is not a moral property at all in the ordinary sense.

The *axiological supremacy* reading holds that God is the ultimate source and ground of all moral value — whatever is morally good is good in virtue of its relation to God.

## The Euthyphro Dilemma

The relation between God and moral value was given its classic formulation by Plato in *Euthyphro* (~399 BCE): is something good because God commands it (divine command theory), or does God command it because it is good?

**Divine command theory (DCT)** takes the first horn. Something is morally good because God wills it; God's will is the source of morality. The obvious objection: if God had commanded cruelty or torture, those would be good. This makes morality arbitrary — God could have made any act morally obligatory, including acts we intuitively recognize as monstrous.

**The independent standard** takes the second horn. God commands what is good because it is independently good. This preserves the objectivity of morality but appears to undercut God's supreme status: there is now a standard of goodness prior to God, by which God's commands can be evaluated.

Neither horn is comfortable. The dilemma has occupied philosophers and theologians since Plato.

## The Theistic Resolution: Divine Nature Theory

The standard contemporary response — associated with Robert Adams and widely accepted — is a third option: God's nature *is* goodness. What God essentially is constitutes the standard of moral perfection. God doesn't command things arbitrarily (so goodness isn't arbitrary), and there is no standard of goodness prior to and independent of God (so God's supreme status is preserved). The divine nature is the ultimate metaphysical grounding of moral value.

On this view, goodness is not a standard external to God that God must measure up to; nor is it simply whatever God happens to will. It is identical with or constituted by God's essential nature — which is not arbitrary, since God cannot have a different nature. Adams argues that what makes acts right is their conforming to what a perfectly loving God would command: not an arbitrary command but one flowing from a perfectly loving nature that could not have been otherwise.

## Objections to Divine Nature Theory

The *circularity objection* presses that if goodness just *is* God's nature, then "God is good" becomes trivially true — God is what God is — and the concept loses its content. What does it mean to say God is perfectly loving if "loving" just means "however God is"?

The *abhorrent commands problem* notes that theistic traditions record instances of apparent divine commands to violence — genocide in parts of the Hebrew Bible, for instance. If these commands flow from God's nature, and God's nature is the standard of goodness, how can we evaluate them critically? Divine nature theory may inadvertently validate any attributed divine command by making it immune to external moral assessment.

The *modal status of morality* issue is philosophically interesting: if moral truths depend on God's nature, and God exists necessarily, then moral truths are necessary truths. A world in which torturing innocents for pleasure is permissible would be a metaphysically impossible world. Atheist moral realists may agree with the conclusion while disputing the theistic grounding.

## A Further Question: Is God Subject to Moral Requirements?

What obligates God to act in accordance with God's own goodness? If God is perfectly good by nature, is God *constrained* by that nature? On some views, God's perfect goodness is not a constraint but a constitutive feature — God just is perfectly good, and there is no separate "self" that must conform to it. On other views, goodness places normative demands on God as it does on anyone else. The question of whether God is "subject to" moral requirements, or whether moral requirements only apply to beings who can fail to meet them (which God cannot), remains genuinely contested.
