# Divine Eternity

Is God inside or outside of time? This question — about the nature of God's relationship to temporality — is one of the most important and most debated in the philosophy of religion, with implications for divine foreknowledge, divine action, prayer, and the general coherence of the classical theistic picture.

The tradition of divine timelessness, associated with Boethius, Aquinas, and much of the scholastic tradition, holds that God does not exist in time at all. God is "eternal" in the sense of being outside of time — not temporal at all. God does not experience a succession of moments; God's "now" is not a moving boundary between past and future but a single, comprehensive, timeless present in which all of time is "simultaneously" available to the divine knowledge. The phrase "nunc stans" — the standing now — captures this picture: God's existence is not stretched across time but gathered into an eternal instant.

Divine timelessness has obvious advantages for the problem of foreknowledge and freedom. If God's knowledge of your future action is not foreknowledge in the temporal sense — if it is not the case that God knows at a time before your action what you will do — then the familiar argument from foreknowledge to fatalism is undermined. God sees your action "from eternity," not from the vantage point of a moment earlier than your action; and seeing something at a different level of reality from the temporal order does not determine it.

The alternative — divine everlastingness — holds that God exists at every moment of time, from the beginning to the end, but is not outside of time. God has a temporal sequence of states, knows the present at the present, and genuinely waits for the future. Open theism, a contemporary variant, holds that God does not have complete foreknowledge of free human actions because genuine freedom is incompatible with God's knowing the outcome in advance.

This section examines the divine eternity debate, the arguments for timelessness and everlastingness, and the implications of each for divine foreknowledge and action.
