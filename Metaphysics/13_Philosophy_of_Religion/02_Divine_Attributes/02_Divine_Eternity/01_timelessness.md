# Divine Timelessness

Boethius gave the doctrine of divine timelessness its definitive classical formulation in *The Consolation of Philosophy* (c. 524): "Eternity is the complete, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unlimited life" (*aeternitas est interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio*). The key word is *simul* — all at once. God's eternity is not an unlimited extension of time but a different mode of being entirely. God does not have a yesterday and a today and a tomorrow; God has a single, timeless "now" in which all of history is simultaneously present.

This is a philosophically extraordinary claim. Let us try to get clear on what it asserts and what arguments support it, before turning to the serious objections.

## Arguments for Timelessness

**From divine simplicity**: Aquinas grounds timelessness in divine simplicity. A temporal being has distinct temporal phases — it exists at different moments with different states. These phases are, in a sense, parts of the temporal being's existence. But a perfectly simple God has no parts of any kind, including temporal ones. If God's whole being is present "at once," there is no temporal sequence in the divine life.

**From divine immutability**: A temporal God who responds to events would change — God at t₁ would have certain knowledge-states, God at t₂ would have others. But a timeless God cannot undergo temporal change. If God is perfectly immutable — unchanging in every respect — then God cannot gain or lose temporal properties. The doctrine of timelessness and the doctrine of immutability thus mutually support each other.

**From divine perfection**: Following Plato and Plotinus, true being is permanent, unchanging, and eternal. Temporal flux is characteristic of imperfect, material being. A perfect God participates in this eternal, non-temporal mode of being rather than in the restless succession of temporal existence.

**Resolution of the foreknowledge problem**: If God is timeless, God's knowledge of your future actions is not *prior* to them in the sense that would generate the argument for incompatibility with freedom. God "sees" your actions in an eternal present that is co-present with every temporal moment, rather than knowing them from a temporal vantage point that preceded them. Boethius's image: God is like someone on a high tower watching a procession — seeing all of it "at once" without causing any of it.

More formally, the timelessness thesis holds that God exists without being located at any time t in the temporal series; that God has no temporal before or after; that every moment of temporal history is "present" to God, though not all simultaneously present to any temporal observer; and that God's eternal present grounds all temporal moments rather than being one moment among them.

## A Refinement: ET-Simultaneity

Eleonore Stump and Norman Kretzmann developed the concept of "ET-simultaneity" (eternity-time simultaneity): a timeless God can be "present to" temporal events through a special atemporal-temporal relation, even though God has no temporal location. This distinguishes strict atemporality (no temporal relations whatever) from extended atemporality (no temporal sequence, but some form of co-presence with temporal events). Critics including Paul Fitzgerald and Brian Leftow have debated whether ET-simultaneity is coherent, since "simultaneous" normally means "at the same time," and if God is not in time, the ordinary meaning of simultaneity may not apply.

## Objections to Timelessness

The **action problem** is the most pressing. If God is timeless and immutable, can God act in history — answer prayers, respond to events, become incarnate? These seem to require temporal location. Creation is an action with a temporal result; prayer answers are responses to temporally located petitions. A timeless God seems unable to perform temporally indexed actions. Defenders respond that God's timeless creative act produces a temporal result; the time of the result is in the world, not in God. Brian Leftow defends this response at length in *Time and Eternity* (1991).

The **relational problem** deepens this: if I repent at 3pm and God forgives me in response, there seems to be a temporal ordering — repentance, then forgiveness. If God's forgiveness is timeless, it seems God "forgives" without responding to anything. The forgiveness was not a response to the repentance at all — it was always already there. This threatens the relational character of God's engagement with creatures in a way that many find theologically unacceptable.

The **incarnation problem** is perhaps the sharpest challenge for Christian defenders of timelessness. Christian theology holds that the second person of the Trinity became incarnate — became human and entered time. At least one divine person apparently became temporal. How can a timeless God enter time?

Finally, the **knowledge problem**: a timeless God cannot know tensed truths — "it is now 3pm," "Caesar just crossed the Rubicon." Tensed truths are genuinely temporal and change their truth-value over time. If God knows all truths, God must know tensed truths; but a timeless being cannot have a temporal perspective from which to know them. Paul Helm denies that there are irreducibly tensed truths; William Hasker takes this as a decisive objection to timelessness.
