# Divine Everlastingness

The God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob is a God who speaks to Moses, who "remembers" the Israelites in Egypt, who "repents" of intended actions in response to intercession, who enters history at particular times. This is a God who inhabits time alongside temporal creatures — not a God who views all of history from a timeless vantage point. The doctrine of divine everlastingness takes this picture seriously and treats it as a theological datum rather than a naive anthropomorphism to be overcome.

On this view, God exists *within* time — at every moment, without beginning or end. God is "eternal" not in the sense of being outside time but in the sense of persisting through all of time without limit. God has a genuine past and future; God acts at particular moments; God knows what time it is.

## The Wolterstorff-Swinburne Case

Nicholas Wolterstorff argues in "God Everlasting" (1975) that God's being in time is required to make sense of divine personhood, action, and responsiveness:

- P1: A being that acts — brings about states of affairs at particular times — must itself be temporal; its actions occur at times.
- P2: The God of classical theism acts: creates at t₁, speaks to Moses at t₂, answers prayers at t₃.
- P3: Therefore, God is in time.

A timeless God's "actions" cannot be genuinely temporally indexed — they cannot occur *at* a time — and so the robust biblical picture of a God who acts in history cannot be maintained on the timeless model. The timelessness view, on this account, preserves an abstract philosophical conception of God while discarding the religiously significant one.

Richard Swinburne extends this in *The Coherence of Theism* (1977). Personhood requires temporal succession — a person is a being who deliberates, intends, and acts in a temporal order. A timeless "person" is not a person in any recognizable sense. Furthermore, omniscience of a temporal world requires knowing what is now the case, not merely what is tenselessly the case. A timeless God cannot know tensed truths, and since these are genuinely informative, a timeless God's knowledge is incomplete.

## Main Objections

The debate is genuinely difficult, and everlastingness faces challenges that have kept classical timelessness philosophically competitive.

**Omniscience and temporal change**: If God is in time, God's knowledge updates as events unfold — God comes to know things God did not know before. A perfectly omniscient being who gains new knowledge across time seems to have been incomplete at earlier times. Defenders respond that omniscience means knowing all truths *at each time*: at every moment, God knows everything true at that moment. Whether this rescues omniscience or redefines it is contested.

**Divine immutability and simplicity**: Classical metaphysical theology holds that divine simplicity and immutability require timelessness. An everlasting God seems to have temporal parts (God-at-t₁, God-at-t₂), which conflicts with simplicity. An everlasting God undergoes relational changes — God becomes "the forgiver of your sin" at a particular moment — if not intrinsic ones. The challenge is whether relational changes compromise theologically significant immutability.

**Divine sovereignty**: If God is in time, God is subject to the unalterable past in the same way finite beings are. There is a genuine sense in which God cannot change what is past. This seems to threaten divine sovereignty in a way that the timeless model avoids: a timeless God "has" all of history in its eternal present, while an everlasting God is subject to the constraint that the past is fixed.

**The threat to divine perfection**: The classical tradition associated temporal change with imperfection. A being that changes acquires what it lacked or loses what it had — this reveals incompleteness. Defenders of everlastingness respond that temporal change is not necessarily imperfect: God's loving responsiveness to new prayers need not imply that God was in any way deficient before those prayers were offered.

## The Philosophy of Time Connection

The debate between timelessness and everlastingness connects to deep disputes in the philosophy of time. **A-theory** (tensed time) favors everlastingness: if there is a genuine objective "now," a real past-present-future distinction, then an everlasting God can be genuinely present to all moments as they occur. **B-theory** (tenseless time) is more naturally hospitable to timelessness: if all times are equally real and there is no privileged "now," then God can be "co-present" with all times without temporal location. The metaphysical question of whether time is fundamentally tensed thus bears directly on what divine eternity can coherently be.

A further question: if time begins with creation (as Big Bang cosmology suggests), does God exist "before" creation? Open theists and everlastingness defenders typically hold either that God exists in some pre-temporal or timeless state before creation and then enters time with it, or that time is itself God's creation and God exists "in" it by virtue of creating it — though this threatens to blur the very distinction between timelessness and everlastingness that the debate is about.
