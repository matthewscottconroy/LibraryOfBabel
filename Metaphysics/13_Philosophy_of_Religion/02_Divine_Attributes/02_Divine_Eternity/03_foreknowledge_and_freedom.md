# Foreknowledge and Freedom

The tension between divine foreknowledge and human freedom is one of the oldest problems in philosophy of religion, and it is worth setting out carefully, because the argument is more subtle than it first appears. The intuitive worry is simple: if God already knows what you will do, how can you be free to do otherwise? But the philosophical challenge is to show that this intuition amounts to a valid argument with true premises — and that has turned out to be surprisingly difficult.

## The Problem Stated Precisely

Suppose God knew yesterday, with infallible certainty, that you would eat cereal for breakfast today. Call this God's past belief B. Now consider:

Let B = "God believed at t₁ that S would do A at t₂" (where t₁ is before t₂).
Let F = "S freely does A at t₂."

- (1) □(B → A at t₂) — God's beliefs are infallible: necessarily, if God believed it, it occurs.
- (2) B is a fact about t₁ — it is in the fixed, unalterable past at t₂.
- (3) □(Alterable(A at t₂) → ~B at t₁) — if S can do otherwise at t₂, God's belief at t₁ was false.
- (4) ~Alterable(past facts) — the past is fixed.
- (5) Therefore, ~Alterable(A at t₂) — S cannot do otherwise.
- (6) If S cannot do otherwise, S does not freely do A (libertarian freedom requires the ability to do otherwise).
- (7) Therefore, S does not freely do A.

Nelson Pike gave this its sharpest contemporary formulation in "Divine Omniscience and Voluntary Action" (1965). The argument is valid. The question is whether all the premises are true.

## The Main Responses

**Compatibilism** accepts the conclusion of step (5) while denying that (6) is true. You are free in the sense that you act according to your own desires and without external compulsion — even if your action was foreknown. Freedom does not require the ability to do otherwise in the strong libertarian sense; it requires only the absence of coercion. God's foreknowledge doesn't compel you; it tracks what you freely do. This response simply denies that libertarian freedom is the relevant concept and shifts the question to the general debate about free will and determinism.

**Molinism** preserves libertarian freedom through the doctrine of middle knowledge, developed by Luis de Molina in *Concordia* (1588). God knows not what you will do simpliciter but what you *would* freely do in any possible circumstance — "counterfactuals of creaturely freedom" (CCFs). God's knowledge has three parts:

- **Natural knowledge**: all necessary truths and all possibilities, known through God's nature.
- **Middle knowledge**: CCFs — what free creatures would do in every possible circumstance.
- **Free knowledge**: what actually happens, following God's decision to actualize a world given the CCFs.

Molinism preserves genuine libertarian freedom: in each possible world, creatures freely choose what they would choose; God simply knows these free choices without determining them. The objection from Robert Adams and William Hasker is that there may be no truths to constitute CCFs. If a counterfactual free action is genuinely undetermined, there is no fact about what a free agent *would* do in a non-actual circumstance — the counterfactual has no determinate truth-value. Middle knowledge presupposes the truth of CCFs, but their truth is philosophically disputed.

**Open theism** is the most straightforwardly compatibilist solution: God voluntarily limits foreknowledge of future free actions. God does not know what you will freely do until you do it. This dissolves the problem by removing the foreknowledge that generates it. Open theists (Clark Pinnock, John Sanders, Gregory Boyd) redefine omniscience: God knows all *knowable* truths, and future free actions may not yet be knowable because they are not yet determined. The cost is that God's providential guidance cannot involve detailed foreknowledge of free human choices — God must respond to what creatures actually do.

**The timelessness solution** (Boethius, Aquinas, Stump and Kretzmann, Paul Helm) holds that if God exists outside time, God's knowledge of your future actions is not *prior* to them in the temporally relevant sense. God "sees" past, present, and future in an eternal present. Since the argument requires that God's belief be a *past* fact relative to your action — and a timeless God's knowledge has no temporal location — the argument's crucial premise (2) does not apply.

Boethius's image: a spectator at a horse race sees all the horses simultaneously; this does not determine how they run. God's eternal knowledge encompasses all temporal events without causing them. The objection is that even a timeless belief that you will do A is a "soft" necessary condition for your doing A — given that God timelessly believes this, you cannot do otherwise without making God's timeless belief false. The timelessness solution may not fully dissolve the logical problem.

**The Ockhamist solution** (Plantinga, Zagzebski) distinguishes *hard* past facts — genuinely about the past, independent of the future — from *soft* past facts — about the past but constitutively dependent on what happens in the future. God's past belief that you would do A is "soft": its content depends on what you actually do at the future time. If soft past facts are not fixed in the relevant sense (4 should be restricted to hard past facts), then your freedom to do otherwise is compatible with God's past belief. Critics argue that the hard/soft distinction is not principled, or that even soft past facts cannot be altered by future actions.

## Why This Matters

The foreknowledge problem is not merely academic. It bears directly on how theists understand prayer: if God knows what you will do, does prayer actually change anything? On the timelessness view, your prayer was always part of God's eternal vision, and God's response was always part of that same vision — so prayer can make a difference, but in a way that makes causal direction hard to characterize. It bears on divine providence: if God cannot know what free creatures will do, how can God guide history toward any definite goal? And it bears on moral responsibility: if your actions were foreknown from eternity, are you genuinely responsible for them? Different solutions — compatibilism, Molinism, open theism, timelessness, Ockhamism — have very different implications for each of these questions, which is why the debate has remained live for more than fifteen centuries.
