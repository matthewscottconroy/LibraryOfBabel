# Divine Simplicity

When Aquinas says that God is "simple," he means something radical. God has no parts — not spatial parts, not temporal parts, and not metaphysical parts of any kind. The distinction between essence and existence that characterizes every created thing does not apply to God. The distinction between God and God's attributes — between "the being that has wisdom" and "the wisdom had" — does not apply either. God is not a thing that possesses wisdom and power and goodness as distinct properties. God simply *is* wisdom, *is* power, *is* goodness, in the sense that these are different names for one utterly simple reality.

This is an extraordinary doctrine and deserves careful exposition before evaluation.

## The Thomistic Argument

Aquinas argues for divine simplicity from the claim that God is pure actuality (*actus purus*). Aquinas constructs the argument in stages in *Summa Theologiae* I, qq. 3–11:

1. God is not a body: spatial parts entail potentiality and dependence.
2. God is not composed of form and matter: matter is potential; God is pure form.
3. God is not distinct from God's own nature: in creatures, the individual differs from the species; in God, the individual *is* the species — God is the divine nature, not an instance of it.
4. God's essence is identical with God's existence: in all created things, essence and existence are distinct — what a thing *is* does not entail *that* it exists. In God alone, essence = existence.
5. God has no accidents: no properties that might have been otherwise. All of God's "attributes" are identical with God's essence.

If God had parts — a composition of actuality and potentiality — then God would depend on those parts, which would contradict divine aseity (self-sufficiency). God must be completely actual, with no unrealized potentiality. A composite being is potentially other than it is; God cannot be.

## The Radical Implications

The implications are striking. If God is simple, then God's wisdom, power, and goodness are not distinct properties God "has" — they are identical with each other and with the divine essence:

- God's wisdom = God's power = God's goodness = God

This does not mean that wisdom and power are the same concept in the abstract — it means that the divine wisdom, the divine power, and the divine goodness are all identical with the same simple divine reality, which human minds understand under multiple aspects, drawing on multiple concepts, without any of those concepts fully capturing the simple truth.

The doctrine serves several purposes: it distinguishes theism from polytheism by making God not a being among beings but being itself (*ipsum esse subsistens* — "subsistent being itself"); it grounds divine necessity (a simple being cannot change, because change requires rearrangement of parts); and it secures divine transcendence (God belongs to no genus or species, no common class with creatures).

## Historical Background

Aquinas inherited the doctrine from multiple sources. Aristotle's Unmoved Mover is pure form without matter — entirely actual. Plotinus's "The One" transcends all predication and composition. Maimonides in *The Guide for the Perplexed* (c. 1190) argued that positive attributes cannot be truly predicated of God — only negative attributes ("God is not finite," "not composite," "not mortal") avoid the error of treating God as composed of a nature and its attributes.

## Objections

**Plantinga's incoherence objection** is the sharpest: if God's goodness = God's power = God, then goodness = power. But goodness and power are clearly different properties — this seems to commit the doctrine to an obvious falsehood. The classical theist's response — that "goodness" and "power" are different names for the same simple reality — can look like a verbal evasion rather than a genuine explanation. Plantinga presses this objection at length in *Does God Have a Nature?* (1980).

**The problem of divine attributes**: if God literally has no properties (since properties would be parts), then how can we say anything true about God? Following Maimonides, defenders of negative theology accept this: all positive predication of God is technically incorrect, and only negative statements are literally true. This preserves simplicity at the cost of theological expressibility.

**The problem of God's freedom**: if God's essence is identical with God's existence, and God cannot but exist, does God's creative act also occur necessarily? Some defenders have held that God creates necessarily. Most Thomists resist this inference, arguing that God's creative act is free even though grounded in a simple nature — though explaining how a necessarily simple nature grounds genuinely free contingent creative acts is the difficult challenge.

**Formal predication** is a contemporary analytic response: we distinguish between God's properties as *predicated of* God (under our descriptions) from what God is *intrinsically*. "God is wise" and "God is powerful" are true under human modes of description while God's intrinsic nature is simple — beyond any distinction we can draw. This preserves the spirit of simplicity while acknowledging that our language cannot directly capture the simple divine reality.
