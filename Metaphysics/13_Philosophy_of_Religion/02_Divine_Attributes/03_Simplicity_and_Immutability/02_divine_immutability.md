# Divine Immutability

"I the Lord do not change" (Malachi 3:6). Classical philosophical theology took this seriously — not as a casual remark but as a deep metaphysical truth about the nature of the divine being. God cannot change, not merely in being, but in will, in knowledge, in character. God is "the same yesterday, today, and forever" (Hebrews 13:8). This is not the temporal persistence of something that stays the same while time flows past it — it is the absolute fixity of a being that is entirely actual, with no potentiality for alteration.

The philosophical motivation derives from what change requires. To change is to acquire what one previously lacked or to lose what one previously had — it is a move from potentiality to actuality. But a perfect being has no unrealized potentiality. Any change in God would either be an improvement (implying prior imperfection) or a deterioration (implying current imperfection). Neither is compatible with divine perfection. God is also fully self-sufficient (*aseitas*): a being that changes is dependent on whatever brings about or sustains the change, but God depends on nothing outside itself. And a simple being, with no parts, cannot change because change requires gaining or losing properties — which are "parts" of a simple being.

There is also a theological motivation: if God changed, God's promises and character would be unreliable. The faithfulness of God — the ground on which any covenant can rest — requires that God's nature does not shift.

## Aquinas's Formulation

Aquinas's argument in *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 9 is clean:

- P1: Whatever is moved is in some respect potential.
- P2: God is pure actuality, with no potentiality.
- C: God cannot be moved (changed) in any respect.

From this Aquinas derives that God's knowledge, will, and being are all unchanging. God does not acquire or lose knowledge; God does not alter God's will; God does not come to be or cease to be in any respect.

## The Challenge from Relational Theology and Process Thought

Immutability faces a direct challenge from any account of God that takes divine responsiveness seriously.

**Process theology** (Whitehead, Hartshorne) makes the challenge explicit. God is the "fellow sufferer who understands." God takes in what happens in the world — Whitehead calls this God's "consequent nature" — and is genuinely affected by it. A God who is genuinely in relationship cannot be immutable: genuine love requires being affected by the beloved's suffering and joy. The immutable God of classical theism is not a lover but an indifferent absolute.

**Relational theology** within more orthodox traditions notes the same tension from the other side: scripture portrays God as responding to prayer, being moved by human sin, changing God's mind (*nacham* in Hebrew), being genuinely grieved. If this language is not merely metaphorical, God changes in ways that conflict with strict immutability.

## The Essential/Relational Distinction

The standard classical response is a distinction: God's *nature, character, and essential attributes* are immutable, but God's *relational properties* can vary. When I pray and God answers, God's relation to me changes — this is a relational fact about God that becomes true at a particular moment — but God's inner nature does not change.

Formally: if R is a relational property (God stands in relation R to creature C), then R can change when C changes, without any change in God's intrinsic nature. "God forgives John at noon" becomes true at noon; it does not require that anything in God's inner nature changes.

This response is associated with Aquinas and many contemporary Thomists, but it is contested. Critics argue in two directions. First, if God "genuinely responds" to my prayer, there must be a real change in God — not merely a relational change in me. Real responses require real changers. Second, if God doesn't intrinsically change in response to prayer, then God's "response" was built into God's eternal will from before creation. Critics (particularly open theists) argue this makes divine "responses" illusory — prayers change nothing, since the responses were always already ordained.

## Impassibility

The question of divine immutability intersects closely with divine impassibility: can God suffer? Classical theism holds that God cannot be acted upon by creatures — God's inner life is not subject to disturbance by creaturely events. Contemporary theologians have largely rejected impassibility. Jürgen Moltmann's *The Crucified God* (1974) holds that God suffers with and for creation; a God who cannot suffer seems remote and incapable of genuine compassion. The standard classical reply distinguishes affective states from volitional ones: God's compassion (*misericordia*) is an act of will — God wills the good of the suffering being — without being internally disrupted. Whether this distinction is satisfying, or whether it empties "compassion" of its most important content, is a live theological and philosophical debate.
