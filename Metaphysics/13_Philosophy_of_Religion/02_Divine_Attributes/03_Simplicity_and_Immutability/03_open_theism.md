# Open Theism

Classical theism holds that God knows, from eternity, everything that will happen — including all future free actions of creatures. Open theism denies this. The future, on this view, is genuinely open, even for God. God does not have exhaustive foreknowledge of future free actions, not because God is ignorant or defective, but because future free actions are not yet determinate facts and therefore cannot be objects of knowledge for any being, however great.

Open theism is a view within Christian theism — not to be confused with process theology. It was developed and defended in the late twentieth century primarily by Clark Pinnock (*The Openness of God*, 1994), John Sanders (*The God Who Risks*, 1998), and Gregory Boyd (*God of the Possible*, 2000). It arose partly from philosophical arguments about the incompatibility of foreknowledge and libertarian freedom, and partly from a felt inadequacy in the classical theist's account of genuine divine-human relationship.

## The Core Claim

The central philosophical claim is this: future free actions are not yet determinate facts. Because they are not yet determined, they are not knowable — not even by an omniscient being. Omniscience is the property of knowing all knowable truths; since undetermined future free actions are not yet knowable, God's not knowing them is no limitation on omniscience. God knows everything that *can* be known.

This contrasts with the classical view, on which God's eternal perspective encompasses all of history — including future events — from an atemporal or temporally infinite vantage point. Open theists respond that this classical picture either collapses genuine human freedom (since what God foreknows is in some sense already fixed) or makes God's "response" to prayer and human choices merely apparent (since those responses were eternally ordained regardless of what creatures do).

## The Relational Vision

Open theism's positive vision emphasizes the genuineness of divine-human relationship. A God who has exhaustive foreknowledge of all free actions — including all prayers, sins, and repentances — seems to be in a fixed, unchanging relationship with creation from eternity. Open theists argue that true love and genuine relationship require real risk, real responsiveness, and real contingency. God "takes risks" in creating free beings whose responses are not predetermined. The divine-human drama is genuinely dramatic, not a performance of a script known in advance.

On this picture, God's grief at human sin is genuine: not foreknown grief, but real-time response. God's joy at repentance is genuine. Prayer genuinely influences what God does, because God responds to prayers that God did not foreknow would be offered. These are not anthropomorphisms to be allegorized away — they are theologically and relationally fundamental.

## Objections from Traditional Theism

**Adequacy of divine guidance**: A God who does not know the future cannot guarantee that history will fulfill divine purposes. Open theists respond that God is sufficiently resourceful to accomplish divine aims in a world with an open future — God can anticipate the range of possible responses and prepare for any of them. God's purposes do not require specific free actions but can be accomplished through a range of scenarios.

**Reliability of prophecy**: If God does not know future free actions, how can God reliably prophesy them? Open theists distinguish conditional prophecy (conditioned on human responses), unconditional prophecy (based on God's own future actions, which God knows perfectly), and highly probable prophecy (based on God's knowledge of characters, circumstances, and overwhelming likelihoods). Not all prophetic speech requires infallible foreknowledge of contingent human choices.

**Divine perfection**: The classical theist holds that knowledge of the future is a perfection — an omniscient being should know everything, including what will happen. The open theist replies that omniscience of what is *knowable* is the perfection, and unknowable facts constitute no defect. Whether future free actions are genuinely unknowable depends on whether libertarian freedom is real and whether undetermined events have any determinate future truth-value. These are contested commitments, and the open theist's position is only as strong as its account of libertarian freedom.

## The Grounding Problem

A deep challenge: if there are no truths about future free actions (because those actions are not yet determined), then God also cannot know the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom that Molinism invokes. Open theism thus aligns with Molinism-skeptics like Adams and Hasker in rejecting middle knowledge — but for the same reason (undetermined actions have no determinate truth-values to constitute CCFs). The consequence is that God's providential planning cannot rely on specific foreknown free choices, making divine providence more general and less specific than traditional accounts allow.

## Relation to Process Theology

Open theism must be carefully distinguished from process theology (Whitehead, Hartshorne). Process theology holds that God's temporal limitations are metaphysically necessary — given the nature of time and freedom, God *cannot* be omniscient about future free actions. Open theism holds that God's limitations are a voluntary self-limitation. God *could* have created fully determined creatures — and then God's knowledge would have been exhaustive — but God chose to create free creatures and to refrain from foreknowing their free choices. This distinction matters theologically: open theists are committed to God's sovereignty and can affirm that God could override the open future if God chose, while process theologians deny this.

## Biblical Interpretation

Open theists appeal to biblical passages in which God appears to change God's mind — *nacham* in Hebrew, variously translated "repent" or "relent": Genesis 6:6 (God regrets making humans), Exodus 32:14 (God relents from destroying Israel after Moses's intercession), Amos 7:3 (God relents after Amos's prayer). Classical interpreters treat these as anthropomorphisms — figurative language accommodated to human understanding. Open theists argue they should be read more literally as reflecting God's genuine responsiveness to what creatures do. The exegetical dispute here is real and not easily settled by appeal to principle alone.
