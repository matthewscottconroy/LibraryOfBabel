# Important Terms and Ideas: Divine Simplicity and Immutability

**Divine Simplicity**
The doctrine that God has no parts, no composition, and no distinction between essence and existence, between subject and attributes, or between one attribute and another. God is identical to God's goodness, knowledge, power, etc.—all are one and the same thing.

**Real Distinction vs. Distinction of Reason**
Aquinas's framework: in creatures, there is a real distinction between essence and existence; in God, there is no real distinction—God's essence just is God's existence. Distinctions between divine attributes are distinctions of reason (in our way of conceiving) not real distinctions in God.

**The Problem of Divine Simplicity**
Plantinga's objections: if God = God's goodness, and goodness is a property, then God is a property—but properties are abstract objects, and God cannot be an abstract object. Also, if all of God's properties are necessary, God cannot have contingent properties (like freely creating this world).

**Divine Immutability**
The doctrine that God does not and cannot change: God's nature, properties, and knowledge are all unchanging. Immutability is often grounded in divine simplicity (if God has no composition, there is nothing to change) and in divine perfection (change in a perfect being would be deterioration).

**Impassibility**
Closely related to immutability: God is not acted upon by external things, does not suffer, and is not moved emotionally by creaturely suffering or joy. Classical theists defend impassibility; contemporary theologians often reject it in favor of a God who genuinely responds to and suffers with creation.

**Process Theology**
A theological tradition (Whitehead, Hartshorne) that rejects classical divine simplicity and immutability: God has two "poles"—a primordial pole (abstract, eternal) and a consequent pole (concrete, temporal, responsive). God grows and changes in response to the world; divine perfection is perfection of process, not of static being.
