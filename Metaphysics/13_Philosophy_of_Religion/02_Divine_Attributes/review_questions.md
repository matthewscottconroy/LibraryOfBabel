# Review Questions — Divine Attributes

1. Omnipotence is standardly refined to mean: God can bring about any intrinsically possible state of affairs. But the chapter identifies two different kinds of apparent limitation — logical impossibility (round squares) and moral impossibility (God cannot sin). The chapter argues that the inability to sin is a perfection rather than a defect. Is this response the same in structure as the response to the round-square case, or does it introduce a genuinely different category? What does it reveal about the relationship between omnipotence and divine nature?

2. The Euthyphro dilemma threatens omnibenevolence from two directions at once: if God's commands determine goodness, morality is arbitrary; if goodness is independent of God, something constrains God from outside. The Divine Nature Theory proposes a third option — goodness just *is* God's nature. Does this escape the dilemma, or does it generate a circularity of its own? What would it take for the claim "God is good" to be genuinely informative rather than trivially true on this view?

3. The foreknowledge problem arises from combining two independently plausible claims — God's beliefs are infallible, and the past is fixed — to conclude that our future actions are not within our power to alter. The Ockhamist response argues that God's past beliefs are "soft" past facts, not "hard" ones, and so are not subject to the fixity of the past. The timelessness response argues that God's knowledge has no temporal location and so is not a past fact at all. Which response, if successful, does more damage to the argument? And are both responses available to the same theist, or does adopting one foreclose the other?

4. Divine timelessness and divine everlastingness are not merely two different theological options — they carry different implications for omniscience, for divine action in history, and for what prayer can accomplish. Wolterstorff argues that a timeless God cannot perform genuinely temporally indexed actions; Leftow argues a timeless God's creative act produces temporal results without being temporally located. Evaluate: does the "action problem" decisively favor everlastingness, or does the timelessness view have adequate resources to respond?

5. Open theism redefines omniscience as knowledge of all *knowable* truths, arguing that undetermined future free actions are not yet knowable. The classical theist responds that this diminishes divine perfection. But the chapter also notes that if God is both omniscient and omnipotent, God's own future choices appear constrained by God's own past knowledge-states. Does this parallel problem of divine self-foreknowledge strengthen or complicate the open theist's case? Could an open theist apply the same logic symmetrically to resolve God's self-knowledge puzzle?

6. Divine simplicity holds that God's wisdom, power, and goodness are all identical with each other and with the divine essence. Plantinga's incoherence objection presses that if God's wisdom = God's power, then wisdom = power — which is manifestly false. The defender of simplicity replies that "wisdom" and "power" are different names for one simple reality that human minds divide conceptually. Is this reply a genuine philosophical explanation, or does it dissolve the puzzle by retreating to ineffability? What does the doctrine of simplicity require for the coherence of any positive theological claim about God?

7. The debate between timelessness and everlastingness turns partly on a background dispute in the philosophy of time. The chapter notes that A-theory (tensed time with a genuine objective "now") is more hospitable to everlastingness, while B-theory (tenseless time) is more hospitable to timelessness. Given what the book has covered elsewhere on the metaphysics of time, which theory of divine eternity does the A/B-theory debate actually favor, and what is at stake for each if one's preferred theory of time turns out to be wrong?

8. The chapter treats immutability and impassibility as closely connected: a God who cannot change intrinsically cannot be affected by creaturely suffering. Process theology rejects both doctrines, arguing that genuine love requires being affected by the beloved. The classical response distinguishes intrinsic change (which God cannot undergo) from relational change (which is merely a change in how God is described relative to changing creatures). Does this distinction preserve the theologically important content of divine responsiveness, or does it make God's "responses" to prayer and sin merely nominal?
