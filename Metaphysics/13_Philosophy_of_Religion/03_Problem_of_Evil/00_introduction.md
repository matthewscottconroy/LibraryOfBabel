# The Problem of Evil

In 1755, an earthquake struck Lisbon on All Saints' Day — the day when the faithful filled the city's churches — and killed between thirty and sixty thousand people. Churches collapsed on their congregations; a tsunami followed; fires spread through the rubble. Voltaire wrote a poem about the disaster, and Rousseau responded, and the resulting controversy made vivid what had been an abstract theological puzzle: if God is omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good, why does this happen? Why does a god with the power to prevent gratuitous suffering, the knowledge to know it occurs, and the goodness to care about it allow it to proceed?

This is the problem of evil, and it is the most powerful challenge to theism. It comes in a logical form and an evidential form. The logical problem, associated with J. L. Mackie, holds that the existence of an omnipotent, omniscient, and omnibenevolent God is logically incompatible with the existence of any evil at all. If God were truly omnipotent, God could have created a world without evil; if God were truly omniscient, God would know how; if God were truly omnibenevolent, God would want to. Therefore, evil's existence demonstrates that no such God exists.

The free will defense, developed by Alvin Plantinga, responds that God could not create free beings without risking their choosing evil. A world containing free beings who sometimes choose good is more valuable than a world of automata who always behave well; therefore, a perfectly good God might permit evil as the cost of freedom. This response handles moral evil (evil resulting from human choices) but does not obviously handle natural evil — earthquakes, cancer, tsunamis — which is not the product of human choices.

The evidential problem, associated with William Rowe, concedes that evil might be logically compatible with God's existence but argues that the amount and distribution of suffering — particularly the suffering of innocent children and animals that seems to serve no higher purpose — constitutes strong evidence against God's existence. Skeptical theism responds that our cognitive limitations prevent us from seeing the goods that justify the suffering; but critics worry that this response proves too much. This chapter develops the problem and its responses with the moral seriousness the subject demands.
