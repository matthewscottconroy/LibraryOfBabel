# The Logical Problem of Evil

The problem of evil is perhaps the most powerful challenge to theism and one of the most discussed problems in the history of philosophy. In its logical form — the form that dominated the mid-twentieth century debate — it is an argument for the logical incompatibility of God's existence and the existence of evil. If God is omnipotent, omniscient, and omnibenevolent, then God could prevent evil, would know about evil, and would want to prevent evil. Therefore, if God exists, there is no evil. But there is evil. Therefore, God does not exist.

Mackie's formulation of the logical problem of evil is the most influential. Mackie held that the propositions "God exists," "God is omnipotent," "God is omnibenevolent," and "Evil exists" are logically inconsistent: no possible world contains all of them. If this is right, then theism is not merely false but necessarily false — as false as a square circle.

The free will defense, associated with Plantinga, is the most celebrated theist response. God could have created human beings who always freely choose the good; but to create beings with genuine freedom is to create beings who can choose evil. Perhaps God could not have created free beings who always freely choose the good — perhaps every possible world containing genuinely free creatures contains some who choose evil. If so, a world with free creatures and some evil may be better than a world without free creatures, even if the latter is free of evil.

Plantinga's defense is logically powerful: it is a possible defense, not a theodicy (an actual explanation of why God permits evil). If it is even possible that God has a reason to permit evil, the logical argument fails. Most contemporary philosophers accept that Plantinga's defense succeeds in defeating the logical problem — but they hold that the evidential problem of evil is a more serious challenge.
