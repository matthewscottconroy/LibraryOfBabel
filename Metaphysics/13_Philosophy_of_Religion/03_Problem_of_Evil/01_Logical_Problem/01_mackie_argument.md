# Mackie's Logical Problem of Evil

The problem of evil can be pressed with different degrees of force. The weakest version says that evil is some evidence against the existence of God. A stronger version says that the existence of God and the existence of evil are *logically incompatible* — that the theistic worldview is, at its core, incoherent. This is the version J. L. Mackie argued for in "Evil and Omnipotence" (*Mind*, 1955). If Mackie is right, no theodicy and no appeal to mystery can help the theist: the problem cannot be mitigated or explained away, because it is a formal contradiction.

## The Argument

Classical theism commits to three propositions:

1. God is omnipotent (all-powerful).
2. God is omnibenevolent (wholly good).
3. Evil exists.

Mackie argues these three cannot all be true together. A wholly good being would want to eliminate evil. An omnipotent being can eliminate any evil. Therefore, evil would not exist — yet it does. This is a formal contradiction within the theistic worldview.

Formally:

- P1: God is omnipotent.
- P2: God is omnibenevolent (wholly good).
- P3: Evil exists.
- P4: A wholly good being always eliminates evil as far as it can.
- P5: There are no limits on what an omnipotent being can do.
- C: Therefore, evil does not exist. [Contradiction with P3]

At least one of P1–P3 must be false. Since classical theism affirms all three, it is internally inconsistent.

## Mackie's Assessment of Escape Routes

Mackie systematically considers the most natural theistic responses.

*"Good cannot exist without evil."* Perhaps evil is a necessary counterpart of good — we could not appreciate or understand good without contrast. Mackie replies: an omnipotent God could have created beings that recognize good directly, without requiring evil for contrast. And even if some evil were needed, the *amount* of evil in the world vastly exceeds what contrast requires.

*"Evil is necessary as a means to good."* Perhaps God permits evil because it produces greater goods — suffering produces courage, compassion, character. Mackie replies: an omnipotent God could produce those goods directly, without requiring evil as a means. This response implicitly denies omnipotence by treating evil as an irreplaceable means.

*"The universe is better with some evil in it."* This might justify some evil — but an omnipotent God could achieve the "better universe" without the particular distribution of suffering we observe. And a universe with less horrendous evil could be made even "better" by reducing the evil, which undermines this escape route.

*"Evil is due to human free will."* This is the response Mackie takes most seriously. The suggestion is that God, in creating free beings, necessarily permitted the possibility of evil. Mackie's reply: an omnipotent God could have created beings that are free yet always choose good. This seems contradictory only if freedom and goodness are incompatible — but Mackie argues there is no contradiction in the notion of a person who is genuinely free and yet always chooses good. Such a person simply has a good character. God could have created beings with good characters who freely choose well.

## The Crux: Can God Create Free Beings Who Always Choose Good?

Mackie's treatment of free will is where subsequent debate has focused. His claim — that God could have created free beings who always choose good — depends on a particular account of freedom. If libertarian freedom is correct, a free choice is not determined by *anything* — not by God's creative act, not by the agent's character, not by prior conditions. A free choice is "up to" the agent in a sense that puts it genuinely beyond God's causal control. If so, it may not be within God's power to *cause* free beings to always choose well: the very act of determining the choice eliminates its freedom. Plantinga's free will defense (see next file) makes this point precisely and has been widely credited with defeating Mackie's objection.

## Mackie's Later Concession

Mackie himself, in *The Miracle of Theism* (1982), substantially concedes that the free will defense makes the logical problem difficult to sustain as a *formal* contradiction. He acknowledges that Plantinga's argument shows the propositions to be logically consistent — there is a possible explanation (free will, transworld depravity) that makes God and evil coexist. But he treats this as a limited concession: the evidential problem of evil — the argument that evil makes God's existence *improbable*, even if not logically impossible — remains a serious challenge. The center of gravity of the debate shifted as a result.

## Historical Significance

The logical problem dominated philosophy of religion for two decades following Mackie's paper. It forced theists to develop far more precise accounts of divine power, freedom, and omnipotence. Plantinga's response is widely regarded as the most important advance in philosophy of religion in the twentieth century — precisely because it neutralized the logical problem and, in doing so, redirected the debate toward the harder, subtler evidential question.
