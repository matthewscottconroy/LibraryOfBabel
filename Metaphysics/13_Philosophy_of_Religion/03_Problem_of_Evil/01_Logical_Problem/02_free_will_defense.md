# The Free Will Defense

Plantinga's response to Mackie begins with a clarification that is not merely terminological. He is not offering a *theodicy* — a positive account of why God actually permits evil, a disclosure of God's reasons. He is offering a *defense*: a demonstration that theism and evil are logically consistent. To show consistency, Plantinga needs only a *possible* reason, not the actual one. He needs only to show that there is some coherent story in which God exists and evil exists — not that this story is true.

The distinction matters. A theodicy claims too much: it purports to know God's reasons, to read the divine mind. A defense claims only what logic requires: that the apparently contradictory set of propositions {God exists, God is omnipotent, God is omnibenevolent, evil exists} is in fact consistent. This is enough to defeat Mackie's charge of formal contradiction.

## The Core Argument

The pivotal concept is *transworld depravity*. A person suffers from transworld depravity if, in every possible world in which that person exists and is free with respect to at least one morally significant action, the person goes wrong with respect to at least one moral action. That is: however God creates the person and however God arranges the circumstances, if the person has libertarian freedom, the person will sin somewhere and somehow.

The argument:

- P1: A world containing creatures who are significantly free and who sometimes perform morally good actions is, all else equal, more valuable than a world containing no free creatures.
- P2: God can create free creatures but cannot cause or determine the choices of such creatures — a caused choice is not a free choice, because no one is free with respect to an action if its performance is caused by something else.
- P3: It is *possible* that every free creature God could have created suffers from transworld depravity — in every world containing that creature, the creature freely does at least one wrong act.
- P4: If P3 is true, there is no possible world in which God creates free creatures who never do wrong. God's options are: create free creatures who commit some evils, or create no free creatures.
- C: Therefore, it is *possible* that God is omnipotent and omnibenevolent and yet permits moral evil, because preventing that evil would require creating no free creatures or creating creatures who are not genuinely free.

This is valid. And note what Plantinga does not need to establish: he does not need to show that actual human beings suffer from transworld depravity, or that free will is the actual reason God permits evil. He needs only to show that P3 is *possibly* true — and that possibility, plus P1 and P2, generates the consistency claim.

## Transworld Depravity: A Closer Look

The concept is technically precise. A person P suffers from transworld depravity if and only if: for every possible world W in which P exists, has libertarian freedom with respect to some morally significant action A, and is in circumstances C, P goes wrong with respect to at least one action in W. This is a fact about the *counterfactuals of creaturely freedom* — facts about what P would freely do in various circumstances. If these counterfactuals are such that P always does at least one wrong thing, then there is no world in which God creates P freely and P is sinless. God cannot both create P as a free being and ensure P's sinlessness — these are logically incompatible if P has transworld depravity.

## Why This Defeats Mackie

Mackie claimed: God could have created free beings who always choose good. Plantinga's response: this is not obviously within God's power. God's omnipotence does not extend to determining what free creatures *would* do in counterfactual circumstances — those facts are not up to God. Mackie's claim presupposes that the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are always favorable, that there is always a possible world in which free creatures do only good. Plantinga challenges this assumption. It is *possible* that no such world is available to God, and that possibility is sufficient to show consistency.

## Objections

**Mackie's counterattack**: If free choice is compatible with determinism (compatibilism), then God could have determined free creatures to always choose good without eliminating their freedom. Plantinga's response: the free will defense presupposes libertarian freedom. If compatibilism is true, the logical problem fails for different reasons — a determined creature who always chooses well is not obviously a morally worse creation than an undetermined one.

**Natural evil**: The defense is most plausible for moral evil caused by free agents. Natural evil — earthquakes, disease, predatory suffering — is harder to attribute to free will. Plantinga suggests natural evil may result from the free choices of non-human agents (angels or demonic powers) whose rebellion corrupts the natural order. He is explicit that this is speculative, offered as a possible story rather than a truth claim.

**Robert Adams's objection**: Adams argues that there may be no determinate facts about what a free creature would do in non-actual circumstances — counterfactuals of creaturely freedom may lack truth-values. If so, the concept of transworld depravity is empty, and Plantinga's defense collapses. This objection is deep and connects to the broader dispute about Molinism and middle knowledge.

**The range of permissible evil**: Even if the free will defense shows that *some* moral evil is consistent with God's existence, it does not show that *this much* evil — the Holocaust, the Gulag, the Rwandan genocide — is consistent with omnibenevolence. This is the transition point to the evidential problem of evil, which is the harder challenge that remains after the logical problem is resolved.

## Significance

Plantinga's free will defense is widely credited with demonstrating that Mackie's logical problem does not succeed. Even Mackie conceded this. The defense's significance is primarily negative: it shows that Mackie's argument fails. It leaves entirely open the more difficult evidential question of whether the *extent* of evil makes theism improbable. The center of gravity of the problem of evil shifted permanently from the logical to the evidential dimension as a result of Plantinga's work, and most subsequent debate has been conducted on that terrain.
