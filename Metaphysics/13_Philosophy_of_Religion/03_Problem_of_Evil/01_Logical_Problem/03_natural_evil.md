# Natural Evil

In 1860, Darwin wrote to the botanist Asa Gray that he could not persuade himself that a beneficent and omnipotent God would have designedly created the Ichneumonidae — parasitoid wasps that lay their eggs in living caterpillars, whose larvae then eat the host from within while it remains alive. The image stayed with Darwin, and it captures a problem that no appeal to human freedom can address. The Ichneumon wasp's behavior predates the existence of human souls by hundreds of millions of years. Nobody chose it. It is a structural feature of the world.

This is natural evil: evil that results from natural processes rather than human agency — earthquakes, tsunamis, disease, animal predation, mass extinctions, the suffering of sentient creatures through the entire evolutionary history of life. It cannot be attributed to the misuse of human free will, and the free will defense, however compelling for moral evil, does not directly apply. The question for the theist is why an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good God would create through processes that produce this.

The challenge is sharpened by evolutionary biology. Natural selection works through differential reproductive success, which in turn depends on predation, starvation, disease, and death. The suffering of sentient animals is not an accidental byproduct of evolution — it is structurally built into how biological complexity arose. God, if God created through evolution, either designed it knowing it would involve vast amounts of animal suffering, or permitted it knowing the same.

## Theodicy-Style Responses

**The soul-making theodicy (John Hick)** argues, following Irenaeus rather than Augustine, that human beings are not created fully formed but must develop into moral and spiritual maturity. Evil provides the conditions for this development. A world without suffering would produce beings incapable of genuine courage, compassion, fortitude, or solidarity. We become who we ought to be through encounter with genuine difficulty.

Natural evil, on this view, creates the environment in which distinctively human virtues can be developed. Without the possibility of illness, there is no compassion for the sick; without the threat of death, no genuine seriousness about how to live. Hick elaborates this in *Evil and the God of Love* (1966) and argues that the soul-making process extends beyond death to an eschatological completion that makes the whole process worthwhile.

Several objections press hard. The amount of suffering vastly exceeds what soul-making requires — Hick's process is not optimized for minimum suffering. Many people suffer in ways that produce no discernible growth: the suffering of infants, the cognitively disabled, those whose suffering merely breaks them. And above all: animal suffering predates human souls by hundreds of millions of years and cannot be any part of soul-making for human beings.

**The natural law theodicy (Richard Swinburne)** argues in *Providence and the Problem of Evil* (1998) that a regular, law-governed natural order is a great good: it makes the world intelligible, allows for reliable action and moral planning, and treats creatures as responsible agents in a predictable environment. If God intervened miraculously to prevent every earthquake and divert every pathogen, the world would be too managed for genuine human agency or scientific knowledge. Natural evil is the price of a world with regular natural laws.

The objection: surely God could have created a world with regular natural laws but without the specific harmful parameters (or could intervene occasionally without undermining overall regularity). Constant miracle would destroy regularity — but occasional intervention to prevent the worst horrors would not.

**The free will extension (Plantinga)** suggests that natural evil may result from the free choices of non-human agents — fallen angelic beings whose rebellion against God corrupts the natural order. Satan's fall, on this account, disrupts not just spiritual reality but the physical world, producing the predation, disease, and natural disaster we observe. Plantinga is explicit that this is a speculative *possibility* offered as part of a defense — it need not be true, only possible. Many philosophers find the demonological extension of the free will defense implausible, though no one denies that it is *logically possible*.

## Animal Suffering and the Scale Problem

The most powerful objection focuses on the sheer scale of animal suffering over evolutionary time. Five mass extinction events in Earth's history each destroyed the majority of complex life. Parasitic diseases cause suffering to billions of sentient creatures. Predation is a fundamental structural feature of ecosystems, not a marginal occurrence.

Paul Draper developed the "argument from biological evolution" against theism using this evidence: the observed pattern of animal suffering — widespread, intense, and useful only in the adaptive sense — is much better predicted by the hypothesis that evolution is an unguided, indifferent process than by theism. The evidential question is whether the actual extent and distribution of natural evil is what one would expect from an omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent creator — or whether it is more consistent with a world that has no such creator.

The soul-making and natural law theodicies can provide some account of why natural evil exists in principle; they struggle to account for its scale and distribution. This is the transition point to the evidential problem of evil (see section 02), which argues not from the existence of natural evil but from its cumulative extent and apparent gratuitousness.
