# The Evidential Problem of Evil

Even if the logical problem of evil can be answered — even if it is logically possible that God and evil coexist — the existence and distribution of evil in the world constitutes powerful evidence against the existence of God. This is the evidential problem of evil: not that evil's existence makes theism logically impossible, but that it makes theism improbable, or at least much less probable than it would otherwise be.

Rowe's formulation is the canonical statement. Consider the case of a fawn that dies in agony in a forest fire, days after the fire has subsided, slowly and in great suffering, with no human witness and no apparent benefit to anyone. Could an omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent God permit such gratuitous suffering — suffering that serves no discernible good? The existence of such "horrendous evils" — evils that seem pointless, that do not seem to be required for any greater good — constitutes evidence against theism.

Theodicy is the attempt to justify God's permission of evil — to explain why an omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent God would permit the evil we observe. Soul-making theodicy (Hick) holds that suffering and evil are necessary conditions for the development of moral virtue and spiritual depth: a world without challenges, without the possibility of suffering, would be a world in which genuine moral growth is impossible. The greater good that evil serves is the development of human character.

The skeptical theist response — associated with Wykstra and Bergmann — holds that we should not expect to be able to see God's reasons for permitting evil, because God's knowledge and perspective are so vastly greater than ours. Just as a small child cannot always understand a good parent's permission of painful medical treatment, we cannot always understand God's permission of evil. Our failure to see a justifying reason is not evidence that there is none.

This section examines the evidential problem of evil, the main theodicies, and the skeptical theist response.
