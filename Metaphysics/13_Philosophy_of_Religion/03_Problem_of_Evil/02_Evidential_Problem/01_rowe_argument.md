# Rowe's Evidential Argument from Evil

Plantinga showed that the logical problem of evil does not succeed: there is a possible story in which God and evil coexist. William Rowe accepted this concession and then pressed a different challenge. Even if God and evil are logically compatible, the *amount*, *distribution*, and *kinds* of suffering we actually observe constitute strong empirical evidence against theism. The question is not whether God and evil *could* coexist but whether a world with this much suffering of this kind is what we would expect from an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good creator.

Rowe's 1979 paper "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism" introduces the *evidential problem of evil* — an argument that evil makes God's existence improbable, without claiming to make it impossible. This is a more modest and, many think, more philosophically durable challenge.

## The Argument

- P1: There exist instances of intense suffering which an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse.
- P2: An omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being would prevent the occurrence of any intense suffering it could prevent without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse.
- C: Therefore, there does not exist an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being.

The argument is probabilistic: Rowe does not claim to have deduced atheism but argues that the evidence from evil strongly supports it. He acknowledges that a theist may have independent grounds — cosmological, ontological, from religious experience — that provide evidence for theism, and that a rational person can weigh these differently. His claim is the modest one that evil is significant evidence against theism.

## The Fawn Case

Rowe illustrates with a concrete case. A fawn is burned in a forest fire, dying slowly and in agony over several days. There seems to be no good served by this particular fawn's particular suffering — no soul-making, no human growth, no narrative significance. If so, an omnipotent God could have prevented it without losing any greater good. Since God did not, God probably does not exist.

The case is chosen deliberately: it involves non-human suffering (not attributable to human free will), suffering that appears entirely isolated from any greater good, and intense suffering over extended time. Rowe acknowledges he cannot *prove* the fawn's suffering is genuinely gratuitous — perhaps there are unknown goods that justify it. But he argues that from our epistemic position, P1 is more likely true than false.

## The Key Concept: Gratuitous Evil

The pivot of Rowe's argument is *gratuitous evil*: suffering that serves no greater good accessible to an omnipotent, omniscient God. Gratuitous evil is evil that makes no difference — a world with the suffering prevented would be just as good or better, given all the goods and evils an omnipotent God could produce. The theist must show either:

(a) That no actual evil is gratuitous — every instance of suffering is justified by some greater good (the theodicy approach); or  
(b) That we are not in a position to judge whether any actual evil is gratuitous — skeptical theism.

## Wykstra's Critique and CORNEA

Stephen Wykstra challenged Rowe's inference pattern in "The Humean Obstacle to Evidential Arguments from Suffering" (1984). Wykstra argues that Rowe's inference from "we don't see any greater good justifying this suffering" to "there is no greater good justifying this suffering" commits what Wykstra calls the "no-see-um" fallacy — inferring from the absence of evidence to evidence of absence in cases where we would not expect to see the evidence even if it existed.

Wykstra's principle CORNEA (Condition of Reasonable Epistemic Access): an inference from "I don't see X" to "X does not exist" is reasonable only if one has reasonable grounds to think that if X existed, one would likely see it. Applied to the problem of evil: we have reason to think that if God had good reasons for permitting a particular instance of suffering, those reasons might involve goods and connections far beyond our cognitive grasp — like a child unable to understand a parent's medical decisions. The "no-see-um" inference is therefore unreliable in the case of divine justifying reasons.

## Rowe's Refinement

Rowe refined his argument in response to Wykstra. He acknowledges that we might not know all of God's reasons, but argues that Wykstra's response proves too much. If we can never reliably infer from "I don't see a justifying reason" to "there is no justifying reason," then theistic ethics itself becomes undermined: we could never reliably judge any apparent evil as actually evil, since God might always have inscrutable reasons for permitting it. Moreover, as we survey more and more instances of apparently gratuitous suffering, the cumulative weight of evidence increases beyond what the "inscrutable reasons" response can absorb.

## Draper's Version

Paul Draper developed an alternative evidential argument in "Pain and Pleasure: An Evidential Problem for Theists" (1989). Draper's argument is explicitly comparative. He compares theism (T) with the "Hypothesis of Indifference" (HI): neither the nature of the universe nor any intelligence behind it has special concern for the welfare of humans or animals. Draper argues that the observed pattern of pain and pleasure is much more probable given HI than given T. This comparative argument is more modest than Rowe's: it does not require showing that any particular suffering is gratuitous, only that the *overall pattern* of suffering better fits a universe without a benevolent creator.

The Bayesian form: P(E | HI) > P(E | T), where E = the observed distribution of pain and pleasure. By Bayes's theorem: P(HI | E) / P(T | E) > P(HI) / P(T) — the evidence raises HI's probability relative to T's.
