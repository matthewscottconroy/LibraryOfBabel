# Skeptical Theism

The most sustained contemporary response to the evidential problem of evil is not a theodicy — it does not claim to know what goods justify the suffering we observe. It is an epistemological challenge to the inference from "we see no justifying reason for this suffering" to "there is no justifying reason." We are cognitively limited beings. The goods that might justify an omniscient God's permissions of suffering might be entirely beyond our conceptual horizon. We are therefore not in a good position to judge whether any particular instance of suffering is genuinely gratuitous.

This is the position Michael Bergmann, Michael Rea, and Daniel Howard-Snyder have developed as skeptical theism.

## The Core Claim

The central idea is that God's justifying reasons for permitting suffering may involve goods entirely unknown to us, connections between goods and evils that we cannot perceive, and temporal scales and relational webs far beyond our cognitive grasp. Given these limitations, the inference from "we see no justifying reason" to "there is no justifying reason" is unreliable. We are simply not epistemically positioned to make this judgment.

Consider the child-parent analogy. A small child cannot understand why a parent permits the pain of a necessary medical procedure. The child, unable to grasp the medical concepts involved — surgery, infection, long-term health outcomes — might infer that the parent is cruel or indifferent. But the child's inference would be unreliable, because the goods involved are beyond the child's comprehension. Similarly, God's reasons for permitting suffering may involve goods and connections that are simply invisible to us.

## CORNEA and the "No-See-Um" Argument

Wykstra formalized this using CORNEA (Condition of Reasonable Epistemic Access). The principle: an inference from "I do not perceive an X" to "there is no X" is legitimate only if, given background information, one would likely perceive an X if it existed.

Applied to evil: Rowe's inference from "I perceive no justifying reason for the fawn's suffering" to "there is no justifying reason" requires that we would likely perceive a justifying reason if one existed. But we have no basis for expecting this — God's justifying reasons might be as far beyond our cognitive reach as ultraviolet light is beyond our visual perception. The "no-see-um" inference is unreliable in precisely this case.

## Bergmann's Development: The G-Goods Argument

Bergmann developed the skeptical theist position more formally. He introduces the concept of "G-goods" (goods we have no reason to think represent the full range of goods available to God) and argues:

- P1: We have no reason to think that the goods we know of constitute a representative sample of all the goods that could justify permitting suffering.
- P2: We have no reason to think that the connections between goods and evils we know of constitute a representative sample of all such connections.
- C: Therefore, we cannot reliably infer from "we see no justifying good for this suffering" to "there is no justifying good for this suffering."

This is not a claim that God has such reasons — only that we are not in a position to judge that God lacks them.

## Skeptical Theism vs. Theodicy

Notice that skeptical theism and theodicy are different epistemic positions with different strengths and vulnerabilities. A theodicy says "here is a reason God has." Skeptical theism says "we cannot tell whether God has a reason or not." The theodicy is more ambitious and more vulnerable to objections — it can be assessed on its merits. Skeptical theism is more modest and more defensive — it asks only whether Rowe's inference is reliable, not whether there is a positive account of divine reasons.

The cost of this modesty is that skeptical theism may prove too much: if we cannot reliably judge that any suffering is gratuitous, this may undermine religious epistemology in general. If our access to divine reasons is so severely limited, then the intuitions about divine goodness that underlie theism — including the intuition that a good God would not permit certain suffering — are themselves unreliable.

## Objections

**The moral skepticism objection** is the most pressing. If we cannot judge that any particular suffering is without justifying reason (because God might have inscrutable reasons), then we risk undermining our capacity to make ordinary moral judgments. Perhaps the suffering caused by human agents is also divinely justified in ways we cannot see — in which case, moral condemnation of human violence is epistemically unreliable. Bergmann responds that his skepticism concerns specifically higher-order goods accessible to an omniscient God, not ordinary human moral reasoning. Our moral judgments about human actions are not similarly undermined.

**The practical implications objection** holds that if any apparently pointless suffering might be divinely justified, this could undermine the moral motivation to prevent suffering. Why intervene in a famine if the children's suffering might be divinely permitted for inscrutable reasons? Skeptical theists respond that this is a misapplication: skeptical theism is an epistemological claim about our ability to *identify* gratuitous evil, not a practical recommendation to tolerate preventable suffering.

**The Rowe rejoinder** notes that as the evidence of apparently gratuitous suffering accumulates, the "inscrutable reasons" defense becomes less plausible — not because any single case defeats it, but because the cumulative weight eventually outruns any plausible story about inscrutable divine goods.

**The commitment paradox (Almeida and Oppy)** argues that skeptical theism commits its proponents to widespread skepticism about moral intuitions. If we cannot trust our intuitions about what is evil without divine permission, then the moral intuitions that underlie theism — including the intuition that God would be good — are similarly unreliable. The skeptic about apparent gratuitous evil cannot coherently rely on intuitions about divine goodness.

## Peter van Inwagen's Contribution

Van Inwagen has offered a "defense" parallel to Plantinga's — not a theodicy but a demonstration of consistency. Van Inwagen sketches a narrative in which God had good reasons for creating free beings who fell into moral and natural disorder, and in which the present state of the world is the natural consequence. He does not claim this is true, only that it is consistent with theism and evil and is not obviously impossible — which is enough to rebut the logical problem and provide some epistemic cover for skeptical theism.
