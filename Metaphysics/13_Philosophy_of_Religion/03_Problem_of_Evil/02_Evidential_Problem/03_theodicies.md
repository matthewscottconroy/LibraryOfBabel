# Theodicies

A defense shows that God and evil are logically consistent. A theodicy aims at something more ambitious: a *positive* account of why an omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent God might have morally sufficient reasons to allow the evils we observe. Where a defense merely says "it is possible that God has a reason," a theodicy says "here is what that reason is." The term comes from Leibniz's *Théodicée* (1710), though the enterprise is ancient.

This ambition makes theodicy more vulnerable. If the reason offered is inadequate — if it fails to account for the kind or scale of evil we actually find — the theodicy fails. Several major theodicies have been developed and subjected to sustained critical pressure.

## The Free Will Theodicy

God allows moral evil because genuine freedom is a great enough good to justify the possibility of its misuse. A world with free beings who sometimes sin is better than a world of automata that are incapable of evil.

The free will theodicy differs from Plantinga's free will *defense*: the defense shows only that free will *might* be God's reason; the theodicy claims it *is* God's reason, and that it *justifies* God's permission of moral evil. The transition is significant — the theodicy is making a substantive claim about God's purposes, not merely a logical claim about consistency.

Objections press hard. Does freedom *really* justify the Holocaust? The scale of moral evil seems disproportionate to the good of freedom — could a perfectly good God not have created free beings whose worst choices produced less catastrophic outcomes? The theodicy applies to moral evil but not to natural evil. And one might ask whether God could have created free beings who, while genuinely free, were substantially less likely to choose catastrophically.

## The Soul-Making Theodicy

John Hick's *Evil and the God of Love* (1966) develops the most influential contemporary theodicy, following Irenaeus rather than Augustine. On the Irenaean view, human beings are not created perfect and then fell; they are created in God's "image" (rational beings) but must develop into God's "likeness" (morally and spiritually mature beings). Evil provides the conditions for this development.

Several elements are central to Hick's account. *Epistemic distance*: God creates humans at a distance from the divine reality, so their turn toward God can be genuinely free. *Soul-making*: suffering, struggle, and moral choice are the conditions in which genuine virtues — courage, compassion, perseverance — are developed. A hedonically perfect world would produce hedonically perfect beings who have never had to choose anything. *Eschatological completion*: the soul-making process does not end at death; God's purposes extend beyond this life, and the evils of this world are justifiable only in light of an eschatological fulfillment that makes the whole process worthwhile.

The objections are serious. The amount of suffering vastly exceeds what soul-making requires — Hick's response is that the process is not optimized for minimum suffering but for genuine character formation through real struggle. Many people suffer in ways that produce no discernible growth: the suffering of infants, the cognitively disabled, those whose suffering merely breaks them. Hick's eschatological response: these sufferers are included in the ultimate fulfillment; the process is not evaluated by its effects in this life alone. Animal suffering, which predates human souls by hundreds of millions of years, cannot be any part of soul-making for humans, and Hick acknowledges this is a genuine difficulty.

## The Greater Good Theodicy and the Natural Law Theodicy

Some goods are logically possible only if certain evils are also possible: courage requires genuine danger, compassion requires genuine suffering, forgiveness requires genuine wrongdoing, gratitude requires genuine need. God permits evils as necessary conditions for higher goods — not as means in an instrumental sense (as if God caused suffering to produce courage) but as structural features of a world in which such goods can exist. A world without any suffering would be a world without these second-order goods, and Richard Swinburne argues in *The Existence of God* (2004) that such a world would be less valuable even if it contained less first-order pain.

Swinburne extends this in *Providence and the Problem of Evil* (1998) with the natural law theodicy: a regular, law-governed natural order is itself a great good — it makes the world intelligible, allows for reliable action and moral planning, and treats creatures as responsible agents. Natural evil is the price of this regularity. If God intervened miraculously to prevent every harmful natural process, the world would be too managed for genuine human agency or scientific knowledge.

The objection: surely God could have created a world with regular natural laws but without some specific harmful constants, or could intervene occasionally without destroying overall regularity. The natural law theodicy may require that God never intervene, which sits uneasily with theistic belief in miracles and answered prayer.

## Marilyn McCord Adams on Horrendous Evils

Marilyn McCord Adams argues in *Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God* (1999) that standard theodicies fail to address what she calls "horrendous evils" — evils so severe that they threaten to destroy the meaning of a person's life entirely: being tortured to death; watching one's child die in agony; surviving genocide. These are not merely bad things requiring justification — they are potentially life-defeating in a way that requires a distinctive response.

Adams argues that only a *participatory* response can address horrendous evils. God must not merely permit and justify such evils from a distance but must participate in them. The incarnation — God becoming a human being and suffering death by torture — is the paradigm: God enters horrendous evil as its victim, thereby incorporating the worst human suffering into a divine life and making it possible for that suffering to be defeated by meaning. Horrendous evil can only be defeated *in the life of the victim*, not by pointing to goods enjoyed by others. This is a specifically Christian theodicy, grounded in the theology of the cross, and it explicitly rejects the project of justifying evil by reference to impersonal greater goods.

## The "No-Theodicy" Position

Peter van Inwagen has argued that theists do not need a theodicy — they need only a defense. A defense shows that the existence of God and the observed evil are logically compatible. Van Inwagen sketches a possible narrative (not a truth claim) connecting God's purposes with the observed evil, which serves as a consistency demonstration rather than an explanation. This is a more defensible position than theodicy: by not claiming to know God's actual reasons, it avoids the vulnerability of being assessed against the actual character and distribution of evil.
