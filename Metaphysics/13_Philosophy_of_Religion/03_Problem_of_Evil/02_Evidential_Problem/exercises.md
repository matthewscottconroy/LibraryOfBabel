# Exercises: The Evidential Problem of Evil

1. **Rowe's argument.** What distinguishes the evidential from the logical problem of evil? Reconstruct Rowe's argument. Is the fawn example well-chosen? What features make it a strong case for gratuitous evil?

2. **Skeptical theism.** The skeptical theist says: "We are in no position to judge that the fawn's suffering is gratuitous." Is this response satisfying? Does it risk making God epistemically inaccessible in ways that undermine religious practice?

3. **CORNEA.** Wykstra's CORNEA condition says we can reason "no seen reason implies no reason" only if we could reasonably expect to see the reason if it existed. Evaluate this condition. Is the parent-child analogy convincing?

4. **Draper's comparative argument.** Draper argues that the Hypothesis of Indifference (nature is indifferent to well-being) explains the observed distribution of pain and pleasure better than theism. Evaluate this argument. What does theism predict about the distribution of suffering?

5. **Horrendous evils.** Marilyn Adams argues that some evils are "horrendous"—they seem to defeat the goodness of a person's life. Can any theodicy handle horrendous evils? How does Adams's own solution (divine participation in suffering) work?

6. **Animal suffering.** The evolutionary history of life involves billions of years of animal suffering (predation, disease, starvation) before any humans existed. Does this pose a special problem for theism that the free will defense cannot address? How might a theodicist respond?
