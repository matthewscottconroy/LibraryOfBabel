# Important Terms and Ideas: The Evidential Problem of Evil

**The Evidential Problem of Evil**
The argument that the amount, distribution, or intensity of evil in the world provides strong inductive evidence against the existence of a perfectly good, omnipotent God—even if the existence of evil is not logically incompatible with God's existence.

**Gratuitous Evil**
Evil for which there is no compensating good that could not have been obtained without it (or a comparable good). If any gratuitous evil exists, it provides evidence against theism. The evidential argument hinges on whether apparently gratuitous evils really are gratuitous.

**CORNEA (Wykstra)**
The Condition of Reasoned Epistemic Access: it is reasonable to assume P only if one has reasonable access to the relevant evidence. Wykstra argues: we lack the cognitive capacities to see whether evils are gratuitous, so the appearance of gratuitousness is weak evidence against God.

**Skeptical Theism**
The response to the evidential problem: our cognitive limitations make it unreasonable to expect to identify God's reasons for permitting evil. We cannot infer from "We see no good reason for this evil" to "There is no good reason for this evil." God's reasons may be wholly beyond our ken.

**Theodicy**
An attempt to justify God's permission of evil by identifying the goods that justify it. Soul-making theodicy (Hick): evil provides opportunities for moral and spiritual development. Free will theodicy: evil results from the free choices of creatures. Natural law theodicy: regular natural laws producing evil are necessary for an orderly world.

**The Distribution of Pain and Pleasure**
Draper's version of the evidential argument: the actual distribution of pain and pleasure in the world (including animal suffering before humans, innocent suffering, and the asymmetry between moral desert and suffering) is much better explained by indifference than by theism.
