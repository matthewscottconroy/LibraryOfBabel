# The Hiddenness of God

Consider someone who has searched sincerely and carefully for God — who has examined the evidence, engaged with religious traditions, genuinely opened themselves to the possibility of divine encounter — and who has come away with no belief, not because they are hostile or indifferent, but simply because they find the evidence insufficient. J. L. Schellenberg asks: if a perfectly loving God exists, why should this happen at all?

The argument from divine hiddenness, developed most extensively in *Divine Hiddenness and Human Reason* (1993), is distinct from and independent of the problem of evil. It does not focus on suffering but on non-belief. Its claim is that a perfectly loving God would not permit inculpable non-belief — sincere, non-resistant failure to believe — among persons who are genuinely open to relationship with God.

## Schellenberg's Argument

- P1: If a perfectly loving God exists, then for any person who is not resistant to relationship with God, God would ensure that person is in a position to be in relationship with God.
- P2: Being in a position to be in relationship with God requires, at minimum, believing that God exists.
- P3: Therefore, if a perfectly loving God exists, then any person who is not resistant to relationship with God would believe that God exists.
- P4: There exist persons who are not resistant to relationship with God and yet do not believe that God exists — inculpable non-believers.
- C: Therefore, a perfectly loving God does not exist.

The argument is logically valid. Its force depends on the premises — particularly P1's claim about what perfect love requires, and P4's claim that inculpable non-belief actually occurs.

## The Central Notion: Inculpable Non-Belief

Schellenberg distinguishes several types of non-belief. *Culpable non-belief* results from willful resistance, moral failure, or deliberate avoidance of evidence — the atheist who refuses to consider the evidence, or who flees from divine encounter. This type does not trouble Schellenberg's argument: a perfectly loving God might reasonably permit resistance.

*Inculpable non-belief* results from sincere inquiry, cognitive limitations, cultural circumstances, or epistemic bad luck — those who have examined the evidence carefully and found it insufficient; those raised in non-religious cultures who have never encountered the concept of God in a convincing way; those whose psychological or cognitive constitution makes religious belief unavailable despite sincere openness. The hiddenness argument concerns this category. If God is perfectly loving and such people are not resistant to relationship, God would not allow them to remain in a state that precludes relationship — namely, non-belief.

The key premise is a claim about what *perfect love* requires. Schellenberg argues that a perfectly loving personal being would always be open to relationship with any creature capable of it; would ensure that the conditions for relationship are available to any non-resistant creature; and would not allow relationship to be impeded by conditions (like non-belief) that the creature cannot help and would remove if they could. A perfectly loving parent, if their child is sincerely seeking connection and cannot find them through no fault of their own, would make themselves available.

## Theistic Responses

**Non-belief serves spiritual goods**: God may have good reasons for allowing periods or states of non-belief. Hiddenness may produce genuine humility, deeper seeking, or authentic faith — belief formed through genuine struggle may be more valuable than belief produced by overwhelming evidence. Schellenberg's response: the argument does not require overwhelming evidence, only enough for reasonable belief. God could make Godself available to sincere seekers without coercing those who prefer distance.

**Autonomy and coercion**: God withdraws to preserve the genuineness of human approach. An overwhelming divine presence would eliminate genuine freedom of response. Schellenberg grants the point in general but presses that moderate, non-coercive evidence is sufficient for relationship; the argument does not demand overpowering revelation.

**Misidentification of inculpable non-believers**: Perhaps all apparently inculpable non-believers are, at some deep level, resistant. This is an empirical claim about the inner states of non-believers that many find psychologically implausible and theologically unappealing.

**Paul Moser's response**: Moser argues in *The Elusive God* (2008) that Schellenberg's argument assumes God should be knowable through propositional evidence and argument. But Moser argues that God may desire a different kind of relationship — one requiring moral transformation and volitional commitment rather than mere intellectual assent. A God who reveals Godself through conscience, moral demand, and transforming encounter rather than through philosophical arguments is not hidden in the theologically relevant sense.

**Skeptical theism by analogy**: One might argue that we cannot reliably judge whether God has good reasons for permitting inculpable non-belief. Perhaps God's reasons involve goods that require the *experience* of divine absence — analogous to the "dark night of the soul" in mystical theology (John of the Cross), where hiddenness is itself a mode of spiritual purification.

## Hiddenness and Religious Diversity

The hiddenness problem intersects with religious diversity. If God exists and desires relationship with all sincere seekers, why is the evidence for God's existence not universally available in all cultures at all times? Why have billions of people lived and died without the specific religious traditions in which God's existence is most prominently affirmed? The hiddenness argument generalizes this concern: the *scope* of non-belief — across cultures, centuries, and sincere individuals — seems incompatible with a God who actively seeks relationship with all.
