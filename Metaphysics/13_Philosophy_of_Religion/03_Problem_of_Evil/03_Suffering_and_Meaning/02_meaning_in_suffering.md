# Meaning in Suffering

The problem of evil asks whether God and evil can logically coexist. But there is a related and distinct question that is, in some ways, more pressing: can suffering be meaningful? Not: is it philosophically justified from God's perspective — but rather: can it be incorporated into a life, or a cosmic narrative, that is genuinely worthwhile rather than simply a negation of value?

These questions are related but not identical. A suffering might be unjustified — no sufficient reason outweighs it — and yet find meaning in the sufferer's response. A suffering might be divinely justified and yet remain meaningless to the sufferer. Theodicy addresses the first question; the question of meaning in suffering addresses the second and extends beyond theodicy into territory that existentialist philosophy, literature, and psychology have occupied alongside philosophy of religion.

## Theistic Frameworks

**Redemptive suffering** occupies a central place in Catholic and Orthodox traditions. Suffering accepted in union with the suffering of Christ has positive spiritual value — it participates in God's redemptive work. The suffering is not merely permitted by God but is incorporated into something salvific. This understanding draws on Paul's language in Colossians 1:24 ("I fill up in my flesh what is still lacking in regard to Christ's afflictions") and on the theology of the cross more broadly.

**Purgatorial or purifying suffering** holds that some suffering is purifying — it removes attachments, deepens character, and orients the sufferer toward what truly matters. Hick's soul-making theodicy is a philosophical elaboration of this: suffering is the crucible in which genuine human virtue is formed, and its value lies in what it produces rather than in itself.

**Participatory suffering** is Marilyn McCord Adams's response to horrendous evils — those that seem to defeat the goodness of a person's life entirely. Adams argues in *Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God* (1999) that standard theodicies fail here: pointing to goods enjoyed by others does not address the life-defeating character of horrendous evil for the one who suffers it. On her view, God must *participate* in horrendous evil — the incarnation and passion of Christ are paradigmatic — thereby incorporating the worst human suffering into a divine life and making it possible for that suffering to acquire meaning. The suffering of the abandoned person, the tortured prisoner, the dying child, is shared by God; and that sharing is the ground of the suffering's potential redemption.

## Secular Frameworks: Frankl and Logotherapy

Viktor Frankl, drawing on his experience as a Holocaust survivor, developed logotherapy — the view that the primary human drive is not pleasure (Freud) or power (Adler) but the search for meaning. In *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946), Frankl argues that meaning can be found in three ways:

1. What we give to the world: creative work, love, contribution.
2. What we take from the world: beauty, truth, love received.
3. The stance we take toward unavoidable suffering: the freedom to choose one's response even when the suffering cannot be avoided.

Frankl found that prisoners in the concentration camps who retained a sense of meaning or purpose endured better than those who lost it. The will to meaning is the deepest dimension of human existence, exercisable even in extremis. Frankl's secular account locates meaning in human freedom and orientation toward value, without invoking God.

## Existentialist Approaches

Albert Camus, in *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), argues that the fundamental question of philosophy is whether life is worth living in the face of its apparent absurdity — including the suffering and injustice that pervade it. Camus's answer is to embrace the absurd: to acknowledge that life has no pre-given cosmic meaning while choosing to live with full awareness and rebellion against meaninglessness. Sisyphus, condemned to roll his stone eternally uphill, "must be imagined happy" — not because the suffering is justified but because the confrontation with it, fully aware, is itself a form of freedom and dignity.

Sartre's atheistic existentialism holds that existence precedes essence — we are not given a nature or purpose but create ourselves through our choices. Suffering can be given meaning by our free response to it, but it has no inherent cosmic significance. Responsibility for meaning lies entirely with the individual. Simone de Beauvoir argues in *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947) that authentic existence requires acknowledging both our freedom and our situatedness — the conditions we cannot change, including suffering. Authentic response involves neither denial nor despair but commitment to act within constraints to extend freedom, both one's own and others'.

## The Necessity Question

Is suffering *necessary* for certain forms of meaning, or is its role contingent? On the *strong necessity* view, some values — courage, compassion, solidarity with the suffering — require suffering as their logical object: they cannot exist in a world without suffering. On the *contingent connection* view, suffering happens to provide the conditions in which certain meanings arise, but those meanings could in principle be achieved otherwise. The most plausible secular view holds that meaning arises *despite* suffering rather than *because* of it — the sufferer's achievement of meaning is admirable precisely because it occurs in the face of conditions hostile to it.

## Ivan Karamazov's Challenge

The deepest existential challenge to theodicy is posed by Ivan Karamazov in Dostoevsky's *The Brothers Karamazov* (1880). Ivan does not argue philosophically against God's existence; he grants, for the sake of argument, that the world will be ultimately harmonized and that its suffering will prove worthwhile. But he refuses to accept this harmony if it requires the tears of even one child:

> "Imagine that it is you yourself who are erecting the edifice of human destiny with the aim of making men happy in the end... and that you are to build it... on the unexpiated blood of a little tortured child. Would you consent to be the architect on those conditions?"

Ivan's challenge is not intellectual but moral: even if suffering could be justified by future goods, the *acceptance* of that justification — the willingness to endorse a cosmic order that includes innocent suffering — is morally intolerable. This is the existential dimension of the problem: not whether God's existence is probable, but whether a God who created this world deserves worship.
