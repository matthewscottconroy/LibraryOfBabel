# Evil and Atheism

For many people, the existence of evil is not primarily an academic puzzle. It is the lived experience that moves them from belief to doubt, or from doubt to atheism. The scale of natural disaster, the suffering of innocents, the occurrence of genocide — these are not merely philosophical data points. They are encounters that make theological explanations ring hollow. Ivan Karamazov's rebellion in Dostoevsky's *The Brothers Karamazov* captures this: Ivan does not argue philosophically against God's existence; he accepts that God might exist and that the ultimate harmony of the universe might justify most suffering — but he refuses to accept any harmony that requires the tears of even one child, and he "returns the ticket." This is not a philosophical argument. It is a moral refusal.

Philosophers of religion distinguish the *intellectual* problem of evil (does evil make God's existence improbable?) from the *existential* problem (how do we live in a world of suffering, and what attitude should we take toward a God who allows it?). These are logically independent: one might be convinced that theism is improbable, or one might retain belief while being profoundly troubled. The existential problem is irreducible to the intellectual one.

## Draper's Comparative Evidential Argument

The philosophical version of the atheistic argument from evil is different from a private existential response. Paul Draper developed the most careful comparative version in "Pain and Pleasure: An Evidential Problem for Theists" (1989).

Draper compares two hypotheses:

- **Theism (T)**: God exists — omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent.
- **The Hypothesis of Indifference (HI)**: Neither the nature of the universe nor any intelligence behind it has special concern for the welfare of humans or animals.

The argument: the observed distribution of pain and pleasure is much more probable given HI than given T. Under HI, we would expect suffering and pleasure to be distributed roughly as we observe: according to natural processes indifferent to the interests of sentient beings. Evolution produces pain and pleasure as by-products of selection for fitness, not as responses to moral desert or spiritual need. Under T, we would expect a distribution of pain and pleasure that reflects a benevolent God's purposes — suffering serving clear goods, distributed with some correspondence to what is needed for those goods.

Since the observed distribution better fits HI than T:

- P(E | HI) > P(E | T), where E = the observed distribution of pain and pleasure
- By Bayes's theorem: P(HI | E) / P(T | E) > P(HI) / P(T) — the evidence raises HI's probability relative to T's.

Draper's argument is more modest than Rowe's: it does not require showing that any particular suffering is gratuitous, only that the *overall pattern* of suffering better fits a universe without a benevolent creator.

## The Cumulative Case Against Theism

Several philosophers (Graham Oppy, Nicholas Everitt, Michael Martin) have argued that the problem of evil is best understood as part of a cumulative evidential case against theism. No single instance of evil provides knock-down proof against God's existence — but the cumulative weight of several considerations together constitutes strong inductive evidence against theism when there is no positive evidence for God's existence that clearly outweighs it:

- The distribution and extent of natural evil
- The suffering of non-human animals over evolutionary history
- The existence of horrendous moral evils — genocide, systemic torture
- The absence of any systematic correlation between suffering and goods served
- The hiddenness of God from sincere inquirers

## Theistic Responses

Theists typically respond in two ways. First, positive evidence for theism from cosmological arguments, fine-tuning, and religious experience may outweigh the evidence from evil. Richard Swinburne's *The Existence of God* (2004) argues that the cumulative case for theism is stronger than the case against, and that evil, while a real cost, does not defeat the overall argument. Second, theism has distinctive resources — doctrines of redemption, eschatological hope, the incarnation — that make even horrendous suffering compatible with a loving God. Critics respond that invoking an afterlife to justify present suffering is a promissory note rather than an explanation.

## Rowe's Friendly Atheism

Rowe's influential position is that the evidential problem of evil is compatible with theistic rationality. In his 1979 paper, Rowe introduced the concept of the "friendly atheist" — an atheist who recognizes that theists can be rational in holding their belief despite the evidence from evil, because they may have independent evidence (religious experience, cosmological arguments) that outweighs the negative evidence. This is philosophically sophisticated: it does not require claiming that theism is irrational, only that atheism is better supported by the evidence as Rowe sees it.

## Evil as a Defeater

Some philosophers argue that evil functions as a *defeater* for theistic belief rather than as a direct argument for atheism. On this view, belief in God can be properly basic — warranted without argument, through the sensus divinitatis or religious experience. But the existence of evil, especially certain kinds and extents of evil, can serve as evidence that defeats the default rationality of theistic belief. Even if theism is not logically refuted by evil, the believer owes an account of how evil is consistent with their faith. If no account is available, the initial warrant for theistic belief is eroded rather than demolished — a subtler but equally serious epistemic result.
