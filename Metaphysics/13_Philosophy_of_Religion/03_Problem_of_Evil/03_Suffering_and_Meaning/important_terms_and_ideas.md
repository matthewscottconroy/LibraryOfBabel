# Important Terms and Ideas: Suffering and Meaning

**Soul-Making Theodicy**
Hick's theodicy: God's purpose is not to create a hedonistic paradise but to develop persons from their initial immature state into mature moral and spiritual beings. Suffering, adversity, and moral challenge are necessary conditions for the development of virtue, compassion, and a genuine relationship with God.

**Horrendous Evils**
Adams's term: evils so severe and destructive—the rape and murder of children, genocide, severe disfigurement—that they seem to defeat the possibility of a meaningful life for those who suffer them. Any adequate theodicy must address how God can make meaning of a life that includes such evils.

**The Problem of Suffering vs. the Problem of Evil**
A distinction: the logical and evidential problems of evil concern the abstract theological problem of reconciling God and evil. The problem of suffering is more personal—how can an individual find meaning in their own suffering? The pastoral and experiential dimensions require a different response from the logical-philosophical ones.

**Greater Goods Defense**
The strategy of justifying God's permission of evil by identifying specific goods (freedom, virtue, knowledge) that either require evil as a means or are sufficiently valuable to outweigh the evil. The challenge is to show that these goods could not be obtained without the evil permitted.

**Meaning-Making Theodicy**
The approach (Adams, Stump) that theodicy must operate at the level of the individual: God must be capable of integrating each individual's suffering into a meaningful narrative for that individual, not just showing that suffering has cosmic value. God's love for each person requires a personal theodicy.

**Protest Theology**
A response to theodicy that refuses to justify God's permission of evil: it is more faithful to protest against evil and to refuse to accept any theodicy that makes suffering "worthwhile." This tradition (Wiesel, Levinas, some Jewish theology after the Holocaust) takes the reality of evil so seriously that theodicy itself is seen as a form of complicity.
