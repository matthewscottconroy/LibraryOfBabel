# Review Questions — The Problem of Evil

1. Mackie's logical problem of evil and Rowe's evidential problem of evil are often treated as successive positions — Plantinga defeating the first, leaving the second as the live challenge. But they are not simply the same challenge in different degrees of force. What is the precise logical difference between them, and why does Plantinga's free will defense, which suffices to defeat Mackie, fail to address Rowe's fawn case? What kind of argument would be needed to meet the evidential problem that the logical defense does not provide?

2. Plantinga's free will defense rests on the concept of transworld depravity — the claim that it is *possible* that every free creature God could have created goes wrong in at least one possible world. Robert Adams objects that this concept requires counterfactuals of creaturely freedom to have determinate truth values, which may be false if libertarian freedom is genuine. How damaging is this objection to Plantinga's defense, and how does it connect to the broader dispute between Molinism and its critics in the chapter on divine omniscience?

3. Rowe argues that the fawn's suffering is gratuitous — no greater good accessible to an omnipotent God justifies it — and that this makes God's existence improbable. Wykstra's CORNEA principle challenges the inference: we would not necessarily perceive a justifying reason even if one existed, just as a child cannot perceive the reasons for a painful medical procedure. Is the child-parent analogy a good one? What features of the situation would need to hold for the analogy to succeed, and does Rowe's "friendly atheist" response give adequate grounds for resisting it?

4. Skeptical theism holds that we cannot reliably judge whether any instance of suffering is gratuitous, because God's justifying reasons may involve goods beyond our conceptual grasp. But the chapter notes the "commitment paradox" (Almeida and Oppy): if we cannot trust our intuitions that some suffering is unjustified, we also undermine the moral intuitions about divine goodness that support theism in the first place. How should the skeptical theist respond to this objection, and does their response commit them to a principled limit on moral skepticism that is independently defensible?

5. The soul-making theodicy and the natural law theodicy are both positive accounts of why an omnipotent, omnibenevolent God permits suffering. Both face the scale problem: the amount of suffering, especially animal suffering over evolutionary history, vastly exceeds what either theodicy needs. Marilyn McCord Adams's response to *horrendous* evils is different in kind from both — it requires divine participation in suffering rather than justification of it from a distance. What are the philosophical strengths and limits of each approach, and is Adams's participatory response a theodicy in the traditional sense or something else?

6. Natural evil — disease, predation, mass extinctions — poses a distinctive challenge because it predates human free will by hundreds of millions of years and cannot be attributed to human moral failure. Plantinga's extension of the free will defense to fallen angelic beings is acknowledged to be speculative. Hick's soul-making theodicy struggles to explain pre-human animal suffering. What would an adequate response to natural evil need to establish, and why is the scale of evolutionary suffering a particularly difficult datum for any response to address?

7. Schellenberg's hiddenness argument concludes that a perfectly loving God would not permit inculpable non-belief — sincere, non-resistant failure to believe in God. Compare this argument's structure to Rowe's evidential argument. Both are probabilistic rather than demonstrative. Both face the response that God may have inscrutable reasons inaccessible to us. Does the hiddenness argument face the same skeptical theist rebuttal that Rowe's argument faces, or is there a relevant asymmetry between our ability to judge divine hiddenness and our ability to judge gratuitous suffering?

8. Ivan Karamazov's challenge in Dostoevsky's *Brothers Karamazov* is moral rather than logical: even granting that the world's suffering will ultimately be harmonized, he refuses to accept any harmony that requires the tears of even one child. The chapter distinguishes the intellectual problem of evil (is God's existence probable?) from the existential one (can we accept a God who created this world?). Why is the existential problem not simply reducible to the intellectual one? What would a satisfying response to Ivan's challenge require that is not required by a successful theodicy?
