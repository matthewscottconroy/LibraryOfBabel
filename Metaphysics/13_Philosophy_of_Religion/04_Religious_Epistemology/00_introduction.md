# Religious Epistemology

Even if the arguments for the existence of God are not conclusive — even if no philosophical argument establishes theism beyond reasonable doubt — a further question arises: can belief in God be rational without argument? And can religious experience provide direct evidence for God's existence in the way that perceptual experience provides evidence for the existence of ordinary physical objects?

These are questions of religious epistemology, and they bear on something fundamental: not whether God exists, but whether theistic belief can be epistemically justified, rational, and warranted, and if so, how. Natural theology — the project of establishing theistic belief through reason and argument — answers that theistic belief requires the support of successful arguments. On this view, the failure of the classical arguments is a serious problem: without argumentative support, theistic belief lacks justification.

Reformed epistemology, associated with Alvin Plantinga and Nicholas Wolterstorff, challenges the assumption that theistic belief requires argumentative support. Plantinga argues that belief in God can be a *properly basic* belief — a belief that is rational to hold without inferring it from other beliefs, in the same way that perceptual beliefs and memory beliefs are rational without requiring argument. The sensus divinitatis — a natural faculty that produces belief in God in appropriate circumstances — grounds theistic belief in a way that requires no philosophical argument.

Religious experience offers a different route to theistic justification. William Alston argues that perceiving God — having experiences as of God's presence, love, or guidance — can provide genuine justification for theistic belief, by a principle analogous to the principle that perceptual experience provides justification for beliefs about the physical world. The analogy is contested: religious experiences are much more variable, more dependent on prior belief, and less consensually interpretable than ordinary perception.

The chapter on pluralism and exclusivism asks how religious belief should be held given the diversity of religions. Is it rational to believe that one's own religion is uniquely correct when equally rational people in different circumstances believe incompatible things? The epistemological challenge of religious diversity has implications for the rationality of religious commitment that this chapter takes seriously.
