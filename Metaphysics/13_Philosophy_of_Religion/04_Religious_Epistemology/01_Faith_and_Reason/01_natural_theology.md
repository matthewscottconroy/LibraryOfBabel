# Natural Theology

Suppose you knew nothing of any religious tradition — no scriptures, no revelations, no prophetic testimony. Could you, by reason and observation alone, come to know that God exists? This is the question natural theology poses, and it has been answered very differently across the history of philosophy. Its claim — that God's existence and some of God's attributes can be established by any rational person through the exercise of unaided human reason — is both its distinctive appeal and the source of its most sustained criticisms.

Natural theology is distinguished from *revealed theology* (knowledge of God through scripture or religious experience) and from *philosophical theology* (philosophical analysis of theological concepts, which may or may not depend on revelation). Its aspiration is the universality of natural knowledge: the cosmological, teleological, and ontological arguments should, if they succeed, be accessible to any sufficiently reflective mind, regardless of religious affiliation.

## Historical Development

The argument traces back in the Western tradition to Aristotle's Unmoved Mover — derived entirely from reflection on motion and change, with no appeal to Hebrew scripture or Greek religious tradition. Change requires a cause, that cause requires a cause, but an infinite regress of causes is impossible: there must be a first unmoved mover. This is natural theology in its purest philosophical form. Cicero's *De Natura Deorum* presents and critiques several natural-theological arguments from Stoic and Epicurean sources.

Aquinas distinguished natural theology (*theologia naturalis*) from revealed theology (*theologia revelata*). Natural theology could establish God's existence, unity, simplicity, and goodness — these are the "preambles of faith" (*praeambula fidei*) that reason can establish and revelation presupposes. The Trinity, the incarnation, and the sacraments transcend reason and are known through revelation alone. Aquinas's Five Ways are the paradigmatic natural theological arguments of the medieval period.

The Enlightenment generated a crisis. Hume's critiques of the cosmological and design arguments in the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779) and the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), and Kant's critiques of all three classical arguments in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781), were widely taken to have undermined the classical theistic proofs. The subsequent period saw natural theology decline in influence, particularly in Protestant theology. Karl Barth's *Nein!* (1934) expressed the most radical Protestant rejection: any knowledge of God must come through God's self-revelation in Christ. Natural theology reflects human pride — the attempt to grasp God by human means. Barth held that natural theology produces not knowledge of God but an idol constructed from human reason. Emil Brunner disagreed, arguing that some natural knowledge of God is preserved in human conscience and moral experience, even if distorted by sin.

## Contemporary Revival

Contemporary natural theology has revived substantially through analytic philosophy of religion, primarily from the 1950s onward.

Richard Swinburne's approach in *The Existence of God* (1979, 2nd ed. 2004) treats natural theology as Bayesian inference. Theism is a simple, powerful explanatory hypothesis. Each piece of evidence — the existence of the universe, its orderliness, fine-tuning, religious experience, the existence of moral facts — raises the probability of theism. No single argument is conclusive, but the joint probability of theism is substantially raised by the cumulative weight of evidence. Swinburne argues that theism is, on balance, more probable than not.

William Lane Craig has defended and developed the Kalam cosmological argument and the fine-tuning argument with sustained philosophical and scientific rigor, arguing that natural theology provides strong evidence for theism by contemporary philosophical standards.

Plantinga occupies an interesting position. He argues that natural theology is not *required* for warranted belief in God — belief in God can be properly basic without inferential support from natural theological arguments. But he does not deny that natural theological arguments can provide evidence for theism; he holds only that they are not the primary grounds for rational theistic belief. In *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000), Plantinga distinguishes the question of warrant from the question of evidence: even if natural theological arguments provide evidence, theistic belief is warranted through the sensus divinitatis even without them.

## The Epistemological Role of Natural Theology

Natural theology plays different roles in different frameworks. For Paley and the Enlightenment tradition, it provides the sole rational foundation for theistic belief. For Aquinas, it establishes presuppositions of revealed theology. For Swinburne, it contributes one strand to a cumulative evidential case. For Plantinga, it may add evidence to what is independently warranted through the sensus divinitatis. And for Barth, it is illegitimate entirely.

We can put this more sharply as a question: is natural theology a replacement for revelation, a supplement to it, or an intrusion into a domain that only revelation can address? How one answers this question shapes everything about how the specific arguments — cosmological, teleological, ontological — are evaluated and what one expects from them.
