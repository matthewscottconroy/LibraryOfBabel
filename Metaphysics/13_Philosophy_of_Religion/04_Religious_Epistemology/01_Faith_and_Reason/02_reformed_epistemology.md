# Reformed Epistemology

The traditional picture of rational religious belief holds that theism must be supported by arguments — cosmological, teleological, ontological — to be epistemically acceptable. Without sufficient evidence from those arguments, theistic belief is irrational. This picture is associated with *classical foundationalism*: the view that all rational beliefs must either be (a) basic — self-evident, incorrigible reports of one's own mental states, or evident to the senses — or (b) supported by inference from beliefs of these kinds. Since God's existence is none of the first three kinds, classical foundationalism demands that theism be inferred.

Reformed epistemology, developed primarily by Alvin Plantinga, Nicholas Wolterstorff, and William Alston, challenges this demand. Its claim is that belief in God can be *properly basic* — rationally held without inferential justification from other beliefs. The view takes its name from the Reformed (Calvinist) theological tradition, which emphasized that knowledge of God (*sensus divinitatis*) is a natural endowment of the human mind.

## Plantinga's Attack on Classical Foundationalism

Plantinga challenges the evidentialism requirement in two stages.

The first stage is an internal critique: classical foundationalism is self-refuting. "Only believe what is self-evident, incorrigible, evident to the senses, or supported by such things" is not itself self-evident, incorrigible, or evident to the senses — and it is not the kind of claim that can be supported by inference from those sources. If classical foundationalism is correct, then classical foundationalism is itself irrational by its own standard.

The second stage is an external critique: many beliefs we hold rationally are not supported by argument from classical basic beliefs. "I had breakfast this morning" (memory), "the person in front of me is conscious" (other minds), "the external world exists" — these are held rationally even though they cannot be inferred from self-evident, incorrigible, or sense-evident beliefs alone. We take them as basic and they are rational. Why should theistic belief be treated differently?

## Proper Basicality and the Sensus Divinitatis

In *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000), Plantinga develops a more elaborate framework built around the concept of *warrant*: a belief has warrant if it is produced by cognitive faculties functioning properly in the right epistemic environment, according to a design plan aimed at truth.

The *sensus divinitatis* — Calvin's "sense of the divine" — is a cognitive faculty that, when functioning properly, produces belief in God in response to features of the natural world: sunsets, moral experience, human dignity, gratitude for life, guilt, the birth of a child. These experiences trigger a natural disposition to believe in God — a disposition that is, if theism is true, a feature of our cognitive design plan aimed at truth.

The conditional structure is crucial:

- **If theism is true**, then the sensus divinitatis likely exists and functions to produce warranted theistic belief.
- **If theism is true**, then theistic belief produced by the properly functioning sensus divinitatis is warranted.
- Therefore: **if theism is true**, then theistic belief is warranted.

Notice what this does not prove: it does not prove that theism is true. But it shows that the rationality of theistic belief depends on its truth, not on the success of arguments. The objection "theism is irrational because it lacks evidential support" is answered: theistic belief is warranted if it is true, and whether it is true is a question to be decided on other grounds. The atheist objection, properly understood, must be that theism is false — not that it is evidentially unsupported.

## Wolterstorff: Reason Within the Bounds of Religion

Wolterstorff argues in *Reason Within the Bounds of Religion* (1976) that all theorizing — including scientific theorizing — proceeds from "control beliefs" that are not themselves fully justified by the data they are used to evaluate. A religious believer's control beliefs include theological commitments, and it is no more epistemically irresponsible to theorize from theological control beliefs than from naturalistic ones. The demand for epistemically neutral inquiry before reasoning about religion is itself an ideological commitment, not a neutral epistemic requirement.

## Alston's Doxastic Practices

Alston argues in *Perceiving God* (1991) that both sense perception and religious experience are *doxastic practices* — socially established ways of forming beliefs that are self-evidently not "proved" by independent external evidence. We justify sensory perception partly by relying on the results of sensory perception; we justify religious experience partly by relying on the results of religious experience. Neither circularity is vicious if the practice is reliable, coherent, and not subject to specific defeaters. Just as sensory perception gives one prima facie reason to believe in the external world, religious experience within an appropriate doxastic practice gives one prima facie reason to believe in God.

## Objections

**The Great Pumpkin objection**: Plantinga himself raises it. If we allow theistic belief to be properly basic, why not belief in the Great Pumpkin? What prevents any arbitrary belief from counting as "properly basic"? Plantinga's response: properly basic beliefs are those produced by reliable cognitive faculties functioning properly in appropriate circumstances. Arbitrary beliefs are not like this. Furthermore, there is a historical and cross-cultural track record of sensus divinitatis-type religious belief that has no parallel in Great Pumpkin belief.

**The rationality of atheism**: If theistic belief can be properly basic and warranted if true, can atheistic belief also be properly basic? Plantinga's framework allows this in principle. But he argues in *Warrant and Proper Function* (1993) that naturalism generates an evolutionary argument against itself: if naturalism is true, there is no reason to think our belief-forming faculties are reliable truth-trackers, since evolution selects for fitness rather than truth. This is a separate argument that Plantinga develops at length.

**The problem of conflicting basicalities**: Different people's sensus divinitatis produces very different basic beliefs — theism, polytheism, animism, pantheism. If all are produced by the sensus divinitatis functioning properly, they cannot all be reliable. This is the diversity objection transposed from religious experience to properly basic belief. Plantinga's response is that sin distorts the sensus divinitatis — not all deliverances of the faculty are deliverances of a *properly functioning* faculty.
