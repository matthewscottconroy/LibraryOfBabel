# Faith and Evidence

There is a tempting picture of the relationship between faith and evidence: evidence comes first, and faith — if rational — follows the evidence. Believe only what the evidence warrants; proportion your belief to the evidence; where evidence is insufficient, suspend judgment. This is evidentialism, and its most uncompromising statement comes from W. K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" (1877): "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence."

Clifford's argument is not merely epistemological but ethical. Believing on insufficient evidence is a moral failure, not just an intellectual one. He illustrates with a shipowner who convinces himself, without adequate investigation, that his ship is seaworthy; his passengers drown. Applied to religion: if the evidence for God's existence is insufficient, then believing in God is not merely epistemically imprudent but morally wrong. The believer is culpable for intellectual irresponsibility.

This is a powerful challenge. But it faces serious difficulties before it even reaches religious belief.

## Problems with Evidentialism

First, the evidentialist principle appears self-undermining. Is "only believe on sufficient evidence" itself supported by sufficient evidence? It is not self-evident, and the evidence for it is the very procedure of rational inquiry whose scope it is meant to define — a suspicious circularity.

Second, evidentialism seems too strong as a general principle. Many beliefs we hold rationally — belief in other minds, the external world, the reliability of memory — are not supported by conclusive evidence in Clifford's sense. If evidentialism's demands are taken seriously, radical skepticism follows.

Third, there is a psychological objection: belief is not fully under voluntary control. Clifford's moral demand to proportion belief to evidence assumes we can simply decide what to believe on the basis of evaluating evidence. Doxastic voluntarism — the view that belief is directly subject to the will — is philosophically contested.

## William James's "Will to Believe"

William James responded to Clifford directly in "The Will to Believe" (1896). James argues that when evidence is genuinely insufficient to decide between two live, forced, and momentous options, we may — and in some cases should — believe what our passional nature inclines us toward. Three conditions define the relevant cases:

- **Live**: both options are genuine possibilities for the person.
- **Forced**: there is no neutral third option; refusing to choose is itself a choice with consequences.
- **Momentous**: the stakes are high enough that waiting for more evidence is itself a significant decision.

The choice between theism and atheism meets all three conditions for many people. In such a case, James argues, the passional element in decision is not an irrationality but a legitimate exercise of judgment under uncertainty. Clifford's demand for evidence before belief is itself a passional commitment — the passion for caution and certainty — and it carries costs just as much as the commitment to believe.

The objection to James is that his argument licenses too much: any strongly felt desire could be framed as a "will to believe" justifying the corresponding belief. James's response is that the believer must be honest about the uncertainty and cannot manufacture evidence for a conclusion already reached for passional reasons.

## Plantinga's Reformed Epistemology

Plantinga's reformed epistemology (discussed at length in the previous file) offers a third position. Theistic belief can be rational without being based on evidence — not because evidence is irrelevant, but because belief in God can be properly basic, produced by the sensus divinitatis functioning properly. This is not fideism: Plantinga does not hold that theistic belief is rational in opposition to reason. He holds that it can be rational *without* requiring the evidential support that classical foundationalism demands, because it is produced by a reliable cognitive faculty.

## Fideism

At the opposite pole from evidentialism, fideism holds that religious faith is beyond or even against reason. Kierkegaard's reading presents faith as a "leap" that goes beyond and against the evidence. The absurdity of Christian claims — God becomes man, the infinite becomes finite — is not a reason against faith but its occasion: faith is precisely the embrace of what reason cannot establish or comprehend. Kierkegaard in *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846) argues that objective certainty is unavailable for the existential claims of religion, and that subjective passionate commitment is what matters.

Wittgensteinian fideism (associated with D. Z. Phillips) holds that religion constitutes its own "language game" with its own internal criteria of rationality — a different form of life, not to be evaluated by the standards of scientific or philosophical evidence. Critics argue that this makes religious claims immune to rational evaluation and revision in a way that seems epistemically irresponsible. If religion is a self-contained language game, it cannot be criticized by the evidential standards of any other game — but it also cannot be defended by them, which seems an uncomfortable place to stand.

## Eschatological Verification

John Hick introduced the notion of "eschatological verification" in "Theology and Verification" (1960): religious beliefs may not be currently verifiable but will be verifiable in the afterlife. If God exists and we survive death, the evidence will be overwhelming — which makes theistic belief at least in principle verifiable. This preserves the empirical character of religious claims: they are genuinely about reality and are in principle confirmable by experience, just not yet. Critics argue that unfalsifiable-in-the-present beliefs cannot be meaningfully confirmed by hypothetical future evidence — the verification principle Hick invokes may cut against him.

## Faith Seeking Understanding

A distinctive Christian theological tradition — associated with Augustine (*credo ut intelligam*, "I believe in order to understand") and Anselm (*fides quaerens intellectum*, "faith seeking understanding") — holds that faith and reason are complementary rather than opposed. Faith provides the starting point from which reason proceeds to deeper understanding. This is neither fideism (reason is genuinely employed) nor classical evidentialism (faith precedes the evidence) — it is a model in which the relationship between faith and reason is dynamic and mutually enriching. Whether this model is philosophically defensible or merely a description of how committed believers engage their commitments is itself a contested question.
