# Alston: Perceiving God

When someone looks at a tree and forms the belief "there is a tree," we regard this as epistemically legitimate. The belief is formed on the basis of sensory experience through a socially established and internally coherent practice of perception. We justify this practice partly by its own results — by appealing to sensory experience to confirm sensory experience — but this circularity is not vicious: what matters is whether the practice is reliable and coherent, not whether it can be grounded in some entirely independent external standpoint.

William Alston's *Perceiving God: The Epistemology of Religious Experience* (1991) argues that a person who has an apparent experience of God and forms the belief "God is present" or "God is comforting me" is engaged in a structurally parallel epistemic activity. The practice of forming beliefs on the basis of apparent experiences of God — what Alston calls "Christian Mystical Practice" (CMP) — is prima facie justified in the same way that sensory perceptual practice is justified. This is his central claim, and it is developed with philosophical precision.

## Doxastic Practices

The key concept is a *doxastic practice* (DP): a socially established, structured way of forming beliefs from inputs of a certain kind. Different practices include:

- **Sensory perceptual practice**: forming beliefs about the physical world on the basis of sensory experience.
- **Introspective practice**: forming beliefs about one's own mental states.
- **Rational intuition practice**: forming beliefs about mathematical and logical truths.
- **Christian Mystical Practice**: forming beliefs about God on the basis of apparent experiential encounters with God.

Each practice has a characteristic set of inputs, a system of overriders (conditions under which beliefs formed by the practice should be revised), and internal consistency criteria. Crucially, we cannot step outside all doxastic practices to justify any of them from a neutral standpoint. Every attempt to justify a doxastic practice must itself employ some doxastic practice. This is an observation about the structure of epistemic justification, not a skeptical conclusion.

Alston argues that a doxastic practice is epistemically acceptable if it is not internally incoherent, not demonstrably unreliable (by reasons accessible within other well-established DPs), has an established track record within a community of practitioners, and yields a coherent system of beliefs that hang together well. CMP meets these criteria.

## The Argument from Parity

The key move: if we accept sensory perceptual practice as generating prima facie epistemic justification, we should accept CMP as doing so — unless we have a principled reason to treat them differently. The burden of proof falls on whoever wants to disqualify CMP while accepting sensory practice.

Alston carefully examines three putative disqualifiers.

**Public checkability**: Sensory experience is publicly testable; religious experience is not. Alston's response: while sensory experience is interpersonally verifiable in some respects, this verifiability is itself internal to the practice — we verify one perceptual claim by making other perceptual claims. Furthermore, religious experience has a kind of inter-subjective checkability within the tradition: practitioners can compare their experiences against established community reports, tradition-specific criteria for authentic religious experience, and the fruits of the experience in the person's life.

**Naturalistic explanation**: We have naturalistic explanations of religious experience (neural correlates, temporal lobe activity, etc.). Alston's response: we also have naturalistic explanations of sensory experience (retinal stimulation, neural processing), yet this does not undermine the epistemic value of perception. If God uses natural mechanisms to produce religious experience, both the naturalistic and the theistic explanations can be simultaneously true — one describes the mechanism, the other the ultimate cause.

**Diversity of outputs**: CMP across traditions generates incompatible beliefs — some mystics experience a personal God, others an impersonal Absolute. Alston acknowledges this as a genuine problem that reduces the overall epistemic standing of CMP. It is a real disqualifier, though not a decisive one. He treats it as evidence that CMP is less reliable than sensory practice, without concluding that CMP has no epistemic value.

## What Alston's Contribution Adds

Before Alston, the argument from religious experience was typically a simple inference: "I experienced God; therefore God probably exists." Alston's contribution is to reframe the argument in terms of doxastic practices. This clarifies three things:

1. The epistemic question is about the *practice* of forming beliefs from religious experience, not about individual experiences in isolation.
2. The justification of any doxastic practice is internal rather than external — we cannot demand that CMP be justified by sensory practice without applying a double standard.
3. The relevant comparison class for evaluating CMP is not "beliefs with no experiential basis" but "other doxastic practices," which themselves face analogous challenges to their justification.

## Objections and Replies

**"Religious experience is merely emotional or physiological, not genuinely perceptual."** Alston responds that the same reductive claim could be made about sensory experience: perceptual beliefs are produced by physical processes, but this does not prevent them from being genuinely about the external world. The origin of an experience does not determine its epistemic value.

**"We have no conception of the mechanism by which God would produce religious experience."** Alston grants this but argues it does not defeat prima facie justification. We also lack a full account of how the external world causes sensory experience — the gap between physical processes and phenomenal consciousness is philosophically unsolved — yet this does not undermine sensory practice.

**"CMP does not produce beliefs confirmable outside the practice."** Alston's response: neither does sensory practice, if "outside the practice" means "without using any sensory experience at all." The demand for external confirmation assumes the neutral standpoint that Alston argues is unavailable for any doxastic practice.
