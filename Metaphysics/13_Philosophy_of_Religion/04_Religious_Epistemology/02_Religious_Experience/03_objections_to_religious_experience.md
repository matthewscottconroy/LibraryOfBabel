# Objections to Religious Experience

The argument from religious experience — that apparent experiences of God provide prima facie evidence for God's existence — faces several powerful objections. We can work through them in order of increasing depth, from the epistemological to the explanatory to the philosophical.

## The Diversity Objection

Religious experiences across traditions are not only different but inconsistent. Christians experience a personal God who creates and loves and responds. Advaita Vedantins experience undifferentiated unity — *brahman* — without personal characteristics. Some Buddhists report no transcendent reality at all: the experience of *sunyata* (emptiness) is not an experience of any positive transcendent being. Sufis experience union with Allah; Kabbalists encounter the Ein Sof. These cannot all be veridical, because their contents contradict each other.

The diversity suggests two conclusions. First, cultural shaping: religious experience is significantly formed by prior belief and cultural context rather than by any common transcendent reality. Different traditions produce different experiences because they train their practitioners to experience what the tradition describes — expectations shape perception. Second, and more seriously, the inconsistency of religious experiences constitutes a *defeater* for any particular tradition's claim that its experiences are veridical. If equally sincere practitioners across independent traditions have contradictory experiences, the reliability of any one tradition's claims is compromised.

Theistic responses attempt to recover. The diversity might reflect different aspects or modes of a single divine reality, as the same physical object might be perceived differently from different angles. John Hick's pluralism (see section 03) develops this line. Alternatively, the most significant experiences — of divine presence, love, or ultimacy — may be cross-culturally convergent even if doctrinal interpretations vary. Critics press that the convergence claim is overstated: when one tradition reports a personal God and another reports an impersonal Absolute, the disagreement is not superficial.

## The Naturalistic Explanation Objection

Religious experience has naturalistic explanations, and several independent lines of evidence converge.

Brain imaging studies show distinctive neural activity patterns associated with meditation, prayer, and mystical states. Andrew Newberg's research (*Why God Won't Go Away*, 2001) identifies peak spiritual experiences with measurable brain states — reduced parietal lobe activity associated with loss of self-other boundaries, increased frontal lobe activity associated with focused attention. Michael Persinger's work suggests that stimulating the temporal lobes produces experiences that subjects describe as "presence of God," "sensed presence," or profound spiritual significance.

Psilocybin, LSD, and other psychedelic substances reliably produce experiences that subjects describe as indistinguishable (at the first-person level) from classical mystical states. Walter Pahnke's "Good Friday Experiment" (1962) showed that psilocybin produced experiences that subjects rated as among the most meaningful of their lives, similar to classical mystical reports. The phenomenology of mystical experience can apparently be produced by chemical means, without any divine input.

Pascal Boyer (*Religion Explained*, 2001) and other cognitive scientists of religion offer evolutionary explanations: religious belief is a byproduct of cognitive systems evolved for other purposes. Justin Barrett's "Hyperactive Agency Detection Device" (HADD) suggests that we evolved to detect agents in ambiguous stimuli, and this overactive detection generates religious experiences as false positives — we are cognitively primed to experience agency and presence even where none exists.

The standard theistic response (Alston, Swinburne) is that naturalistic explanations are not automatically defeaters. We have naturalistic explanations of ordinary perceptual experience too — retinal stimulation, neural processing — yet this does not undermine perception's epistemic value. If God uses natural mechanisms to produce religious experience, both the naturalistic explanation and the theistic explanation can be simultaneously true. This response is philosophically coherent, but it requires that theistic belief not depend on the absence of a naturalistic explanation. Critics argue that the simpler hypothesis — naturalistic processes fully explain the phenomenon without invoking God — is preferable on parsimony grounds.

## The Problem of Specific Content

Religious experiences vary not only across traditions but in their specific content within traditions. Some report God as wrathful, some as loving; some report visual experiences of saints, others only formless presence; some receive verbal messages with specific content, others ineffable impressions. This creates a dilemma: if all such experiences are veridical, God's character is inconsistent. If some are veridical and others are not, we need a principle for distinguishing them — and the proposed criteria (consistency with tradition, moral fruits, etc.) are themselves tradition-specific and provide no neutral standpoint. We cannot independently verify the character of God and then use that verification to adjudicate between experiences.

## The Doxastic Practice Objection

Alston argues that CMP is a legitimate doxastic practice. Critics respond that many practices are socially established and internally coherent, yet we have good reason to consider them unreliable: astrology, folk medicine, conspiracy theorizing, and UFO-contact beliefs all have established communities, internal consistency, and track records within those communities. What distinguishes legitimate doxastic practices from illegitimate ones?

Alston's response: practices that conflict with well-established rival practices (sensory practice, scientific practice) lose their epistemic standing. Astrology conflicts with established astronomy; folk medicine is tested and often found wanting by medical science. CMP does not obviously conflict with any well-established competing practice. Critics argue this is too permissive: CMP's apparent compatibility with science might simply reflect that religious experience claims are not scientifically testable at all — unfalsifiability is not a virtue.

## The Problem of Identifying the Cause

Even granting that someone has an experience of genuinely divine origin, the experience may be caused by a being other than the God of classical theism. It might be a lesser deity or spirit, a demonic being, or — as Freud argued in *The Future of an Illusion* (1927) — one's own unconscious projecting an idealized father figure onto the structure of experience. Ludwig Feuerbach (*The Essence of Christianity*, 1841) argued that God is a projection of human ideals: what humans aspire to be but cannot fully realize — infinite, omnipotent, perfectly good — is projected onto an imaginary divine being. Religious experience, on this account, is genuine but self-referential.

Neither the Freudian nor the Feuerbachian explanation can be conclusively established; they may be true, or the experiences may be genuine — these are not mutually exclusive. But the availability of these explanations reduces the evidential weight of religious experience for those who have not had such experiences themselves and are evaluating the testimony from the outside.
