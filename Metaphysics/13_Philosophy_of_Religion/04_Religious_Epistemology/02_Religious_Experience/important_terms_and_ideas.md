# Important Terms and Ideas: Religious Experience

**Religious Experience**
An experience that the subject takes to be an encounter with or manifestation of the divine—God's presence, a mystical sense of unity with all reality, a vision of the divine, or a powerful sense of being addressed by a transcendent being. The epistemic question is whether such experiences can justify religious belief.

**Principle of Credulity**
Swinburne's principle: in the absence of defeaters, if it seems to someone that X is present, then X probably is present. Applied to religious experience: if someone has a genuine seeming of God's presence, this is prima facie evidence that God is present, just as a seeming of a physical object is prima facie evidence of that object.

**Mystical Experience**
A form of religious experience characterized by a sense of unity, ineffability, and a noetic quality—a feeling of direct insight into deep reality. James identified four marks: transiency, passivity, ineffability, and noetic quality. Mystical experiences are reported across all major religious traditions.

**The Argument from Religious Experience**
The argument that the widespread occurrence of religious experiences, across cultures and traditions, provides evidence for the existence of God. The argument is probabilistic: the best explanation of so many people having such experiences is that God (or some transcendent reality) is genuinely encountered.

**Defeaters for Religious Experience**
Factors that undermine the evidential force of religious experience: the cross-cultural diversity of religious experiences supporting incompatible religions; the possibility of naturalistic explanations (neurological, psychological); the role of expectation and cultural conditioning in shaping religious experience.

**Doxastic Practices**
Alston's concept: practices of forming beliefs based on certain inputs. Sense perception, introspection, and mystical perception are all doxastic practices. Alston argues that mystical perception cannot be shown to be less reliable than sense perception from a neutral standpoint, so it deserves the same epistemic credit.
