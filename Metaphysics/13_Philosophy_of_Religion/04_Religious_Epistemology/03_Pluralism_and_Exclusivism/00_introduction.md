# Religious Pluralism and Exclusivism

The world's religious traditions — Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Hinduism, Buddhism, and many others — make conflicting claims about the nature of ultimate reality, the path to salvation or liberation, and the proper way to live. They cannot all be correct in their doctrinal claims: if Jesus is the unique incarnation of God, then the Islamic doctrine that Jesus was a prophet but not divine is false; if nirvana is the ultimate goal, then the Christian doctrine of eternal life with a personal God is mistaken. How should one navigate this diversity?

Religious exclusivism holds that one's own religious tradition is correct and that other traditions, insofar as they conflict, are mistaken. This is the straightforward position: religious doctrines make truth claims, and conflicting truth claims cannot both be true. The exclusivist need not hold that other traditions have nothing of value or no partial truth; she holds that on the central doctrinal questions, only one tradition is correct.

The epistemological challenge to exclusivism — pressed by Hick and others — is the argument from religious diversity: if a person had been born in a different culture, with different religious formation, they would almost certainly have a different religious belief. The Christian believes in Christianity because she was raised in a Christian culture; the Muslim believes in Islam because he was raised in a Muslim culture. This symmetry between the processes that produce different religious beliefs creates doubt about whether any of them is reliably truth-tracking.

Religious pluralism — associated above all with John Hick — holds that the world's great religious traditions are all culturally conditioned responses to the same ultimate reality (which Hick calls "the Real"). Each tradition grasps partial truths about the Real, filtered through its own cultural and historical lens. No tradition has a monopoly on religious truth; salvation/liberation is available through many paths.

Religious inclusivism — associated with Rahner's "anonymous Christianity" — holds that one tradition is definitively correct but that members of other traditions can be saved through that tradition's grace without explicitly belonging to it.

This section examines religious pluralism, exclusivism, and inclusivism, assessing the arguments for each position and the epistemological challenges they face.
