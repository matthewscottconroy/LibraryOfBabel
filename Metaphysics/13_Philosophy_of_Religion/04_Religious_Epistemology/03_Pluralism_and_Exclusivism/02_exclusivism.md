# Religious Exclusivism

Most traditional religious communities have been, at least implicitly, exclusivist. The belief that Jesus is uniquely the savior of humanity, or that Muhammad is the final prophet of God, or that the Torah is God's binding covenant with the Jewish people, are claims about what is uniquely true — not claims that are equally well expressed in other traditions. Exclusivism is not a peripheral feature of traditional religious commitment; it is, in many traditions, close to the center.

But exclusivism faces a serious epistemological challenge when we take religious diversity seriously. Equally rational, morally serious, spiritually engaged people hold incompatible religious beliefs. They have examined the evidence available to them and arrived at very different conclusions. If such people are our epistemic peers — if they are as well-positioned as we are to evaluate the relevant evidence — shouldn't we reduce our confidence in our own exclusivist commitments?

## The Epistemic Peer Objection

The argument runs as follows:

- P1: Equally rational, informed, and sincere people hold religious beliefs that are incompatible with my own.
- P2: If equally rational people disagree with me, I should assign roughly equal probability to their view and mine.
- P3: If I must assign equal probability to incompatible religious views, I cannot rationally hold any one of them with confidence.
- C: Therefore, confident exclusivist commitment is epistemically unjustified.

This is sometimes called the demographic argument or the epistemic peer objection to exclusivism, and it has considerable intuitive force.

## Plantinga's Defense of Exclusivism

Alvin Plantinga defends exclusivism as rationally permissible in "Pluralism: A Defense of Religious Exclusivism" (1995). The argument proceeds in two stages.

The first stage challenges the inference in P2. If I believe P and I know that others believe not-P, am I thereby obligated to revise P? Only if the fact of disagreement gives me *new evidence* against P. But merely knowing that others disagree doesn't constitute new evidence, especially if I have no reason to think their epistemic situation is superior to mine. Disagreement is evidence against my belief only insofar as the other person has access to evidence or reasoning I lack. If I already know about Islam and have considered its claims, Muslim disagreement with my Christian belief gives me no new information that I did not already factor in.

The second stage appeals to proper basicality. On Plantinga's reformed epistemology, Christian belief may be properly basic — produced by the sensus divinitatis functioning properly. If so, it has warrant even without inferential support. The fact that Muslim belief is also held on the basis of the Muslim's sensus divinitatis does not show that my Christian belief lacks warrant. It may be that one of us has a malfunctioning or distorted sensus divinitatis (the effects of sin on cognitive faculties, for Plantinga). Which of us is in this situation cannot be determined from a neutral standpoint.

## The Moral Dimension: Arrogance or Respect?

Critics argue that exclusivism involves a kind of arrogance: it requires holding that billions of sincere, thoughtful, morally serious people are deeply wrong in their most fundamental convictions — often through no fault of their own. Is this compatible with genuine respect for those people?

Plantinga's response inverts the charge. Genuine respect for others includes the possibility of genuine disagreement. Condescension or indifference — not honest engagement — is what disrespect looks like. The exclusivist who says "I believe your view is mistaken, and here is why" treats the other person with more respect than the pluralist who says "all views are equally valid" — since the latter implicitly denies that the content of religious belief matters enough to argue about. Authentic dialogue requires taking the other person's claims seriously enough to assess them.

Even so, many exclusivists concede that epistemic humility is appropriate — holding one's belief firmly because one has good grounds for it, while acknowledging the genuine epistemic situation that makes alternative views available to equally sincere inquirers.

## The Problem of Religious Accidents

A specific version of the challenge: if I had been born in medieval Arabia to devout Muslim parents, I would almost certainly be a Muslim; if born in contemporary Mumbai, perhaps a Hindu. My religious belief is largely a function of accidents of birth, culture, and history. Should this give me pause?

Plantinga's response: the same "cosmic accident" argument applies to all beliefs. My belief in the external world is a function of sensory faculties I did not choose; my belief in the reliability of memory is a function of a memory faculty I was born with. The fact that these faculties were produced by forces I did not control does not undermine their reliability. Similarly, even if my sensus divinitatis was formed by my religious community, this does not undermine the warrant of my beliefs if the sensus divinitatis is reliable.

Critics find this response unsatisfying in one respect: the sensus divinitatis (if it exists) presumably produces broadly theistic belief in all humans, but it does not produce specifically Christian, Muslim, or Jewish beliefs — those are products of historical and cultural transmission. The "accident" problem applies precisely to the particular exclusivist content of religious belief, not to generic theism.

## Varieties of Exclusivism

Exclusivism comes in stronger and weaker forms. **Strong exclusivism** holds that only one religion is true, all others are entirely false, and salvation is available only within the one true tradition. **Moderate exclusivism** holds that one religion is uniquely true or most complete while others contain significant errors, but sincere seekers in other traditions may achieve partial goods. **Epistemological exclusivism** holds only that one's own tradition provides the best-warranted access to religious truth, even if other traditions contain genuine truth. Most contemporary philosophical defenders of exclusivism hold moderate or epistemological versions rather than the strong form.
