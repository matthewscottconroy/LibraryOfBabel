# Review Questions — Religious Epistemology

1. Classical foundationalism demands that belief in God be supported by arguments from self-evident, incorrigible, or sense-evident beliefs. Plantinga attacks this demand in two stages: first by arguing that classical foundationalism is self-refuting, and second by arguing that many beliefs we hold rationally (memory, other minds) are not so supported either. Does Plantinga's two-stage critique defeat evidentialism entirely, or does it leave open a reformed evidentialism that drops classical foundationalism's constraints while still requiring that theistic belief be responsive to evidence?

2. Plantinga's reformed epistemology holds that theistic belief is warranted *if theism is true* — the sensus divinitatis, functioning properly in the right epistemic environment, produces warranted belief. Critics note that this conditional structure does not settle whether theism is true, only whether the belief is warranted given its truth. How does this relate to the project of natural theology? Is natural theology required for theism if reformed epistemology is correct, or does it play a different and supplementary role in Plantinga's framework?

3. Alston's doxastic practices argument holds that Christian Mystical Practice (CMP) is epistemically on a par with sensory perceptual practice — both are socially established, internally coherent, and not demonstrably unreliable by rival well-established practices. The diversity objection is Alston's own acknowledged weak point: CMP produces inconsistent outputs across traditions, which reduces its reliability. Does the diversity of religious experience constitute a genuine asymmetry between CMP and sensory practice that defeats Alston's parity argument, or can the inconsistency be absorbed within a refined version of the claim?

4. The constructivist objection to religious experience (Katz) holds that there is no pre-interpretive mystical experience — all experience is culturally shaped. The perennial philosophy thesis (Hick, Stace) holds that cross-cultural convergence in mystical experience supports a common transcendent reality underlying the diversity of descriptions. These positions make conflicting empirical and philosophical assumptions. What would it take to adjudicate between them — what kind of evidence or argument could settle whether "pure consciousness events" (Forman) are genuine or whether all experience is irreducibly culturally constituted?

5. The naturalistic explanation objection to religious experience (neural correlates, psychedelic induction, hyperactive agency detection) seems to threaten the evidential value of religious experience by providing alternative causal explanations. Alston responds that we also have naturalistic explanations of sensory experience without this undermining perception. Is this response fully satisfying? Does the availability of a complete naturalistic causal chain from brain state to religious experience have a different significance from the availability of a causal chain from physical object to retinal stimulation to perceptual belief?

6. Clifford's evidentialist thesis — that it is wrong to believe on insufficient evidence — and James's "will to believe" represent opposing poles. James argues that the choice between theism and atheism is live, forced, and momentous, and that withholding judgment in such cases is itself a passional commitment with real costs. But reformed epistemology suggests a third position: theistic belief may be warranted without being either evidence-based or a "passionate leap." Map the logical space among these three positions. Under what conditions is each position most defensible, and what background assumptions about epistemology and the nature of religious belief does each require?

7. Religious pluralism (Hick), exclusivism (Plantinga), and inclusivism (Rahner) are not merely theological positions — they carry different epistemological commitments about how beliefs from different traditions should be weighed against each other. Hick's pluralism invokes a Kantian distinction between the Real in itself and the Real as experienced, but faces the "self-undermining" objection that pluralism is itself a culturally conditioned perspective claiming meta-standpoint it cannot coherently occupy. How does this objection connect to the broader question of whether there is a neutral epistemic standpoint from which to evaluate competing religious traditions — and what does Plantinga's response to the epistemic peer objection reveal about whether such a standpoint exists?

8. The hiddenness argument (Schellenberg), reformed epistemology (Plantinga), and the diversity objection to religious experience (Katz) all concern, from different angles, the epistemic accessibility of God. Schellenberg concludes that a perfectly loving God would make divine existence available to sincere seekers; Plantinga argues that divine existence is available through the sensus divinitatis to those whose faculty is functioning properly; and the diversity objection suggests that different traditions' religious experience delivers inconsistent deliverances. Do these three discussions, taken together, produce a coherent picture of how God could be epistemically accessible? Or do the constraints they impose pull in directions that are difficult to reconcile?
