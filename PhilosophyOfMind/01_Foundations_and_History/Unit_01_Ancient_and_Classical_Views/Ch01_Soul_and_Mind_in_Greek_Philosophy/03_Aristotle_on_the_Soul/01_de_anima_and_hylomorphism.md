# De Anima and Hylomorphism

Aristotle's *De Anima* ("On the Soul") is one of the most important philosophical texts ever written on the nature of mind and life. Written in the fourth century BCE, it offers an account of the soul that is strikingly different from Plato's — and that many contemporary philosophers find more congenial than the Cartesian dualism that has dominated Western thinking since the seventeenth century.

**The Hylomorphic Framework**

To understand Aristotle's psychology, you need to understand his general metaphysics. Aristotle holds that natural things are composites of *matter* (*hyle*) and *form* (*morphe*). The matter of a bronze statue is the bronze; the form is the shape. But form is not just shape in the geometrical sense — it is what makes something the kind of thing it is, what gives it its characteristic organization and activity.

Crucially, form is not a separate thing from matter. You cannot have the form of a statue without some matter, and without the form the bronze is just a lump, not a statue. Form and matter are inseparable aspects of a single substance.

This hylomorphic framework applies to living things as well. The soul, for Aristotle, is the form of a natural body that has the capacity for life. It is not a distinct substance that inhabits the body like a ghost in a machine; it is what makes the body a *living* body rather than a corpse.

**The Classic Analogy: The Eye**

Aristotle offers a clarifying analogy. If the eye were an animal, its soul would be sight — the capacity for vision that makes an eye an eye rather than just a fluid-filled sphere. A glass eye is not really an eye at all, except in name; it lacks the form that constitutes eyeness.

Similarly, a dead body is not really a body in the full sense — it lacks the form (soul) that constitutes being alive. The soul is not something inside the body; it is the organizing principle that makes the matter a living body.

This is sometimes called the "functional" reading of Aristotle, because it identifies the soul with a set of functional capacities rather than a kind of stuff. The soul is what a body does — or more precisely, what a body has the first-order capacity to do — rather than what a body contains.

**Levels of Soul**

Aristotle distinguishes three levels of soul:

1. **Nutritive soul** (*threptikon*): The most basic, shared by all living things including plants. It encompasses the capacities for nutrition, growth, and reproduction. Even plants have souls in this minimal sense.

2. **Sensitive soul** (*aisthetikon*): Found in all animals. In addition to the nutritive capacities, animals can perceive, experience pleasure and pain, and desire. Aristotle is clear that sensation requires a body — perception is a physical process — but the soul is what makes the body's physical changes into perceptual states.

3. **Rational soul** (*noetikon*): Unique to humans (and divine beings). In addition to the lower capacities, humans can reason, deliberate, and grasp universal truths.

Each higher level includes the lower levels: all humans have rational, sensitive, and nutritive souls; all animals have sensitive and nutritive souls; only plants have the nutritive soul alone.

**Against Platonic Separation**

Aristotle explicitly criticizes Plato's view that the soul is a separate, immortal substance. On Plato's view, the soul uses the body as a tool and is imprisoned in it. On Aristotle's view, asking whether the soul and body are one is like asking whether the wax and the shape of the wax are one — the question is misconceived, because the soul and body are two aspects of a single thing.

This has important consequences. If the soul is the form of the body, then most of the soul's activities cannot be understood apart from the body. Anger, Aristotle says, is not a pure mental event; it involves a boiling of blood around the heart (as the physiologist describes it) as well as a desire for revenge (as the philosopher describes it). A complete account requires both dimensions.

**The Contemporary Relevance**

Aristotelian hylomorphism fell out of fashion with the rise of Cartesian dualism in the seventeenth century, which sharply distinguished mind from matter. But in recent decades, philosophers have returned to it with renewed interest.

The hylomorphic view avoids the interaction problem that plagues Cartesian dualism — if mind and body are radically different substances, how do they causally influence each other? On the Aristotelian view, there is no such problem because the mental and the physical are not two different substances but two aspects of one substance.

It also offers a middle path between eliminative physicalism (which denies that mental concepts pick out real things) and property dualism (which holds that mental properties are genuinely distinct from physical properties). The soul is a real form of the body — not reducible to any particular material configuration, but also not separable from matter.

Philosophers like Hilary Putnam, later in his career, turned to Aristotelian hylomorphism as an alternative to the reductionism of mainstream physicalism. Whether this revival is philosophically successful is an active debate, but it testifies to the enduring power of Aristotle's original insight: that the mind is the life of the body, not its reluctant tenant.
