# The Faculties of Soul

Having established the general hylomorphic framework, Aristotle develops a detailed account of the specific capacities that constitute animal and human life. This faculty psychology — as it has come to be called — treats the soul not as a single power but as a structured set of distinct capacities, each with its own characteristic activities and objects.

**Sensation and Its Objects**

The sensitive soul encompasses the capacity for *aisthesis* — sensation or perception. Aristotle's account of how perception works is both philosophically careful and empirically informed. When we perceive, we receive the *form* of the perceived object without its *matter*. When the hand touches the hot object, the hand becomes hot; when the eye sees the red object, the eye does not become red. What the eye receives is the *form* of redness — the structure or quality that makes something red — without the underlying matter.

This is a subtle and important distinction. It means that perception, for Aristotle, is not the mere mechanical impact of matter on matter. It is a formally structured transaction in which the perceiving organ comes to be like the perceived object in a specific way: it takes on the object's form, but only in a sensory or perceptual manner, not in the way matter takes on form.

Aristotle distinguishes between the **proper sensibles** and the **common sensibles**. Proper sensibles are qualities that each sense exclusively detects: color for sight, sound for hearing, smell for the olfactory faculty, and so forth. Common sensibles are properties that can be detected by more than one sense: motion, rest, number, shape, size. He also notes a capacity for perceiving *incidentally* — when we see Cleon's son, we are not literally seeing "Cleon's son" but are perceiving a colored shape that we recognize or categorize.

**The Common Sense**

Aristotle recognizes that we are aware not just of specific sensory qualities but also of the fact that we are seeing, of the unity of a perceptual experience across sense modalities, and of the contrast between different senses. He posits a *koinê aisthesis* or "common sense" as the capacity responsible for this integrative function.

This is not the common sense of popular discourse but a technical term: a central capacity that receives inputs from the various specific senses and integrates them into a unified perceptual experience. It is also responsible for our ability to perceive that we are perceiving — a form of perceptual self-awareness that is distinct from the activity of any particular sense.

The common sense is philosophically important because it points to a level of mental organization above the individual senses. Aristotle is not just describing a list of sensory modalities but is asking how they are unified into a coherent experience of an object — a question that remains live in contemporary discussions of the binding problem in neuroscience and the unity of consciousness.

**Imagination and Memory**

Between sensation and intellection, Aristotle places *phantasia* (imagination). When we perceive something, we receive its form; the afterimage or memory that remains is the work of imagination. Imagination allows us to have mental representations in the absence of the corresponding sensory input — it is what makes dreaming possible, for example.

Memory (*mneme*) involves a further relationship to time. To remember is not merely to have a mental image but to have an image that one relates to one's own past. This self-temporal dimension distinguishes memory from pure imagination: when I imagine a phoenix, I am not claiming to have encountered one; when I remember yesterday's conversation, I am relating the image to my own past experience.

**Desire and Appetite**

The sensitive soul also includes the capacity for *orexis* — desire or appetite. This encompasses three types: *epithumia* (appetite, associated with pleasure and pain), *thumos* (passion or spirit, associated with the emotions), and *boulesis* (wish or rational desire, the kind of desire that is specifically responsive to reason).

Aristotle is careful to note that movement — voluntary action — requires both reason and desire. Reason alone is not sufficient to move; pure intellect does not produce action. And desire without reason produces only instinctual or impulsive behavior. The specifically human capacity for deliberation and choice (*prohairesis*) involves the integration of rational and desiderative elements.

**The Vegetative Soul and Life**

Even the most basic capacities — nutrition, growth, reproduction — are functions of the soul. Plants have souls. This is striking: it means that for Aristotle, the concept of soul is not specifically psychological in the modern sense but is the concept of a life-organizing principle.

This has the philosophically interesting consequence that the mind-body problem, as Aristotle frames it, is not specifically about consciousness or experience but about the broader question of what distinguishes living matter from non-living matter. The soul is the form that makes matter into a living body, and the capacities of the mind are just the higher manifestations of the same kind of organization.

**Legacy**

Faculty psychology in the Aristotelian tradition was extremely influential throughout the medieval period and into early modern thought. Locke, Hume, and Kant all work within a broadly faculty-psychological framework, even as they reject many of Aristotle's specific claims. The question of what mental faculties exist, how they are related, and whether they can be understood independently of one another is still an active area of research in cognitive science — where modules, systems, and functional architectures play the role that faculties played for Aristotle.
