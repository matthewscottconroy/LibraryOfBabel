# Nous and the Intellect

If the soul is the form of the body, and if form cannot exist without matter, then it would seem to follow that the soul must perish with the body. For most of the soul, Aristotle accepts this conclusion. The nutritive and sensitive faculties depend essentially on bodily organs; without the body, there is nothing for them to be the form of.

But intellect — *nous* — presents a special problem. The capacity for rational thought seems to have a different character from sensation. Sensation requires a specific organ: sight requires eyes, hearing requires ears. But thinking, Aristotle suggests, does not seem to require a specific organ in the same way. And if intellect lacks a specific material organ, perhaps it is not subject to the same mortality that afflicts the rest of the soul.

**The Problem of Thought**

Aristotle's account of thought in *De Anima* Book III is one of the most difficult and disputed passages in all of ancient philosophy. He begins with a structural analogy to perception. Just as sense receives the forms of sensible objects without their matter, so intellect receives the forms of intelligible objects without their matter. The intellect thinking a circle does not thereby become circular — it takes on the form of circularity in a purely intellectual manner.

This means that intellect must itself be, in a sense, formless. If thought receives forms, it cannot itself be any particular form; it must be like a blank tablet before anything is written on it. Aristotle explicitly says that before it thinks, intellect is nothing *in actuality* — it is pure potentiality for thought.

**Active and Passive Intellect**

Here Aristotle introduces his most enigmatic and contested distinction: the difference between the *passive intellect* (*nous pathetikos*) and the *active intellect* (*nous poietikos*). The terminology does not actually appear in Aristotle's text; it comes from later interpreters, but it reflects a genuine distinction Aristotle makes.

The passive intellect is the potentiality for thought — the capacity to think any intelligible object. Like matter in general, it can take on any form.

The active intellect is described by Aristotle in highly metaphorical language. It is like light, which actualizes colors that are only potentially visible in darkness. It "makes" intelligible what is otherwise only potentially intelligible — it brings the universal forms latent in sensory experience to actual intellectual apprehension.

The active intellect, Aristotle says, is "separable, unmixed, and impassive, being in its essential nature activity." It is immortal and eternal. "This alone is immortal and eternal."

**What Is the Active Intellect?**

The interpretation of the active intellect has been one of the great contested questions in the history of philosophy. Three main readings have been proposed:

1. **The cosmic or divine intellect**: The active intellect is not part of the individual human soul at all but is the divine intellect — Aristotle's *nous* — that illuminates the intelligible realm for all thinkers. On this reading, individual human thought is always dependent on a transcendent rational principle. This was the reading of the Islamic philosopher Averroes (Ibn Rushd), who held that there is a single, shared active intellect for all humanity.

2. **Part of the individual soul**: The active intellect is a part of the individual human soul — the part that can separate from the body and survive death. This was the reading preferred by Alexander of Aphrodisias and many later commentators. It makes Aristotle more like Plato: individual immortality is secured for the rational part of the soul.

3. **God or divine thought**: The active intellect is identified with the unmoved mover, the self-thinking thought that Aristotle describes in the *Metaphysics*. On this reading, human thought is in some way a participation in divine thought, though Aristotle is unclear on exactly how.

Each reading creates different puzzles, and none is entirely satisfactory given the text.

**Why the Active Intellect Matters**

The puzzle of the active intellect is not merely historical. It points to a genuine difficulty in any naturalistic account of mind: how do we account for the capacity to grasp universal, necessary truths on the basis of finite, contingent sensory experience?

Aristotle's answer appeals to an active intellectual power that somehow "illuminates" the intelligible content latent in experience. Contemporary cognitive scientists who invoke innate learning mechanisms, domain-specific modules, or rational intuitions are working on analogous problems. The "active intellect" is a placeholder for whatever it is in the structure of the mind that makes the mind's engagement with reality more than mere passive recording of sensory inputs.

The medieval debate about the active intellect — involving Avicenna, Averroes, Aquinas, and others — is not just a scholastic curiosity. It is a serious engagement with the question of whether rational cognition can be fully explained within a natural, empirical framework, or whether it requires an appeal to something beyond individual psychology.
