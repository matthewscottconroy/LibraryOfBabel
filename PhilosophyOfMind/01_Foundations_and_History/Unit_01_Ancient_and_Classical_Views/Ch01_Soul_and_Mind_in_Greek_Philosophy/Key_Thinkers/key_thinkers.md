# Key Thinkers: Soul and Mind in Greek Philosophy

---

**Plato (428–348 BCE)**
Athenian philosopher and founder of the Academy. Plato advanced a sharp dualism between the immortal, rational soul and the mortal body; the soul's true nature is revealed through philosophical reasoning that turns away from sensory appearance toward the Forms. He divided the soul into reason, spirit, and appetite, each associated with a class of desires and virtues.
*Key works:* *Phaedo*, *Republic*, *Timaeus*, *Phaedrus*

---

**Aristotle (384–322 BCE)**
Student of Plato and founder of the Lyceum. Aristotle rejected Platonic dualism in favor of hylomorphism: the soul is the *form* of a living body — its organizational principle and functional essence — not a separate substance. He distinguished nutritive, sensitive, and rational souls corresponding to the capacities of plants, animals, and humans respectively, and analyzed perception, imagination, and nous (intellect) with unprecedented precision.
*Key works:* *De Anima*, *Nicomachean Ethics*, *On Memory and Recollection*, *On the Parts of Animals*

---

**Socrates (469–399 BCE)**
As reconstructed through Plato's dialogues, Socrates took philosophical self-examination — "knowing thyself" — as the essential human task. He consistently argued for the priority of the soul over the body and for the connection between knowledge, virtue, and the soul's proper care. Whether the historical Socrates held Plato's metaphysical commitments remains debated.
*Key works:* Accessible primarily through Plato's early dialogues: *Apology*, *Meno*, *Protagoras*

---

**Heraclitus (c. 535–475 BCE)**
Pre-Socratic thinker who identified the *logos* (rational principle) as governing all things and connected the deep soul to wisdom. His fragments influenced later accounts of nous (intellect) and the relationship between individual minds and a universal rational structure.
*Key works:* Fragments (collected in various editions of the Pre-Socratics)
