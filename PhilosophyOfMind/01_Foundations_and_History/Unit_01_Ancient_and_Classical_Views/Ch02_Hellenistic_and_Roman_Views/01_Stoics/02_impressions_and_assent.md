# Impressions, Assent, and the Problem of Action

The Stoic theory of action is built around a three-stage model: impression, assent, and impulse. To understand why you do what you do — and why you sometimes do things you know you shouldn't — requires understanding all three stages and how they interrelate. This model remains one of the most compelling accounts of rational agency ever developed.

**Impressions (Phantasiai)**

Everything begins with an impression. An impression is a modification of the soul caused by something — by a perceived object, a memory, an imaginative construction, or a proposition that presents itself for consideration. The Stoics describe impressions as "movements of the soul's commanding part."

Impressions come in different kinds. Some are sensory, arising from perception. Some are memory-based — recurrences of past perceptions. Some are imaginative — combinations of remembered elements. And some are propositional — the kind that present themselves as the content of a possible judgment.

The impression presents its content in a certain way: it seems to say "it is the case that p" or "x is approaching." But the impression merely presents; it does not by itself determine what the soul does in response.

**Assent (Synkatathesis)**

Assent is the soul's response to an impression — its endorsement or rejection of what the impression presents. Crucially, assent is *up to us*. We cannot fully control what impressions arise, but we can control whether we assent to them.

This is the Stoic basis for freedom and moral responsibility. When you assent to the impression that some external good is genuinely important, you are making a cognitive commitment that will govern your subsequent behavior. The error lies not in having the impression but in assenting to it without adequate scrutiny.

For the Stoics, the capacity to withhold assent is the distinctive mark of rational agency. Animals are carried along by their impressions; they lack the reflective distance needed to examine and evaluate what presents itself. Humans, by contrast, can step back, assess, and decide whether to endorse what appears. This is the core of Stoic freedom: not freedom from causal determination in general, but the freedom of rational self-determination that comes from exercising the capacity for critical assent.

**Impulse (Hormê)**

Once assent is given, an impulse arises. An impulse is the soul's movement toward or away from something — the motivational upshot of assent. If you assent to the impression that crossing the street now is safe and appropriate, the corresponding impulse moves you toward the crossing. If you assent to the impression that the dog is dangerous, the impulse moves you away.

Impulse is thus always *post-cognitive*: it is downstream of assent. This is why the Stoics say that virtue is sufficient for happy action: if you have correct judgments (give assent to the right things), your impulses will be appropriate, and your actions will be virtuous.

**The Problem of Akrasia**

A persistent problem for any rationalist psychology is the phenomenon of *akrasia* — weakness of will, or acting against one's better judgment. You know it is bad to eat too much, but you eat too much anyway. You know you should get out of bed, but you lie there. How is this possible on the Stoic model?

The Stoics have an answer, though it is contested. They deny that genuine akrasia — in the full Aristotelian sense of doing what you sincerely judge to be wrong — is possible. What we call weakness of will is better understood as a kind of *rapid alternation* of assent: you assent to "exercising today is good," then immediately assent to the contrary impression "but rest feels good and I deserve it." The appearance of conflict is real, but what happens is a succession of judgments, not a single judgment simultaneously endorsed and violated.

This deflationary response is interesting but arguably misses something. The phenomenology of weakness of will — the sense of being dragged along by an impulse that you are simultaneously judging to be the wrong one — seems harder to explain away than the Stoics suggest. Aristotle's alternative account, which allows for a kind of perception-based desire that can override the deliverances of deliberative reason, may be more phenomenologically adequate.

**Stoic Practice: The Exercise of Attention (Prosoche)**

The practical implication of the Stoic model is that wisdom requires constant vigilance about one's own impressions and assents. The Stoic practice of *prosoche* — attention to one's own cognitive states — involves monitoring what impressions arise and carefully examining them before assenting. It is, in effect, a discipline of metacognition.

Modern mindfulness practice has been compared to Stoic prosoche, though the theoretical frameworks differ. Both involve a trained capacity to notice one's own mental states without being immediately carried along by them. The Stoic version is more cognitively demanding: it involves not just noticing but actively evaluating and re-evaluating the judgments implicit in one's impressions.

The Stoic model of action — impression, assent, impulse — remains one of the most carefully articulated accounts of rational agency in the philosophical tradition. Its influence on later philosophy is immense, and its core claim that the exercise of rational agency consists in the disciplined management of assent continues to find defenders.
