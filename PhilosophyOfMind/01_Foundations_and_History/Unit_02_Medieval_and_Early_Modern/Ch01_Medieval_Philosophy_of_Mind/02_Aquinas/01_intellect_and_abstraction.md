# Aquinas: Intellect, Abstraction, and the Immaterial Soul

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) produced the most sustained and systematic philosophy of mind of the medieval period, drawing together Aristotelian hylomorphism, Augustinian Platonism, and Islamic Aristotelianism into a rigorously argued synthesis. His account of the intellect and its relationship to the body remains philosophically important, not least because it represents a kind of "middle path" between dualism and full-blown materialism.

**The Soul as Form of the Body**

Aquinas follows Aristotle in defining the soul as the form of a living body. The soul is not a substance separate from the body but the organizing principle that makes matter into a specific kind of living thing. Human beings are composites of matter and form; neither the body alone nor the soul alone constitutes the complete person.

This distinguishes Aquinas sharply from Plato and from Cartesian dualism. The soul is not imprisoned in the body; it is the form of the body, and the body is genuinely part of what the human being is. Aquinas takes resurrection of the body seriously as a theological doctrine partly because he takes seriously the philosophical claim that persons are embodied beings: a disembodied soul is not a complete human person.

**Two Intellects**

Following the Aristotelian tradition, Aquinas distinguishes between the *agent intellect* (*intellectus agens*) and the *possible* or *receptive intellect* (*intellectus possibilis*). This distinction is central to his account of how we come to know universals.

Sensory experience gives us particular objects: this dog, that triangle. But intellectual knowledge is of universals: dog-in-general, triangularity as such. How do we get from the particulars of sensory experience to the universals of intellectual knowledge?

Aquinas's answer involves a process called *abstraction*. The agent intellect "illuminates" the sensory *phantasms* (images) received through the senses, stripping away the particular, material features to extract the intelligible form. The possible intellect then receives this abstract form and knows the universal.

This process is thoroughly natural — it is how the human cognitive system works — but the resulting knowledge is of something genuinely universal and immaterial. The form of dog, as known by the intellect, has no particular color or size; it captures what all dogs share.

**Why the Intellect Must Be Immaterial**

Aquinas argues that the intellect itself must be immaterial. The argument draws on the hylomorphic analysis of cognition: like perceives like. To know a form, the knower must *become* (in a cognitive sense) that form. If the intellect were material — if it were constituted by some particular kind of matter with its own specific form — it could not "become" every form it encounters. It would be like trying to see all colors with an eye that was already dyed red.

The intellect is therefore not the act of any bodily organ. It has no organ of its own in the way that sight has the eye and hearing has the ear. It is the form of the body only *incidentally*, not because it is directly actualized in any particular part of the body.

This argument is controversial. Critics point out that we know the brain is involved in thinking, and the brain is a specific material organ. Aquinas would respond that the brain is involved in producing the phantasms that the intellect operates on, but the intellectual operation itself is immaterial. Whether this distinction is sustainable in light of contemporary neuroscience is a live question.

**Individuation and the Problem of Multiple Intellects**

Aquinas faced the challenge of Averroes (Ibn Rushd), who had argued that the active intellect is a single, shared, cosmic intellect rather than something possessed by each individual. On Averroes's reading, individual humans participate in intellect but do not each have their own; this would seem to make individual immortality impossible.

Aquinas's response involves a sophisticated account of how the soul is individuated. Although intellectual operations are immaterial, the intellectual soul is *individuated* by its relationship to the particular human body it informs. Even after death — even after separation from the body — the soul retains a kind of orientation toward the particular body it once animated, and this provides the basis for the resurrection account.

**Legacy**

Aquinas's philosophy of mind is important for several reasons beyond its historical influence. His attempt to hold together the materiality of the person (as embodied) with the immateriality of intellectual cognition (as abstractive) represents a position that many contemporary philosophers find attractive: a non-Cartesian account of mind-body unity combined with a defense of the distinctive character of conceptual thought.

Philosophers like John Haldane and Peter Geach have argued for neo-Aristotelian accounts of mind that are closer to Aquinas than to any mainstream contemporary position. The question of whether there is something special about intellectual cognition — whether it genuinely "becomes" its objects in a way that mere physical processes do not — is at the heart of ongoing debates between representationalist and non-representationalist accounts of mind.
