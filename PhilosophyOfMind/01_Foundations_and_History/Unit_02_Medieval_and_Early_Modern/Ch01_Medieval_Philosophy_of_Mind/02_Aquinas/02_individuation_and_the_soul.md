# Individuation and the Soul

One of the most technically demanding problems in medieval philosophy of mind concerns individuation: what makes one soul distinct from another? If souls are immaterial forms, and if forms are universals, then it seems difficult to explain how one person's soul is numerically distinct from another's. Aquinas's treatment of this problem is philosophically sophisticated and connects to deep questions about personal identity.

**The Problem**

In Aristotle's hylomorphism, matter is the principle of individuation. Two bronze statues with the same form — two Hermes statues cast from the same mold — are numerically distinct because they are made of different parcels of bronze. Matter differentiates; form unifies.

But if souls are the forms of bodies, and if (as Aquinas argues) the intellectual soul has a kind of immateriality to it, then what individuates souls? Matter cannot do the job for an immaterial soul. But if souls are not individuated by matter, are there really multiple individual souls at all, or is there just one intellect shared by all humanity (the Averroist view)?

**Aquinas's Solution**

Aquinas accepts that the principle of individuation for material things is "designated matter" — matter understood as occupying a particular position in space and time. Two tokens of the same type are two because they are made of different, spatiotemporally distinct parcels of matter.

For the human soul, Aquinas takes an unusual position. The soul is individuated *by its relationship to the body it informs*. Each soul is ordered toward a particular, individual body — it is the form of *this* body, and this orientation is not merely accidental but is built into the soul's nature.

This is not quite the same as saying matter individuates the soul, because the soul is not material. But the soul's individuation is mediated through matter: it is individual because it is the form of a materially individuated body.

After death, when the soul is separated from the body, it retains its individual character. Aquinas says the soul "remains ordered" to the body it once informed, even in separation. This ordering preserves numerical individuality: my soul after death is still *my* soul, not just soul-in-general, because it retains the orientation toward my body.

**The Haecceity Alternative**

The Franciscan philosopher John Duns Scotus (1265/66–1308) offered a different account of individuation that has been very influential. Scotus holds that besides the common nature shared by members of a species, each individual has a *haecceity* — "thisness" — that makes it the particular individual it is. Haecceity is not a further property that something has; it is the principle of numerical individuality as such.

For Scotus, the soul's individuation does not depend on its relationship to matter. Each soul has its own haecceity that makes it the particular soul it is. This makes Scotus more comfortable than Aquinas with the idea of a fully individual immaterial soul, and his account has found defenders among contemporary philosophers who want to ground individuality in something other than matter or spatiotemporal location.

**Connections to Personal Identity**

The medieval debate about individuation connects directly to modern debates about personal identity. What makes you the same person you were ten years ago? What would it take for you to survive the destruction of your body?

Aquinas's view implies that personal identity through time requires some continuity of the soul, understood as a form with a determinate orientation toward a particular body. The Thomistic account of resurrection is not just the "reassembly" of the same atoms — it is the reunification of the same soul with a glorified body, where "same soul" is defined by the soul's intrinsic orientation toward the individual.

This differs significantly from the Lockean account of personal identity, which grounds identity in psychological continuity (memory, personality) rather than in the metaphysical persistence of a soul. The two accounts make different predictions about fission cases, about persons with radical amnesia, and about the moral significance of biological continuity.

**The Significance of Individuation**

Why does individuation matter for philosophy of mind? Because the answer constrains what you can say about survival, about moral responsibility, and about the unity of the self.

If persons are individuated by their bodies, then drastic changes to the body — gradual neuron replacement, progressive dementia — raise genuine questions about whether the same person persists. If persons are individuated by psychological continuity, then a person can persist even through radical bodily change, as long as the right psychological connections are maintained. And if persons are individuated by haecceity or some metaphysical thisness, then personal identity might be more robust than either bodily or psychological accounts suggest.

These are not merely medieval concerns. They are live issues in contemporary ethics, in debates about end-of-life decision-making, and in the philosophical analysis of what matters in survival.
