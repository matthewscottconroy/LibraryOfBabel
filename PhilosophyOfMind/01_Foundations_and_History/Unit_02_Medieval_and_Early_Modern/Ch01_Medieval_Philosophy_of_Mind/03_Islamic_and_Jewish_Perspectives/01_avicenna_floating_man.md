# Avicenna's Floating Man

Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 980–1037 CE) was the greatest philosopher-physician of the medieval Islamic world, and his contributions to philosophy of mind are remarkable for their depth and originality. His "Floating Man" thought experiment is one of the most penetrating philosophical arguments ever made for the distinctness of the soul from the body — and it anticipates Descartes's *cogito* by six centuries.

**The Thought Experiment**

Imagine that you are created fully formed and rational — but floating in space, deprived of all sensory input. Your eyes are covered, your limbs are spread so that they touch nothing, there is no proprioceptive feedback from your body. You have no sensory experience whatsoever.

Avicenna's claim is that even in this situation, you would still be aware of yourself as existing. You would know that *you* exist. This self-awareness would be present even in the total absence of any sensory or bodily input.

The conclusion Avicenna draws: the soul — the thinking self — is not the body, and self-awareness does not depend on bodily sensation. The self that one is aware of in this extreme deprivation is the soul, and the soul is something distinct from the body.

**Structure of the Argument**

The argument can be formalized:

1. If you were created floating with no sensory experience, you would still be aware of yourself.
2. The thing you would be aware of in that situation cannot be any bodily thing, since all bodily input is removed.
3. Therefore, the self of which you are aware is not bodily.
4. Therefore, the soul (the thinking self) is distinct from the body.

This is a precursor to Descartes's argument from doubt: by stripping away everything that might be doubted, Descartes reaches the indubitability of the thinking self. Avicenna strips away everything external and sensory and reaches the same indubitability of the thinking self.

**Differences from the Cartesian Cogito**

Despite the structural similarity, there are important differences. Descartes's argument is about indubitability: the thinking self cannot be doubted, because any doubting presupposes a doubter. Avicenna's argument is about content: what you would be aware of in extreme deprivation is an existence without bodily content.

Avicenna does not go as far as Descartes in treating the soul as a substance whose essential property is thought. For Avicenna, the soul is the form of the body in the Aristotelian sense, but it is also separable and, in its highest functions, independent of the body. He holds a kind of interactive dualism that is philosophically more nuanced than simple Cartesian substance dualism.

**Avicenna on the Faculties of the Soul**

Avicenna developed Aristotle's faculty psychology in considerable detail, adding to it:

- **The internal senses**: in addition to the five external senses, Avicenna posited a set of internal cognitive powers. These include *common sense* (which integrates inputs from the external senses), *fantasy* (which stores sensory forms), *estimation* (*wahm*, which perceives non-sensory intentions — the wolf's hostility, the friend's trustworthiness), *imagination* (which combines and separates stored forms), and *memory* (which stores the outputs of estimation).

The faculty of *wahm* (estimation) is philosophically significant. It is the power by which we perceive not sensory qualities but what Avicenna calls "intentions" — meaning-laden features of objects that are not directly sensible. The sheep that sees the wolf perceives not just a certain shape and color but something threatening, something to be fled. This evaluative, affective perception requires a faculty beyond mere sensory reception.

**The Active Intellect**

Following the Aristotelian tradition as mediated through Al-Farabi, Avicenna holds that human intellects require the illumination of a separate, cosmic Active Intellect (*intellectus agens*) — identified with Gabriel in his theological writings — to actualize their potential for knowledge. The human intellect is essentially a receiver; abstraction and intellectual illumination come from without, from a cosmic rational principle.

This is controversial. Does it mean that human intellect is merely passive? Avicenna's answer is that the human intellect cooperates with the illumination from the Active Intellect: the preparation of the mind through experience, imagination, and the internal senses creates the readiness to receive intellectual forms. But the actual intellectual apprehension of universals requires an external illuminating cause.

**Legacy**

The Floating Man thought experiment was studied carefully in the Latin West, where it influenced Aquinas and, through intermediaries, Descartes. It represents one of the most powerful arguments in the history of philosophy for taking the first-person perspective as philosophically primary — for the claim that understanding the mind requires attending to how things appear from the inside, not just to how they look from an external, third-person standpoint.
