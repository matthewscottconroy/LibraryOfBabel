# Averroes and the Unity of the Intellect

Ibn Rushd (Averroes, 1126–1198) was the greatest medieval commentator on Aristotle — so authoritative that in the Latin West he was simply called "The Commentator." His reading of Aristotle's doctrine of the intellect produced one of the most controversial positions in medieval philosophy: the doctrine that there is a single shared intellect for all of humanity.

**The Problem**

In *De Anima* Book III, Aristotle discusses the intellect in terms that are notoriously obscure. The "possible intellect" (the intellect as receptive to intelligible forms) must be uncontaminated by any specific form in order to receive all forms. The "active intellect" makes the intelligible objects in sensory experience actually intelligible for the possible intellect.

Aristotle says the active intellect is "separable, unmixed, and impassive, being in its essential nature activity" and that it "does not sometimes think and sometimes not." This suggests it is always thinking, always active — which would be very strange for an individual human mind, which clearly sometimes thinks and sometimes doesn't.

**Averroes's Solution**

Averroes argues that both the active intellect *and* the possible intellect are substances separate from individual human beings. There is one possible intellect and one active intellect for all of humanity. Individual human beings access the intellect but do not each have their own.

On this reading, when I think about mathematics or philosophy, I am participating in a single, shared intellectual substance — not deploying a private cognitive faculty. The images (*phantasms*) in my individual mind provide the particular starting-point for intellectual cognition, but the intellectual cognition itself takes place in the shared, separate intellect.

**Implications**

This position has several striking implications:

1. **No individual immortality**: If there is only one intellect, then individual souls do not survive death as distinct individuals. There is no "your intellect" that persists; there is just the intellect, which is eternal.

2. **No individual responsibility?**: Critics worried that the monopsychist view undermined individual moral responsibility. If my thinking is actually done by a shared intellect, am I the author of my thoughts?

3. **A cosmic picture of mind**: The intellect, on Averroes's view, is more like a cosmic feature of reality than a property of individual minds. This has affinities with Platonic accounts of the Forms and with later accounts of objective reason as something supra-individual.

**Aquinas's Critique**

Aquinas wrote an entire treatise — *De Unitate Intellectus* — refuting the monopsychist position. His objection is essentially phenomenological: each of us experiences thinking from the first person; it is *I* who think, *I* who understand this proposition. If the intellect were a separate, shared substance, then the connection between my understanding and me would be merely accidental — like a ship being moved by a sailor, where the sailor's activity is not truly the sailor's *personal* activity.

Aquinas's point is that the first-person character of cognition — the fact that it is *my* thinking, *my* understanding — cannot be accounted for if the intellect is a substance entirely separate from the individual human being.

**Monopsychism and Contemporary Philosophy**

Averroes's monopsychism is not merely an historical curiosity. Several contemporary positions have structural similarities:

- The view that reasons, norms, and logical laws are objective, supra-individual entities that we "access" rather than create has some Averroist resonance. When John McDowell speaks of the "space of reasons" as something that human beings are initiated into through language and education, he is describing cognition as participation in something that transcends any individual.

- Some accounts of collective intelligence, distributed cognition, and group knowledge suggest that there are genuinely cognitive phenomena at the level of groups that cannot be reduced to individual cognitive states.

- Hegel's account of *Geist* — spirit as a collective, historical, self-developing rational reality — can be read as a radically transformed version of the Averroist idea that intellect is fundamentally supra-individual.

Whether any of these contemporary analogues are genuinely continuous with Averroes's position is a matter of interpretation. But the underlying question — whether rational cognition is irreducibly individual or whether it essentially involves participation in something supra-individual — is a genuine and important one.
