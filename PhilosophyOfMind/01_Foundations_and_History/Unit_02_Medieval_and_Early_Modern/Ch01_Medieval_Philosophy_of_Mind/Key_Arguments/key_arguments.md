# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Medieval Philosophy of Mind

## Key Arguments

**Aquinas's Argument That the Soul Is the Form of the Body**
Following Aristotle, Aquinas argues that the soul is the substantial form of the body—what makes a human being a human being rather than a mere pile of matter. Yet Aquinas also maintains that the intellective soul is subsistent (capable of existing without the body) because intellectual operations do not use a bodily organ in the way that sensation does. This dual claim—soul as form and as subsistent—represents Aquinas's synthesis of Aristotelian hylomorphism with Christian immortality doctrine.

**Aquinas on Agent and Possible Intellect**
Aquinas argues that human cognition requires two distinct intellectual powers: the agent intellect, which abstracts intelligible species (universal concepts) from sensory phantasms, and the possible intellect, which receives and retains those species. The debate over whether these intellects are individual or shared (as Averroes held) had major implications for personal immortality and individual cognitive identity. Aquinas insisted on individual intellects to preserve personal responsibility and resurrection.

**Averroes's Monopsychism and Its Rejection**
Averroes argued that the material (possible) intellect is a single, separate substance shared by all human beings, not individuated in each person. Aquinas contested this vigorously, arguing that if one intellect thinks in all persons, it would follow that when one person understands, all persons understand—which is clearly false. The debate crystallizes the problem of how general concepts can be possessed by individual minds, anticipating debates about concept individuation and mental content.

**Augustine's Argument from Memory to the Soul's Immateriality**
Augustine argues in the Confessions and De Trinitate that the soul's capacity to retain vast and non-spatial contents in memory—including mathematical truths, past experiences, and even the memory of forgetting—cannot be explained by any material or spatial container. The mind transcends space in its self-reflexive awareness; "I remember that I have remembered." This prefigures later arguments from the unity and self-intimacy of consciousness against physicalism.

**The Universals Debate: Realism vs. Nominalism**
Realists (following Plato and Anselm) held that universals such as "humanity" exist independently of particular humans; nominalists (Roscelin, later Ockham) held that only particulars exist and universals are merely names or concepts. The debate directly concerns how the intellect can cognize general truths and whether cognition puts the mind in contact with a mind-independent structure of reality. This controversy shaped subsequent debates about the nature of concepts and the relationship between language and thought.

## Core Concepts

**Intellective Soul (Aquinas)**
For Aquinas, the intellective soul is the highest of three types of soul (vegetative, sensitive, intellective), and it is the formal principle of the human being as a whole. Unlike vegetative and sensitive functions, which require bodily organs, the intellective soul's operations—abstraction and rational judgment—are intrinsically immaterial, enabling the soul to survive bodily death. This account attempts to reconcile Aristotelian functionalism about most mental operations with the Christian doctrine of personal immortality.

**Intelligible Species**
In scholastic epistemology, an intelligible species is the immaterial likeness or representation of a universal form that the agent intellect abstracts from sensory input and by which the possible intellect thinks. The species is not itself the object of thought but the medium through which the intellect grasps universal natures. Debates about whether we think "through" species (direct realism) or "at" species (representationalism) anticipate early modern disputes about ideas and direct vs. indirect perception.

**Illumination (Augustine)**
Augustine argues that human knowledge of eternal truths (mathematical and moral) requires divine illumination: God as the source of eternal truth somehow illuminates the intellect, enabling it to recognize necessary truths it could not derive from contingent sensory experience alone. This doctrine blends Platonic reminiscence with Christian theology and raises epistemological questions about the relationship between natural cognitive faculties and supernatural assistance.

**Intentional Inexistence (Scholastic)**
The scholastic concept of intentional being (esse intentionale) holds that when the intellect thinks of a horse, the form of the horse exists "in" the intellect in a special, diminished mode—not as a real horse but as an object of thought. This concept of intentional inexistence was revived by Brentano in the nineteenth century and became central to phenomenological accounts of mental content. It addresses how mental states can be about objects that may not exist.

**Memory (Augustine)**
Augustine's account in the Confessions treats memory not merely as a storehouse of past experiences but as a vast interior space where God can be sought: "I shall mount beyond this power of my nature also, still rising by degrees towards Him who made me." Memory contains not just images of past perceptions but also mathematical truths, the soul's own states, and even a kind of "memory of the present." This rich conception of memory as self-knowledge prefigures phenomenological accounts of inner time-consciousness.

## Thought Experiments

**Avicenna's Floating Man**
Avicenna asks us to imagine a person created fully formed but floating in a void, deprived of all sensory input—including sensations of their own body. This person would still be aware of their own existence and would affirm "I exist." The argument is designed to show that self-awareness does not depend on bodily or sensory experience, and thus that the soul is something the intellect knows directly and independently of matter. It anticipates Descartes' cogito and is a precursor to thought experiments about pure self-awareness.

**Anselm's Ontological Argument (Mind and Existence)**
Anselm argues that God, defined as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived," must exist in reality and not only in the understanding, because a being that existed only in the understanding would be surpassed by one that also existed in reality. While primarily a theological argument, it raises deep questions about the relationship between mental representation and existence, and about what the mind can establish through conceptual analysis alone—questions directly relevant to debates about a priori knowledge and mental content.

**The Divided Line and Degrees of Cognition (Boethius, after Plato)**
Boethius, in the Consolation of Philosophy, presents a hierarchy of cognitive faculties—sensation, imagination, reason, and intelligence—each grasping a higher level of reality. Lower faculties cannot grasp what higher ones apprehend, yet a human being possesses all levels. This thought experiment about the limits of different cognitive faculties raises questions about the unity of mind across levels of cognition and about whether rational and sensory modes of awareness are genuinely continuous or fundamentally different.
