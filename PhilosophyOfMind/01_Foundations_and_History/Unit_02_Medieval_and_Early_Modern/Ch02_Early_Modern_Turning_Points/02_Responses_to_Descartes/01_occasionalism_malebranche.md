# Occasionalism: Malebranche and the Problem of Interaction

Nicolas Malebranche (1638–1715), the most systematic of the Cartesian thinkers, took seriously what Descartes's critics had noticed: if mind and body are genuinely distinct substances, there is no intelligible causal connection between them. Malebranche's solution was radical: there is no genuine causation between finite things at all. Only God causes; finite events are merely occasions on which God acts.

**The Critique of Finite Causation**

Malebranche's argument begins with an analysis of what it would mean for one finite thing to cause another. For A to cause B, A would have to have the power to produce B necessarily. But when we examine finite things — minds and bodies alike — we find no such necessary connections. We see minds and bodies correlated, but we do not see anything in the mind that necessitates bodily movement, nor anything in bodily impact that necessitates mental sensation.

The only being we can conceive as having genuine causal power is God — an omnipotent being whose will necessarily brings about what it wills. All apparent causation between finite things is really God's action on the occasion of those things.

**Occasionalism Applied to Mind-Body**

Applied to mind-body interaction, occasionalism delivers this result: when I form the intention to move my arm, this mental event is the occasion on which God produces the corresponding bodily movement. When my hand contacts the hot stove, this physical event is the occasion on which God produces the sensation of pain in my mind.

There is no genuine causal connection between mental and physical events. God is the perpetual intermediary, continuously coordinating the two series so that they match up.

**The Theological Cost**

Occasionalism is philosophically elegant in one respect: it completely dissolves the interaction problem. If God coordinates mind and body, there is no puzzle about how two radically different substances could interact — they never do interact directly; God does all the real causal work.

But the theological cost is high. Malebranche is committed to a form of universal divine occasionalism: every event in nature, not just mind-body interactions, is directly caused by God. The "laws of nature" are not autonomous regularities but God's habitual way of acting. This picture makes the universe entirely dependent on continuous divine action, and it leaves no room for secondary causes of any kind.

**Malebranche's Epistemology: Vision in God**

Malebranche's metaphysics of mind extends beyond the interaction problem. He holds that we know things not by having mental images or representations that somehow resemble external objects, but by seeing things *in God*. The ideas in God's mind are the archetypes of all finite things; we perceive these divine ideas directly.

This "vision in God" doctrine is Malebranche's response to the problem of intentionality: how do our mental states manage to be *about* external things? For Malebranche, they are about external things because they are, strictly speaking, about the divine ideas that God has of those things — and those divine ideas are identical to the things themselves, in the sense of being what makes the things what they are.

**Influence and Legacy**

Malebranche was enormously influential on Berkeley, who adapted occasionalism in a radical direction: if all we know are ideas, and if ideas only exist in minds, then perhaps there is no material substance at all. Berkeley's idealism is in part a radicalization of the occasionalist insight that finite causal relations are philosophically mysterious.

Leibniz also responded to Malebranche's occasionalism, proposing his own alternative: pre-established harmony. Rather than continuous divine intervention, Leibniz posited that God pre-programmed every monad at creation to unfold in perfect coordination with every other monad. Mind and body do not interact (even via God's constant intervention); they simply run in perfect parallel, like two clocks set to the same time.

Both occasionalism and pre-established harmony reveal the depth of the problem created by Cartesian dualism. When substance dualism creates an interaction problem that can only be solved by making God the intermediary between everything, it is a sign that something has gone wrong at a fundamental level.
