# Spinoza: Parallelism and Attribute Dualism

Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677) offered the most radical response to Cartesian dualism: instead of two substances, there is only one — a single infinite substance that Spinoza calls "God or Nature" (*Deus sive Natura*). Mind and body are not two substances but two attributes of this one substance: two different ways of expressing or describing the same underlying reality.

**One Substance, Infinite Attributes**

Spinoza's *Ethics* (published posthumously in 1677) builds its metaphysics from definitions and axioms in the manner of Euclidean geometry. He defines substance as "that which is in itself and is conceived through itself" — something whose concept requires no other concept to explain it.

Spinoza argues that there can only be one such substance. Multiple substances would have to be distinguished by their attributes, and distinguishable substances would have to be able to limit or affect each other — but if they could, neither would be truly "in itself." Therefore, there is only one substance: infinite, eternal, self-causing.

This substance has infinitely many attributes — infinitely many ways of being expressed — of which human beings can know only two: *thought* and *extension*. Mind and body are, respectively, the mode of the one substance as described under the attribute of thought and the mode of the one substance as described under the attribute of extension.

**Parallelism**

The key doctrine for philosophy of mind is what Spinoza calls the "identity of the order and connection of ideas and the order and connection of things." Mental events and physical events are parallel: every mental event corresponds to a physical event, and vice versa. But they do not interact — indeed, they cannot. Being different attributes of the same substance, they are incommensurable: an idea and an extension cannot stand in causal relations to each other.

This makes Spinoza a kind of dual-aspect theorist: one and the same event can be described as a mental event (under the attribute of thought) or a physical event (under the attribute of extension). The descriptions are genuinely different and irreducible to each other, but they describe the same thing.

**The Identity of Mind and Body**

Spinoza's position is not quite the same as property dualism or the dual-aspect theory that will later be associated with Fechner or with Nagel. Spinoza does not say that physical events have mental properties in addition to physical ones. He says that the physical and the mental are two attributes of a single substance — they are two complete descriptions of the same reality, not two layers of properties in a single thing.

The human mind, for Spinoza, is "the idea of the human body." The mind just is the body as conceived under the attribute of thought. This is a strong identity claim: there is not the body and then, separately, the mind. There is the body/mind, which is one thing with two adequate descriptions.

**Emotions, Freedom, and Bondage**

Parts III and IV of the *Ethics* apply this metaphysics to psychology. Spinoza analyzes the emotions (*affectus*) as modifications of the body/mind that increase or decrease its power of acting. Passive emotions — joy when caused by external factors, sadness, fear, desire controlled by external things — constitute what Spinoza calls "bondage": being determined by external causes rather than by one's own nature.

Active emotions — those arising from adequate ideas, from the mind's understanding of itself and its causes — are expressions of freedom. The path to freedom is the path to understanding: as we come to understand ourselves and our situation clearly, our passive emotions are transformed into active ones, and we achieve a kind of intellectual love of God/Nature.

This is a striking synthesis: the metaphysics of one-substance parallelism yields a psychology of emotional liberation through understanding. There are clear affinities with Stoic psychology (freedom through correct judgment) and with later Romantic ideas about human freedom as self-understanding.

**Legacy**

Spinoza's monism has been enormously influential. The dual-aspect theory — the view that mental and physical are two aspects of a single underlying reality — is a live option in contemporary philosophy of mind, often advocated by those who find both substance dualism and standard physicalism inadequate.

His influence on Freud (the idea that unconscious emotional dynamics are as determined and intelligible as physical processes) and on 20th-century functionalists (mind as a formal structure that can be described at multiple levels) is substantial. More recently, thinkers like Antonio Damasio and Gilles Deleuze have drawn explicitly on Spinoza's psychology of affects.

The deepest resonance is with contemporary neutral monism and with attempts to understand mind-body identity without collapsing mind into matter. If mind and body are two descriptions of one reality, neither is "more real" than the other — a thought that contemporary philosophers of mind continue to find both attractive and difficult to cash out.
