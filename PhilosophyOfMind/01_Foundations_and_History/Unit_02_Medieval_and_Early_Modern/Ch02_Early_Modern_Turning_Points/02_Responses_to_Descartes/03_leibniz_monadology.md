# Leibniz: Monadology and Pre-Established Harmony

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) proposed one of the most original and striking solutions to the mind-body problem: pre-established harmony. The solution is embedded in his metaphysics of monads — a picture of reality so radically different from Cartesian mechanism that it requires a complete rethinking of what minds and bodies are.

**The Problem with Cartesian Interaction**

Leibniz shared with Malebranche the conviction that Cartesian interactionism was unintelligible. How can an unextended, non-spatial thinking substance causally affect an extended, spatial substance? The notion seems incoherent. But Leibniz rejected Malebranche's occasionalism too: to say that God constantly intervenes to produce coordination between mind and body makes the world a kind of perpetual miracle, violating the principle of sufficient reason (everything has a reason for being as it is and not otherwise).

Leibniz's alternative: the world is so arranged from the beginning that mind and body, while never causally interacting, unfold in perfect synchrony — as if two clocks were separately set to the same time.

**Monads: The True Substances**

To understand this, we need Leibniz's metaphysics of monads. Leibniz argues that there can be no real compound without real simples. Matter is infinitely divisible — any extended thing can in principle be divided further. So if there are to be any real, independent substances at all, they must be simple, without parts.

These simple substances are *monads* — from the Greek *monas*, unity. Monads are not atoms in the physical sense; they have no extension and no spatial location (strictly speaking). Each monad is a distinct center of perception and appetite.

Every monad perceives — has an internal representation of the entire universe from its particular perspective. Most of these perceptions are confused and unconscious (*petites perceptions*); it is only in beings with sufficient reflective capacity that perceptions become clear and distinct enough to constitute genuine consciousness (*apperception*).

**Pre-Established Harmony**

In creating the world, God selected the best possible set of compossible monads — monads whose internal developments would be mutually consistent and would constitute the best possible universe. Each monad is entirely self-contained ("windowless" — it receives no influences from outside itself), but its internal development perfectly mirrors what is happening in every other monad.

The human "body" is, strictly speaking, an infinitely complex collection of monads — each perceiving in confused ways — organized under the dominating monad that is the soul. The soul and body do not interact; they unfold in pre-established harmony, like musicians who have each learned their parts separately and play together without any communication during the performance.

**Petites Perceptions and Unconscious Mind**

One of Leibniz's most significant contributions to philosophy of mind is the concept of *petites perceptions* — minute, unconscious perceptions. The sound of the ocean is composed of millions of tiny sounds, each too small to be consciously perceived individually. The experience of the ocean's roar is built up from infinitely many such tiny perceptions.

This doctrine anticipates the concept of the unconscious mind by nearly two centuries. Leibniz holds that our conscious experience is the iceberg tip of a vast unconscious representational activity. The soul is always perceiving — even in dreamless sleep — but most of its perceptions are not at the level of consciousness.

This has interesting implications for the relationship between mind and matter. The difference between a stone and a human being is not that the stone is entirely mindless and the human being has a mind. It is that the stone's monadic activity consists of infinitely confused perceptions, while the human being's dominant monad has rich, clear representations. Mind, in a sense, goes all the way down.

**Leibniz's Argument Against Materialism**

Leibniz offers an influential argument against the possibility of explaining mental life materialistically. Imagine that the brain could be enlarged to the size of a mill, while preserving all its functional relationships. Walking through it, you would find only parts that push on other parts — "but never anything by which to explain a perception." Mechanical interaction can only produce more mechanical interaction; it cannot produce experience.

This "mill argument" anticipates the contemporary hard problem of consciousness. The argument is that no matter how detailed our description of the physical processes, we will never find in that description the thing that needs explaining: the phenomenal character of experience.

Whether the argument is sound is debated. Physicalists respond that the argument assumes what it needs to prove: that phenomenal experience is non-physical. But as an intuition pump, Leibniz's mill has been extremely influential, and the underlying intuition — that there is an explanatory gap between physical processes and conscious experience — drives much of contemporary philosophy of mind.
