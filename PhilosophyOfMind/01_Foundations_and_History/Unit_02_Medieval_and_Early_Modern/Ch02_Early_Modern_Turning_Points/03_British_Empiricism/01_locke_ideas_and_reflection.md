# Locke: Ideas and the Empiricist Theory of Mind

John Locke (1632–1704) set the agenda for British Empiricism with his *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689). His project was explicitly psychological: to examine the nature, origin, and limits of human understanding by inquiring into how the mind acquires its ideas. The result is one of the most systematic and influential philosophical psychologies ever written.

**The Blank Slate**

Locke begins with a famous polemic against innate ideas — the rationalist claim (associated with Descartes, Leibniz, and others) that the mind comes into the world already equipped with certain ideas or principles. Locke argues that there are no innate ideas. If there were, every human being would be aware of them — but there is no idea that is genuinely universal across all human beings and cultures.

The positive alternative is that the mind at birth is a *tabula rasa* — a blank slate. All the material of thought comes from experience. "Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas; how comes it to be furnished?"

Locke's answer: through experience, which has two sources.

**Two Sources of Ideas: Sensation and Reflection**

The two sources of experience are *sensation* (the operation of external objects on the sense organs, producing ideas of external things) and *reflection* (the mind's observation of its own operations — perceiving, thinking, doubting, believing, willing, reasoning, knowing, and so forth).

Reflection is a philosophically significant category. It means that the mind is not only outward-looking; it has access to its own operations and can form ideas of those operations. This provides the raw material for philosophy of mind itself: by reflecting on how my own mind works, I can form ideas of thinking, willing, perceiving, and so on.

This is an empiricist account of self-knowledge: I know about my mental states not by some special non-empirical faculty but by a form of inner observation analogous to outer perception.

**Simple and Complex Ideas**

Locke distinguishes *simple ideas* (which the mind cannot make or destroy, only receive from experience — redness, hardness, sweetness, pain) from *complex ideas* (which the mind actively constructs by combining, comparing, and abstracting from simple ideas). The idea of gold combines ideas of a particular color, weight, malleability, and so on. The idea of a mathematical theorem is constructed through a series of mental operations on simpler ideas.

This compositional account of complex ideas is philosophically significant: it suggests that the mind is not a passive receiver of experience but an active constructor of its conceptual repertoire. The "raw material" comes from experience; but the architecture of knowledge is built by the mind's own activity.

**Primary and Secondary Qualities**

Locke inherits from Descartes the distinction between *primary qualities* — features of physical objects that resemble the ideas they cause (extension, solidity, figure, motion, number) — and *secondary qualities* — features of objects whose ideas do not resemble anything in the objects (color, sound, taste, smell). Red is not a feature of the surface of the apple; it is a power to produce the idea of redness in normal perceivers, a power grounded in the primary-quality microstructure of the surface.

This distinction sets up a gap between the world as it is (described by primary qualities, as in Newtonian mechanics) and the world as we experience it (full of colors, smells, and flavors). The experiential world is partly mind-dependent — a product of the interaction between the world's primary-quality structure and the human sensory apparatus.

**Personal Identity**

Locke makes a critical move in philosophy of personal identity that will prove enormously influential. He distinguishes *man* (the biological human animal) from *person* (a psychological entity — a thinking, conscious being that is aware of itself as the same thinking thing across time). Personal identity, he argues, consists in continuity of consciousness — specifically, memory. I am the same person as the person who committed some act if and only if I can remember committing that act.

This psychological theory of personal identity places consciousness at the center of personhood and shifts the criteria for identity away from biological or material criteria. It has been enormously influential in contemporary debates about personal identity, personal responsibility, and the ethics of decisions made for future or past selves.

**The Lockean Legacy**

Locke's empiricism defines the basic framework within which the British tradition developed. His blank-slate nativism is still invoked in debates about the origins of knowledge; his primary-secondary quality distinction shapes contemporary discussions of perception and representation; his theory of personal identity is the starting point for all subsequent work in that area. The *Essay* is a foundational text not just in the history of philosophy but in the history of psychology and cognitive science.
