# Berkeley: Immaterialism and the Mind-Dependence of Reality

George Berkeley (1685–1753), Bishop of Cloyne, pushed the empiricist logic of Locke's account of ideas to a surprising conclusion: there is no material substance. The very notion of matter existing independently of any mind is incoherent. Everything that exists is either a mind or an idea in a mind.

**The Master Argument**

Berkeley's central argument is disarmingly simple. Try to conceive of a tree existing unperceived — to think of a sensible object existing when no mind is perceiving it. When you do this, you are conceiving of it; the object is in your mind. The attempt to conceive of something existing unperceived always ends up being a conception of something being perceived by the conceiver.

From this, Berkeley draws a more general principle: *esse est percipi* (to be is to be perceived). For sensible objects — the things we encounter through sensation — existence just is being perceived. There is no coherent notion of a sensible object existing apart from any perception of it.

**Against Lockean Primary Qualities**

Berkeley attacks Locke's distinction between primary and secondary qualities. Locke held that secondary qualities (color, taste, smell) are mind-dependent but primary qualities (extension, figure, motion) are in the objects themselves. Berkeley argues that the same considerations that show secondary qualities to be mind-dependent apply equally to primary qualities.

Size is relative to the perceiver — what seems large to one perceiver seems small to another. Motion is relative to a frame of reference. Even solidity and extension, Berkeley argues, involve tactile and visual ideas that are as much products of the mind's processing as color is. There is no principled distinction that leaves primary qualities in the object while secondary qualities are projected onto it by the mind.

**Immaterialism**

If all we can know are ideas, and ideas exist only in minds, what is the "matter" that is supposed to underlie our ideas? Locke had posited material substance as the "something I know not what" that supports the primary qualities of things. Berkeley responds: this is an incoherent notion. An unthinkable substrate for unthinkable primary qualities is literally nothing — or rather, it is a philosopher's fiction without any content.

Berkeley is not saying there are no physical objects — tables, stones, mountains exist. He is saying that their existence consists in being perceivable by minds. When he says "there is a tree in the quad," he means something like "any normal perceiver in appropriate conditions would perceive a tree if they went to the quad." Physical objects are systematic clusters of actual and possible perceptions.

**God as the Perennial Perceiver**

A famous objection: what happens to the tree in the quad when nobody is looking? On Berkeley's view, does it cease to exist? Berkeley's answer invokes God. God is an omnipresent perceiver who continuously perceives everything. Physical objects exist continuously because God is always perceiving them. The regularities and laws of nature are God's habitual ways of arranging perceptions for finite minds.

This is a fascinating position. Berkeley combines an extreme mind-idealism about physical objects with a traditional theistic metaphysics. Physical reality is constituted by divine perception, and finite minds participate in reality by having the perceptions that God causes in them.

**Berkeley on the Mind Itself**

Berkeley's philosophy raises an acute question about the status of minds. If everything that exists is a mind or idea in a mind, what is a mind? It cannot be an idea — ideas are passive, while minds are active; ideas depend on minds, while minds do not depend on other minds.

Berkeley holds that the self or mind is known not through an idea but through a "notion" — a different kind of cognitive relation. I know myself through my activity: perceiving, willing, imagining. I am not a bundle of perceptions (as Hume will later argue); I am the active subject *behind* the perceptions.

This distinction between ideas and notions maps onto the Cartesian distinction between the known objects and the knowing subject. For Berkeley, the subject — the active mind — is not itself an object of ordinary cognition. It is known by its operations, not by any direct inspection.

**Legacy**

Berkeley is often dismissed as a philosophical eccentric, but his arguments are more serious than their conclusions suggest. His demonstration that the Lockean distinction between primary and secondary qualities is unstable has been influential. His anticipation of the "phenomenalist" tradition in 20th-century philosophy — the view that physical-object statements are analyzable in terms of statements about perceptions — was significant. And his radical mind-first metaphysics has found new life in contemporary discussions of idealism as an alternative to both physicalism and dualism.
