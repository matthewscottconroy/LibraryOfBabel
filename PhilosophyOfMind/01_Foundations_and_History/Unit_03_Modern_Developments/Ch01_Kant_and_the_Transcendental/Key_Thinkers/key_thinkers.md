# Key Thinkers: Kant and the Transcendental

---

**Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)**
Prussian philosopher whose *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781/1787) transformed philosophy. For philosophy of mind, his key contributions include: (1) the transcendental unity of apperception — the "I think" that must be able to accompany all representations — as the formal condition of unified experience; (2) the distinction between phenomena (appearances) and noumena (things in themselves), which means the mind cannot know itself as it is in itself; (3) refutation of the Paralogisms — the rationalist arguments that the soul is a simple, identical, persisting substance; and (4) the schematism, which shows how abstract concepts are applied to experience through temporal forms.
*Key works:* *Critique of Pure Reason*, *Critique of Practical Reason*, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, *Anthropology from a Pragmatic Point of View*

---

**David Hume (1711–1776)**
Scottish philosopher whose radical empiricism challenged the very existence of a persisting, unified self. Hume's "bundle theory" — the self is nothing beyond a bundle of perceptions with no persisting substrate — is the foil against which Kant's transcendental philosophy is partly constructed. Hume's influence on cognitive psychology and personal identity theory remains profound.
*Key works:* *A Treatise of Human Nature*, *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*

---

**Johann Gottlieb Fichte (1762–1814)**
German idealist who radicalized Kant by making the self-positing "I" (the *Tathandlung* or self-act) the starting point of all philosophy. Fichte's account of the self as constitutively active — not a substance but an activity — influenced subsequent phenomenology and existentialism.
*Key works:* *Science of Knowledge* (*Wissenschaftslehre*), *Vocation of Man*
