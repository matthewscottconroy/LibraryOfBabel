# Brentano and Descriptive Psychology

Franz Brentano (1838–1917) is one of the most important figures in the history of philosophy of mind, though his name is less well-known than the movements he inspired. He is the teacher of Edmund Husserl and Sigmund Freud, and his central concept — intentionality — is perhaps the single most important concept in 20th-century philosophy of mind.

**The Project of Descriptive Psychology**

Brentano distinguished between *descriptive psychology* (which he sometimes called *phenomenology* or *psychognosis*) and *genetic* or *explanatory* psychology. Explanatory psychology (which was becoming a natural science under Wundt and others) asks for causal explanations of mental phenomena: what physical stimuli produce what psychological effects? Descriptive psychology asks a prior question: what are the mental phenomena that need to be explained?

Before you can explain mind, you need an accurate description of what kinds of things mental phenomena are. Brentano wanted to develop a rigorous, systematic classification of mental phenomena — a taxonomy of the forms of mental life — conducted from a first-person, introspective standpoint.

**Mental and Physical Phenomena**

Brentano's first move is to distinguish mental from physical phenomena. Physical phenomena are the objects of the external senses: colors, sounds, warmth, pressure, and spatial form. Mental phenomena are what we experience in inner perception: beliefs, desires, pleasures, judgments, love, hate.

The crucial difference is not that mental phenomena lack causal connections to the physical world — they obviously have them. The difference is that mental phenomena are the objects of *inner perception*, and inner perception, for Brentano, is infallible. When I directly perceive my own mental state, I cannot be mistaken about whether I am in that state. (This is the Cartesian transparency thesis in a new form.)

**Intentionality: The Mark of the Mental**

The most famous contribution of Brentano's *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874) is the thesis that intentionality — the "aboutness" or "directedness" of mental states — is the defining characteristic of mental phenomena.

Every mental phenomenon, Brentano argues, is directed toward an object. When I believe, there is something I believe *about*. When I fear, there is something I fear. When I love, there is an object of my love. This directedness is what Brentano, borrowing from the scholastic tradition, calls "intentional inexistence" — the object is "in" the mental act in a special way, as the target of the act's directedness.

Physical phenomena, by contrast, are not intrinsically directed at anything. The physical event of light hitting a surface does not "mean" or "refer to" anything; it is just a physical event. The directedness of mental acts is what makes the mental irreducibly different from the physical.

**The Structure of Mental Acts**

Brentano distinguished three fundamental classes of mental acts:

1. **Presentations** (*Vorstellungen*): The most basic form — simply having something "in mind," representing something. Every more complex mental act is built on a presentation.

2. **Judgments**: Mental acts that either affirm or deny the existence of their object. Belief and disbelief are judgments; they take a stance toward the existence of their object. Brentano held that judgments are specifically two-valued (affirmation or denial), not a matter of degree.

3. **Emotions** (broadly conceived, including will and desire): Mental acts that are not about whether something exists but that take a pro-attitude or con-attitude toward their object. Brentano tried to develop an objective theory of value grounded in these attitudes.

**Legacy**

Brentano's influence was enormous. Husserl took the concept of intentionality and made it the central concept of phenomenology — but radicalized it and made it the subject of a rigorous "transcendental" investigation. The entire phenomenological tradition (Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, Sartre) is inconceivable without Brentano.

The concept of intentionality also became central to analytic philosophy of mind. Questions about mental content, the nature of belief and desire, and the relationship between mental states and their objects — all of these are at bottom questions about intentionality. When contemporary philosophers debate whether intentionality is "original" (intrinsic to the mind) or "derived" (dependent on interpretation by others), they are working on problems first clearly articulated by Brentano.
