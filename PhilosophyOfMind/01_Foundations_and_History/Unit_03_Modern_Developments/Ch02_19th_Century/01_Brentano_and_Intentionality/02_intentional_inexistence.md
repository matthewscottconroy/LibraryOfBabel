# Intentional Inexistence

Brentano's doctrine of "intentional inexistence" is one of the most discussed and debated ideas in the history of philosophy of mind. Understanding what Brentano meant, why it seemed to him to be the right way to characterize mental life, and why later philosophers found it problematic, gives a sense of how contemporary questions about intentionality and mental content arose.

**The Original Claim**

Brentano writes: "Every mental phenomenon is characterized by what the Scholastics of the Middle Ages called the intentional (or mental) inexistence of an object, and what we might call, though not wholly unambiguously, reference to a content, direction toward an object (which is not to be understood here as meaning a thing), or immanent objectivity."

"Intentional inexistence" is a technical term. The Latin *inexistentia* means "existing in" — not non-existence, but a special mode of being within something. The intentional object "exists in" the mental act — it has "immanent objectivity" within the act itself.

When I think about Hamlet, Hamlet does not exist in the ordinary sense. But there is something that my thought is directed at — something that has a kind of being *within* my thought, as its intentional object. Hamlet "inexists" in my thought.

**The Problem: What Is the Intentional Object?**

This generates an immediate puzzle. When I think about Hamlet, what exactly is the intentional object? It cannot be the fictional character as an abstract object, because Brentano is not a Platonist. It cannot be a mental image, because Brentano wants to distinguish the directedness of thought (which is toward Hamlet, not toward an image of Hamlet) from the vehicle of thought. It cannot be nothing, because there is clearly something that the thought is about — it is about Hamlet, not about Napoleon.

This puzzle — the problem of non-existent objects — is one of the central puzzles in philosophy of mind and philosophy of language. Meinong (a student of Brentano) tried to solve it by positing a rich realm of "objects" that includes both existent and merely "subsistent" or "impossible" objects. Frege tried to solve it by distinguishing sense from reference. Contemporary philosophers of language and mind continue to grapple with it.

**Husserl's Transformation**

Husserl accepted Brentano's insight that intentionality is the defining characteristic of consciousness but was dissatisfied with the Brentanian notion of intentional inexistence. On Husserl's account, the intentional object is not something immanent in the mental act; rather, mental acts are directed toward transcendent objects — objects that are *meant* or *intended*, not "contained in" the act.

What is immanent to the act is the *noema* — the "sense" or "meaning" of the intentional act — not the object itself. The same object can be meant in different ways (Napoleon as a military genius vs. Napoleon as a short man with a big ego), and what differs between these is the noema, not the object. This allows Husserl to avoid making the object a mental content while still explaining how the act is directed at a particular object.

**The Original vs. Derived Intentionality Debate**

A major contemporary debate about intentionality concerns whether mental intentionality is "original" or "derived." John Searle argues that mental intentionality is "intrinsic" — it is built into the nature of mental states themselves, not conferred on them by anything outside the mind. Linguistic and other representational systems have only "derived" intentionality — they mean what they mean because minds use them to mean things.

Others, like Dennett and some cognitive scientists, hold that even mental intentionality is "derived" — it is a product of evolution, learning, and interpretation, not a brute, irreducible feature of mental states. On this view, there is no sharp line between "original" mental intentionality and the "derived" intentionality of thermostats and computers.

The Brentanian insight that intentionality is the mark of the mental is thus still generating philosophical controversy, because it is unclear whether intentionality can be given a naturalistically acceptable account or whether it is irreducibly mental in a way that resists naturalization.
