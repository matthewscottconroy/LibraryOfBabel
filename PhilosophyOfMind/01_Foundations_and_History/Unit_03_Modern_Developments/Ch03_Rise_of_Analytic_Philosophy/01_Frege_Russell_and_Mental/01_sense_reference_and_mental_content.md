# Frege, Sense, and Reference

Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) transformed philosophy of language and, with it, philosophy of mind. His distinction between *sense* and *reference*, developed in his 1892 paper "On Sense and Reference," introduced conceptual tools that remain central to contemporary accounts of mental content, propositional attitudes, and the relationship between thought and language.

**The Puzzle of Identity**

Frege's argument begins with a puzzle about identity statements. Consider: "Hesperus is Phosphorus." This is an astronomical discovery: Hesperus (the evening star) and Phosphorus (the morning star) are the same object — the planet Venus. But "Hesperus is Hesperus" is not a discovery; it is trivially true. How can two true identity statements have such different cognitive values?

If both "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" just refer to their object (Venus), then the two names are interchangeable. But then "Hesperus is Phosphorus" should have the same meaning as "Hesperus is Hesperus," and it manifestly does not.

**Sense and Reference**

Frege's solution: every meaningful expression has two semantic dimensions. The *reference* (*Bedeutung*) is what the expression picks out in the world — the actual object, property, or truth-value. The *sense* (*Sinn*) is the *mode of presentation* of the reference — the way in which the reference is presented or determined by the expression.

"Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" have the same reference (Venus) but different senses. "Hesperus" presents Venus as the brightest celestial body visible in the evening; "Phosphorus" presents Venus as the brightest celestial body visible in the morning. These different modes of presentation explain why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative while "Hesperus is Hesperus" is trivial.

**Implications for Mental Content**

The sense/reference distinction has profound implications for the theory of mental content — what our mental states are *about* and *how* they are about it.

When I believe that Hesperus is bright, what I believe is not just a relationship to the planet Venus directly. I believe something about Venus *as presented in a certain way*. The sense through which I think about Venus is part of the content of my belief. This is why two people who believe different things about the same object (one who thinks of it as Hesperus, one who thinks of it as Phosphorus) are not in the same belief state, even though their beliefs are about the same object.

This insight generates the distinction between *de re* and *de dicto* belief content, and it underlies debates between content externalism and internalism. Externalists (Putnam, Burge) argue that what a belief is about — its reference — is partly determined by factors outside the head. Internalists argue that psychological states are individuated by their sense or cognitive role, not by their reference. The sense/reference distinction is the conceptual ancestor of this debate.

**Frege's Anti-Psychologism**

Frege was militantly anti-psychologistic. He distinguished sharply between the logical and the psychological: logical laws are objective, universal, normative; psychological laws are subjective, variable, descriptive. The thought expressed by "2 + 2 = 4" is not a mental event in anyone's head; it is an objective content that different minds can apprehend.

This anti-psychologism creates a tension in philosophy of mind. Frege needs thoughts (senses, propositional contents) to be objective and mind-independent to explain how different people can think the same thought. But he also needs thoughts to be the contents of mental acts — what beliefs and judgments are "about." How can thoughts be objective and yet serve as the contents of subjective mental acts?

This tension drives 20th-century debates about the nature of mental content: are the contents of mental states objective, mind-independent entities (Fregean senses, Platonic propositions), or are they somehow constituted by the mind's own activities?

**Russell's Response**

Bertrand Russell, in contrast to Frege, was suspicious of the Fregean notion of sense as an intermediate entity between mind and world. Russell's theory of *direct reference* (for names and logically proper names) holds that the content of a name just is its referent — there is no intermediate sense. This leads to different views about belief reports: for Russell, to believe that Hesperus is bright is simply to stand in a belief relation to a proposition that directly contains the planet Venus.

The tensions between Frege's sense-based and Russell's object-based theories of propositional content remain live in contemporary philosophy of mind and language, particularly in debates about singular thought, de se belief, and the semantics of mental state reports.
