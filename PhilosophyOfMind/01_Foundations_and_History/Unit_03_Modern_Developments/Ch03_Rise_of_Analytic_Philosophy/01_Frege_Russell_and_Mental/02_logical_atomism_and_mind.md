# Logical Atomism and the Logical Analysis of Mind

Bertrand Russell's logical atomism — developed around 1918 in his lectures "The Philosophy of Logical Atomism" — represents an attempt to apply the resources of the new mathematical logic to fundamental metaphysical questions. For philosophy of mind, logical atomism is significant both for what it says about the mind and for the method it exemplifies: the use of logical analysis to dissolve philosophical puzzles.

**The Logical Atomist Picture**

Russell held that the world consists of facts — ultimately, atomic facts consisting of simple particulars standing in simple relations. The structure of language (properly analyzed) mirrors the structure of reality: a true atomic proposition consists of a name for each particular involved plus a predicate expressing the relation, and it is true if and only if the corresponding atomic fact obtains.

Everything more complex — general facts, modal facts, negative facts — is in some sense built from atomic facts. Philosophy's job is to find the logical forms that correctly represent reality, stripping away the misleading surface grammar of ordinary language.

**Mind and Neutral Monism**

Russell's own view of the mind underwent significant changes. In his early logical atomist period, he held a roughly Cartesian picture of mental entities (sense-data, acts of consciousness) as distinct from physical entities. But under the influence of William James's radical empiricism, he moved toward neutral monism.

In *The Analysis of Mind* (1921), Russell argues that both mind and matter can be constructed out of a neutral stuff — "neutral events" or "sensibilia" — that are neither mental nor physical in themselves. What we call a "physical object" is a logical construction out of actual and possible sense-data; what we call a "mental event" is a logical construction out of the same neutral events plus their logical relations.

This dissolves the mind-body problem by eliminating the hard ontological distinction between mental and physical substances. There is only one kind of basic stuff; "mental" and "physical" are different ways of organizing and describing the same underlying events.

**Logical Analysis and Mental Concepts**

Russell's method of logical analysis was applied to mental concepts with illuminating and sometimes deflationary results. The analysis of belief, for instance, turns out to be surprisingly complex. Russell at various times held that belief is a "multiple relation" (relating the believer to multiple constituents of the believed proposition) rather than a two-term relation (between the believer and a proposition).

The multiple relation theory has the advantage of not requiring abstract proposition-entities as the objects of belief. But it faced serious difficulties: Wittgenstein pointed out that Russell's analysis of "A believes that p" — which relates A to the logical parts of p — fails to account for the fact that what A believes must make sense, must have the right logical form. You can believe "Othello believes that Desdemona loves Cassio" only if the constituents hang together in the right way.

**Legacy of Logical Analysis**

The logical-atomist program of analyzing mental concepts using the tools of mathematical logic was enormously influential. Gilbert Ryle's logical behaviorism — the view that mental concepts are to be analyzed as dispositions to behave — is one descendant. The conceptual analysis of mental concepts that dominates much of ordinary-language philosophy is another.

The method also shaped the development of formal semantics for attitude reports — the study of sentences like "John believes that p," "Mary knows that q," and so on. The puzzles about opacity, substitutivity, and belief reports that Frege and Russell identified remain central topics in philosophy of language and philosophy of mind.

Russell's neutral monism, meanwhile, has found unexpected defenders in recent decades. Philip Goff, Bertrand Russell himself in his later work, and others have argued that the best solution to the hard problem of consciousness may be something close to Russellian neutral monism: the "intrinsic nature" of what physics describes as matter may be experiential or proto-experiential in character.
