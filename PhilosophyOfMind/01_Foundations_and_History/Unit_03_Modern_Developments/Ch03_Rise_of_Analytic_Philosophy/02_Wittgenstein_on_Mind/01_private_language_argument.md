# Wittgenstein's Private Language Argument

Wittgenstein's private language argument, developed in the *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), is one of the most significant arguments in 20th-century philosophy. It challenges the Cartesian picture of the mind as a private theater accessible only to its owner, and it has profound implications for how we understand consciousness, mental states, and the relationship between mind and language.

**The Cartesian Picture Under Attack**

The dominant picture of the mind since Descartes is something like this: each person has privileged, private access to their own mental states; the mind is an "inner" domain of experience that others can at most infer from external behavior; mental words like "pain" and "belief" get their meanings by being correlated with these private inner states through a kind of inner ostensive definition.

Wittgenstein targets this picture. His method is not to refute it with a direct counter-argument but to show that it is incoherent — that the picture cannot be made to work, even in principle.

**The Scenario: A Private Language**

Imagine trying to define a word for a private sensation — something that only you can feel. You have this sensation, you want to give it a name, say "S." You point (inwardly) at the sensation and call it "S." Now whenever you have the sensation again, you will call it "S."

The question is: what would make it correct or incorrect to apply "S"? You cannot compare your current sensation to a public standard — it is private. You can only compare it to your memory of previous sensations. But how do you know that your memory is accurate? In the private language case, there is no check on memory. And if there is no check on memory, then "whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'right.'"

**The Core Point**

The argument's conclusion is that genuine language — language that has a distinction between correct and incorrect applications — requires a *public* criterion of correctness. A rule that is followed correctly in one way or another must be something against which behavior can be measured and found to be correct or incorrect. But for private sensations, cut off from any public criterion, there is no such measure.

A private language is not just a language no one else happens to use; it is a language that no one *could* understand — including the speaker, if "understanding" requires the possibility of checking whether one is using words correctly.

**What Follows for Philosophy of Mind?**

The argument, if successful, undermines the Cartesian picture in several ways:

1. **Mental concepts get their meaning from public criteria**. Pain-talk is meaningful because there are public criteria for pain: pain-behavior, circumstances, etc. The meaning of "pain" is not fixed by a private ostensive definition directed at an inner state.

2. **Introspection is not infallible**. If my use of mental words is constrained by public criteria, then I can in principle misuse them — even in introspective reports. The Cartesian doctrine that the mind is transparent to itself is undermined.

3. **Other minds are not the problem they seemed**. The Cartesian picture makes other minds a deep epistemological problem: I know my own mind with certainty, but I can only infer others' minds from behavior. Wittgenstein's picture inverts this: mental concepts are learned and applied in first-person and third-person contexts alike; the first-person use is not epistemically special in the way the Cartesian assumes.

**Interpretive Debates**

The private language argument is one of the most disputed passages in the philosophical canon. Main interpretive debates include:

- **Does the argument target all private language, or only a specific Cartesian variety?** Some interpreters hold that Wittgenstein only targets the idea of a language based on inner ostensive definition, not the existence of private experience as such.

- **What is the relationship to Kripke's interpretation?** In *Wittgenstein on Rules and Following*, Saul Kripke reads the private language argument as a consequence of a deeper skeptical argument about rule-following: even public languages face the problem that there is no fact about what a rule means. Wittgenstein's solution is social: the standards for rule-following are provided by the community.

- **Does the argument succeed?** Critics argue that Wittgenstein misrepresents the private language case, that memory can provide a genuine standard even in the absence of public checking, and that private ostensive definition is coherent.

The argument continues to generate philosophical controversy, which is itself a testament to its depth and importance.
