# Rule Following and the Kripkenstein Problem

In sections 138–242 of the *Philosophical Investigations*, Wittgenstein develops what many consider his deepest argument: the problem of rule-following. Saul Kripke's influential reconstruction of this argument in *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) — the "Kripkenstein" interpretation — turned it into one of the most discussed puzzles in philosophy of language and mind.

**The Rule-Following Problem**

Suppose you have been taught to add numbers, and you have successfully added many pairs. Now someone asks you to compute 68 + 57. You answer: 125. But consider an alternative rule: *quaddition* (*quus*), which agrees with addition for all numbers you have previously considered but yields 5 for any pair where either number is greater than 57. Your entire history of practice and training is consistent with *both* adding and quadding.

This is the skeptical puzzle Kripke finds in Wittgenstein: there is no fact about your past behavior, your past mental states, or your past intentions that fixes which rule you were following. Any description of your past practice is consistent with infinitely many different rules.

**The Skeptical Threat**

If there is no fact about which rule you were following, then there is no fact about whether your answer to "68 + 57 = ?" is correct or incorrect. It would only be correct if you were following the addition rule — but if nothing fixes that you were following addition rather than quaddition, the notion of "correct" loses its grip.

This is not just a puzzle about arithmetic. It generalizes to all rule-governed behavior — all linguistic practice, all intentional action. If there is no fact about which rule I am following in speaking, then there is no fact about whether I am using "rabbit" correctly, whether my use of "pain" means pain or something else, whether my belief that it is raining is *about* rain.

**Proposed Solutions**

Several responses to the rule-following problem have been proposed:

**Dispositionalism**: You were following addition because you are *disposed* to add, not quadd. The fact about which rule you follow is a dispositional fact — how you would answer in various situations. Kripke objects that dispositions can be finite (I have dispositions to compute correctly for a limited range of inputs) while rules are infinite in their application. And dispositions can malfunction — if I would compute incorrectly due to a brain glitch, should that count?

**Meaning is use**: The meaning of a term is its use in a practice. What fixes that "plus" means addition is the practice of addition in the community. The community's responses provide the standard against which individual use is measured. This is Kripke's own "skeptical solution": he accepts that there is no metaphysical fact about meaning, but argues that attributions of meaning function not to describe inner states but to certify participation in communal practices.

**Non-Reductionist Realism**: Some philosophers (McDowell, Wright) argue that rule-following facts are sui generis — they cannot be reduced to dispositional facts, behavioral facts, or any other lower-level facts, but they are nonetheless real. Understanding a rule is knowing how to go on, and this knowing how is a genuine cognitive achievement that cannot be analyzed further without distortion.

**Implications for Philosophy of Mind**

The rule-following problem has deep implications for philosophy of mind:

1. **Intentionality cannot be fully naturalized**: If meaning facts cannot be reduced to physical or dispositional facts, and if mental intentionality is a form of meaning, then intentionality may resist full naturalization.

2. **Content and normativity**: The problem reveals that mental content has a normative dimension. Beliefs are supposed to be true; intentions are supposed to guide action correctly. This normative dimension is what generates the rule-following problem, and it is notoriously difficult to account for within a purely causal-mechanical framework.

3. **Social constitution of mind**: If meaning is social (determined by communal practice), then mental content is partly socially constituted. This has implications for debates about internalism vs. externalism about mental content: some content is fixed not just by the physical environment (Putnam's Twin Earth) but by the social environment (Burge's arthritis case).

The rule-following problem remains one of the most productive puzzles in philosophy of language and mind, and the debate it has generated shows no signs of being resolved.
