# Watson and Methodological Behaviorism

John B. Watson's 1913 paper "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" is often called the behaviorist manifesto. In it, Watson argued that psychology should abandon the study of consciousness and mental states entirely and become the science of behavior. This radical proposal reshaped scientific psychology for decades and set the stage for the cognitive revolution that would eventually overthrow it.

**Watson's Argument**

Watson's motivating concern was scientific respectability. The psychology of his day — Wundt's experimental introspectionism, Titchener's structuralism — relied on trained subjects reporting their inner experiences. But Watson argued that introspective reports were unreliable, non-replicable, and subject to rampant theoretical bias. Different laboratories using introspective methods were getting incompatible results and had no principled way to adjudicate between them.

The solution: restrict psychology to what can be publicly observed and measured. Behavior is publicly observable; it can be measured, recorded, and replicated. Mental states, by contrast, are private and cannot be directly observed. Science requires objectivity; objectivity requires public observability; therefore, science must confine itself to behavior.

Watson did not deny that mental events exist (he had views about how thinking consists in sub-vocal muscular movements in the larynx). His methodological behaviorism is a claim about the proper subject matter and method of scientific psychology, not a metaphysical claim about the non-existence of minds.

**Conditioning and Learning**

Watson adopted Pavlov's classical conditioning as the basic mechanism of behavioral change. The organism is a stimulus-response machine: stimuli produce responses; through conditioning, previously neutral stimuli can come to produce responses that were previously elicited by other stimuli.

Watson's famous "Little Albert" experiment — conditioning a fear response in an infant to a white rat — was intended to demonstrate that even complex emotional responses could be understood as conditioned reactions to neutral stimuli. Emotions, personality, and psychopathology were, on this view, all products of conditioning history.

**The Attraction of Behaviorism**

Why was behaviorism so attractive? Partly for reasons of scientific methodology. But there was also a philosophical rationale. Behaviorism seemed to offer an escape from the mind-body problem: if psychological science is about behavior, we need not ask how mental states are related to physical states. We can study the physical causes and effects of behavior without ever invoking inner mental states.

There was also a political/philosophical appeal to the blank-slate picture that behaviorism naturally combines with: if behavior is entirely shaped by conditioning history (and thus by the environment), then human nature is infinitely malleable, and social engineering — changing behavior by changing the environment — is in principle unlimited. Watson himself wrote popular books about child-rearing based on these principles.

**The Limits**

Behaviorism's dominance began to crack in the 1950s and 1960s, not from philosophical arguments but from within science. Chomsky's devastating review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* (1957) argued that language acquisition cannot be explained by stimulus-response conditioning: the productivity and systematicity of language, and the speed with which children acquire it on the basis of limited evidence, requires positing an innate language faculty.

Meanwhile, work in animal learning had been accumulating evidence that organisms form cognitive maps and mental representations, not just conditioned response patterns. Tolman's rats in mazes could reach a goal by novel routes after the original route was blocked — evidence of a cognitive map that went beyond any learned stimulus-response chain.

These empirical challenges, combined with the growing success of information-processing models imported from computing (the digital computer as a model of the mind), made the full return to mental states in cognitive science seem not just philosophically attractive but scientifically necessary.

Behaviorism is now largely abandoned as a complete theory of the mind, but its legacy persists: the emphasis on public, measurable behavior as the primary data of psychological science; the behavioral approach to therapy (cognitive-behavioral therapy); and the caution about over-relying on introspective reports. These are genuine and lasting contributions.
