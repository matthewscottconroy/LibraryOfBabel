# Skinner and Radical Behaviorism

B.F. Skinner developed behaviorism into its most influential and most philosophically ambitious form. Where Watson's methodological behaviorism was a recommendation about scientific method (study behavior, not minds), Skinner's radical behaviorism made the stronger metaphysical claim that mental concepts are either eliminable or reducible to behavioral concepts.

**Operant Conditioning**

Skinner's most important scientific contribution was the development of operant conditioning theory. While Pavlov's classical conditioning involved the pairing of stimuli, Skinner focused on the consequences of behavior: behavior that is reinforced (followed by a positive outcome) becomes more frequent; behavior that is punished becomes less frequent.

This "law of effect" (originally proposed by Thorndike) became the foundation of Skinner's account of all learned behavior. The pigeon in the Skinner box that has learned to peck a lever to get food is a model — Skinner held — not just of animal behavior but of all voluntary human behavior, including language, reasoning, and even "self-control."

**Verbal Behavior**

In *Verbal Behavior* (1957), Skinner attempted to extend operant conditioning to language. On his account, verbal behavior is shaped by reinforcement from the verbal community. A child produces sounds; some sounds are reinforced by parental response; gradually, the child's verbal behavior is shaped into the patterns of the community's language.

Skinner tried to analyze all features of linguistic behavior in terms of schedules of reinforcement, stimulus control, and response strength, without invoking rules, mental representations, or grammatical knowledge. This was an ambitious attempt to naturalize language within a behaviorist framework.

Chomsky's 1959 review demolished this program. Chomsky argued that Skinner's key concepts (stimulus, response, reinforcement) were either so vague as to be unfalsifiable or, when given precise meanings, obviously inadequate to explain language acquisition. The core problem: language is productive (speakers produce and understand sentences they have never encountered before), and this productivity cannot be explained by associative learning on any finite training set.

**Radical Behaviorism as Philosophy**

Skinner was not just a scientist but a philosopher. He argued that folk-psychological concepts — belief, desire, intention, feeling — are "mentalistic" explanations that should be replaced by behavioral descriptions. To say that someone did X because they wanted Y is to invoke a mysterious inner cause. The proper scientific account replaces the inner "want" with the reinforcement history that makes Y-seeking behavior likely.

This is not quite eliminativism (the view that mental states don't exist) but rather a translation program: mental talk can be translated into behavioral talk without loss.

**The Philosophical Objections**

Logical behaviorism (associated more with Ryle and Hempel than with Skinner) tried to show that mental concepts *mean* behavioral dispositions — to be in pain means to be disposed to cry out, protect the injured area, and so on. This was shown to be inadequate: any behavioral disposition can be overridden by other mental states (I may want to conceal my pain), so no finite set of behavioral dispositions suffices to analyze any mental state. The analysis always needs to include "other things being equal" clauses that invoke more mental states, generating an infinite regress.

Hilary Putnam's multiple realizability argument also applies: if mental states can be realized in many different physical (and behavioral) substrates, then no behavioral property can be necessary or sufficient for any mental state.

**Skinner's Legacy**

Skinner's radical behaviorism is now largely rejected by cognitive scientists and philosophers of mind. But his work had lasting effects:

- Applied behavior analysis (ABA), derived from Skinnerian principles, is widely used in behavioral therapy, particularly for autism spectrum disorders.
- Reinforcement learning in AI is explicitly modeled on Skinnerian principles and has achieved remarkable successes.
- The insight that behavior can be modified without insight into underlying mental states has practical therapeutic value.

Skinner's philosophical error was to confuse a useful methodological restriction (studying behavior) with a metaphysical claim (mind is nothing more than behavior). The usefulness of the behavioral approach as a tool does not establish behaviorism as a complete theory of mind.
