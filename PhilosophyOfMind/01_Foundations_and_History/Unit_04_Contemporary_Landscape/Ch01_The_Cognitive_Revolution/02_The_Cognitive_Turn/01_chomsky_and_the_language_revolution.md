# Chomsky and the Language Revolution

Noam Chomsky's work in the late 1950s and 1960s triggered one of the most significant shifts in the history of the human sciences: the cognitive revolution. By demonstrating that language acquisition requires positing innate mental structures — a "language acquisition device" — Chomsky helped restore the legitimacy of mentalistic explanation and opened the way for cognitive science.

**The Problem of Language Acquisition**

The core of Chomsky's argument is what he calls "the poverty of the stimulus." Children acquire their native languages remarkably quickly, on the basis of limited and often impoverished linguistic input (adults do not speak in perfectly formed sentences; children are not explicitly corrected for grammatical errors in any systematic way). Yet all normally developing children achieve full grammatical competence — not just a set of phrases they have heard but the capacity to produce and understand an unlimited number of novel sentences.

If language were learned by stimulus-response conditioning (as Skinner held), the input available to children would be vastly insufficient to explain the richness and speed of the output. Something else must be going on.

**Universal Grammar**

Chomsky's hypothesis: language acquisition is possible because humans have an innate, species-specific "language acquisition device" — a biologically given set of constraints that determines the possible forms of human language (Universal Grammar). All human languages, despite their surface diversity, share deep structural properties because they are all products of the same innate UG.

The child learning language does not start from scratch. They bring to the task a rich set of expectations about what kind of thing a language can be — what kinds of rules, what kinds of structures, what kinds of dependencies. These expectations narrow the hypothesis space so dramatically that learning from limited input becomes feasible.

**The Rationalist Revival**

Chomsky's argument was read as a revival of Cartesian rationalism — the view that the mind comes equipped with innate ideas and principles. Chomsky himself made this connection explicit, arguing that his linguistics was in the spirit of Descartes's doctrine of innate ideas and Leibniz's claim that the mind is not a blank slate.

This was a direct challenge to Skinnerian behaviorism, which presupposed a blank-slate view of the mind. If the mind has innate structure — if it is not infinitely malleable by experience — then the behaviorist program of explaining behavior purely through conditioning history is inadequate.

**Competence and Performance**

Chomsky introduced an important distinction between *linguistic competence* (the speaker's tacit knowledge of the rules of their language) and *linguistic performance* (actual language use, which involves memory limitations, performance errors, distractions, etc.). Cognitive science is concerned with competence — the underlying knowledge — not just with observed behavior.

This distinction is philosophically significant because it introduces a level of description — the level of tacit knowledge — that is genuinely cognitive, not just behavioral. Cognitive science became the science of mental representations and computational processes, not just the science of behavior.

**The Impact on Philosophy of Mind**

Chomsky's work had enormous impact on philosophy of mind:

1. **Legitimacy of mental representations**: If linguistics needs to posit mental representations (phrase structure rules, transformations, underlying representations) to explain language behavior, then mental representations are scientifically legitimate — not merely "folk" posits.

2. **Nativism and innateness**: Chomsky revived the philosophical discussion of innateness and the extent to which the mind's structure is given rather than learned. This connects to debates about the origin of concepts, the a priori, and the role of evolution in shaping cognitive architecture.

3. **Modularity**: Chomsky's linguistics provided a model for the idea that the mind is organized into domain-specific modules — specialized subsystems with their own structures and principles. This influenced Fodor's modularity thesis and the broader cognitive science discussion of cognitive architecture.

Chomsky remains one of the most controversial figures in the human sciences — his specific linguistic theories have been revised many times and are contested — but the cognitive revolution he helped trigger remains the dominant framework for scientific and philosophical study of the mind.
