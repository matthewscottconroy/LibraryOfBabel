# The Early Cognitive Science Program

The cognitive revolution of the 1950s and 1960s was not just Chomsky. It was a convergence of several intellectual streams — computer science, information theory, experimental psychology, linguistics, and philosophy — that together produced a new interdisciplinary field: cognitive science.

**The Computer as a Model**

The development of digital computers in the 1940s provided cognitive scientists with both a tool and a metaphor. The computer is a device that manipulates symbols according to formal rules; it takes inputs, operates on them, and produces outputs — all without any understanding of what the symbols mean. The brain, cognitive scientists hypothesized, might work similarly: it might be a kind of biological computer, processing information according to formal rules.

This "physical symbol system hypothesis" — developed explicitly by Newell and Simon in the 1960s and 1970s — held that intelligent behavior requires a physical system capable of manipulating symbols according to formal rules. Computers were intelligent (in a limited sense) because they were physical symbol systems. Brains were intelligent for the same reason.

**Information Theory**

Claude Shannon's information theory (1948) provided mathematical tools for thinking about communication and storage of information. The concept of "information" as a mathematically precise quantity — measurable in bits — gave cognitive scientists a framework for thinking about how the brain represents and processes data. Perception, memory, and thought could all be described in informational terms.

**The Birth of Cognitive Psychology**

George Miller's 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two" demonstrated that working memory has a limited capacity — people can hold about seven "chunks" of information in immediate memory. This was important because it suggested that the mind had specific, measurable cognitive capacities — not just behavioral dispositions but internal information-processing limits.

Miller, along with Jerome Bruner and Ulric Neisser, helped establish cognitive psychology as a science of internal mental processes — perception, attention, memory, problem-solving, language — studied through reaction times, error patterns, and other indirect measures of internal processing, rather than through introspection or behavioral observation alone.

**Newell and Simon: General Problem Solver**

Allen Newell and Herbert Simon's General Problem Solver (GPS) program (1957) was an early AI system that could solve problems in domains like logic and chess by means-ends analysis: identifying differences between current state and goal state, then applying operations that reduce the difference. Newell and Simon argued that GPS was not just a useful tool but a model of human problem-solving — that humans and computers solve problems by the same kind of symbol manipulation.

This "information-processing psychology" became a dominant framework: psychological theories took the form of flowcharts and computer programs specifying the information-processing steps by which cognitive tasks were accomplished.

**The Interdisciplinary Ideal**

Cognitive science from the beginning was explicitly interdisciplinary. The field brought together:
- Experimental psychology (providing data about human cognition)
- Linguistics (Chomsky's generative grammar)
- Artificial intelligence (computational models of intelligent behavior)
- Philosophy (clarifying concepts of representation, computation, intentionality)
- Neuroscience (providing constraints from biology)

This interdisciplinary ideal was and remains productive but also a source of tension. Different disciplines bring different standards of evidence, different theoretical commitments, and sometimes different implicit assumptions about what minds are.

**The Limits of the Classical Program**

The classical cognitive science program — based on physical symbol systems and information processing — was enormously productive but eventually faced challenges. The frame problem (how does a cognitive system decide what is relevant in a given context?), the symbol grounding problem (how do symbols acquire their meaning?), and the difficulty of modeling real-time perception and action led to alternative approaches: connectionism, embodied cognition, and dynamical systems theory.

These challenges did not destroy cognitive science but transformed it. The field became more pluralistic, and the debate between classical (computational) and alternative approaches remains one of the central discussions in contemporary philosophy of mind.
