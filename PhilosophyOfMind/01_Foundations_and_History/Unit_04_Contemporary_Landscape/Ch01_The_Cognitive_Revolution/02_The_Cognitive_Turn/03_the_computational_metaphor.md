# The Computational Metaphor

"The mind is a computer" — this claim, or something like it, became the central working hypothesis of cognitive science in the second half of the 20th century. Understanding what this hypothesis means, what evidence supports it, and what its limits are is essential for understanding the current state of philosophy of mind.

**Turing and Computation**

The theoretical foundation of the computational metaphor is Alan Turing's analysis of computation. Turing showed that any well-defined, finite procedure — any algorithm — could be executed by a very simple abstract machine: the Turing machine. A Turing machine reads and writes symbols on an infinite tape according to a finite table of rules; it is equivalent in computational power to any physical computer.

Turing also proposed that the question "Can machines think?" should be reframed as a question about behavior: if a machine can produce behavior indistinguishable from intelligent human behavior (the "Turing Test"), it should be counted as thinking. This behavioral criterion, while controversial, shifted attention from the internal nature of minds to their functional capacities — a move congenial to cognitive science.

**Functionalism**

The computational metaphor found its philosophical expression in functionalism — the view that mental states are defined by their causal-functional roles, not by the physical stuff they are realized in. Just as the same computation can be implemented in silicon, vacuum tubes, or neurons, the same mental state can be realized in very different physical substrates.

Functionalism explains the multiple realizability of mental states: pain in a human, pain in an octopus, and pain in a hypothetical silicon-based alien might all count as pain if they play the same functional role — being caused by tissue damage, causing avoidance behavior, representing something as harmful.

**The Levels of Analysis**

David Marr's levels-of-analysis framework (1982) provided a useful way of thinking about the computational metaphor. Marr distinguished:

- **Computational level**: What problem is being solved? What is the goal of the computation?
- **Algorithmic level**: What representations are used, and what algorithm operates on them?
- **Implementation level**: How is the algorithm physically realized?

This framework allows cognitive scientists to ask questions at each level independently. You can describe the function of vision (computing a representation of the 3D structure of the scene from 2D retinal images) without knowing the algorithm, and describe the algorithm without knowing the neural implementation.

**Limitations of the Computational Metaphor**

Despite its productivity, the computational metaphor has faced serious challenges:

**The symbol grounding problem** (Harnad 1990): Symbols in a computer have meaning only derivatively — their meaning is assigned by programmers. But mental representations are supposed to have "original" or "intrinsic" intentionality — they mean what they mean independently of any external interpretation. How do mental symbols get grounded in meaning?

**The frame problem**: In classical AI, systems require explicit representations of all the facts relevant to a situation. But intelligent behavior requires knowing what is relevant — and relevance cannot itself be represented explicitly without infinite regress. Embodied, situated cognition seems to handle relevance effortlessly, but classical computation struggles.

**Consciousness**: The computational metaphor has nothing to say about why information processing in the brain is accompanied by conscious experience. A computer runs programs, but there is nothing it is like to be a computer running a program. The "hard problem" of consciousness is not a problem for computer science but is a problem for any view that tries to understand minds purely in computational terms.

**The Chinese Room**: Searle's famous thought experiment (see the chapter on functionalism) argues that computation is syntactic — it operates on formal symbols — while thought is semantic — it involves genuine understanding of meaning. A system can manipulate symbols according to rules without understanding anything.

The computational metaphor remains the dominant framework in cognitive science, but it is increasingly complemented — and challenged — by connectionist, embodied, and dynamical alternatives. The question is not whether computation plays any role in cognition but whether the computational perspective captures what is most important about minds.
