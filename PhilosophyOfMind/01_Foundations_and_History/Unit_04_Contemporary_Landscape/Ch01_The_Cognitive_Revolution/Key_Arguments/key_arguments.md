# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: The Cognitive Revolution

## Key Arguments

**Chomsky's Argument Against Behaviorism**
Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior argued that stimulus-response-reinforcement analysis cannot account for the productivity and systematicity of natural language: speakers routinely produce and understand sentences they have never encountered before, which no finite learning history of reinforcement could explain. The argument implies that the mind possesses internal, rule-governed representations—a generative grammar—that mediate behavior, making behaviorism explanatorily inadequate. This is widely credited as the most decisive blow against behaviorism and the founding argument of the cognitive revolution.

**Turing's Argument That Computing Machines Can Think**
Turing argued in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950) that the question "can machines think?" should be replaced by an operationally defined imitation game: if a machine can answer questions in a way indistinguishable from a human, then it thinks. He pre-empted objections (Lady Lovelace's objection, the mathematical objection, the argument from consciousness) and proposed that a sufficiently complex discrete state machine could in principle pass this test. The argument established the computational conception of intelligence and set the terms for subsequent AI and philosophy of mind debates.

**The Information Processing Argument for Mental Representations**
Early cognitive scientists (Newell, Simon, Miller) argued that intelligent behavior—problem solving, language comprehension, reasoning—can only be explained by positing internal symbolic representations and processes that operate over them. Since the same computational processes can be implemented in different physical media, the level of description relevant to psychology is the computational (software) level, not the neural (hardware) level. This argument established the autonomy of cognitive science from both behaviorism and neuroscience.

**Miller's Argument for Limited Working Memory Capacity**
George Miller's 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two" argued that human short-term memory is limited to approximately seven chunks of information, regardless of the complexity of each chunk. The argument showed that cognitive processes have characteristic architectural limits—"cognitive capacity" is a meaningful, measurable variable—and established the information processing framework as empirically tractable. It helped define the research program of cognitive psychology.

**The Chomskyan Argument for an Innate Language Acquisition Device**
Chomsky argued that the speed and uniformity of language acquisition across children, despite the "poverty of the stimulus" (the underdetermination of grammar by the input), implies that children bring innate, species-specific grammatical knowledge to the task. No general-purpose learning mechanism could converge on a grammar as complex as natural language from the available data without strong innate constraints. This argument for nativism shaped the cognitive science of language and the debate between empiricist and rationalist approaches to the mind.

## Core Concepts

**Computationalism**
Computationalism (the Computational Theory of Mind) holds that mental processes are computations over mental representations: the mind is a symbol-processing system, and thinking is the manipulation of representational states according to rules. This view makes cognition substrate-independent (multiple realizability) and provides a principled distinction between the study of mind and the study of brain. It underpins classical AI, cognitive psychology, and much of cognitive linguistics, but faces challenges from consciousness, semantic content, and the frame problem.

**Turing Machine**
A Turing machine is an idealized computational device that reads and writes symbols on an infinite tape according to a finite table of instructions. Turing's argument that any computation can be performed by such a machine—and that a universal Turing machine can simulate any other—established the concept of algorithmic computation as precise and general. For philosophy of mind, the Turing machine provides a formal model of a cognitive system whose intelligence is fully characterized by its functional organization, independent of its physical substrate.

**The Information Processing Metaphor**
The information processing metaphor treats the mind as analogous to a digital computer: sensory systems encode input, central systems process and transform representations, and output systems generate behavior. This metaphor unified disparate research programs (psycholinguistics, memory research, problem solving, decision making) under a single theoretical framework. It also shaped the methodology of cognitive science: studying the mind means describing the algorithms and representations that mediate stimulus and response, not just the behavioral regularities themselves.

**Modularity (Fodor)**
Fodor's modularity hypothesis holds that the mind is composed of domain-specific, informationally encapsulated input systems (perceptual modules) that process their inputs automatically, in parallel, and without access to background knowledge held elsewhere in the system. Central systems (reasoning, belief fixation) are non-modular and are the locus of the hardest unsolved problems in cognitive science. The concept of modularity shaped debates about the architecture of the mind, innateness, and the prospects for a science of cognition.

**Poverty of the Stimulus**
The poverty of the stimulus argument holds that the linguistic input available to language-acquiring children is insufficient to determine the specific grammar they acquire: the data underdeterminate the correct grammar, and children never make certain grammatically possible errors (structure-dependent errors). This evidential gap must be filled by innate linguistic knowledge. The argument has been extended to other domains (folk physics, folk psychology) and is central to debates about nativism, learning, and the degree to which knowledge is innate.

## Thought Experiments

**Turing's Imitation Game**
Turing proposes a game in which a human interrogator communicates by text with two respondents—one human, one machine—and must determine which is which. A machine that passes this test would have demonstrated genuine intelligence in the only sense that matters: indistinguishable performance. The thought experiment displaced metaphysical questions ("what is thinking really?") with behavioral ones, and its legacy is contested: Searle's Chinese Room is explicitly designed as a counter-example to the imitation game's sufficiency criterion.

**Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver**
Newell and Simon's GPS program instantiated the information processing approach: human problem-solving is means-ends analysis—compare current state to goal state, select operators that reduce the difference, apply recursively. By programming this strategy and comparing its output to human problem-solving protocols, they demonstrated that mentalistic, representational vocabulary could be given precise computational content. GPS served as an existence proof that computational models of mind are scientifically tractable, not mere metaphor.

**Chomsky's "Colorless Green Ideas Sleep Furiously"**
Chomsky's famous sentence is grammatical but semantically anomalous, demonstrating that grammaticality and meaningfulness are independent properties of linguistic expressions. A purely distributional (behaviorist) account of grammar cannot explain why this sentence is grammatically well-formed: it cannot occur in response to any stimulus or serve any communicative function, yet native speakers immediately recognize its grammatical structure. The example motivates the conclusion that grammatical knowledge is internal, productive, and not reducible to behavioral regularities.
