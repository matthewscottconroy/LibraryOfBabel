# The Hard Problem Emerges

In 1995, David Chalmers's paper "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" crystallized a distinction that would reshape debate in philosophy of mind. Chalmers distinguished between the "easy problems" and the "hard problem" of consciousness — and by doing so, he clarified why a century of progress in cognitive science and neuroscience had somehow failed to address the central mystery of conscious experience.

**The Easy Problems**

The "easy problems" are not, of course, easy in the sense of being simple. They are the problems of explaining cognitive and behavioral functions: how does the brain discriminate stimuli and react appropriately? How does it integrate information? How does it focus attention? How does it produce verbal reports? How does it control behavior?

These problems are "easy" in the sense that there is a clear methodology for solving them: you find the neural mechanisms that implement the relevant functions. You may not have the answer yet, but you know what kind of answer you are looking for. Progress in neuroscience and cognitive science is straightforward in principle, even if difficult in practice.

**The Hard Problem**

The hard problem is different: why is the performance of all these functions accompanied by subjective, phenomenal experience? Why is there something it is like to see red, to hear middle C, to feel pain?

Even if we had a complete neuroscientific account of how the brain processes color information — the receptors activated, the neural pathways involved, the computational operations performed — we would not thereby have explained why seeing red feels the way it does. We would have explained the *function* of color vision, not the *experience* of color.

This is the explanatory gap: there seems to be a gap between any functional-physical description of the brain and any description of phenomenal experience. No matter how detailed the physical account, the phenomenal facts seem to require a further, additional explanation.

**Why This Matters**

Chalmers's distinction matters because it separates two things that had often been run together: explaining cognitive function (which cognitive science can do) and explaining conscious experience (which seems to require something more).

Before Chalmers, many philosophers and scientists assumed that once we had explained the cognitive functions associated with consciousness — attention, integration, access — we would have explained consciousness itself. Chalmers argued this assumption was unwarranted. You can conceive of a being — a "philosophical zombie" — that has all the same cognitive functions and behavioral capacities as a conscious being but lacks phenomenal experience entirely. If this is conceivable, the phenomenal is not the same as the functional.

**The Responses**

The hard problem has generated an enormous literature. The main responses:

**Illusionism** (Frankish, Dennett): There is no hard problem because phenomenal consciousness, as standardly conceived, is an illusion. What we take to be "intrinsic" phenomenal properties are actually relational or functional properties that we systematically misrepresent. The hard problem dissolves once we correct this introspective error.

**Type-B physicalism** (Levine, Block): The hard problem is real, but its solution is physicalist: phenomenal properties are identical to physical properties, but this identity is a posteriori, not a priori. The explanatory gap is an epistemological gap, not an ontological one.

**Panpsychism** (Chalmers, Goff): If consciousness cannot be derived from non-conscious physical processes, and if we want to avoid dualism, perhaps consciousness goes all the way down — proto-experiential properties are fundamental features of reality.

**Mysterianism** (McGinn): The hard problem is real, and it cannot be solved by humans, whose cognitive faculties are constitutionally incapable of grasping the relevant necessary connections between brain and experience.

None of these responses commands consensus, which is itself a sign of how deep the hard problem goes.
