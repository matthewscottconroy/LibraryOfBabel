# Multiple Realizability and the Rise of Functionalism

The notion of multiple realizability is one of the most important ideas in 20th-century philosophy of mind. It provided the philosophical rationale for functionalism and, for a time, seemed to offer the long-sought reconciliation between taking mental states seriously and accepting the findings of modern science.

**The Identity Theory and Its Problem**

In the late 1950s and 1960s, philosophers U.T. Place and J.J.C. Smart proposed the type identity theory: mental state types are identical to brain state types. Pain (the mental state type) is identical to some brain state type — say, C-fiber firing. This reduces psychology to neuroscience and gives mental states a clean place in the physical world.

The decisive objection came from Hilary Putnam (1967, 1975): multiple realizability. Pain is felt not only by humans but by other animals — dogs, squid, lobsters — whose nervous systems are very different from ours. If pain were identical to C-fiber firing in humans, pain in an octopus (which has no C-fibers) would not be pain at all. But this seems wrong — there is something it is like to be an octopus that steps on a sea urchin.

More generally, if the same mental state type could be realized in many different physical substrate types, no single physical type is the type of that mental state. The type identity theory collapses.

**Functionalism as the Solution**

Putnam's own solution was functionalism: mental states are defined not by their physical substrate but by their functional role — their causal relations to inputs, outputs, and other mental states. Pain is whatever state is caused by tissue damage, causes avoidance behavior and distress, and interacts in characteristic ways with beliefs and desires.

This allows pain to be realized differently in different creatures while remaining pain: what makes something pain is the functional role it plays, not the physical stuff it is made of.

Functionalism has several attractive features:
- It explains multiple realizability
- It makes psychological kinds autonomous from physical kinds (psychology is not reducible to neuroscience)
- It is compatible with computational models of the mind (a mental state is a computational state with a certain functional role)
- It accommodates the first-person/third-person asymmetry better than eliminative materialism

**The Qualia Objections**

Functionalism's most serious challenges concern qualia — the "felt" or "phenomenal" qualities of experience. Ned Block distinguished "access consciousness" (information being available for use in reasoning, reporting, and action) from "phenomenal consciousness" (the qualitative, "what it's like" aspect of experience).

Block argues that functional role can fully explain access consciousness but not phenomenal consciousness. You can imagine a being with the same functional organization as a conscious human being but with no phenomenal experience — or with inverted qualia (what we call "red" feels to them the way "green" feels to us). If so, phenomenal consciousness is not determined by functional role.

**The Contemporary Landscape**

The debate generated by multiple realizability and functionalism defines much of contemporary philosophy of mind. It ranges across the following questions:

- Can qualia be given a functionalist analysis, or do they resist it?
- Is the mind autonomous from the brain in the way functionalism suggests, or are there important reductionist constraints?
- What is the relationship between functional organization and conscious experience?
- Can functionalism be made to work if we take seriously the "hard problem"?

Functionalism in various forms remains the dominant position in philosophy of mind, but it faces sustained pressure from all sides. Its future depends on whether phenomenal consciousness can be incorporated into the picture — or whether phenomenal consciousness turns out to require something more than any functional analysis can provide.
