# Two Kinds of Substance

Substance dualism is the view that mind and body are two fundamentally different kinds of thing — different in their essential natures, their properties, their causal powers, and their relationship to space and time. It is the most natural way of accommodating the common intuition that there is an irreducible difference between the physical and the mental, and it has had defenders across two and a half millennia of philosophy.

**What Is a Substance?**

In the philosophical tradition, a "substance" is a thing that exists in its own right — not as a property or modification of something else, but as an independent entity that can bear properties. The table, the tree, the person, the planet: these are substances. Colors, shapes, sizes, and movements are not substances but properties or modes of substances.

For substance dualists, both minds and bodies are substances — not in the sense that they are made of the same kind of stuff, but in the sense that each has independent existence (in principle, at least) and each is the bearer of its own distinctive properties.

**The Physical Substance**

The physical substance — *res extensa* in Descartes's terminology — is defined by extension: it occupies space, has spatial dimensions, is divisible into parts, and is subject to the laws of physics. The essential property of physical substance is that it has location and size. Everything material fits this description: tables, brains, neurotransmitters, electromagnetic fields — all are extended and spatially located.

Physical substances interact through physical forces. They affect each other by contact, by gravitational pull, by electromagnetic interaction. They are — according to our best physics — governed by deterministic (or probabilistic) causal laws.

**The Mental Substance**

The mental substance — *res cogitans* — is defined by thought. It is not spatial, not divisible, not located anywhere. Minds, on this view, do not have a weight or a size; you cannot cut a mind in half; you cannot say "my mind is 15 centimeters to the left of my brain." The essential property of mind is thinking — having thoughts, experiences, sensations, beliefs, desires.

One of the most striking features of mental substance, according to dualists, is its unity. Unlike the body, which can be divided into parts, the mind seems to be simple and indivisible. My pain is not located in my foot; my belief that the Earth is round is not distributed across my neurons. Mental states have a unity that no extended thing possesses.

**The Historical Variety**

Substance dualism is not a single, monolithic view. Several varieties are distinguishable:

- **Cartesian interactionism**: Two substances that causally interact. The mind affects the body (I decide to raise my arm; my arm rises) and the body affects the mind (I stub my toe; I feel pain). The interaction is mysterious, as critics noted immediately.

- **Occasionalism**: There is no genuine causal interaction between mind and body. God is the sole real cause; mental and physical events are occasions on which God produces the correlated events. (Malebranche)

- **Pre-established harmony**: Mind and body unfold in perfect synchrony, pre-programmed by God at creation, without any genuine interaction. (Leibniz)

- **Epiphenomenalism**: Causal interaction runs in one direction only — from body to mind. Mental events are caused by physical events but do not in turn cause anything. Mental events are causally inert epiphenomena.

**The Intuitive Appeal**

Why does substance dualism attract adherents even today? Several considerations:

1. **The first-person perspective**: There is a distinctive, immediate character to one's own experience that seems different from anything described in third-person physical terms. The "what it is like" of experience seems to require a different kind of reality.

2. **Free will**: If minds are physical objects governed by physical laws, free will seems to be in jeopardy. A non-physical mind might escape physical determinism.

3. **The knowledge argument**: We can imagine knowing all the physical facts about a brain and yet not knowing what experience is like — suggesting experience involves something non-physical.

These intuitions do not by themselves establish substance dualism, but they motivate taking it seriously rather than dismissing it outright.
