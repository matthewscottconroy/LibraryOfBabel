# The Knowledge Argument

Frank Jackson's knowledge argument, presented in "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982) and elaborated in subsequent work, is perhaps the most discussed argument in contemporary philosophy of mind. Its central thought experiment — the case of Mary the color scientist — has generated an enormous literature and crystallized the debate between physicalism and dualism.

**Mary's Room**

Mary is a brilliant neuroscientist who lives her entire life in a black-and-white room. She has access to all the physical information about color — she knows everything about wavelengths of light, the physiology of the eye, the neural processing of color information, the behavioral effects of color experience. She has, in Jackson's formulation, "all the physical information there is to know" about color and color experience.

One day, Mary leaves the black-and-white room and sees red for the first time. Jackson contends that she learns something new — she learns what it is like to see red. Before her release, she knew everything physical about color experience; afterward, she knows something she did not know before. Therefore, what she learns is not physical — it is a phenomenal fact, a fact about experience that goes beyond all the physical facts.

**The Argument Reconstructed**

1. Mary knows all the physical facts about color vision before leaving the room.
2. When Mary leaves the room and sees red, she learns something new.
3. What she learns is not a physical fact (since she already knew all physical facts).
4. Therefore, there are non-physical facts — phenomenal facts — about color vision.
5. Therefore, physicalism is false.

**Physicalist Responses**

The knowledge argument has provoked an impressive variety of physicalist responses:

**The Ability Hypothesis** (Lewis, Nemirow): When Mary leaves the room, she does not learn a new fact; she acquires new abilities — the ability to recognize red, to imagine red, to remember what red looks like. These abilities are not the same as knowing a proposition. Since knowing-how is not knowing-that, no new propositional knowledge has been acquired, and no non-physical fact has been discovered.

**The Phenomenal Concepts Strategy** (Loar, Papineau, Levine): Mary does learn a new fact, but the fact she learns is a physical fact — the same neural state she already knew about theoretically, but now known through a new phenomenal concept. Physical properties can be known in multiple ways: theoretically (via physical description) and phenomenally (via direct experience). The "new knowledge" is a new mode of knowing the same physical property, not a new property.

**The Old Fact/New Knowledge View** (Tye, Dretske): Mary acquires genuine phenomenal knowledge — she learns what it is like to see red — but this knowledge is of a physical state that she already knew about in a different way. No new non-physical entity is discovered.

**The Dualist Response**

Jackson himself, in his original paper, accepted the conclusion: phenomenal properties (qualia) are non-physical. He drew the further conclusion that they are epiphenomenal — causally inert. This was meant to explain why physicalism about causation seemed right even while acknowledging non-physical qualia. But epiphenomenalism has its own difficulties (see that section).

Interestingly, Jackson later changed his mind and became a physicalist — he came to accept the phenomenal concepts strategy. His recanting of his own famous argument is itself philosophically instructive.

**The Significance of the Debate**

What the knowledge argument reveals, whatever its ultimate verdict, is that there seems to be a genuinely different way of knowing phenomenal properties than of knowing physical properties. Knowing what it is like to see red is not the same as knowing the neural correlates of red perception, however detailed your neuroscientific knowledge.

Whether this difference reflects a difference in kinds of properties (dualism) or a difference in modes of knowing the same properties (physicalism via the phenomenal concepts strategy) is the crux of the debate. This question — about the nature of phenomenal concepts and what they pick out — is one of the central unresolved problems in contemporary philosophy of mind.
