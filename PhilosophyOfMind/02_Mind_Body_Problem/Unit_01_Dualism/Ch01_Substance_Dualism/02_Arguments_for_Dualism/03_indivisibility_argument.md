# The Indivisibility Argument

One of Descartes's own arguments for substance dualism is the indivisibility argument — the claim that the mind is simple and indivisible, while the body is complex and divisible, and that this difference in nature establishes that they are distinct substances.

**The Argument**

The argument appears in the Sixth Meditation. Descartes observes that everything physical can be divided into parts. A body has spatial extent; you can always take half of it, and half of that half, and so on in principle indefinitely. Even if physical division is not practically possible at some level, the parts are still there in principle — extension always implies divisibility.

The mind, by contrast, is not divisible in this way. When I consider my own mind, I find no parts. I cannot separate my power of understanding from my power of willing; I cannot take "half" my consciousness. The mind presents itself as a simple, unified whole.

Since the mind is indivisible and the body is divisible, and since indivisibility and divisibility are incompatible properties, the mind and body cannot be the same thing. Therefore, they are distinct substances.

**The Phenomenological Version**

There is a phenomenological version of this argument that does not rely on Cartesian metaphysics. From the first-person perspective, consciousness presents itself as unified. My current experience — the visual field, the background sounds, the bodily sensations, the ongoing train of thought — presents itself as a single, unified experience. There are no seams, no joints, no parts that could be reassembled differently.

This unity of experience — what Tim Bayne calls the "unity of consciousness" — does seem to differ from the unity of any physical object. A rock has spatial unity; each part of the rock is somewhere, and the parts add up to the whole. But my conscious experience does not seem to have this additive, compositional structure. It is a whole that is not built up from independently existing parts.

**Objections**

**Split-brain cases**: Neuroscientific experiments with corpus callosotomy patients suggest that the unity of consciousness can be disrupted by severing the neural connections between the two hemispheres. Patients' left and right hemispheres seem to have different preferences and beliefs. If the unity of consciousness depends on neural connections that can be surgically severed, consciousness is not as simple and indivisible as Descartes suggested.

**Attention and consciousness**: Our attention can be divided, our consciousness streams can be interrupted, and different mental subsystems can run in parallel without full integration. This suggests that the "unity" of consciousness is an achievement that can fail, not an essential property of mind as such.

**The neural implementation**: Modern neuroscience has shown that cognitive processes are distributed across different brain regions. Working memory, emotion, attention, and reasoning are all associated with different neural systems. If the mind is implemented in a distributed neural system, its apparent simplicity from the inside may be a feature of how information is presented to consciousness, not a feature of its underlying architecture.

**The Unity Still Matters**

Even if the indivisibility argument does not establish substance dualism, the phenomenon it points to — the unity of consciousness — is real and philosophically significant. Explaining how a physical system can generate a unified experiential field is part of the hard problem of consciousness. The unity of experience is one of the most striking features of mind, and any adequate theory needs to account for it.

Contemporary theories of consciousness — global workspace theory, IIT, predictive processing — each have different accounts of what produces experiential unity, and debates about these accounts are in part debates about what the unity of experience consists in and what would count as explaining it.
