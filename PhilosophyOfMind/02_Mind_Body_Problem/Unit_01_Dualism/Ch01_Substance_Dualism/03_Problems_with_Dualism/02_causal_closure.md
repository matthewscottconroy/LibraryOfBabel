# Causal Closure and Its Implications

The principle of causal closure — sometimes called "physical completeness" — states that every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. This principle, central to the natural sciences, has become one of the main obstacles to substance dualism and one of the key constraints on any philosophy of mind that takes science seriously.

**Formulating Causal Closure**

The principle needs careful formulation to be philosophically useful. Here are several versions:

**Strong closure**: Every physical event is fully causally determined by prior physical events (according to physical laws).

**Probabilistic closure**: Every physical event's probability is fully determined by prior physical events (for probabilistic physical processes).

**No-gap closure**: There are no physical events that have any causal antecedents that are non-physical.

The strong version is too strong — quantum mechanics shows that physical processes can be probabilistically indeterministic. The probabilistic version may be more defensible.

**The Empirical Basis**

Causal closure is an empirically supported generalization, not an a priori truth. Its support comes from:

- **Energy conservation**: Physics recognizes a finite set of fundamental forces and energy forms. There is no "mental energy" in the physicist's inventory. If mental events had physical causal effects, we would expect to find some form of energy transfer that corresponds to mental causation — but none has been found.

- **The neurological picture**: Neural causation is understood in terms of electrochemical processes, action potentials, synaptic transmission, and so on. These processes are physical through and through. There is no point in the causal story of neural activity where a non-physical cause is invoked.

- **The success of physical explanations**: Physics, chemistry, and biology have been remarkably successful in explaining physical phenomena without invoking non-physical causes. The closure principle is a generalization from this success.

**The Exclusion Problem**

Kim Jaegwon has developed the most systematic treatment of the problems causal closure creates for mental causation. His "exclusion argument" runs roughly:

1. Every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause.
2. Mental events cause physical events.
3. Mental events are not physical events (dualism).
4. Therefore, physical events caused by mental events have two sufficient causes: a physical cause and a mental cause.
5. Systematic causal overdetermination (having two sufficient independent causes) is implausible.
6. Therefore, either (2) is false (epiphenomenalism) or (3) is false (physicalism).

This argument puts enormous pressure on non-reductive positions: if you want to maintain both the causal efficacy of the mental and the causal closure of the physical, you are driven toward identifying mental properties with physical properties.

**Responses from Non-Reductivists**

Non-reductive physicalists — those who hold that mental properties are distinct from physical properties but still physical (in the sense of being realized in physical processes) — have developed responses to the exclusion argument:

**Distinct causal contributions**: Perhaps higher-level (mental) properties make distinct causal contributions from lower-level (physical) properties — contributions that are not simply reduplicative. On this view, there is no overdetermination because the mental and physical causes operate at different levels and make different explanatory contributions.

**Causal explanatory pluralism**: Different levels of description provide different causal explanations without competing with each other. The mental-level explanation and the physical-level explanation of the same event are both valid but serve different explanatory purposes.

These responses remain contested. The exclusion argument continues to be one of the most debated issues in philosophy of mind.

**Implications for Physicalism**

Causal closure does not just create problems for dualism; it also creates challenges for non-reductive physicalism. If physical causal closure holds, and if mental properties are genuinely distinct from physical properties, the question is how mental properties can make any difference to how events unfold. Kim himself eventually concluded that non-reductive physicalism is unstable — you either get full reduction (type identity) or epiphenomenalism about mental properties.

Whether Kim is right is one of the central debates in contemporary philosophy of mind. The issue turns on how to understand the relationship between levels of description and causal power, and on whether there can be genuine causal explanation at multiple levels of the same system without overdetermination.
