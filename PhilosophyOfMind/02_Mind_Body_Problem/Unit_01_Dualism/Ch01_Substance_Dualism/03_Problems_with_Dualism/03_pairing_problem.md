# The Pairing Problem

The pairing problem is a less well-known but philosophically sharp objection to substance dualism, developed by Jaegwon Kim. It targets not the existence of interaction (as the interaction problem does) but the specificity of interaction: if minds are non-spatial, how can a particular mind be paired with a particular body rather than some other body?

**The Problem**

Consider two people, Adam and Eve. Adam has a mind (soul) and a body; Eve has a mind and a body. When Adam decides to raise his hand, it is Adam's hand that goes up, not Eve's. When Eve has a headache, it is Eve who feels it, not Adam.

This seems obvious, but it is not obvious why it should be true if minds are genuinely non-spatial. The spatial location of physical things is what normally accounts for why one thing causally influences another rather than a third. Adam's hand is connected to Adam's nervous system by physical proximity; that is why Adam's neural events affect Adam's hand. But if Adam's mind is non-spatial — if it has no location — then there is no proximity relation to explain why his mental decisions affect his body rather than Eve's.

**The Spatial Account of Causal Pairing**

In the ordinary physical case, what pairs a cause with its effect is spatial proximity, physical contact, or the transmission of a signal through space. The billiard ball goes in this direction because the cue struck it here rather than there. The pairing problem asks: what is the analogous account for mind-body causation?

For Cartesian dualism, no answer is available. Minds are non-spatial; they cannot be "near" or "far" from any body. Kim argues that without a principled account of what pairs a particular mind with a particular body, the claim that they causally interact becomes mysterious in a deep way.

**Proposed Responses**

Several responses have been attempted:

**God pairs them**: Leibniz (pre-established harmony) and Malebranche (occasionalism) in effect invoke God as the principle of pairing — God set up the harmony or continuously coordinates the right mind with the right body. But this requires miraculous divine intervention and does not explain the pairing so much as defer it to God.

**Primitive pairing**: Some dualists suggest that the pairing of a mind and a body is a primitive, unanalyzable fact — a basic feature of the world that cannot be reduced to spatial or physical relations. But invoking primitive, unexplained facts is philosophically unsatisfying, especially when the problem could be dissolved by adopting a different metaphysics.

**Emergent spatial properties**: Some modern dualists have suggested that minds might have something like a spatial location — not full Cartesian spatial extension, but something like a spatial point or region. If mental events occur "at" the location of the brain they are associated with, the pairing problem is solved. But this requires significantly revising the Cartesian conception of mental substance.

**The Problem for Property Dualism**

The pairing problem also applies, in a modified form, to property dualism — the view that mental properties are distinct from but co-instantiated with physical properties. The question becomes: what determines which mental properties are co-instantiated with which physical properties? Why is this neural state associated with this kind of experience?

Property dualists typically invoke supervenience: mental properties supervene on physical properties — same physical properties, same mental properties. But supervenience is a correlation, not an explanation. It says that mental properties track physical properties, but it does not say why. The pairing problem, applied to property dualism, becomes the question of why there are the specific supervenience relations there are.

**Kim's Conclusion**

Kim concludes that the pairing problem, together with the interaction problem and the exclusion argument, makes substance dualism deeply untenable. Physical causation is grounded in physical processes and structures; mental causation, if real, must be similarly grounded. The most natural grounding is identification: mental properties are physical properties. But this is just physicalism.

Whether Kim's argument is decisive is disputed. Some philosophers think property dualism can survive the pairing problem by providing an account of why mental properties are "tied to" physical ones through supervenience relations. But the problem at minimum demands that dualists do more than assert the interaction; they must explain what makes specific mental-physical pairings the ones that obtain.
