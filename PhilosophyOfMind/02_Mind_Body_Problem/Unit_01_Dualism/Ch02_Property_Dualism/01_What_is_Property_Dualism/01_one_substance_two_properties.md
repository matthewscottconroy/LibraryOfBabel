# Property Dualism: One Substance, Two Kinds of Properties

Property dualism is the view that there is only one substance (physical substance), but that substance has two genuinely distinct kinds of properties: physical properties and mental properties. Physical properties are the kind described by physics, chemistry, and neuroscience. Mental properties are phenomenal, experiential properties — the qualitative character of experience. The two kinds of property are not reducible to each other, even though they are properties of the same substance.

**The Motivation**

Property dualism tries to have it both ways. It takes physicalism seriously by rejecting the Cartesian claim that there are two substances — mind is not a separate thing but is a property of the physical system. But it takes the phenomenological tradition seriously by rejecting full reductionism — phenomenal properties are not nothing, and they cannot be analyzed away without remainder into physical properties.

The motivating intuition is the hard problem: even if you knew everything about the physical structure and processes of a brain, you would not thereby know what it is like to be that brain's owner. Phenomenal properties are not derivable from physical properties, even in principle. This "explanatory gap" suggests that phenomenal properties are genuinely distinct from physical ones.

**Supervenience**

The standard property-dualist account of the mind-body relationship invokes supervenience. Mental properties supervene on physical properties: there cannot be a difference in mental properties without a corresponding difference in physical properties. Same physical facts → same mental facts.

This is weaker than identity: it does not say that mental properties are physical properties, only that mental properties are determined by (without being identical to) physical properties. A zombie (physically identical to a human but with no consciousness) would be impossible, because the same physical properties would bring the same mental properties.

But supervenience is one-way: the mental supervenes on the physical, but not vice versa. You can have the same mental properties realized by different physical properties (multiple realizability). And two beings with the same physical properties must have the same mental properties.

**Non-Reductive Physicalism**

Property dualism overlaps significantly with what is often called "non-reductive physicalism" — the view that mental properties are not reducible to physical properties but are still in some sense physical (because they are properties of physical substances and they supervene on physical properties).

The label "property dualism" emphasizes the genuinely distinct character of phenomenal properties. The label "non-reductive physicalism" emphasizes that no dualist ontology of substances is involved. Whether these are the same position depends on exactly how you understand "physical."

**Varieties**

Several distinct positions fall under the property dualism umbrella:

**Emergent property dualism**: Mental properties emerge from physical organization when that organization reaches sufficient complexity. They are not reducible to lower-level physical properties but are in some sense produced by or grounded in them.

**Epiphenomenalism**: Mental properties supervene on physical properties but have no causal efficacy. They are by-products of the physical processes, not causes of them. (Many property dualists reject epiphenomenalism.)

**Interactionist property dualism**: Mental properties have causal power — they influence physical processes. This is attractive but faces the same causal closure problem as substance dualism.

**Russellian monism**: The intrinsic nature of physical properties (what physics leaves out) is phenomenal or proto-phenomenal. This is sometimes classified as a form of property dualism and sometimes as a form of neutral monism.

**The Main Objection**

The main objection to property dualism is Kim's exclusion argument (discussed in the previous chapter): if mental properties are distinct from physical properties, and if physical causal closure holds, then mental properties cannot have causal power without producing overdetermination. If they lack causal power, we get epiphenomenalism — which seems to contradict the evident fact that mental states make a difference to behavior.

Property dualists have worked hard to respond to this argument, but no response has achieved consensus.
