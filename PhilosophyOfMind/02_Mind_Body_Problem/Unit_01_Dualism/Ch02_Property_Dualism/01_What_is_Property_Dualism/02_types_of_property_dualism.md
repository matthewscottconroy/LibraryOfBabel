# Types of Property Dualism

Property dualism is not a single view but a family of views, united by the claim that phenomenal properties are not reducible to physical properties but sharing different commitments about what this entails. Distinguishing the varieties is important for evaluating the view and understanding the options in the mind-body debate.

**Double-Aspect Theory**

The double-aspect theory, most associated with Spinoza and later with Gustav Fechner and Thomas Nagel, holds that mind and body are two aspects of a single underlying reality that is itself neither mental nor physical. The mental and physical are two ways of describing or experiencing the same underlying substance.

On this view, there is no problem of interaction because there is only one thing — the interaction occurs "within" the single substance, not between two distinct substances. The relationship between mental and physical is not causal but constitutive: the mental and physical aspects are different manifestations of the same underlying nature.

Nagel's view — that experience is a feature of the physical world but one that requires both objective (physical) and subjective (phenomenal) perspectives to describe — has this double-aspect character. He argues that a complete account of the world requires acknowledging both perspectives, and that the relationship between them is not identity but correlation or co-instantiation.

**Emergentism**

Emergentist property dualism holds that mental properties are "emergent" from physical properties — they arise when physical systems reach sufficient complexity, but they are not predictable from or reducible to the lower-level physical properties.

Two kinds of emergence are usually distinguished:

- **Weak emergence**: The higher-level property is in principle derivable from lower-level properties, given sufficient computational resources. Consciousness might be weakly emergent from neural processes.

- **Strong emergence**: The higher-level property is not even in principle derivable from lower-level properties — it is a genuinely new kind of phenomenon. Phenomenal consciousness is often argued to be strongly emergent.

The philosophical interest lies in strong emergence. If phenomenal consciousness is strongly emergent — if no amount of lower-level information suffices to predict or explain it — then there are genuine, fundamental discontinuities in nature.

British emergentists like C.D. Broad and Samuel Alexander in the early 20th century developed sophisticated accounts of strong emergence. Contemporary property dualists like David Chalmers embrace strong emergence as the most honest characterization of the relationship between physics and phenomenology.

**Naturalistic Dualism**

Chalmers's own position is sometimes called "naturalistic dualism." He accepts that phenomenal consciousness is a genuinely distinct feature of the world — not reducible to physical properties — but insists that it is not supernatural. It is a natural phenomenon, subject to natural laws (including psychophysical laws linking physical states to phenomenal states), and it can be studied scientifically.

On this view, the laws of nature include not only physical laws but also "psychophysical laws" that specify which phenomenal states are associated with which physical states. These laws are as fundamental as the physical laws and cannot be derived from them.

The attractiveness of this view is that it takes phenomenal consciousness seriously without invoking the supernatural. Its difficulty is that it is unclear what these psychophysical laws are, how we could discover them, and whether the notion of such fundamental laws is coherent.

**Russellian Monism**

A growing position is Russellian monism (or panpsychism in its modern form). Bertrand Russell noted that physics describes the *structural-relational* properties of matter — how things are arranged, how they interact — but does not tell us about the *intrinsic* properties of matter, what matter is in itself.

Russellian monism proposes that the intrinsic properties of matter are phenomenal or proto-phenomenal. Consciousness is not a new kind of thing that emerges at a certain level of complexity; it is the intrinsic nature of matter that was always there, becoming organized in richer ways as complexity increases.

This view is sometimes property dualist (mental and physical are different kinds of properties) and sometimes monist (there is only one kind of intrinsic property, and it is phenomenal). Its relationship to standard property dualism is complex.

**The Taxonomy**

The key distinctions among property-dualist views:

1. What is the relationship between mental and physical properties — supervenience, identity (rejected), or co-instantiation?
2. Are mental properties causally efficacious?
3. What grounds the mental-physical relationship — natural laws, emergence, constitution?
4. Does the view have idealist or neutral-monist implications?

Working through these questions for each variety illuminates the conceptual space available to someone who wants to take phenomenal consciousness seriously without either Cartesian substance dualism or straightforward physicalist reduction.
