# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Property Dualism

## Key Arguments

**The Knowledge Argument (Frank Jackson)**
Jackson argues that Mary, a neuroscientist who has lived in a black-and-white room and knows every physical fact about color vision, learns something new when she first sees red—she learns what it is like to see red. Since she already knew all physical facts, the new knowledge must be of a non-physical fact (a phenomenal fact). The argument's structure: complete physical knowledge does not entail complete knowledge of phenomenal experience, therefore phenomenal facts are not physical facts, and phenomenal properties are not physical properties.

**The Zombie Argument (Chalmers)**
Chalmers argues that we can coherently conceive of beings that are physical and functional duplicates of human beings but lack any phenomenal consciousness—philosophical zombies. Since conceivability is evidence of metaphysical possibility (especially for claims about essential natures), zombies are possible, and this means phenomenal consciousness is not logically entailed by physical-functional facts. If phenomenal properties could be absent while all physical properties were present, they cannot be identical to any physical properties.

**The Conceivability-to-Possibility Inference**
A crucial step in both the zombie argument and the knowledge argument is the inference from conceivability to metaphysical possibility: if we can coherently conceive of a scenario, then that scenario is possible in the relevant metaphysical sense. Chalmers defends this inference using a two-dimensional semantic framework: primary intension (what a concept picks out in the scenario conceived) vs. secondary intension (what the concept rigidly designates). Phenomenal concepts have the same primary and secondary intensions, so the conceivability of zombies implies their genuine possibility.

**The Explanatory Gap Argument (Joseph Levine)**
Levine argues that even if phenomenal states turn out to be identical to physical states, there would remain an unexplained explanatory gap: we can understand why C-fiber firing is correlated with pain, but we cannot understand why it should feel like anything at all. The gap is not just epistemic (we happen not to see the connection) but reflects a structural feature of phenomenal concepts—they don't "fit" physical concepts in the way that other physical identities fit. This argument supports property dualism by showing that phenomenal properties resist the kind of functional explanation that other properties receive.

**The Argument from Natural Supervenience**
Chalmers' "naturalistic dualism" holds that phenomenal properties supervene on physical properties (no phenomenal difference without a physical difference) but are not logically entailed by or reducible to physical properties. The supervenience is a natural law, not a logical necessity. This position attempts to honor both the systematic correlation between brain states and experience (supervenience) and the irreducibility of phenomenal properties (their non-entailment by functional facts). Critics argue that supervenience without reduction is unstable and leaves the nature of the supervenience relation unexplained.

## Core Concepts

**Property Dualism**
Property dualism holds that while there is only one type of substance (matter), there are two types of properties: physical properties and phenomenal (or qualia) properties. Mental properties are real and causally relevant but are not identical to any physical or functional properties. This position preserves the physicalist monism about substance while acknowledging the special character of phenomenal experience, and it includes both epiphenomenalist versions (phenomenal properties are causally inert) and interactionist versions (phenomenal properties have causal effects over and above physical ones).

**Phenomenal Consciousness**
Phenomenal consciousness (or P-consciousness) refers to the subjective, qualitative character of experience—what it is like to perceive, feel, or think. It is the redness of red, the painfulness of pain, the specific character of a musical tone. Block introduced the term to distinguish phenomenal from access consciousness (A-consciousness): an internal state can be available for reasoning and report (A-conscious) without it necessarily being phenomenally conscious, and vice versa. The distinction is fundamental to property dualism because phenomenal properties are precisely what resist functional analysis.

**Qualia**
Qualia (singular: quale) are the intrinsic, qualitative, subjective properties of conscious experience—the "raw feels" of sensation. They are typically characterized as intrinsic (not relational), private (directly accessible only to the subject), and ineffable (not fully capturable in functional or physical description). Property dualists argue that qualia are real phenomenal properties not reducible to functional or physical properties, while eliminativists (Dennett) deny that qualia, so characterized, exist. The existence and nature of qualia is perhaps the most hotly contested topic in contemporary philosophy of mind.

**Two-Dimensionalism**
Chalmers' two-dimensional semantics holds that every expression has two intensions: a primary intension (what the expression picks out as a function of which world is actual, used when reasoning about scenarios) and a secondary intension (what the expression picks out as a function of possible worlds, given the actual world). For phenomenal terms, the primary and secondary intensions coincide: "pain" refers to its phenomenal nature essentially, not contingently, so there is no room for a posteriori identity that could bridge the explanatory gap. Two-dimensionalism underwrites the conceivability-to-possibility inference in the zombie argument.

**Epiphenomenalism (Property Version)**
Property epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal properties are caused by physical properties but have no further causal effects on the physical world. My experience of pain is caused by C-fiber firing, but the phenomenal character of that experience plays no causal role in my subsequent behavior. This view avoids the problem of causal overdetermination but has the deeply counterintuitive consequence that phenomenal experience never makes any difference to what we do or say, including our philosophical theorizing about phenomenal experience.

## Thought Experiments

**Mary's Room**
Mary knows every physical and functional fact about human color vision—wavelength absorption, neural pathways, behavioral responses—but has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room. Upon seeing red for the first time, does she learn something new? Jackson's answer is yes: she learns what it is like to see red, a phenomenal fact not entailed by her prior physical knowledge. The thought experiment has generated an extensive literature: ability hypothesis (Lewis, Nemirow), phenomenal concepts strategy (Loar), and representationalist responses all attempt to explain away the apparent knowledge gain without conceding property dualism.

**The Philosophical Zombie**
A zombie is a being physically and functionally identical to a normal human being in every respect except that there is nothing it is like to be it—it lacks phenomenal consciousness entirely. Chalmers argues this scenario is coherently conceivable: nothing in our concept of physical or functional organization entails phenomenal consciousness. The conceivability of zombies is designed to show that physicalism cannot capture everything there is to say about the mind. Dennett's denial that zombies are genuinely conceivable—because we cannot coherently hold all the functional details constant while varying phenomenal consciousness—is the main physicalist response.

**The Inverted Qualia Scenario**
Imagine a person whose phenomenal experience of red is qualitatively identical to my experience of green, and vice versa—but whose color behavior is perfectly normal (they stop at red lights, call grass green, etc.). If such inversion is possible—and both the subject and I function identically—then phenomenal properties are not constituted by functional role. The thought experiment targets functionalism specifically and supports property dualism by showing that what it is like to have an experience is not fully determined by the functional role of that experience. Shoemaker's response invokes the functional role of introspective concepts to argue inverted qualia are impossible.
