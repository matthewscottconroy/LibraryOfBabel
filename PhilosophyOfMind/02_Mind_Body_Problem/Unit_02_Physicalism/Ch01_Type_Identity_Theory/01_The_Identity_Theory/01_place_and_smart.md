# Place and Smart: The Type Identity Theory

The type identity theory, proposed independently by U.T. Place and J.J.C. Smart in the 1950s, was the first systematic physicalist account of mind in the analytic tradition. It represented a bold attempt to solve the mind-body problem by simply identifying mental state types with brain state types — pain is C-fiber firing, the visual experience of blue is such-and-such neural process, and so on.

**Place's Paper**

U.T. Place's 1956 paper "Is Consciousness a Brain Process?" is typically cited as the founding document of the type identity theory. Place was motivated by a philosophical concern: the prevailing options seemed to be either Cartesian dualism (which faced the interaction problem) or behaviorism (which seemed to leave out consciousness). Could there be a third option?

Place suggested there could: consciousness might simply *be* a brain process. Not "correlated with" or "caused by" but genuinely, literally identical to. Just as lightning is (we now know) an electrical discharge, and temperature is mean molecular kinetic energy, consciousness might be a form of neural activity.

**Smart's Elaboration**

J.J.C. Smart's 1959 paper "Sensations and Brain Processes" elaborated and defended the identity theory with more philosophical rigor. Smart deployed "Occam's razor" for psychological entities: if we can explain everything about mental phenomena without positing anything beyond physical brain processes, we should not posit anything more.

Smart also introduced a distinction between *topic-neutral* and *topic-committed* descriptions. When I say "I am in pain," I am not describing a specifically mental entity; I am using a topic-neutral description that is compatible with the pain's being a brain process. The word "pain" does not commit me to any particular ontology; it just picks out whatever internal state plays the pain role.

**The Contingent Identity Claim**

The type identity claim is that mental-physical identities are contingent, empirical discoveries — like the identity of water and H₂O, or of lightning and electrical discharge. These were not known a priori; they were discovered through scientific investigation.

The appropriate attitude, on this view, is to treat psychophysical identity claims as hypotheses to be confirmed or disconfirmed by neuroscience. The program is: find the neural correlates of specific mental state types, and propose that the mental type is identical to the neural type.

**The Theory's Appeal**

The identity theory is ontologically economical: it does not multiply entities beyond necessity. Mental states exist; brain states exist; these are the same things. No additional non-physical entities are needed. The mind-body problem is solved by elimination: once we recognize that mental states are brain states, the apparent difficulty of relating two different kinds of thing disappears.

The theory is also scientifically respectable: it is the kind of thing neuroscience might in principle establish. The research program of finding the neural correlates of consciousness is, in effect, the research program of the identity theory.

**The Multiple Realizability Objection**

The decisive objection to the type identity theory came from Hilary Putnam: multiple realizability. Pain, as a mental state type, can be realized in many different physical substrates. The pain of a human being involves C-fibers (in some sense); the pain of an octopus involves different neural processes; the pain of a hypothetical silicon-based alien would involve still different processes. But pain is pain in all these cases.

If pain is identical to C-fiber firing in humans, then by the logic of identity, the octopus's pain is not pain — it cannot be C-fiber firing because octopuses have no C-fibers. But this seems absurd. Octopuses feel pain; their pain just happens to be neurally implemented differently.

Multiple realizability shows that if mental state types are defined by their functional role (causing avoidance, being caused by tissue damage, etc.), they cannot be identified with any specific physical type, since the same functional role can be realized by different physical processes.

This objection drove the move from type identity theory to functionalism — the view that mental states are defined by functional role rather than physical constitution. But whether the identity theory can be salvaged by moving to "local" or "species-specific" identities is a matter of ongoing debate.
