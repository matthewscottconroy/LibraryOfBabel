# Putnam's Multiple Realizability Challenge

Hilary Putnam's argument from multiple realizability, developed in a series of papers in the 1960s and 1970s, had the effect of a philosophical earthquake. It forced a major reconception of the mind-body problem and, for a time, seemed to establish functionalism as the only viable physicalist alternative to the identity theory. Understanding the argument and its reception is essential for understanding contemporary philosophy of mind.

**The Core Argument**

The argument has a simple core. Consider pain. Pain in humans is presumably correlated with a specific neural state — some kind of activity in the nociceptive system, involving C-fibers and processing in the anterior cingulate cortex. The type identity theory says that pain *is* this neural state.

But other animals feel pain too. Vertebrates with very different nervous systems, non-vertebrates, perhaps even very different kinds of organisms. The neural realizer of pain in an octopus is very different from the neural realizer of pain in a human. The neural realizer of pain in a hypothetical silicon-based life form (if one could experience pain) would be different again.

If pain = C-fiber activation in humans, and the octopus's pain state is not C-fiber activation, then either the octopus's state is not pain (false) or the identity claim is wrong about something (we must say: pain in humans = C-fiber activation, but pain as such is not identical to any single physical type).

More generally: any mental state that can be realized by multiple different physical types cannot be identical to any single physical type. Mental state types "criss-cross" physical types; any given mental type can be multiply realized.

**The Autonomy of Psychology**

Putnam drew a strong conclusion from multiple realizability: psychology is an autonomous special science — it cannot be reduced to neuroscience. Mental types pick out real patterns that cut across different physical realizations. To reduce psychology to neuroscience would be to miss these patterns.

This connects to the general philosophy of special sciences articulated by Fodor: geology, economics, psychology — these sciences discover generalizations that are genuinely lawlike but are not reducible to more fundamental sciences. The reducibility of a higher-level science to a lower one is not guaranteed by physicalism; it must be established case by case.

**The Functionalist Alternative**

The natural response to multiple realizability is functionalism: define mental states by their functional roles — their causal relationships to inputs, outputs, and other mental states — rather than by their physical constitution. Pain is whatever state is caused by tissue damage and causes avoidance and distress, regardless of what physical process plays that role.

Functionalism accommodates multiple realizability because it does not require any specific physical realization. The same function can be implemented in neurons, silicon, or anything else with the right causal organization.

**Putnam's Self-Criticism**

In a remarkable philosophical about-face, Putnam later repudiated his own multiple realizability argument — not because he thought the identity theory was right, but because he thought functionalism was also wrong. Functionalism, he argued, faces its own problems:

- **Wide content and the environment**: What a mental state is about depends on the external environment, not just on internal functional organization (the Twin Earth argument, which Putnam himself developed).
- **The absent qualia problem**: Functionally identical systems might lack phenomenal experience.
- **The nature of functional properties**: What makes two states have the "same" functional role? This question is harder to answer than it seems.

Putnam's intellectual trajectory — from identity theory to functionalism to a deep skepticism about all standard positions — reflects the genuine difficulty of the mind-body problem. He ended up defending something like Aristotelian hylomorphism: minds are the forms of living bodies, and this cannot be analyzed in terms of either physical substance or functional organization.

**The Legacy**

Multiple realizability remains a central concept in philosophy of mind and philosophy of science, even if its implications are more contested than they initially appeared. It has sparked debates about:
- What counts as "multiple realization"?
- Does multiple realizability establish the autonomy of psychology, or can it be accommodated within a reductionist framework?
- What are the implications for mind-brain identity if only "token" identity is maintained?

These debates continue to be productive, and multiple realizability remains one of the most important tools in the philosophical analysis of the mind-body relationship.
