# Responses to the Multiple Realizability Argument

Multiple realizability was initially seen as decisive against the type identity theory and in favor of functionalism. But subsequent work has complicated this picture considerably. Several physicalist responses have been developed, and the debate has refined our understanding of what reduction amounts to and what it would take.

**Local Reduction (Species-Specific Identity)**

The most conservative response is to accept that universal type identities are unavailable but maintain local identities. Pain in humans = C-fiber activation; pain in octopuses = some octopus-specific neural state; pain in aliens = some alien-specific state. Each realization gets its own identity claim.

This move is sometimes accused of missing the point of the psychological generalization: what is interesting about pain is that it functions similarly across different creatures, regardless of its physical realization. If we decompose pain into human-pain, octopus-pain, etc., we lose the cross-species generalization.

But defenders of local reduction argue that the cross-species generalization is just that — a generalization about things that play similar functional roles — and does not require a single type identity. The generalization "pain causes avoidance" is true because all realizers of the pain-role cause avoidance, not because there is a single type to which the generalization applies.

**The Disjunctive Response**

A related move: define the mental type as the disjunction of all its physical realizers. Pain = C-fiber activation OR octopus-nociceptor-activation OR silicon-pain-analog OR... Each disjunct is a physical type; the disjunction of physical types is still a physical type; so pain is still a physical type.

Functionalists and non-reductivists object that this "pain" — understood as a disjunction — is not a natural kind. It is not a scientifically significant grouping; there is no explanation of why these disjuncts belong together other than their functional role. And if the unifying factor is the functional role, then it is the functional type that does the real explanatory work.

**Revisiting the Independence of Psychology**

Fodor's argument from multiple realizability to the autonomy of special sciences has been challenged. Ned Block and Jerry Fodor eventually acknowledged that multiple realizability does not straightforwardly establish the autonomy of psychology; it depends on substantive empirical claims about whether psychological generalizations have physical exceptions.

Kim argues that multiply realizable properties are not "natural" enough to support genuine laws and generalizations. If pain is realized differently in humans, octopuses, and aliens, the "law" connecting pain to avoidance is really a set of separate, possibly unrelated generalizations. Psychology would then be a taxonomy of functional similarities rather than a science of natural kinds.

**Graded Realizability**

A more nuanced response distinguishes degrees of realizability. Some mental states are more constrained in their physical realization than others. Color experience is closely tied to the specific photoreceptors and processing pathways of vision; it may not be multiply realizable in any very different substrate. Pain, by contrast, might be more widely realizable because its functional role is simpler.

If some mental states are more tightly constrained in their physical realization, local reduction might apply straightforwardly. Only the most abstract functional kinds — those defined by very general functional roles — might resist reduction.

**The Computational Level**

Marr's levels-of-analysis framework suggests a way of thinking about multiple realizability that does not force a choice between identity and functionalism. At the computational level, a cognitive process is characterized by the function it computes. At the algorithmic level, we specify how it computes that function. At the implementation level, we specify how the algorithm is physically realized.

Multiple realizability operates at the algorithmic and implementation levels: the same computation can be carried out by different algorithms and different physical implementations. But this does not undermine the reality of the computational-level description; it shows that the computational level is explanatorily autonomous.

This framework allows for genuine explanation at the mental/computational level without requiring type identity. The debate is then about whether this level of description suffices to account for consciousness and intentionality — or whether something about the specific physical implementation matters for the specifically phenomenal aspects of mind.
