# Key Thinkers: Type Identity Theory

---

**U.T. Place (1924–2000)**
British philosopher. Place's 1956 paper "Is Consciousness a Brain Process?" launched type identity theory. He argued for a contingent empirical identity between consciousness and brain processes — not a logical or conceptual identity but a genuine scientific discovery like the identity of water with H₂O. His "topic neutral" strategy deflected linguistic objections.
*Key works:* "Is Consciousness a Brain Process?", collected papers in *Identifying the Mind* (2004)

---

**J.J.C. Smart (1920–2012)**
Australian philosopher. Smart extended and defended Place's identity theory, introducing the key methodological move of "topic-neutral" mental descriptions that neither commit to nor preclude a physical identity. His response to Leibniz's Law objections (via the notion of contingent identity) shaped subsequent debates.
*Key works:* "Sensations and Brain Processes," *Philosophy and Scientific Realism*, *Our Place in the Universe*

---

**Herbert Feigl (1902–1988)**
Austro-American logical positivist who independently argued for the identity of mental and physical states through his concept of "nomological danglers" — the inelegant theoretical posits required if mental states are not identical to physical ones.
*Key works:* "The 'Mental' and the 'Physical'" (in *Minnesota Studies in the Philosophy of Science*, Vol. II)

---

**Jaegwon Kim (1934–2019)**
Korean-American philosopher. Kim's causal exclusion argument showed that non-reductive physicalism faces a dilemma: either mental properties are causally irrelevant (epiphenomenal) or they are reducible to physical properties. He concluded that type identity theory — or some form of reduction — is required for mental causation to be genuine.
*Key works:* *Supervenience and Mind*, *Mind in a Physical World*, *Physicalism, Or Something Near Enough*
