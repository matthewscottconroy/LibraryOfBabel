# Davidson's Anomalous Monism

Donald Davidson's anomalous monism, presented in "Mental Events" (1970), is one of the most influential positions in philosophy of mind. It attempts to hold together three apparently inconsistent claims: (1) mental events causally interact with physical events, (2) causal interactions are governed by strict laws, and (3) there are no strict laws governing mental events. How can these be consistent?

**The Three Principles**

Davidson's three principles:

1. **The Principle of Causal Interaction**: Mental events — events like decisions, perceptions, beliefs — causally interact with physical events. When I decide to move my arm (a mental event), my arm moves (a physical event). When I see the red light (a physical event starting with photons), I have a visual experience (a mental event).

2. **The Nomological Character of Causality**: Events stand in causal relations only when there are strict, exceptionless laws that subsume them. Causation is nomologically backed.

3. **The Anomalism of the Mental**: Mental events, under their mental descriptions, are not subsumed by strict (exceptionless, deterministic) laws. There are no strict psychophysical laws connecting mental and physical descriptions, and no strict psychological laws connecting mental events to each other.

These three principles generate a tension: principles (1) and (2) together imply that mental events are subsumable under laws; principle (3) says they are not. Davidson's resolution: the causal laws that subsume mental events are physical laws, not mental ones. Mental events, under their physical descriptions, fall under physical laws.

**Token Identity**

The resolution requires that every mental event token is identical to some physical event token. When I make a decision (mental event), this event is identical to some neural event (physical event). As a neural event, it can be subsumed under physical laws; as a decision, it resists subsumption under strict laws.

This is *token* identity: each particular mental event is identical to some particular physical event. But there are no *type* identities: the mental type "decision" is not identical to any physical type. Different decisions (or decisions in different organisms) might be tokens of different physical types.

**The Anomalism of the Mental**

Why are there no strict laws governing mental events? Davidson's answer: mental concepts are constitutively rational. Beliefs and desires are interpreted as rational attitudes — as making sense in light of other beliefs and desires. The norms of rationality that govern mental-concept application are holistic and indeterminate in ways that physical laws are not.

Physical laws are "exceptionless": they cover all cases without exception, given the right description. Mental generalizations are ceteris paribus ("other things being equal"): "people believe what they perceive" is true other things being equal, but it has exceptions and cannot be sharpened into an exceptionless law without abandoning the mental vocabulary.

**The Problem: Mental Causation**

Davidson's anomalous monism has a well-known problem: it seems to make mental properties epiphenomenal. If causation is backed by physical laws, and if the mental properties of mental events are not picked out by those laws, then the mental properties are causally irrelevant. The event causes something as a physical event (under a physical description), but not as a mental event (under a mental description).

This is a version of the exclusion problem. Even if token identity holds — each mental event is a physical event — the mental properties of those events might still be explanatorily idle.

Davidson's response: the anomalousness of the mental is not epiphenomenalism. Mental properties are not causally inert — they are causally relevant under their mental descriptions. It is just that the laws backing the causation are physical. The mental and physical descriptions both capture genuine features of the events; neither is eliminable.

Whether this response adequately addresses the concern is debated. Kim argues that Davidson's position cannot make mental properties genuinely causally relevant — that it is epiphenomenalism in disguise. The debate continues.

**Supervenience**

Davidson also introduces the concept of supervenience into the mind-body debate: "mental characteristics are in some sense dependent, or supervenient, on physical characteristics." Same physical facts → same mental facts. But this does not entail reducibility — there can be supervenience without reduction.

Davidson's use of supervenience was influential in establishing it as a key concept for characterizing the mind-body relationship while avoiding both reduction and dualism.
