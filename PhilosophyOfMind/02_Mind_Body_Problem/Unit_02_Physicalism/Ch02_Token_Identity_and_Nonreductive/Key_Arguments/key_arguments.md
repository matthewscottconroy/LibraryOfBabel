# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Token Identity and Non-Reductive Physicalism

## Key Arguments

**Davidson's Argument for Anomalous Monism**
Davidson argues that mental events are identical to physical events (token identity) but that there are no strict psychophysical laws. The argument has three premises: (1) mental events causally interact with physical events; (2) where there is causation there is a strict law (the nomological character of causality); (3) there are no strict psychophysical laws (the anomalism of the mental). The only consistent position is that mental events are physical events—they fall under strict physical laws in their physical descriptions, while lacking strict laws under mental descriptions. This position tries to have physicalism without reduction.

**The Anomalism of the Mental**
Davidson argues that mental descriptions are inherently holistic and normative: attributing beliefs and desires requires that the attributed states cohere with one another and with behavior according to the principle of charity. Physical descriptions carry no such holistic-normative constraint. Because mental and physical descriptions are governed by fundamentally different constitutive principles, there cannot be strict, exceptionless laws connecting mental and physical descriptions. This means the mental is not reducible to the physical, even though mental events are physical events.

**Kim's Exclusion Argument Against Non-Reductive Physicalism**
Kim argues that non-reductive physicalism faces a fatal dilemma: either mental properties are causally efficacious in virtue of their physical realizers (in which case mental causation reduces to physical causation, and the mental has no distinctive causal role), or mental properties are efficacious in their own right (in which case physical events are causally overdetermined—they have both a sufficient physical cause and an independent mental cause). Since overdetermination is implausible as the general rule, non-reductive physicalism cannot coherently maintain both the causal relevance of mental properties and the causal closure of the physical.

**The Supervenience Argument for Non-Reductive Physicalism**
Non-reductive physicalists argue that mental properties supervene on physical properties—there can be no mental difference without a physical difference—while not being reducible to them. Supervenience secures a dependence of mind on body (ruling out dualism) without requiring that mental descriptions be translatable into physical ones (ruling out type identity). The challenge is to explain what supervenience amounts to without collapsing into either reduction or mere epiphenomenalism.

**Multiple Realizability as a Defense of Non-Reduction**
Putnam's multiple realizability argument supports non-reductive physicalism: since mental state types can be realized in different physical substrates, there is no single physical type to identify the mental type with. But mental states are still physical events—each token mental state is some token physical state. The correct level of description for psychological generalizations is functional, not physical. Non-reductive physicalism thus grounds psychological laws in functional-level descriptions while maintaining that their instances are physical events.

## Core Concepts

**Token Identity**
Token identity theory holds that every particular (token) mental event is identical to some particular (token) physical event, without requiring that mental types be identical to physical types. Davidson's anomalous monism is the canonical token identity view. This position accommodates multiple realizability (different token pains can be different physical tokens) while maintaining that there are no non-physical events. The challenge it faces is explaining how mental properties, not being physical properties, can be causally relevant.

**Supervenience**
Supervenience is a relation of dependence: A-properties supervene on B-properties if and only if there can be no difference in A-properties without a difference in B-properties. For the mind-body problem, strong supervenience holds that for any possible world, if two things are physically identical, they are mentally identical. Supervenience is weaker than reduction (it doesn't specify any functional or structural translation) but stronger than mere correlation (it's a modal claim). Non-reductive physicalists embrace supervenience as the right way to articulate physical dependence without reductive commitment.

**Anomalous Monism**
Davidson's anomalous monism holds three theses simultaneously: (1) at least some mental events causally interact with physical events; (2) events related as cause and effect fall under strict laws; (3) there are no strict psychophysical laws. The resolution is that mental events are identical to physical events; under their physical descriptions they instantiate strict laws, but under their mental descriptions they don't. The position is "monist" because there is only one kind of event, and "anomalous" because mental events, as mental, are lawless.

**Mental Causation**
Mental causation is the causal efficacy of mental states: the way in which beliefs, desires, and intentions cause behavior. Non-reductive physicalists need to explain how mental properties, which are distinct from physical properties, can be causally efficacious without violating the causal closure of the physical. Kim argues that this is not possible: either mental properties make a causal difference beyond physical properties (violation of closure) or they don't make a causal difference (epiphenomenalism). This is the central problem for non-reductive physicalism.

**The Exclusion Principle**
The exclusion principle (Kim) holds that if a physical event E has a sufficient physical cause C at t, then no other event at t is a cause of E (unless there is genuine overdetermination). Applied to mental causation: if my arm movement has a sufficient neural cause, the mental cause (my decision) is either identical to the neural cause (reduction) or excluded from being a genuine cause. This principle forces a choice between reductive physicalism and epiphenomenalism and is the main argument against non-reductive positions.

## Thought Experiments

**Davidson's Swerving Car**
Davidson asks us to consider a mental event—say, my noticing a red light—and a physical event—say, my foot pressing the brake. These are causally related, but under which descriptions? The mental event is caused by and causes other events in a nomologically irregular way when described in mental terms. But under its physical description, it falls under strict laws. The thought experiment illustrates anomalous monism: the same event is both mental (described as a noticing) and physical (described as a neural firing), with different nomic properties under each description.

**Kim's Problem of Epiphenomenal Properties**
Kim invites us to imagine a world in which mental properties are real, physical events have sufficient physical causes, and mental properties supervene on physical properties. In this world, my decision (as a mental property) never makes any difference to what I do, because the physical events that realize my decision always have sufficient physical causes. But then why do I ever report having mental states? My reports are physical events too, and they have physical causes—not my phenomenal states. The thought experiment reveals that non-reductive physicalism faces a self-undermining regress: if mental properties are causally impotent, the argument for non-reductive physicalism cannot be based on our mental knowledge of our own states.

**The Absent-Minded Professor (Supervenience)**
Consider two possible worlds that are atom-for-atom identical. Non-reductive physicalists claim these worlds must also be mentally identical (strong supervenience). Now imagine a case where two people are in identical physical states but differ mentally—one feels genuine sadness, the other feels nothing. Supervenience rules this out. But the thought experiment probes the modal force of supervenience: is the dependence of mental on physical a necessary truth (as physicalism requires) or a mere natural law that could be broken? Answering this question determines whether supervenience is strong enough to count as a genuinely physicalist thesis.
