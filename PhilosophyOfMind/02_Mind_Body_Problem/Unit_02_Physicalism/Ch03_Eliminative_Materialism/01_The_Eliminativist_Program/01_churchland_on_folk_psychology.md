# The Churchlands on Folk Psychology

Paul and Patricia Churchland are the most influential proponents of eliminative materialism — the view that folk psychological concepts (belief, desire, intention, pain) will eventually be replaced, not reduced, by a mature neuroscience. Their program is radical but rigorously argued, and engaging with it forces a clear articulation of what is at stake in the mind-body debate.

**What Is Folk Psychology?**

Folk psychology is the everyday theory of mind that we all deploy when we explain and predict human behavior. When we say "She ate because she was hungry," "He ran away because he was afraid," "She studied because she wanted to pass," we are using folk psychology. The theory posits mental states — beliefs, desires, pains, emotions, memories — as the causes of behavior.

Paul Churchland, in *Scientific Realism and the Plasticity of Mind* (1979) and *Matter and Consciousness* (1984), argues that folk psychology is a theory in the full philosophical sense: it makes empirical predictions, it attributes theoretical entities (beliefs, desires), and it can be evaluated for its success in explaining and predicting behavior.

**The Empirical Track Record of Folk Psychology**

Churchland argues that folk psychology has a poor empirical track record. It has failed to explain:
- The nature of mental illness and its treatment
- The mechanisms of perception, memory, and learning
- The basis of intelligence
- The sleep/wakefulness cycle
- The causes and significance of dreams

These are not peripheral failures. They concern central aspects of mental life. A theory with this kind of explanatory poverty should prompt serious reconsideration. Perhaps the categories of folk psychology do not carve mental reality at its joints.

**The Analogy with Past Replacements**

Churchland draws the analogy with past scientific revolutions. Folk theories of heat (phlogiston), of vital force (élan vital), of caloric fluid, and of impetus have all been replaced by modern science. Not reduced — replaced. The old categories turned out not to refer to anything real. We do not say that phlogiston is identical to oxygen; we say that the phlogiston theory was simply wrong.

Similarly, Churchland predicts, folk psychological categories — belief, desire, and so on — will be replaced by neuroscientific categories. We will explain behavior in terms of neural activation patterns, synaptic weights, attractor states, and so on. The old folk categories will be retired, not translated.

**Patricia Churchland's Neurophilosophy**

Patricia Churchland's *Neurophilosophy* (1986) develops the positive program: philosophy of mind should be conducted in close dialogue with neuroscience. Philosophical theories about the nature of perception, memory, consciousness, and emotion should be constrained by and responsive to neuroscientific findings.

This is a methodological claim as much as a metaphysical one. Patricia Churchland is skeptical of armchair theorizing about the nature of mental concepts when neuroscience is available to constrain and inform the discussion. The philosopher's job is not to analyze folk psychological concepts but to help develop an adequate empirical theory of the mind.

**Connectionism and Eliminativism**

The Churchlands have been enthusiastic advocates of connectionist (neural network) models of cognition. On their view, the distributed, activation-pattern-based representations characteristic of neural networks are much closer to the actual computational architecture of the brain than the discrete, symbolic representations of classical cognitive science.

Classical cognitive science uses folk psychological categories like "belief" and "desire" as its theoretical vocabulary. If connectionist models are more accurate, these classical categories may be simply wrong — not approximations of the truth but fundamentally misconceived. The right description of cognitive processes may not use belief/desire vocabulary at all.

**The Significance of the Churchland Program**

Whether or not eliminative materialism is correct, the Churchland program has had an important effect: it forced philosophers to take seriously the possibility that folk psychology might be wrong, and to think carefully about what the criteria for evaluating folk psychological concepts should be. The debate about eliminativism is a debate about the evidential standards for psychological ontology — about what kinds of considerations determine whether mental state types are real.
