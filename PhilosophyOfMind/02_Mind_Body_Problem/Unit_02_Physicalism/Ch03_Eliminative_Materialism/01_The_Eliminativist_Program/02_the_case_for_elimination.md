# The Case for Elimination

Eliminative materialism is counterintuitive: the idea that beliefs and desires might not exist, in the way that phlogiston and witches do not exist, strikes most people as obviously wrong. We know we have beliefs; the very act of reasoning about eliminative materialism seems to require beliefs. How could eliminativism be right?

The case for elimination proceeds in several steps. Understanding the positive argument makes clear that eliminativism is a serious philosophical position, not mere contrarianism.

**Step One: Folk Psychology Is a Theory**

The first step is to establish that folk psychology — the common-sense framework of beliefs, desires, intentions, and emotions — is a theory in the philosophical sense. It is not a direct report of evident facts; it is an implicit theoretical framework that posits unobservable entities (mental states) to explain observable behavior.

This theoretical character is established by noting that folk psychology has a structure analogous to scientific theories: it has laws ("People tend to believe what they perceive"), theoretical entities (beliefs, desires), and makes predictions ("Given that Jones believes the train leaves at five and wants to catch it, she will head for the station").

**Step Two: Theories Can Be Wrong**

Once folk psychology is a theory, it is subject to the same epistemic standards as other theories. Scientific history is full of theories that seemed obviously right and turned out to be radically wrong. Aristotelian physics seemed obviously right. Vitalism seemed obviously right. Ptolemaic astronomy seemed obviously right. Each was replaced, not because it was completely without predictive success, but because a better alternative emerged that explained more and assumed less.

The eliminativist does not claim that folk psychology is useless; it has obvious practical value. The claim is that its ontological categories — the entities it posits — may not be real. Just as Ptolemaic astronomy made correct predictions while positing fictitious epicycles, folk psychology might make correct predictions while positing fictitious mental entities.

**Step Three: Folk Psychology Has Systematic Failures**

Churchland cites the systematic explanatory failures of folk psychology as evidence that its categories do not carve reality at its joints. Consider the phenomena that folk psychology cannot explain:
- Why does anesthesia produce unconsciousness?
- Why does alcohol impair coordination and judgment?
- Why do some people wake from comas with personality changes?
- What is the neural basis of working memory?

These are not fringe questions. They are central to understanding how the mind works, and folk psychology has nothing to say about them. A theory that cannot even approach these questions is a theory in trouble.

**Step Four: Better Alternatives Are Emerging**

The eliminativist's prediction is not just that folk psychology is wrong, but that neuroscience will provide better categories. The promise of neuroscience is to replace vague folk categories (belief, desire) with precise, empirically grounded categories (activation patterns, synaptic connections, attractor states).

Whether neuroscience has actually delivered on this promise is debated. Contemporary neuroscience has produced enormous insight into specific processes (visual processing, memory consolidation, motor control) but has not yet produced a comprehensive alternative to folk psychology for describing the full range of intentional behavior.

**The Self-Refutation Problem (Revisited)**

The most common objection to eliminative materialism is that it is self-refuting: the eliminativist uses beliefs and desires (their own) to argue that there are no beliefs and desires. If beliefs do not exist, the eliminativist cannot believe that eliminativism is true.

Churchland's response: this is question-begging. Of course we currently use folk psychological categories to describe the eliminativist's assertions. But the claim is that these categories may be false. If they are false, then the description of the eliminativist as "believing" eliminativism is false — not a refutation of eliminativism but a consequence of it. To argue that eliminativism is self-refuting already assumes folk psychology is correct.

**The Verdict**

Eliminative materialism has few outright adherents — most philosophers think folk psychology is at least approximately correct, and that reduction rather than elimination is the appropriate relationship between psychology and neuroscience. But the eliminativist challenge has been philosophically productive: it forces clarity about what folk psychological ontology involves and what evidence could support it.
