# The Indispensability of Folk Psychology

Critics of eliminative materialism argue that folk psychology is not merely a useful theory but is in some sense indispensable — that we could not understand human behavior without it, and that its categories will survive any neuroscientific advance.

**The Predictive Success Argument**

The most straightforward defense of folk psychology is its predictive success. We use beliefs and desires to predict and explain human behavior remarkably well, far better than any currently available alternative. If I know what you believe and what you want, I can predict what you will do in a wide range of situations.

The eliminativist's response — that this success is compatible with the falsity of the ontology — is correct in principle but requires a positive alternative to be convincing. Until neuroscience can make predictions as accurate as folk psychology, the case for elimination is premature.

More importantly: predictive success is evidence for the reality of the entities posited. The success of a theory is our best evidence that its theoretical entities are real. Folk psychology's extraordinary predictive success is strong evidence that beliefs and desires are real things.

**The Indispensability of the Intentional Stance**

Dennett argues that the intentional stance — adopting the perspective from which behavior is interpreted in terms of beliefs, desires, and rationality — is not just useful but is the only perspective from which certain patterns of behavior are visible.

When we describe a chess computer as "wanting to protect its queen" or "believing that the rook is under attack," we are not saying there is a little homunculus inside the computer with these mental states. We are saying that the computer's behavior is best described and predicted by attributing these states. And this is genuinely true: the chess computer's behavior is predictable only from the intentional stance.

Dennett calls folk psychological attributions "real patterns" in the sense that they carve the behavioral space at genuine joints, even if there is no underlying "belief-substance." On this view, folk psychology is not to be eliminated but is to be understood as a legitimate and indispensable way of describing certain kinds of highly complex systems.

**The Holism Defense**

A different defense of folk psychology is holistic. Beliefs and desires form a tightly interconnected system. My belief that it will rain is connected to thousands of other beliefs (about weather, about what rain is, about the reliability of weather forecasts) and to my desires (to stay dry, to plan my day). This holistic structure is what makes folk psychological attributions make sense.

Any replacement theory would need to replicate this holistic structure to capture the same patterns. But a holistic, interconnected system of content-laden states just is what folk psychology describes. The elimination of beliefs and desires would require eliminating the entire holistic web — and it is unclear what would replace it.

**The Argument from Self-Knowledge**

A further argument against eliminativism appeals to self-knowledge. I know directly — not by inference or observation — that I have beliefs and desires. When I introspect, I find mental states. The eliminativist says these introspective reports are systematically misleading. But introspection is not just a source of information about oneself; it seems constitutive of the kind of mental life we have.

Eliminativism would require us to say: not only are there no beliefs; there is also no introspective awareness of beliefs. But the eliminativist cannot easily account for the introspective experience of having an inner life, other than by dismissing it as systematic illusion — and the costs of that dismissal seem very high.

**The Irreducibility of Intentional Content**

Philosophers like Fodor have argued that intentional content — the fact that mental states are about things — is irreducible to any non-intentional vocabulary. You cannot say, in purely physical or functional terms, what it is for a state to be about rain rather than about snow. If intentional content is irreducible, then folk psychology describes something real that no alternative vocabulary can capture.

This connects to the broader debate about naturalism about content: can the content of mental states be explained in terms of causal or functional relations, or is intentionality a primitive feature that resists naturalization?
