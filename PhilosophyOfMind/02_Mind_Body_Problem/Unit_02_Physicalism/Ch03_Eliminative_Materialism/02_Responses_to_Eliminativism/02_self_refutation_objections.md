# Self-Refutation and Performative Contradiction

The self-refutation objection to eliminative materialism has been one of the most discussed in the literature. It comes in several versions, each targeting a slightly different aspect of the eliminativist's position, and the Churchlands have offered responses to each. Working through these exchanges is philosophically illuminating.

**The Basic Self-Refutation Argument**

The simplest version: If eliminative materialism is true, there are no beliefs. But to assert eliminative materialism is to express a belief (the belief that eliminative materialism is true). So the very act of asserting eliminative materialism requires the existence of at least one belief. Therefore, eliminative materialism is self-refuting — it cannot be asserted without being contradicted.

Paul Churchland's response: this argument assumes that any assertion of a view involves having a "belief" in the folk psychological sense. But this is question-begging. The eliminativist denies that there are folk psychological beliefs; therefore, the eliminativist denies that asserting eliminative materialism involves having a folk psychological belief. The eliminativist's "asserting" involves some neural states that play a functional role — but those states are not beliefs in the folk psychological sense. To say they are is to assume what the objection is supposed to prove.

**The Performative Contradiction Version**

A more sophisticated version of the objection draws on Habermas's notion of performative contradiction: some statements cannot be sincerely asserted without being self-undermining. The Churchlands try to convince us of eliminative materialism by arguing. But arguing involves reasoning. Reasoning involves drawing inferences from premises. The concepts of "premise," "inference," and "conclusion" are folk psychological concepts. Arguing for eliminative materialism therefore uses the very framework the Churchlands claim should be eliminated.

This version is harder to dismiss. The Churchlands cannot simply say they are not "reasoning" in the folk psychological sense, because their activity of providing arguments, addressing objections, and drawing conclusions certainly looks like reasoning, whatever the underlying neural story is.

**The Neuroscientific Vocabulary Challenge**

A related challenge: the Churchlands' own positive program — replacing folk psychology with neuroscience — makes claims about the accuracy and value of various neuroscientific descriptions. These claims are normative (some descriptions are better than others) and representational (descriptions are accurate or inaccurate). But normativity and representation are exactly the kinds of things that folk psychological categories are designed to describe. How does eliminativism account for the normative dimension of scientific inquiry itself?

If folk psychological categories are eliminated, the eliminativist needs an alternative account of what it means for a theory to be more accurate than another, for evidence to support a hypothesis, for one inference to be valid rather than invalid. These are normative-epistemic notions, and it is unclear how to ground them purely in the neural vocabulary the eliminativist recommends.

**Patricia Churchland's Response**

Patricia Churchland has been more nuanced than Paul Churchland in responding to these objections. She acknowledges that the eliminativist program faces real challenges in accounting for normativity and the epistemology of science. Her approach is to argue that these challenges can be addressed — that a naturalized epistemology can explain norms of reasoning in terms of evolved cognitive heuristics, and that the accuracy of scientific theories can be understood in terms of their predictive and technological success.

Whether this naturalized approach to normativity is adequate is itself a major debate, connecting philosophy of mind to epistemology, philosophy of science, and meta-ethics.

**The Significance of the Debate**

The self-refutation debate ultimately concerns the conceptual independence of the intentional idiom. If intentional and normative concepts — belief, truth, evidence, validity — cannot be fully captured in non-intentional terms, then eliminative materialism cannot be coherently stated, let alone defended. If they can be naturalized, then eliminativism faces no self-refutation problem.

This debate is unresolved, and its resolution would settle not just the fate of eliminative materialism but the prospects for naturalism in philosophy of mind and epistemology generally.
