# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Eliminative Materialism

## Key Arguments

**The Theory-Theory of Folk Psychology**
Eliminativists argue that folk psychology—our everyday explanatory framework of beliefs, desires, intentions, and perceptions—is not a collection of platitudes about the mind but an empirical theory, which makes specific predictions about behavior and cognition. Like all empirical theories, folk psychology can in principle be false and subject to revolutionary replacement. This theoretical status of folk psychology is the first step in the eliminativist argument: if it is a theory, it can be evaluated and found wanting.

**Churchland's Pessimistic Meta-Induction**
Paul Churchland argues that the history of science is littered with the replacement of common-sense theories by radically different scientific ones: phlogiston replaced by oxygen chemistry, caloric replaced by kinetic theory of heat, vital spirits replaced by biochemistry. Each replacement showed that the old ontology (phlogiston, caloric, vital spirits) was simply false—there are no such entities. Folk psychology is likely to undergo the same fate: its ontology (beliefs, desires, pains) will be replaced by, not reduced to, a mature neuroscience.

**The Explanatory Poverty of Folk Psychology**
Folk psychology fails to explain mental illness in any illuminating way, makes poor predictions about the effects of brain damage, sleep, drugs, and developmental change, and has made essentially no progress in understanding learning, creativity, or imagination in millennia of use. By contrast, neuroscience has made rapid progress in understanding these phenomena. Churchland argues this track record is evidence that folk psychology is not merely incomplete but radically false—its predictive and explanatory failures reveal that its central theoretical categories carve nature at the wrong joints.

**Stich's Syntactic Theory of Mind as an Alternative**
Stephen Stich argues that if folk psychological categories do not correspond to natural kinds in cognitive science, we should replace them with a "syntactic" theory of mind that tracks computationally relevant internal states without worrying about their semantic or representational content. This is a more conservative eliminativism than Churchland's: it targets folk content-ascriptions rather than all mentalistic vocabulary. The proposal reveals a tension within eliminativism between eliminating folk psychology entirely and merely refining it.

**Fodor's Defense of Folk Psychology**
Jerry Fodor argues that folk psychology is successful in the way that matters: it is the best predictive system we have for explaining and predicting human behavior, and its central categories (belief, desire) are likely to correspond to genuine natural kinds in the cognitive science of the future. No scientific alternative comes close to matching folk psychology's predictive accuracy over the broadest range of human behavior. This response challenges the pessimistic meta-induction by arguing that the relevant comparison class for folk psychology is not physics but intentional systems, where it performs well.

## Core Concepts

**Eliminative Materialism**
Eliminative materialism holds that folk psychological entities—beliefs, desires, intentions, qualia—do not exist; folk psychological descriptions of mental life are false, not approximately true or in need of refinement but false in the way that descriptions of caloric or phlogiston are false. The position is more radical than reductive physicalism (which identifies mental states with physical states) or non-reductive physicalism (which preserves mental descriptions as a higher-level science): eliminativism claims that the categories of folk psychology will simply have no place in the completed scientific account of the mind.

**Folk Psychology**
Folk psychology is the everyday theoretical framework humans use to explain and predict behavior by attributing mental states—beliefs, desires, perceptions, emotions, intentions. It is the implicit theory expressed in statements like "She did X because she believed P and desired Q." Eliminativists treat it as an empirical theory amenable to scientific evaluation, not as a set of conceptual truths. The status of folk psychology—whether it is approximately correct, reducible to neuroscience, or radically false—is one of the central questions in contemporary philosophy of mind.

**Connectionism and the Churchlands**
Patricia Churchland and Paul Churchland argued that connectionist neural network models—which represent information as patterns of activation across large arrays of units rather than as discrete symbolic representations—provide a better model of cognition than classical computational models, and they do not naturally accommodate the sentential, propositional structure that folk psychology presupposes. If cognition is more accurately described in terms of activation vectors and weight matrices than in terms of beliefs and inferences, folk psychology may be radically incorrect about the structure of cognition.

**The Intentional Stance (Dennett)**
Dennett's intentional stance is the interpretive strategy of treating a system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires in order to predict its behavior. Dennett argues that folk psychology is correct as an interpretive strategy—attributing beliefs and desires is useful and predictively successful—but that there are no "real" beliefs and desires in a deeper metaphysical sense; there are only patterns of behavior that make the intentional stance apt. This is sometimes classified as a mild eliminativism (about "real" mental states) and sometimes as a sophisticated instrumentalism.

**The Pessimistic Meta-Induction**
The pessimistic meta-induction is an argument from the history of science: the empirical theories of the past have almost universally turned out to be false, and our current theories will likely share their fate. Applied to folk psychology, it suggests that the ontology of beliefs, desires, and qualia has no more claim to be real than caloric, phlogiston, or vital spirits. The meta-induction is both a methodological argument (folk psychology should be treated as a theory subject to empirical revision) and a substantive one (its chances of approximate truth are poor given the historical record).

## Thought Experiments

**Churchland's Replacement Analogy: Caloric and Beliefs**
Churchland asks us to take seriously the possibility that "belief" is to future cognitive science as "caloric" was to thermodynamics: a term in a false theoretical framework that picks out no genuine natural kind and will be replaced rather than reduced. The thought experiment invites us to view the apparent certainty we feel about our beliefs from a third-person, historical perspective. If a scientist in 1800 could be confident about caloric and still be radically wrong, our confidence in folk psychological categories provides no guarantee they will survive scientific scrutiny.

**Stich's Lobotomy Case**
Stich asks us to consider a patient who, after a radical lobotomy, has lost all rational connections between their mental states—they still "have beliefs" in some sense but these states no longer cohere or guide behavior in rational ways. Are these still genuine beliefs? Stich argues that the case shows folk psychological categories are not natural-kind terms but rather terms tied to patterns of normatively appropriate behavior, and that when such patterns are disrupted, it is unclear whether the folk psychological categories apply. This motivates a more theoretically flexible approach to mental state attribution.

**The Super-Scientific Successor**
Eliminativists invite us to imagine a completed neuroscience that describes all behavior in terms of neural states, synaptic weights, and activation patterns—with no remainder attributable to beliefs or desires. In this scenario, every behavioral prediction now made by folk psychology would be made, more accurately, by neuroscience. The question is whether this would represent the discovery that folk psychology was approximately true (its categories correspond to functional states in the network), or whether it would represent the elimination of folk psychology (the neural descriptions simply replace it, the way chemical descriptions replaced alchemical ones). The thought experiment crystallizes the debate between reduction and elimination.
