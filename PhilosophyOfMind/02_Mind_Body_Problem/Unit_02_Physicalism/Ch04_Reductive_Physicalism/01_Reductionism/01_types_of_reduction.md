# Types of Reduction in Philosophy of Mind

Reduction is not a single relationship but a family of relationships, and distinguishing them is essential for evaluating claims about the reduction of mental to physical. Different types of reduction make different demands and have different philosophical implications.

**Definitional Reduction**

The strongest form of reduction: the reduced theory's concepts are defined in terms of the reducing theory's concepts. If folk psychological terms could be defined in purely physical terms — "pain" = "C-fiber firing" as a matter of definition — then psychology would be definitionally reduced to neuroscience.

Logical behaviorism attempted something like this: mental terms were to be defined in behavioral terms. This project failed because no behavioral definition of any mental term was adequate (the Rylean holism problem: any behavioral disposition can be overridden by other mental states).

**Explanatory Reduction**

A more modest form: the laws and generalizations of the higher-level theory are explained by the lower-level theory, even if the higher-level terms are not explicitly defined at the lower level. We explain why Mendel's laws hold by appeal to molecular genetics; we don't reduce "gene" to any specific molecular formula, but we do explain genetic behavior in molecular terms.

Applied to psychology: we would explain why folk psychological generalizations hold — why people tend to avoid things that cause pain, why beliefs cause certain behaviors — in neuroscientific terms. The folk psychological generalizations are not definitions of neural states, but they are derived from neuroscientific theory as special cases.

**Ontological Reduction**

The reduced entities (mental states) are identical to the reducing entities (neural states). This is what the type identity theorists proposed: the ontology of psychology is absorbed into the ontology of neuroscience. There are no mental entities over and above neural entities.

Ontological reduction does not require definitional reduction: the identity of water with H₂O was discovered empirically, not established by definition. Similarly, mental-neural identities (if true) would be a posteriori necessities, not conceptual truths.

**Eliminative Reduction**

The reduced theory's entities turn out not to exist; they are replaced by the entities of the reducing theory. Phlogiston was not reduced to oxygen; it was eliminated. Similarly, eliminative materialism holds that folk psychological entities (beliefs, desires as folk psychology conceives them) will be eliminated rather than reduced.

**Nagel's Model of Reduction**

Ernest Nagel's classical model of intertheoretic reduction (1961) requires:
1. **Bridge laws**: Statements linking the predicates of the reduced theory to the predicates of the reducing theory.
2. **Derivability**: The laws of the reduced theory are derivable from the laws of the reducing theory plus the bridge laws.

Applied to psychology: we need bridge laws connecting psychological predicates ("pain") to neural predicates ("C-fiber firing"), and then we derive psychological laws from neural laws plus bridge laws.

This model faces problems:
- Are there bridge laws? The multiple realizability argument suggests there are no type-type correlations for psychological and neural states.
- Even if bridge laws exist, do they "explain" the psychological laws, or merely show a correlation?

**The New Wave of Reduction**

More recent philosophers (John Bickle, Jaegwon Kim) have developed "new wave" accounts of reduction that don't require Nagelian bridge laws. On these accounts, reduction proceeds by showing that there is a physical mechanism that produces the effects attributed to the reduced-level entity. The reduction of temperature to mean molecular kinetic energy showed that the same mechanism (molecular motion) produces all the thermodynamic effects we associate with temperature.

Applied to mind: a successful reduction of, say, memory would show that the same neural mechanism produces all the behavioral and phenomenological effects we associate with memory. Whether any psychological state has been successfully reduced in this sense is debated.

**The Asymmetric Dependence of Reduction and Science**

A general point: whether reduction is possible in a given domain is partly an empirical question, to be settled by science. Philosophy can clarify what reduction would mean and what it would require; it cannot determine from the armchair whether the required bridge laws or mechanisms exist. The prospects for reduction of the mental to the physical depend on how neuroscience develops.
