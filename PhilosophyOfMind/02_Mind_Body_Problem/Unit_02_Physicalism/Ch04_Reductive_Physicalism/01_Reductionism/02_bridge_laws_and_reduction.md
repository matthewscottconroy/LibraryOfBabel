# Bridge Laws and the Reduction of Psychology

The Nagelian account of reduction requires bridge laws — biconditional statements connecting the theoretical terms of two theories. For the reduction of psychology to neuroscience, bridge laws would have the form: "X is in mental state M if and only if X is in neural state N." These laws are the linchpins of reduction; without them, the derivation of psychological from neuroscientific laws is impossible.

**What Bridge Laws Are**

Bridge laws are not merely empirical correlations; they are supposed to express nomological connections — connections holding by natural law. The difference matters: a mere correlation between mental and neural states would show that the two are always found together but would not show that one explains the other. A nomological connection would show that the mental state is in some sense identified with or grounded in the neural state.

The bridge law "Pain ↔ C-fiber firing" (if true) would tell us that being in pain is nomologically equivalent to having C-fibers fire. This would allow us to replace talk of pain with talk of C-fiber firing in the relevant contexts, and to derive psychological generalizations from neuroscientific ones.

**The Problem of Multiple Realizability**

The multiple realizability argument shows that type-type bridge laws are unavailable for psychology. There is no single neural state N such that: "X is in pain ↔ X is in neural state N" holds for all possible pain-feeling creatures (humans, octopuses, aliens). The psychological predicate "pain" is connected to different neural predicates in different organisms.

The Nagelian reduction model requires bridge laws that hold universally (or across the relevant domain). If psychological predicates are multiply realizable, they do not have universal neural correlates, and the Nagelian bridge laws cannot be formulated.

**Local Bridge Laws**

A response: formulate local bridge laws that hold for specific organisms. "X is human and X is in pain ↔ X is human and X has C-fiber activation." This holds universally for humans, even if not for all possible pain-feeling beings.

The objection to local bridge laws is that they do not achieve genuine reduction. A local bridge law for human pain tells us that in humans, pain is realized by C-fiber activation. But it does not explain *why* pain (in general) has the functional and experiential properties it has. For that explanation, we would need a law connecting pain-as-such (the functional kind) to some neural correlate — and multiple realizability shows that no such law is available.

**Functional Reduction**

Some philosophers have proposed that the reduction should target functional descriptions, not folk psychological ones. The function of pain — the causal role it plays in detecting tissue damage, motivating avoidance — could in principle be explained in terms of the specific neural mechanisms that implement that function.

On this view, what is reduced is not "pain" as folk psychology conceives it but "pain-role" — the functional specification of pain. The neural mechanism explains why that function is performed.

But even this functional reduction leaves out the phenomenal character: why does the neural mechanism that performs the pain-role produce the specific qualitative character of pain? The bridge between functional role and phenomenal character is exactly what seems most resistant to reduction.

**The Qualia Gap in Bridge Laws**

The deepest problem for bridge laws is the explanatory gap regarding qualia. Even if we have a bridge law "Pain ↔ C-fiber activation," this does not explain why C-fiber activation is *painful* — why it produces the characteristic burning, pressing, unpleasant quality. The bridge law states a correlation; it does not explain the phenomenal character.

Joseph Levine's analysis of this problem introduced the term "explanatory gap" to describe the space between the neural description and the phenomenal description that bridge laws cannot bridge. Even if the identity is true, it does not feel necessary the way mathematical identities feel necessary: we can always ask "but why does C-fiber firing hurt?" and this question seems to have no answer within the physical framework.

This gap is what motivates the "phenomenal concepts strategy" — the attempt to explain why there is an apparent explanatory gap even if the underlying identity is true — and the hard problem of consciousness more generally.
