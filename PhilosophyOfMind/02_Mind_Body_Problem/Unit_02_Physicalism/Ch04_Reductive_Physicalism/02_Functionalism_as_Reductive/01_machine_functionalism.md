# Machine Functionalism

Machine functionalism is the first systematic formulation of functionalism about the mind, developed by Hilary Putnam in the 1960s. Drawing on the theory of Turing machines, Putnam argued that mental states can be understood as functional states — states defined by their causal roles — rather than by their physical realization.

**Turing Machines as Models**

A Turing machine is defined by: (a) a finite set of states, (b) an alphabet of symbols, (c) a transition function that specifies, for each state-symbol pair, what state to enter and what symbol to write.

The key feature of a Turing machine is that its behavior is determined by its current state and input, not by the physical material of which it is made. The same Turing machine can be implemented in different physical media (vacuum tubes, transistors, neurons, billiard balls theoretically) — the physical implementation is irrelevant to the machine's computational identity.

Putnam proposed that the mind is a kind of probabilistic automaton — a generalization of a Turing machine that handles probabilistic rather than deterministic transitions. Mental states are functional states of this automaton: pain is the state that is entered upon tissue damage, causes avoidance behavior, and has certain causal relations to other mental states.

**The Functionalist Definition**

On machine functionalism, a system has a mental state M if and only if it has some internal state that plays the causal-functional role characteristic of M. The role is specified in terms of:
- Input conditions (what causes the state to arise)
- Output conditions (what behavior the state tends to produce)
- Relations to other internal states (how the state interacts with other mental states)

This definition abstracts over the physical implementation. Any physical system — silicon, neurons, or hypothetically a large collection of humans playing functional roles — could have mental states, as long as it instantiates the right functional organization.

**The Strength of Machine Functionalism**

Machine functionalism has several important features:

**Multiple realizability**: Directly accommodates the intuition that pain in humans and pain in octopuses are both pain, as long as they play the same functional role.

**Scientific respectability**: Functional states are operationalizable — we can in principle describe the computational structure of a cognitive system and test hypotheses about which states play which roles.

**Explaining cognitive integration**: Functionalism naturally accommodates the way mental states interact — how belief interacts with desire to produce action, how perception generates belief, and so on.

**The Limitations: Putnam's Reconsideration**

As noted in the section on multiple realizability, Putnam himself came to doubt machine functionalism. His objections:

**Wide content**: Mental states have content that depends on the external environment, not just on internal functional organization. Replacing the water in my environment with XYZ would change the content of my water-thoughts, even without changing my functional organization. Functionalism cannot account for this content-dependence on the environment.

**The absent qualia problem**: A system that implements the right functional organization might have no phenomenal consciousness at all. The Chinese nation (seven billion people, each playing the role of a neuron) might implement the functional organization of a human brain, but it seems unlikely to be conscious. Functional organization seems insufficient for phenomenal experience.

**Psychofunctionalism**

Putnam's original formulation appealed to abstract Turing machines. A later development — psychofunctionalism — ties the functional roles to the generalizations of actual cognitive psychology, rather than to abstract machine table descriptions.

On this view, the relevant functional roles are those that cognitive psychology investigates: the roles played in perception, memory, reasoning, emotion. A system has a mental state M if it has a state that plays the role identified by psychological theory as characteristic of M.

This is a more naturalistic and empirically grounded version of functionalism, and it remains influential in cognitive science and philosophy of psychology.
