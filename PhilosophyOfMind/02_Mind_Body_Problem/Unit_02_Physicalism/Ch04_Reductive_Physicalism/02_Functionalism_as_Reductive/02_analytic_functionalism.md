# Analytic Functionalism and the Ramsification Strategy

Analytic functionalism, associated with David Lewis and Frank Jackson, attempts to derive functionalism from conceptual analysis of folk psychological terms rather than from scientific theory. It represents a different approach to functionalism than Putnam's machine functionalism — one that is more closely connected to the classical project of conceptual analysis.

**The Ramsification Strategy**

The Ramsification strategy, introduced by Frank Ramsey, provides a way of characterizing the theoretical terms of a theory without presupposing their reference. The idea: take all the postulates of a theory T and replace each theoretical term with a variable. The result is a Ramsey sentence — an existential claim that there are entities satisfying the structural roles specified by T.

Applied to folk psychology: take all the truisms of folk psychology ("Pain is typically caused by tissue damage," "Pain causes avoidance behavior," "Pain is typically accompanied by a desire to have it cease," etc.). Replace "pain" and other folk psychological terms with variables. The Ramsey sentence asserts that there exist states satisfying these roles.

Analytic functionalism then holds: to be in pain is just to be in whatever state plays the pain role (as specified by the Ramsey sentence). The folk psychological concept of pain is exhausted by its place in the network of folk psychological truisms.

**The Connection to Lewis's Causal Theory of Mind**

David Lewis developed analytic functionalism in conjunction with a causal theory of mental states. Lewis argues that mental state terms are introduced by folk psychology as terms for whatever causal roles they specify. The term "pain" picks out (rigidly, in Lewis's framework) whatever physical state in the actual world plays the pain role in human beings.

This has an interesting consequence: mental state terms turn out to refer to physical (neural) states, via the mediating role of functional descriptions. "Pain" rigidly designates the neural state that plays the pain role — which is a different approach to psychophysical identity than the Kripkean necessitarian one.

**The Advantages of Analytic Functionalism**

Analytic functionalism has several attractions:
- It grounds functionalism in the analysis of folk psychological concepts, giving it a firmer philosophical foundation than empirical science-based versions.
- It explains the relationship between folk psychology and neuroscience: folk psychology specifies the functional roles; neuroscience identifies the physical states that fill those roles.
- It accommodates multiple realizability: different physical states can fill the same functional role in different organisms.

**The Problems**

Analytic functionalism faces several objections:

**Are there folk psychological truisms?** The Ramsification strategy requires a stable set of truisms about each mental state. But folk psychology may be too heterogeneous and context-sensitive to generate a determinate set of truisms. Different people, cultures, and contexts may use mental concepts in different ways.

**The Qualia Problem**: Analytic functionalism, like all functionalism, struggles with phenomenal consciousness. The functional role of pain can be defined without specifying its qualitative character. Two states with identical functional roles might feel entirely different (inverted qualia) or feel like nothing at all (absent qualia). This suggests that phenomenal character is not captured by functional role, and hence not by analytic functionalism.

**Lewis's Response to Qualia**: Lewis argued for a "mad pain" and "Martian pain" case. A Martian has pain that plays a completely different causal role but feels qualitatively just like human pain; a "madman" has something that plays all the same causal roles as pain but feels qualitatively different. Lewis proposed that mental concept terms are doubly indexed — they refer to what plays the pain role in the actual human population, not to any abstract functional role.

**The Current Status**

Analytic functionalism has largely been superseded by more nuanced versions of functionalism that don't claim to reduce mental concepts to functional descriptions by conceptual analysis. But the Ramsification strategy remains a useful tool, and the general idea that mental concepts can be analyzed by their roles in a theoretical network continues to influence philosophy of mind.
