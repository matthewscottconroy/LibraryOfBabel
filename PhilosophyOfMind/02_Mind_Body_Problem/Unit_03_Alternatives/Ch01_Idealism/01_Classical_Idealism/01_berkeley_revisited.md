# Berkeley Revisited: Idealism as a Live Option

Berkeley's immaterialism — the view that only minds and their ideas exist — was once dismissed as a philosophical curiosity that nobody sincerely believed. But in recent decades, idealism has experienced a philosophical rehabilitation. Several serious contemporary thinkers have argued that idealism, properly understood, provides a more coherent account of reality than either dualism or standard physicalism.

**What Idealism Claims**

Idealism, in its various forms, holds that mind is fundamental — that physical reality is either composed of, constituted by, or dependent on mental reality. This is not the trivial claim that we can only know the world through mental representations (that's a mere epistemological point). It is the metaphysical claim that the world as it is in itself has a mental character.

Berkeley's version: everything that exists is either a mind or an idea in a mind. Physical objects are bundles of ideas, sustained in existence by God's continuous perception.

Contemporary versions are more sophisticated. Analytic idealism (Kastrup) holds that the universe is "one mind" — a single consciousness — and individual human minds are localized "alters" within it. Cosmopsychism (a form of panpsychist idealism) holds that the cosmos as a whole is conscious, and individual minds are aspects of cosmic consciousness.

**The Arguments for Idealism**

Contemporary idealists offer several arguments:

**The Hard Problem Dissolves**: If matter is mental, there is no hard problem of explaining how consciousness arises from non-conscious matter. Consciousness does not arise from matter; matter arises from (or consists in) consciousness.

**The Parsimonious Reality**: We are acquainted with only one kind of thing: experience. Our acquaintance with the external world is always mediated by experience. Positing a realm of experience-independent matter is therefore an additional theoretical commitment that idealism avoids. Idealism posits less: only mental reality.

**The Self-Reference Problem for Physicalism**: Physicalism holds that minds are physical. But physical theories are formulated by minds. There is a puzzling circularity: the physical theory that explains minds is itself a product of minds that the theory says are physical. Idealism avoids this circularity by taking mind as the starting point rather than trying to derive it from the non-mental.

**Kastrup's Analytic Idealism**

Bernardo Kastrup has been the most active contemporary defender of analytic idealism. His version holds that universal consciousness is the fundamental reality, and that what we call "matter" is how consciousness appears to itself when observed from the outside — a "view from without" of mental processes occurring "within."

Individual human minds are "alters" — dissociated streams within the single cosmic consciousness — analogous to the way multiple personality disorder creates the appearance of separate persons within a single consciousness.

Kastrup argues that this picture is more internally coherent than physicalism and makes correct predictions about the relationship between brain activity and consciousness: since consciousness is fundamental, brain states are expressions of mental states rather than causes of them. The relationship between brain and mind is like the relationship between the appearance of a storm in meteorological instruments and the storm itself.

**Objections**

Idealism faces the obvious objection: what about when nobody is looking? Does the moon cease to exist when no one perceives it? Berkeley's answer (God always perceives everything) is available only to theists. Non-theist idealism must find another answer.

Kastrup's answer: the "appearance" of matter that we observe when nobody is looking is maintained by the background universal consciousness. Unobserved particles are processes occurring in the cosmic mind that become accessible to individual minds when observed.

This answer is coherent but raises its own questions: what is the relationship between cosmic consciousness and physical processes? Why do physical processes follow the specific mathematical laws they do?

**The Current Status**

Idealism is not the mainstream view in philosophy of mind — physicalism in various forms remains dominant. But the contemporary revival of idealism has shown that it cannot be dismissed as incoherent. It raises genuine questions about the relationship between consciousness and matter, and its critique of physicalism's treatment of consciousness is often acute.
