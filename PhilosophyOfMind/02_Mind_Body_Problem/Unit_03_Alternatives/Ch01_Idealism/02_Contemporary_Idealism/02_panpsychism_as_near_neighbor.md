# Panpsychism as Near Neighbor: Idealism and Panpsychism Compared

Idealism and panpsychism are often discussed together, and it is easy to see why: both take consciousness seriously as a feature of the fundamental fabric of reality, and both are motivated by the failure of standard physicalism to account for experience. But they are distinct positions, and the differences between them matter philosophically.

**What the Positions Share**

Both idealism and panpsychism reject the standard physicalist picture in which consciousness is either identical with, reducible to, or eliminable in favor of purely physical processes. Both hold that consciousness is not a latecomer to reality — not an accident of biological evolution on a small planet — but something deeply woven into the structure of things.

Both positions are motivated by the hard problem of consciousness: the difficulty of explaining why there is something it is like to be a conscious creature. Physicalism struggles with this problem because it must derive phenomenal properties from structural-relational properties that seem to leave no room for the qualitative character of experience. Both panpsychism and idealism sidestep this derivation by making consciousness (or proto-consciousness) more fundamental.

Both are currently experiencing philosophical rehabilitation after long periods of dismissal. The growing recognition that the hard problem is genuinely difficult — not a confusion to be dissolved — has opened space for positions that would have been considered non-starters thirty years ago.

**The Key Difference**

The fundamental difference is about what is most basic. Panpsychism holds that the fundamental physical constituents of reality — particles, fields, whatever physics ultimately identifies as basic — have intrinsic experiential or proto-experiential properties. Mind is real and fundamental, but it is a property of physical things, not the ground from which physical things emerge.

Idealism holds that consciousness (or mind) is the fundamental reality, and what we call "physical matter" is an expression, appearance, or construction from consciousness. On idealism, matter is not the substrate that has mental properties; mind is the substrate from which the appearance of matter is generated.

This is not just a verbal distinction. On panpsychism, there is a real physical world of particles and fields that have both structural-relational properties (described by physics) and intrinsic experiential properties (inaccessible to physics). On idealism, the particles and fields are themselves mental — they are patterns in consciousness, not things that consciousness is a property of.

**The Cosmopsychism Bridge**

The distinction between idealism and panpsychism blurs when we consider cosmopsychism. Cosmopsychism holds that the universe as a whole is conscious, and individual minds are aspects or limitations of cosmic consciousness rather than aggregates of micro-conscious particles.

Cosmopsychism looks very much like idealism: if the universe is a single cosmic consciousness and everything in it is an expression of that consciousness, we are very close to Kastrup's analytic idealism. The difference is mainly one of emphasis and framing: cosmopsychists typically situate themselves within debates about panpsychism and the combination problem, while idealists are more likely to reject the physical ontology altogether.

Philip Goff's work explicitly bridges the two: he defends cosmopsychism as the best response to the combination problem for panpsychism, and acknowledges that cosmopsychism shades into idealism. The view that the cosmos is conscious and that individual minds are derivations from cosmic consciousness is practically equivalent to Kastrup's view that individual minds are dissociated alters within universal consciousness.

**The Combination Problem and Its Absence**

One place where the contrast matters is the combination problem. Panpsychism — particularly micro-level panpsychism — faces the problem of explaining how micro-level experiential properties combine to constitute macro-level consciousness. This is a serious challenge, as we saw earlier.

Idealism in its top-down form (cosmopsychism or Kastrup's analytic idealism) avoids the combination problem by starting from the top. The cosmic consciousness is primary; individual minds are carved out of it by dissociation, not built up from smaller units. The challenge is not combination but individuation: how does a single consciousness become differentiated into individual streams of experience?

Panpsychists argue that individuation is easier than combination: it is more intelligible how a unified consciousness could be differentiated into individuals than how separate micro-experiences could be unified into a single macro-consciousness. Panpsychists who favor cosmopsychism agree with this and effectively concede that top-down idealism is more defensible than bottom-up panpsychism.

**Epistemic Starting Points**

There is also a difference in epistemic orientation. Panpsychism typically starts from the existence of a physical world that science has revealed and asks what the intrinsic nature of that world must be to accommodate consciousness. It is, in this sense, a view from within the natural sciences, supplemented by a philosophical claim about intrinsic properties.

Idealism, particularly Kastrup's version, starts from consciousness itself as the one thing we know directly and asks what the external world must be for consciousness to be fundamental. It is, in this sense, more Cartesian in its method — taking experience as epistemically prior.

This difference in starting point leads to different dialogs with science. Panpsychism sees itself as continuous with scientific inquiry, adding only the claim about intrinsic properties. Idealism offers a more radical reinterpretation: it accepts the mathematical structure of science but gives it a different ontological interpretation, placing consciousness rather than matter at the bottom.

**Current Assessment**

The convergence of idealism and cosmopsychism represents one of the more interesting developments in recent philosophy of mind. Whether these positions are genuinely distinct or merely notational variants is debated. What is clear is that they represent a family of views committed to taking consciousness seriously as a fundamental feature of reality — a commitment that looks increasingly plausible as the hard problem proves resistant to physicalist dissolution.

The choice between them often comes down to which challenges seem more tractable: the individuation problem of idealism or the combination problem of bottom-up panpsychism.
