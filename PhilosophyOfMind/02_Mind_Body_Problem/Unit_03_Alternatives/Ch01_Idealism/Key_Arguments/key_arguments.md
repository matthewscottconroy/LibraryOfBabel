# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Idealism

## Key Arguments

**Berkeley's Master Argument (Esse Est Percipi)**
Berkeley argues that we cannot coherently conceive of a material object existing unperceived: any attempt to form the idea of an unperceived tree merely results in conceiving of a perceived tree—ourselves perceiving it. The master argument holds that to exist is to be perceived (esse est percipi) for sensible things, and to perceive or to think for minds. Because "matter" as something that exists independently of any mind is literally inconceivable, there is no reason to posit it, and every reason to identify the objects of perception with ideas in minds.

**The Problem of Unperceived Objects**
If esse est percipi, then objects cease to exist when no one perceives them—which conflicts with common experience (the table persists when I leave the room). Berkeley resolves this by positing that God continuously perceives all things; objects are ideas in the divine mind. This solution maintains idealism but requires theological commitment. Alternative solutions (Berkeleyan phenomenalism, regularities of perception) have been proposed by later idealists, but the problem of unperceived persistence remains the most pressing objection to subjective idealism.

**Kant's Transcendental Idealism as a Regulative Position**
Kant argues that space and time are not features of things in themselves but forms of human sensible intuition—they are contributed by the mind to experience. Objects as we know them are empirically real (genuinely objective within experience) but transcendentally ideal (dependent on the mind's forms of intuition). This is neither subjective idealism (which makes objects mental) nor naive realism (which makes them mind-independent): objects are real within the domain of possible experience but the nature of things in themselves is permanently inaccessible. Kant intends this as a refutation of both Berkeley and dogmatic realism.

**Hegel's Argument for Absolute Idealism**
Hegel argues that the distinction Kant draws between phenomena and things-in-themselves is incoherent: if we can identify the thing-in-itself as something, we have some cognitive access to it, which undermines its status as genuinely unknowable. The only consistent position is absolute idealism: reality is identical with rational thought in its fullest development, and the apparent opposition between thought and being is itself a moment in the self-development of Geist. Physical nature is not external to mind but a self-alienated form of spirit.

**Kastrup's Argument for Analytic Idealism**
Bernardo Kastrup argues that positing a material substrate for experience is an unnecessary explanatory assumption: we have direct acquaintance only with experience, and positing anything beyond experience to explain it creates a harder problem than the one it solves. A monism of consciousness—in which the physical world is the appearance (from a third-person perspective) of mental processes in a universal mind—is more parsimonious. Individual minds are "dissociative alters" of a universal consciousness, analogous to dissociative identity disorder in individual humans. This is a contemporary analytic reformulation of objective idealism.

## Core Concepts

**Subjective Idealism (Berkeley)**
Berkeley's subjective idealism holds that only minds and ideas exist: there is no material substance, and the objects of perception are ideas in minds. Sensible objects like tables and trees are collections of ideas—bundles of sensory qualities—that exist only as they are perceived. This position aims to avoid the interaction problem (how does matter cause ideas?) by eliminating matter, and to avoid skepticism (we have no access to matter) by making the objects of knowledge directly available to mind. Berkeley's idealism preserves commonsense realism about the immediate objects of perception while eliminating matter as an underlying substrate.

**Esse Est Percipi**
"To be is to be perceived" is Berkeley's formula for the existence conditions of sensible things: a tree, a stone, or a sound exists when and only when it is perceived. Minds themselves exist not by being perceived but by perceiving (esse est percipere). This formula captures the core idealist thesis that existence and appearance are not distinct for sensible objects: to be real is to appear to some mind. The formula motivates the question of what guarantees the existence of objects when no finite mind perceives them—Berkeley's answer being God's continuous perception.

**Transcendental Idealism (Kant)**
Kant's transcendental idealism holds that space, time, and the categories of the understanding are contributions of the knowing subject to experience, not features of mind-independent reality. Objects as we know them—phenomena—are constituted by the forms of intuition and understanding; things in themselves—noumena—are entirely inaccessible to human cognition. Transcendental idealism is "empirically realistic" (physical objects are objectively real within experience) and "transcendentally ideal" (the form of experience is mind-dependent). Kant intends this as a middle path between Berkeleyan idealism and dogmatic realism.

**Absolute Idealism (Hegel)**
Hegel's absolute idealism holds that reality is the self-development of the Absolute—infinite, self-knowing rationality (Geist)—through a dialectical process in which thought and being are ultimately identical. Nature is spirit in its "otherness," and human thought is spirit returning to itself through finite minds. The Absolute is not a static unity but a process of self-differentiation and self-integration. Absolute idealism avoids Berkeley's dependence on a personal God and Kant's unknowable thing-in-itself by insisting that the distinction between mind and world is itself a moment within a more comprehensive rational whole.

**Analytic Idealism (Kastrup)**
Contemporary analytic idealism (Kastrup, Goff in part) reformulates idealism within the analytic tradition. It holds that consciousness is ontologically primary and that the physical world is the external appearance of inner mental processes—specifically, of a universal mind whose dissociative processes give rise to the illusion of multiple individual minds and a mind-independent physical world. Analytic idealism claims to dissolve the hard problem: there is no problem of how physical processes give rise to consciousness because consciousness is the ground, not the product, of the physical.

## Thought Experiments

**Berkeley's Unperceived Tree**
Berkeley asks us to genuinely conceive of a tree existing without being perceived. Every time we try, we find we are conceiving of ourselves perceiving it—we cannot get outside the perceptual relationship to conceive of an independently existing material object. The thought experiment is meant to show that "material object existing unperceived" is not merely unknowable but inconceivable. Critics respond that the inability to conceive of something in a certain way (without imagining it) does not prove it cannot exist in that way.

**Kant's Refutation of Idealism (Experiment in Experience)**
Kant asks: how do I know that my inner experiences occur in a determinate temporal order? He argues that temporal determination requires an enduring standard—something outside my inner states to which they can be related. Outer, persistent objects in space provide this standard. The thought experiment is designed to show that the Cartesian quest for certainty about the inner world, while doubting the outer world, gets things backwards: outer experience is presupposed by inner experience, not the other way around.

**The Simulation Argument and Idealism**
Bostrom's simulation argument—that we may be living in a computer simulation—is philosophically close to Berkeleyan idealism: in a simulated universe, the underlying "hardware" does not directly determine our experience; what we take to be physical objects are in fact computational processes in a higher-level mind. Whether or not the simulation hypothesis is plausible, it makes vivid the coherence of an idealist picture in which the apparent solidity of the physical world is maintained by mental processes at a deeper level. Contemporary idealists often cite simulation scenarios as illustrations of their position.
