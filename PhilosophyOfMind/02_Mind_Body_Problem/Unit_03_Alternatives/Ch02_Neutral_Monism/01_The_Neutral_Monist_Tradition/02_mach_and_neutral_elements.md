# Mach and the Neutral Elements

Ernst Mach (1838–1916) was a physicist and philosopher of science whose work on the foundations of mechanics and sensory psychology led him to a distinctive metaphysical position: the fundamental constituents of reality are neither mental nor physical, but neutral elements that can be organized in ways that yield either physical objects or mental experiences depending on the relationships in which they stand.

**Mach's Starting Point: The Economy of Thought**

Mach was a thoroughgoing empiricist and positivist. His principle of "economy of thought" held that scientific theories should be as simple and parsimonious as possible, and should not posit entities beyond what experience requires. Applying this principle to the mind-body problem, Mach argued that both the mental and the physical should be analyzed in terms of more basic elements.

What are these basic elements? Mach's answer, developed in *The Analysis of Sensations* (1886), is that the fundamental elements of reality are sensations or, more neutrally, "elements" — colors, tones, pressures, warmths, smells, and so on. These elements are the ultimate data of both science and experience.

The crucial point is that these elements are not intrinsically mental or physical. What makes an element "mental" or "physical" is not its intrinsic character but the context of relationships in which it is considered. When we consider colors as contents of an experience, related to memories, emotions, and other experiential states, they are mental. When we consider the same colors as properties of surfaces, related to wavelengths, illumination conditions, and physical contexts, they are physical. The elements themselves are neutral.

**The Analysis of the Self**

Mach famously denied the existence of a substantial self. In his *Analysis of Sensations* he included a sketch labeled "Das Ich ist unrettbar" — the self is unsalvageable — depicting how the contents of experience might look to an introspecting eye that cannot see itself as a substance.

The self, for Mach, is not an entity over and above the stream of sensory elements. It is a particular complex of elements — memories, dispositions, emotional tendencies, sensations — organized in a characteristic way. The unity of the self is not the unity of a substance but the functional and associative unity of a complex.

This anticipates Hume's bundle theory but gives it a more scientifically sophisticated formulation. Hume analyzed the self into perceptions; Mach analyzes it into sensory elements that are simultaneously the basic data of physics and psychology.

**Physical Objects as Complexes of Elements**

The same analysis applies to physical objects. A table is not a substance with properties; it is a stable complex of elements (colors, hardnesses, resistances, temperatures) that tend to co-occur and that we find it convenient to treat as a single persistent thing. The "reality" of physical objects is the stability and predictability of these complexes, not the existence of a substrate beneath them.

This is a form of phenomenalism about physical objects: physical objects are logical constructions from sensory data rather than mind-independent substances. Combined with Mach's neutral analysis of the elements themselves, it yields a picture in which neither mental nor physical substances exist — only neutral elements organized into complexes.

**Mach's Influence**

Mach's neutral monism influenced several important thinkers. William James, who knew Mach's work, developed his own "radical empiricism" along similar lines: the world is made of "pure experience," and the distinction between subject and object is a distinction in the context and relations of elements, not a difference in their intrinsic character.

Bertrand Russell, who spent years working on the foundations of physics and psychology, drew explicitly on Mach and James in developing his own version of neutral monism in the 1920s. The Vienna Circle and logical positivism were heavily influenced by Mach's empiricism and the idea that scientific concepts should be analyzed in terms of observational elements.

**Objections to Mach's Position**

The most fundamental objection to Mach's neutral monism is that the supposedly "neutral" elements are in fact mental — they are sensations, colored by their essentially experiential character. Calling them "neutral" does not make them so. If colors are intrinsically the kinds of things that can be components of experience, then they are mental, not neutral.

A related objection: if Mach's elements are sensory — capable of being experienced — then there must be something it is like to have them. But then the neutral base already contains experiential properties, and the physicalist demand for a non-experiential basis for mind is not met. The "neutrality" is achieved by not explicitly choosing sides, but if one side's properties are already present, the neutrality is merely verbal.

Mach would respond that his elements, described most fundamentally, are neither mental nor physical — the experiential and physical characterizations are both impositions of relational context onto what is intrinsically characterless. But critics find this difficult to accept: elements that are intrinsically characterless seem to be nothing at all.

Despite these objections, Mach's project was important: he showed that the mind-body problem could be approached by questioning the starting assumptions of both sides — the assumption that there is a substantial mind on one side and a substantial material world on the other — rather than trying to reduce one to the other.
