# Russell's Neutral Monism

Bertrand Russell's engagement with the mind-body problem spans several decades and multiple positions. He began as a dualist of sorts, moved through various forms of realism and neutral monism, and ultimately settled on a position that he called neutral monism, influenced by William James and Ernst Mach. Russell's version of neutral monism is philosophically sophisticated and continues to generate discussion, partly because his structural analysis of physics has been taken up by contemporary Russellian monists in ways that go beyond neutral monism proper.

**The Background: Dissatisfaction with Both Dualism and Physicalism**

In his early work, Russell was not primarily concerned with the mind-body problem. But by the 1920s, his work on the foundations of physics and on the theory of knowledge converged on a series of questions about the relationship between the world as described by physics and the world as experienced. *The Analysis of Mind* (1921) and *The Analysis of Matter* (1927) contain his most sustained treatments of these issues.

Russell's problem was essentially Mach's problem: the world of physics seems entirely different from the world of experience. Physics describes electrons, electromagnetic fields, spacetime geometries — abstract structural entities with no hint of the red of a sunset or the pain of a toothache. Yet physics is supposed to describe the same world that we experience. How do these two descriptions connect?

**Pure Experience as the Neutral Basis**

Influenced by William James's "radical empiricism," Russell proposed that the fundamental stuff of the universe is neither mental nor physical but a neutral something from which both are constructed. Following James, he called this "neutral stuff" and was deliberately vague about its characterization: it is whatever it is that constitutes the basic data from which both physics and psychology build their theories.

Mental events — experiences, perceptions, thoughts — are structures built from this neutral stuff when it is organized by psychological laws of association, memory, and inference. Physical objects — electrons, tables, planets — are structures built from the same neutral stuff when it is organized by the laws of physics. The same elements participate in both kinds of structure.

This is not to say that mental and physical structures are the same structures. They are organized differently and governed by different laws. But they share a common basis.

**Russell's Key Insight: Physics Describes Structure, Not Nature**

What makes Russell's version of neutral monism philosophically distinctive is his analysis of what physics actually tells us. In *The Analysis of Matter*, Russell argues that physics is a purely structural science. It tells us the mathematical structure of the world — which quantities stand in which relations, and how those relations evolve over time — but it is entirely silent about the intrinsic nature of the things that have these structural properties.

This is the claim later called "structural realism": all we can know about the external world is its abstract structure, not its qualitative intrinsic character. The implication for mind is significant: the world of physics is compatible with having any intrinsic nature whatsoever, including an experiential one. Physics does not tell us that electrons are non-experiential; it just tells us nothing about their intrinsic nature at all.

This insight is what has been taken up by contemporary "Russellian monists" like Chalmers, Goff, and Strawson. On their reading, the intrinsic nature of physical properties might be experiential or proto-experiential — and this suggestion is not ruled out by physics, since physics describes structure rather than intrinsic nature.

**Russell vs. Contemporary Russellian Monism**

There is an important distinction between Russell's own neutral monism and what contemporary philosophers call "Russellian monism." Russell's neutral monism holds that the neutral elements are neither mental nor physical — they are genuinely neutral. Contemporary Russellian monism tends to hold that the intrinsic nature of physical properties is experiential or proto-experiential — which looks more like panpsychism or idealism than genuine neutral monism.

The difference matters: genuine neutral monism holds that the basic elements are not characterized as experiential (they are neutral), while contemporary Russellian monism holds that the basic elements are experiential or proto-experiential. The former avoids a commitment to consciousness being fundamental; the latter makes consciousness (or something like it) fundamental.

Russell himself was ambivalent. He sometimes described his neutral elements in ways that make them sound experiential (as "percepts" with qualitative character), and sometimes in ways that make them sound genuinely characterless. The tension in his position is part of what makes it philosophically interesting — and part of what has led to the variety of positions that claim descent from him.

**The Problem of the Structureless**

The fundamental difficulty for Russell's neutral monism is what might be called the problem of the structureless basis. If the neutral elements are genuinely characterless — neither mental nor physical, neither experienced nor unexperienced — then in what sense are they anything? A truly neutral stuff seems to be nothing in particular.

Russell's response is that the neutral stuff need not be characterless — it just needs to be such that neither "mental" nor "physical" applies to it in any fundamental sense. The mental and physical characterizations are both ways of organizing and describing elements that are more basic than either. But this response has struck many readers as unstable: the elements must have some character, and if their character cannot be described mentally or physically, we have no idea what that character is.

**Legacy**

Despite its difficulties, Russell's neutral monism has had lasting influence. His structural analysis of physics has been vindicated by the philosophy of physics, where structural realism is a serious position. His point that physics does not reveal the intrinsic nature of matter has been central to contemporary debates about panpsychism and the hard problem. And his attempt to construct both minds and physical objects from a common neutral basis remains a live option for philosophers dissatisfied with both dualism and straight physicalism.
