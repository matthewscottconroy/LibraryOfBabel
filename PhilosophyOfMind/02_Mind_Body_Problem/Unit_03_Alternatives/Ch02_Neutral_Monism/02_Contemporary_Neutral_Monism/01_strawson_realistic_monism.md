# Strawson's Realistic Monism

Galen Strawson's "realistic monism" is one of the more provocative and discussed positions in contemporary philosophy of mind. Strawson begins from a straightforward commitment to the reality of consciousness and draws what he regards as the inescapable consequence: if consciousness is real and if physicalism is true, then the physical must itself be experiential. The result is a version of panpsychism — or panpsychism-adjacent realistic monism — that challenges the standard assumptions of both physicalists and dualists.

**The Argument from Real Physicalism**

Strawson distinguishes between two kinds of physicalism. Realistic physicalism accepts that phenomenal consciousness is genuinely real — that there is something it is like to see red, to feel pain, to think about mathematics — and holds that this genuine reality is physical. Unrealistic physicalism claims to be physicalist but effectively denies that consciousness is genuinely real by reducing it to functional or behavioral states that lack any phenomenal character.

Strawson insists on the first and argues that it leads somewhere unexpected. If phenomenal consciousness is genuinely real, and if it is physical, then the physical must include phenomenal properties. But then the standard physicalist picture — matter without any experiential character — is false. Matter must be the kind of thing that has, or can give rise to, phenomenal experience.

But "give rise to" in a genuinely explanatory sense means that there must be some continuity between the basis (matter) and the result (experience). Strawson argues that it is unintelligible how wholly non-experiential matter could give rise to genuine experience. This would require consciousness to spring into existence from something radically unlike it — genuine ontological emergence, which Strawson regards as brute, unintelligible magic.

The alternative: experience does not emerge from non-experiential matter. Either experience was always present (in some form) in the physical, or experience is identical with some physical states in a way that does not require emergence.

**Panpsychism as the Consequence**

Strawson concludes that real physicalism entails panpsychism — not that experience goes "all the way down" in the sense that fundamental particles have rich conscious experiences, but that the physical must have intrinsic experiential or proto-experiential properties that are the basis from which more complex forms of experience are built.

This is Russellian monism as panpsychism rather than as neutral monism. Strawson is explicit that the neutral stuff of Russell's neutral monism must be experiential in character, not genuinely neutral. The intrinsic nature of physical properties — what physics describes structurally — is experiential. There is no genuine neutrality: the moment you take seriously that experience is real and physical, you are committed to the experiential character of the physical.

**The Priority of Experience**

A distinctive feature of Strawson's position is its methodological starting point. He insists that experience is the one thing we know with certainty and directly. Everything else — including the external physical world — is known indirectly through inference or theory. This Cartesian starting point leads Strawson to insist that any adequate theory of reality must make room for experience as it actually is, not dissolve it into something that is not experience.

This generates his critique of eliminativist and reductive views: they don't solve the problem of consciousness, they deny it. Any position that concludes by saying consciousness is really nothing but neural firing patterns (described purely functionally) has simply changed the subject. Strawson insists on staying with the explanandum: the phenomenal character of experience, the redness of red, the painfulness of pain.

**Objections and Responses**

The most common objection is the combination problem: even if micro-level experiential properties exist, how do they combine to constitute macro-level consciousness? Strawson accepts the force of the combination problem and acknowledges it as the central remaining challenge for his position.

His response is not to solve the combination problem but to argue that it is less mysterious than the alternative. Asking how experience combines to form experience maintains a continuity of kind that the physicalist's demand — explain experience from entirely non-experiential matter — does not. The combination problem is hard, but it is hard in a different way than the hard problem of consciousness is for physicalism.

A second objection is that Strawson's position simply relabels the mystery. Calling the intrinsic nature of matter "experiential" without specifying what this means for an electron does not explain anything. Strawson replies that this criticism proves too much: all fundamental concepts are primitive. The concept of experience is precisely what we understand most directly, and positing it at the fundamental level is not obscure in the way that positing an utterly alien "neutral" basis would be.

**Strawson and the Space of Positions**

Strawson's realistic monism occupies an interesting position in the logical space of mind-body views. It is not substance dualism: there is one substance, the physical. It is not standard type-identity physicalism: the physical includes experiential properties that are not captured by standard functional-physical descriptions. It is not property dualism: there is no separate realm of mental properties added to a non-mental physical substrate. It is panpsychism: experiential properties belong to the physical fabric of reality.

The position illustrates how the choice between physicalism and dualism is less clear-cut than it initially appears. A view can claim to be physicalist while holding that the physical is fundamentally experiential — in which case "physicalism" and "idealism" converge, and the traditional categorizations cease to be useful.
