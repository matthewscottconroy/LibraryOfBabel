# Comparing Neutral Monism to Panpsychism

Neutral monism and panpsychism are often conflated, and they do share important features. Both reject standard physicalism's picture of a non-experiential matter as the fundamental basis of reality. Both hold that the mind-body problem cannot be solved by reducing consciousness to purely structural-functional descriptions. But the positions are genuinely distinct, and appreciating the difference illuminates what is at stake in the broader debate about the metaphysics of mind.

**The Core Contrast**

Panpsychism holds that consciousness, or proto-consciousness, is a genuine property of the fundamental physical constituents of reality. Electrons, quarks, or whatever the basic physical entities turn out to be, have intrinsic experiential properties in addition to (or as the intrinsic nature of) their structural properties. The experiential is not neutral — it is specifically experiential, characterized by subjective, qualitative features.

Neutral monism holds that the fundamental constituents of reality are neither mental nor physical. The mental and the physical are both higher-level structures built from neutral elements. Neither mind nor matter is fundamental; both are constructions.

The contrast can be sharpened: panpsychism makes mind (or proto-mind) fundamental and derives matter from it (or alongside it). Neutral monism claims to make neither mental nor physical fundamental and derives both from something more basic.

**The Collapse Toward Panpsychism**

The history of neutral monism suggests that it tends to collapse toward panpsychism. Russell's neutral elements turn out to be described in terms that sound experiential (they are "percepts," they have qualitative character). Mach's "elements" are sensations — and sensations are standardly understood as experiential. James's "pure experience" sounds like experience, even when James insists it is neutral.

Galen Strawson has pressed this objection most forcefully: there is no genuinely neutral stuff. The moment the neutral elements are characterized concretely enough to play their theoretical role, they turn out to be experiential. A truly neutral element — without any experiential or physical character — would be nothing at all.

If this is right, neutral monism is not a stable third option between physicalism and panpsychism. It either collapses toward physicalism (if the neutral elements turn out to be physical after all) or toward panpsychism (if they turn out to be experiential).

**The Collapse Toward Physicalism**

On the other side, some versions of neutral monism look like physicalism with a different emphasis. If the neutral elements are characterized primarily in terms of their causal-structural roles, and if these roles are what physics describes, then the "neutral" elements are just physical entities described abstractly. The claim that they are "also" mental in some sense is either empty (it makes no difference) or reduces to something like functionalism (the mental just is the structural-causal pattern).

Neutral monists who want to avoid this collapse need to say something substantive about what the neutral elements are like intrinsically — what their nature is over and above their structural-causal role. But as soon as they do this, the question of whether these intrinsic properties are experiential or not becomes pressing, and the position tends toward either panpsychism or eliminativism.

**What Neutral Monism Gets Right**

Despite its instability, neutral monism captures an important insight: the standard framing of the mind-body problem as a choice between "physical stuff that generates mind" and "mental stuff that is added to the physical" may be the wrong framing. Both physicalism and dualism accept the dichotomy and then try to navigate it. Neutral monism says: reject the dichotomy itself.

This is a valuable methodological point. If the concepts "physical" and "mental" are both abstractions from something more fundamental — if they are both ways of organizing and describing the world rather than descriptions of what the world fundamentally is — then the traditional mind-body problem may be ill-posed. We are not looking for a way to connect two things that are already given; we are looking for the level of description at which the distinction between mental and physical has not yet been drawn.

**Russellian Monism as a Middle Path**

Contemporary "Russellian monism," as defended by Chalmers, Goff, and others, occupies the conceptual space between neutral monism and panpsychism. It holds:

- Physics describes only structural-relational properties of matter.
- There must also be intrinsic properties that realize these structural relations.
- The intrinsic properties may be experiential or proto-experiential.

If the intrinsic properties are experiential, this is panpsychism. If they are genuinely neutral, this is neutral monism. If they are unknown but possibly either, this is "open Russellian monism" — a position that holds that the intrinsic nature of matter is a live question that current knowledge cannot settle.

David Chalmers has defended this "open Russellian monism" as the epistemically honest position: we know from the structural analysis of physics that the intrinsic nature of matter is not captured by physics; we do not yet know whether that nature is experiential, neutral, or something else entirely.

**Practical Implications for the Mind-Body Problem**

For the practical purposes of philosophy of mind, the distinction between neutral monism and panpsychism matters mainly when it comes to explaining the specific phenomenal character of human consciousness. Both positions have a similar story about why there is experience at all (the fundamental level has it). They diverge on whether fundamental experience is specifically phenomenal — qualitatively characterized — or merely proto-experiential.

The advantage of genuine neutral monism over panpsychism is that it avoids the question of what it is like to be an electron. But this advantage comes at the cost of leaving the intrinsic nature of the neutral elements entirely uncharacterized — which may be a greater philosophical cost than the panpsychist's commitment to micro-experience.

The debate remains open. The key insight shared by all positions in this family — neutral monism, Russellian monism, panpsychism — is that the standard physicalist assumption of a non-experiential matter is not mandated by science and creates more problems than it solves.
