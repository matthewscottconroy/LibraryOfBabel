# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Neutral Monism

## Key Arguments

**Russell's Argument for Neutral Monism**
Russell argued in The Analysis of Mind (1921) that both mind and matter can be analyzed as constructs from a more fundamental neutral "stuff"—events or particulars that are neither mental nor physical in themselves. Mental states (percepts, images, desires) and physical objects are different arrangements or series of the same neutral elements. The argument is driven by the parsimony of avoiding two fundamentally distinct substances (Cartesian dualism) and by the recognition that physics increasingly undermines our intuitive picture of matter as something solid and distinct from experience.

**Mach's Phenomenalist Monism**
Ernst Mach argued that both physics and psychology are ultimately sciences of "elements"—primitive sensory qualities (colors, sounds, spatial relations, temporal durations)—which are neutral between mental and physical. Physical objects are groups of elements organized by spatial-temporal laws; mental states are groups of elements organized by psychological laws. The same element can be a physical fact (as a member of the nexus of physical causation) and a mental fact (as a component of someone's experience). Mach's view collapses the distinction between appearance and reality by making elements the common currency of both sciences.

**The Dual-Aspect Argument for Russellian Monism**
Contemporary Russellian monists (Chalmers, Strawson) argue that the hard problem of consciousness arises because physics characterizes matter purely in terms of dispositional-relational properties (mass, charge, spin) without specifying any categorical, intrinsic nature. If we identify the intrinsic categorical basis of physical properties with phenomenal or proto-phenomenal properties, we get a version of neutral monism that can explain both the existence of consciousness and its physical correlates: the same intrinsic properties are described from inside as phenomenal and from outside as physical.

**The Argument from Structural-Dispositional Physics**
Physics tells us only how things are structured and what they do—it characterizes matter in terms of dispositions and relations, never intrinsic nature. This is Bertrand Russell's observation in The Analysis of Matter (1927). The intrinsic nature of the physical world—what fills in the structural description—is unknown to physics. Neutral monists argue that phenomenal properties are the best candidate for these intrinsic categorical bases: consciousness is not something over and above physical structure but what realizes physical structure from the inside.

**Spinoza's Dual-Aspect Monism as Neutral Monism**
Spinoza held that God or Nature is a single substance with two known attributes—Thought and Extension—neither of which is more fundamental than the other. Mental and physical descriptions are two aspects of a single neutral reality, not two different ontologies. Each attribute is complete in itself and follows its own laws; "the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things." Interpreted as neutral monism rather than idealism or materialism, Spinoza offers a historically deep precedent for the view that mind and matter are different modes of describing a single neutral underlying reality.

## Core Concepts

**Neutral Monism**
Neutral monism holds that the fundamental constituents of reality are neither mental nor physical but neutral—they can give rise to both mental and physical properties depending on how they are organized or related. It is monist in positing a single fundamental kind of stuff and neutral in refusing to identify that stuff with either mind or matter. William James, Ernst Mach, and Bertrand Russell are its most famous proponents. The position avoids the interaction problem (there are not two substances to interact) and the emergence problem (consciousness does not mysteriously arise from non-conscious matter) at the cost of making the nature of the neutral elements obscure.

**Russellian Monism**
Russellian monism is the contemporary version of neutral monism, associated with Chalmers, Goff, and Strawson, which holds that the intrinsic categorical properties of physical things are phenomenal or proto-phenomenal. Physics describes the world in purely structural-relational terms; it leaves open what fills in the structure. Russellian monists propose that qualia or phenomenal properties fill this role. This is distinct from both substance dualism (qualia are not a second substance) and standard physicalism (qualia are not reducible to purely relational-functional properties). It claims to solve the hard problem by identifying consciousness with the intrinsic nature of matter.

**Dual-Aspect Theory**
Dual-aspect theory holds that mind and matter are two aspects—two modes of describing or knowing—a single underlying reality that is itself neither mental nor physical. Spinoza's attributes are the classical version; contemporary formulations (Chalmers' "property dualism with monist metaphysics") treat phenomenal and physical properties as different aspects of neutral properties. The view avoids dualism (there is one underlying substance) and materialism (the mental is not reducible to the physical) but faces the challenge of specifying what the neutral underlying reality is.

**Proto-Phenomenal Properties**
Proto-phenomenal properties are properties that are not themselves fully phenomenal (not experiences) but that combine to constitute phenomenal properties. Russellian monists posit proto-phenomenal properties as the intrinsic categorical bases of physical dispositions; when organized in the right way (as in a human brain), they give rise to full phenomenal consciousness. This concept is introduced to avoid the full commitment to panpsychism (all matter is conscious) while still grounding consciousness in something closer to it than purely functional properties. Its coherence is disputed.

**The Combination Problem**
If the fundamental constituents of reality are micro-phenomenal or neutral-with-phenomenal-aspects, then the full phenomenal experience of a conscious subject must result from the combination or integration of these micro-properties. But it is unclear how the subjective, unified experience of a human being could result from the combination of many sub-personal, micro-level proto-phenomenal properties. This combination problem is the main internal difficulty for neutral monism and panpsychism: the transition from micro-level phenomenal bits to macro-level unified consciousness is as unexplained as the transition from physical to phenomenal properties in standard accounts.

## Thought Experiments

**James's Radical Empiricism: What Are Pure Experiences?**
William James proposed that the most elementary constituents of reality are "pure experiences"—unsorted sensory events that are neither subjective nor objective until sorted by context. The same pure experience of a white field can be "the paper in my hand" (a physical object) or "my perception of white" (a mental state) depending on which context of relations it is placed in. The thought experiment is meant to show that the mental/physical distinction is not intrinsic to the elements of experience but emerges from their organization, making neutral monism seem more plausible than either dualism or reductive materialism.

**The Intrinsic Nature of Physics (Russell)**
Russell invites us to consider what physics actually tells us about electrons, protons, and fields. Physics gives us equations describing how these entities behave in relation to one another and to measuring instruments—purely structural information. But equations tell us nothing about what electrons are in themselves—their intrinsic nature. Something must fill this structural role, and the only intrinsic natures we have any access to are phenomenal properties. The thought experiment motivates Russellian monism by showing that physics, far from explaining consciousness in its own terms, necessarily leaves the intrinsic nature of matter open.

**Chalmers' Fading Qualia and Neutral Monism**
Chalmers' fading qualia thought experiment—progressive replacement of neurons with functional equivalents—challenges all theories of consciousness. For neutral monists, it raises a specific question: do the neutral properties migrate with the substrate or with the functional organization? If neutral properties are intrinsic to the neural substrate, replacing neurons would change them; if they go with functional organization, the theory approaches functionalism. The thought experiment tests whether neutral monism can make principled predictions about which physical changes affect consciousness.
