# Leibniz and the Panpsychist Roots of Monadology

Leibniz's monadology, discussed in the historical section as a response to Descartes, deserves revisiting as a founding document of the panpsychist tradition in philosophy of mind. Leibniz was not calling himself a panpsychist — the term did not exist — but his view that all substances have perceptual properties of some kind is the clearest early formulation of the panpsychist idea.

**The Mill Argument and Its Conclusion**

Recall Leibniz's mill argument: if you walked through a mill that thought and perceived, enlarged to the size of a building, you would find only mechanical parts pushing on each other — "but never anything by which to explain a perception." From this Leibniz concludes that perception cannot be explained by the arrangement or motion of parts.

This leaves Leibniz with a choice: either perception is not real, or it is a fundamental feature of reality that cannot be derived from mechanical interaction. Leibniz chooses the second option: perception is fundamental. Every monad — every truly simple substance — perceives.

**Degrees of Perception**

Leibniz holds that monads differ not in whether they perceive but in the clarity and consciousness of their perceptions. At the lowest level: bare monads, which have the dimmest, most confused perceptions. The monad that constitutes an ordinary piece of matter has perceptions so dim and confused that it seems completely mindless from the outside. At an intermediate level: animal souls, which have perception accompanied by attention and memory. At the highest level: rational souls (like human minds), which have apperception — self-conscious, reflexive awareness.

The crucial point: the difference between a human mind and a stone is not the presence or absence of perception but the clarity and consciousness of perception. Minds are not a different kind of thing from matter; they are the same kind of thing (perceptual-appetitive activity) at a higher degree of organization and clarity.

**Why This Matters for Contemporary Panpsychism**

Leibniz's panpsychist monadology anticipates several features of contemporary panpsychism:

1. **Proto-experiential properties as fundamental**: Leibniz holds that perception is a fundamental feature of reality, not something that emerges from non-perceptual components. This is precisely what contemporary panpsychists like Chalmers and Goff argue.

2. **Degrees of mentality**: Leibniz's picture of mentality as coming in degrees — from barely-there dim perceptions in simple substances to full self-conscious apperception in rational beings — avoids the sharp emergence of consciousness at a particular level of complexity. There is no "consciousness threshold"; there is only greater and lesser organization of always-present experiential properties.

3. **The Combination Problem**: Leibniz's monadology faces a version of what contemporary philosophers call the "combination problem": how do the dim perceptions of many simple monads combine to constitute the rich, unified consciousness of a rational being? Leibniz's answer — that there is a dominant monad that represents all others — is not fully satisfying, and this problem has not been solved in contemporary panpsychism either.

**Leibniz's Difference from Modern Panpsychism**

Leibniz differs from modern panpsychism in important ways. His panpsychism is embedded in a theistic metaphysics: monads are created and maintained by God, and their pre-established harmony is divinely arranged. Contemporary panpsychists typically try to make their view naturalistic and compatible with modern science.

Leibniz also holds that monads are entirely immaterial. Matter, on his view, is a "well-founded phenomenon" — a systematic appearance produced by the activity of immaterial monads — rather than a genuinely extended substance. This idealist dimension of his monadology distinguishes it from the physicalist panpsychism of most contemporary proponents, who typically want to say that the experiential properties are properties of physical stuff, not properties of immaterial substances.

**Legacy**

Leibniz's monadology remained an important alternative to both Cartesian dualism and materialist mechanism throughout the 18th and 19th centuries. It influenced the German Idealists (who developed his emphasis on mind as fundamental) and, through them, Whitehead's process philosophy. The contemporary revival of panpsychism as a serious philosophical option owes much to the Leibnizian tradition, even when contemporary panpsychists formulate their views very differently from Leibniz.
