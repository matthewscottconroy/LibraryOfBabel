# Whitehead's Process Philosophy

Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) developed a comprehensive metaphysical system — process philosophy — in which experience is a fundamental feature of all reality. His philosophy of mind is embedded in a broader metaphysics that replaces the traditional notion of "substance" with "events" or "occasions of experience," and it represents one of the most ambitious and systematic attempts to overcome the Cartesian dualism of mind and matter.

**Against the Bifurcation of Nature**

Whitehead's central target is what he calls the "bifurcation of nature" — the Cartesian division between the world of primary qualities (extension, motion, mathematical structure) that science describes and the world of secondary qualities and experience that constitutes the life of the mind. On the Cartesian picture, the "real" world is colorless, soundless, and valueless; color, sound, and value exist only in the mind.

Whitehead argues that this bifurcation is incoherent: you cannot consistently hold both that nature is the colorless, mindless world of physics and that nature contains minds which experience color and sound. Either the natural world is richer than physics suggests, or minds are not part of nature.

Whitehead's solution: give up the bifurcation. Nature includes both the structural features described by physics and the experiential features described from the inside. Experience is not an add-on to nature; it is a fundamental feature of nature.

**Actual Occasions**

The basic building blocks of Whitehead's universe are "actual occasions" — momentary events of experience. Everything that exists is either an actual occasion or a composition of actual occasions. There are no "substances" in the traditional sense — no enduring, passive things that "have" properties while remaining otherwise unchanged.

Each actual occasion is a process of "concrescing" — of coming together and achieving a particular, determinate character. Each occasion has:
- **Physical pole**: its relationship to the past, its inheritance from prior occasions
- **Mental pole**: its conceptual grasp of possibilities, its creative addition of novelty

The mental pole is present, at least to a minimal degree, in every actual occasion. Even an electron has some minimal "experience" — some minimal engagement with its past and its possibilities. But the mental pole is vanishingly small in simple physical systems and enormously developed in human beings.

**Prehension and Experience**

Each actual occasion "prehends" prior occasions — it feels them, grasps them, incorporates them into its own becoming. Prehension is the fundamental relation in Whitehead's universe, and it is an experiential relation: each occasion experiences what has gone before.

This is why Whitehead's system is panexperientialist (his preferred term, rather than panpsychist): all actual occasions have some form of experience, however dim and rudimentary. Human experience is a highly organized and developed form of a universally present capacity.

**Societies and Living Creatures**

Actual occasions do not exist in isolation; they form "societies" — organized groups of occasions that share a common character and that persist through time by inheriting properties from prior members. What we call "physical objects" are societies of very simple occasions; what we call "living creatures" are societies of more complex occasions that include a dominant strand of high-grade occasions (the "occasions of experience" of the organism).

The human soul or mind, on this picture, is a sequence of dominant occasions of experience that guide the organism's behavior. The mind is not a substance separate from the body; it is the temporally ordered succession of dominant experiential occasions in the living body.

**The Metaphysical Achievement**

Whitehead's process philosophy avoids the mind-body problem by rejecting its premise: that matter is fundamentally mindless and mind is fundamentally immaterial. If all reality is experience in some form or another, there is no gap to bridge between mind and matter. The apparent gap arises from an impoverished conception of matter — one that strips it of its experiential dimensions.

**The Problems**

Whitehead's metaphysics faces formidable objections:
- It is very difficult to square with modern physics, which does not obviously support the attribution of "experience" to electrons.
- The combination problem: how do the tiny experiences of electrons combine to form the rich, unified experience of a human being?
- The proliferation of entities: a universe composed of innumerable actual occasions is ontologically extravagant.

Despite these difficulties, Whitehead's influence on contemporary philosophy of mind has grown significantly, particularly among thinkers who find both physicalism and dualism inadequate.
