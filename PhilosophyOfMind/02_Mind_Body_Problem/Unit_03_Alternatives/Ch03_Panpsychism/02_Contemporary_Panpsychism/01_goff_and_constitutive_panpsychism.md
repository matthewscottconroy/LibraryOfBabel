# Goff and Constitutive Panpsychism

Philip Goff is one of the most prominent contemporary defenders of panpsychism, and his book *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality* (2017) provides the most detailed and philosophically rigorous defense of a constitutive panpsychist position. Understanding his argument clarifies both the appeal and the difficulties of panpsychism.

**The Motivation: The Explanatory Gap**

Goff's starting point is the explanatory gap between physical descriptions and phenomenal consciousness. No description of neural processes, however detailed, seems to logically entail anything about what it is like to have the corresponding experiences. There is an irreducible gap between objective, third-person descriptions and the subjective, first-person character of experience.

Goff argues that both dualism and standard physicalism fail to bridge this gap. Dualism is unacceptable because it leaves the relationship between mind and matter completely mysterious. Standard physicalism fails because it cannot derive phenomenal consciousness from physical properties that have no experiential character.

The panpsychist solution: give physical properties an experiential character. If the fundamental constituents of matter are in some sense experiential — if they have simple, proto-experiential properties — then consciousness in complex organisms is not a mysterious addition to the physical world but an organization of what was always there.

**Constitutive Panpsychism**

Goff defends a specific version of panpsychism called constitutive panpsychism: the experiential properties of complex beings (like humans) are constituted by, built up from, the experiential properties of their physical components. The experience of seeing red is constituted by the experiences of the billions of neurons involved in visual processing, which are in turn constituted by the experiences of the fundamental particles that make up those neurons.

This is contrasted with "emergent panpsychism," which would hold that macro-level consciousness emerges from micro-level experience but is not constituted by it. Constitutive panpsychism is more committal about the mechanism of composition.

**The Combination Problem**

Goff acknowledges that constitutive panpsychism faces the "combination problem": how do micro-level experiential properties combine to form macro-level consciousness? The simple experience of an electron (if it has one) seems utterly different from the rich, unified, intentional experience of a human being. How does the complexity arise?

This is analogous to the hard problem but at the level of panpsychism: just as the hard problem asks how physical processes give rise to consciousness, the combination problem asks how simple micro-experiences give rise to complex macro-experiences.

Goff offers several partial responses:
- The combination might involve forms of experience not found at either the micro or macro levels.
- Certain phenomenal properties might be "universal" across levels of complexity.
- Some form of holism about experience might dissolve the combination problem.

He acknowledges that the combination problem is serious and not fully solved.

**The Positive Case**

Goff's positive argument for panpsychism is structured around four theses:

1. **The Transparent World Thesis**: Physics describes the quantitative-relational structure of the world but does not tell us about the intrinsic nature of what has that structure. Physics tells us that electrons have charge, but not what it is for an electron to have charge — what charge consists in, intrinsically.

2. **The Qualitative Nature of Consciousness**: Consciousness involves intrinsic qualities — the redness of red, the painfulness of pain. We know about these intrinsic qualities through direct experience.

3. **The Intelligibility of Combination**: If the fundamental constituents of matter have intrinsic properties that are proto-experiential, then the combination of those properties to form complex consciousness is at least intelligible — more intelligible than the emergence of consciousness from intrinsically non-experiential properties.

4. **The Best Available Account**: Panpsychism, combined with some account of how micro-experiences combine, provides a better account of the place of consciousness in nature than any alternative.

**The Response to Objections**

Goff addresses the common objections:
- *Implausibility of electron consciousness*: Goff argues that attributing proto-experiential properties to electrons is no more implausible than attributing any other intrinsic properties to them. We don't know what electrons are like in themselves; proto-experiential properties are as good a candidate as any.
- *The combination problem*: Acknowledged as serious, but argued to be less mysterious than the emergence of consciousness from intrinsically non-experiential matter.
- *Scientific disconfirmation*: Goff argues panpsychism is compatible with all scientific findings, since science is concerned with structure, not intrinsic nature.
