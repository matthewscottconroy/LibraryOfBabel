# The Combination Problem

The combination problem is the central challenge for all panpsychist positions. It asks: how do the micro-level experiential properties of fundamental particles combine to constitute the macro-level consciousness of complex beings like human beings?

**The Structure of the Problem**

The combination problem is not one problem but several related problems:

**The Subject Combination Problem**: Fundamental particles (or whatever the basic experiential subjects are) have their own micro-level experiences. A human brain consists of billions of neurons, which consist of trillions of atoms, which consist of countless subatomic particles — each of which (on panpsychism) has some micro-experience. But a human being has a single, unified subject of experience. How do billions of micro-subjects combine to form one macro-subject?

**The Quality Combination Problem**: Even if micro-subjects can combine, the qualities of micro-experiences seem entirely unlike the qualities of macro-experience. If an electron has some form of proto-experience, it is presumably nothing like seeing red or hearing middle C. How do utterly simple, alien micro-experiences combine to form the rich, qualitatively specific macro-experiences of conscious beings?

**The Unity Problem**: Macro-consciousness is unified in a specific way — all the components of a conscious experience are experienced *together*, as aspects of a single field. But micro-experiences seem to lack this unity: there is no reason to think that the experience of one neuron is unified with the experience of another. What produces the unified structure of macro-consciousness?

**Historical Attempts**

Leibniz faced the combination problem and offered what he took to be a solution: each monad has its own full-scale representations of the entire universe (at various degrees of clarity), and "combination" is not literal combination of micro-experiences but the domination of a central monad. The human soul is the monad that represents the organism's body most clearly and guides its behavior.

William James raised the combination problem explicitly and found it devastating for panpsychism: "The complication in the theory of combination is enormous. The more clearly we conceive of the problem, the less obviously is there any solution." James found no way to explain how a "combination" of feeling-atoms could produce a unified feeling.

**Contemporary Responses**

Several responses to the combination problem have been developed:

**Cosmopsychism** (Goff, Shani): Instead of building up from micro-experience to macro-experience, we should start with the cosmos itself as the basic unit of experience. The universe as a whole is conscious; smaller systems have derived experiences that are constraints or aspects of cosmic consciousness. This inverts the standard panpsychist direction.

**Phenomenal Bonding** (Goff): There might be a specific kind of phenomenal relation — "phenomenal bonding" — that binds micro-experiences into unified macro-experiences. This relation is not reducible to physical relations but is a fundamental feature of the experiential domain.

**Panpsychist Relations as Constitutive**: Perhaps some forms of experiential combination are primitive — not further analyzable — and these primitive combinations produce the unified wholes we find in macro-consciousness.

**Illusionism**: Frankish argues that the combination problem dissolves if we accept that the features of consciousness that make the problem hard — the specific qualitative character, the unity, the subjectivity — are illusory. If macro-consciousness is an introspective illusion, there is nothing that needs to be "built up from" micro-experiences.

**The Significance of the Combination Problem**

The combination problem is significant because it shows that panpsychism does not obviously solve the hard problem — it just relocates it. Instead of asking how non-experiential matter gives rise to experience (the hard problem for physicalism), we must ask how micro-experience gives rise to macro-experience (the combination problem for panpsychism). Whether the combination problem is easier than the hard problem is debated.

Panpsychists argue that there is at least a continuity of kind in the combination problem — experience gives rise to experience — that is absent in the physicalist's problem, where non-experiential matter is supposed to give rise to experience. Whether this continuity makes the combination problem genuinely easier is one of the central issues in the current debate.
