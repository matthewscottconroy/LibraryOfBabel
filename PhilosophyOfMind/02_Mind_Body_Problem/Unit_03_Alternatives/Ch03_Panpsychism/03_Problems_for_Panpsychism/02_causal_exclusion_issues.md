# Causal Exclusion and Panpsychism

One of panpsychism's supposed advantages is that it avoids the causal exclusion problem that troubles property dualism. If consciousness is the intrinsic nature of physical matter, there is no competition between mental and physical causes — they are one and the same process described in two ways. But the causal story for panpsychism is more complicated than this initially suggests.

**The Apparent Advantage**

For property dualism, the exclusion problem is acute: phenomenal properties are distinct from physical properties, and causal closure means physical effects have sufficient physical causes. If phenomenal properties also cause effects, physical effects are overdetermined. If they don't, epiphenomenalism follows.

Russellian monism seems to avoid this: if phenomenal properties just are the intrinsic nature of physical properties, there is no duplication of causal work. The physical and the phenomenal are the same process — the physical description gives the structure, the phenomenal description gives the intrinsic character. One causal process, two descriptions.

**The Remaining Tension**

But the causal exclusion problem has not fully evaporated. Consider: what does the causal work in producing behavior? On the Russellian monist picture, physics describes the causal structure of the process: neurons fire, activating downstream neurons, producing muscle contractions. The intrinsic nature of these processes is experiential — but does the experiential aspect make any causal contribution beyond what the structural-relational aspect already provides?

If the experiential aspect contributes nothing beyond the structural aspect, we are back to something like epiphenomenalism about the specifically phenomenal dimension. The phenomenal character is "along for the ride" in the causal process but does not change the causal structure.

Panpsychists respond that this worry presupposes an overly narrow conception of causation. If the intrinsic nature of physical processes is phenomenal, then phenomenal properties are causally relevant — they are the intrinsic nature of causally efficacious processes. There is no sense in which they are "extra" to the causes; they are the causes, described from the inside.

**Emergence and Panpsychism**

A related issue arises for emergent panpsychism. If macro-level consciousness emergences from micro-level experiential properties — and if the emergent consciousness has properties not present at the micro level — does the emergent consciousness have its own causal powers?

If yes, we face a version of the exclusion problem: the micro-level physical processes have sufficient causal powers to produce behavior, and the emergent macro-level consciousness has additional causal powers. Overdetermination threatens.

If no, emergent macro-consciousness is causally inert — epiphenomenalism about emergent consciousness, even within a panpsychist framework.

**The Response from Holism**

Some panpsychists invoke holism about causation to respond. If causes are always the entire state of a system (or a relevant aspect of it), and if the state includes phenomenal properties, then phenomenal properties are always part of the causes. There is no problem of additional causal contribution because causation is always holistic.

This is related to debates about causal explanation in general: when we give a causal explanation of an event, we always select some aspects of the total cause as "the cause" for explanatory purposes. The phenomenal aspects might be legitimately selected as causally relevant for certain explanatory purposes, even if the structural aspects are also relevant.

**The Deep Issue**

The deep issue underlying causal exclusion discussions for panpsychism is whether phenomenal properties contribute anything causal over and above the structural-functional properties. If panpsychists cannot answer this question satisfactorily, they face either causal overdetermination or epiphenomenalism — the same dilemma that faces property dualists.

This is one reason that some contemporary panpsychists have moved toward cosmopsychism — starting with the whole rather than building up from parts — hoping to avoid the exclusion problem by making the consciousness of the whole the fundamental causal agent.
