# Unit Introduction: Consciousness

## Central Questions

Consciousness is both the most familiar feature of mental life and the most resistant to scientific explanation. We are conscious, and this is what makes our lives worth having; yet what consciousness *is* — why it exists, what it consists in, how it relates to the physical processes in the brain — remains deeply contested. The questions driving this unit are: What exactly is consciousness, and how do we demarcate the phenomenon? Why does physical processing give rise to subjective experience? What is the structure of consciousness — is it unified, does it come in degrees, are there multiple distinct forms of it? And what are the most promising theoretical accounts of consciousness, and what would it take for one of them to succeed?

David Chalmers' distinction between the *easy problems* and the *hard problem* remains the sharpest organizing framework for these questions. The easy problems — explaining how the brain integrates information, controls behavior, discriminates stimuli, generates verbal reports — are tractable by the standard methods of cognitive science. The hard problem — explaining why all this processing is accompanied by *experience*, why there is something it is like to be a conscious being — resists reduction to mechanism. This distinction is contested (some deny it is coherent; others deny it marks anything deep), but engaging with it carefully is essential for understanding the field.

A second set of questions concerns the *varieties* of consciousness. Ned Block's distinction between *phenomenal consciousness* and *access consciousness* — between the qualitative feel of experience and the availability of information for reasoning and report — has generated substantial debate. The distinction matters for assessing theories: a theory that explains access consciousness (Global Workspace Theory, for instance) may not thereby explain phenomenal consciousness, and vice versa. Self-consciousness, temporal consciousness, and altered states of consciousness (in sleep, anesthesia, psychedelic experience) provide further theoretical leverage by revealing the conditions under which different aspects of consciousness come apart.

## Major Positions and Debates

### The Hard Problem and Its Responses

Chalmers' hard problem has generated three broad types of response:

*Type-A physicalism* (Daniel Dennett, eliminativists) denies that there is a hard problem at all. The phenomenal/functional distinction is an illusion; consciousness is nothing over and above what functional and behavioral analysis reveals. Dennett's *heterophenomenology* holds that we should treat subjects' first-person reports as data to be interpreted rather than taken at face value — the contents of consciousness are just the contents of intentional states that produce verbal reports. On this view, there are no qualia in the philosophically loaded sense, only functional properties that seem (to us) like more than functional properties.

*Type-B physicalism* (Brian Loar, Ned Block in some moods) holds that consciousness is a physical property but that we have two *conceptual routes* to this property: physical/functional concepts (picking out the property by its causal-physical role) and phenomenal concepts (picking out the property by acquaintance or by its intrinsic feel). The explanatory gap is a feature of the concepts, not the world. There is no ontological gap; there is a *conceptual* gap that generates the illusion of an ontological one.

*Property dualism* (Chalmers, Jackson before his recantation) holds that phenomenal properties are genuinely distinct from physical properties, not reducible to them. The conceivability of zombies and the knowledge argument (Mary's room) show that physical facts do not entail phenomenal facts.

*Illusionism* (Keith Frankish) holds that phenomenal consciousness — the intrinsic, ineffable, non-relational feel of experience — is an illusion. What we introspect as a rich phenomenal property is actually a functional/intentional state that represents itself (inaccurately) as having phenomenal properties. Illusionism is Type-A physicalism with a specific diagnosis of where the illusion comes from.

### Qualia, Mary's Room, and the Knowledge Argument

Frank Jackson's Mary is a scientist who knows all physical facts about color vision — every wavelength, every neural process — but has been confined to a black-and-white environment. When Mary first sees red, does she learn something new? Jackson's intuition: yes. What she learns is what it *is like* to see red — a phenomenal fact not entailed by the complete physical story. The knowledge argument concludes that physicalism is false.

Responses to the knowledge argument have been extensive and subtle. The *Ability Hypothesis* (Lawrence Nemirow, David Lewis) holds that Mary gains new *abilities* — to remember, recognize, and imagine colors — not new propositional knowledge. The *phenomenal concepts strategy* holds that Mary gains a new *concept* of an old physical property, not knowledge of a new property. The *acquaintance hypothesis* holds that Mary gains *knowledge by acquaintance* rather than propositional knowledge, but this is compatible with physicalism if acquaintance is a species of physical relation.

### Global Workspace Theory and Higher-Order Theories

The two major neuroscientifically grounded theories of consciousness are Global Workspace Theory (GWT, Bernard Baars, Stanislas Dehaene) and Integrated Information Theory (IIT, Giulio Tononi). GWT holds that consciousness consists in the global broadcasting of information across a workspace accessible to multiple cognitive processes — it is an account of *access consciousness*. When information enters the global workspace, it becomes conscious; when it is processed locally, it remains unconscious.

IIT holds that consciousness is identical to integrated information (Φ), a mathematical measure of the degree to which a system's causal powers are irreducible to the sum of its parts. IIT is a theory of *phenomenal consciousness* — it claims that the intrinsic, qualitative character of experience is identical to high Φ. IIT has the virtue of making testable predictions (feedforward networks should be less conscious than recurrent ones; split-brain patients should have two streams of consciousness) but its mathematics is controversial, and its implication that simple networks can be conscious (while feedforward networks cannot) strikes many as counterintuitive.

Higher-order theories (David Rosenthal, Peter Carruthers) hold that a mental state is conscious when it is accompanied by a higher-order representation of it — a thought *about* the state or a perception of it. Higher-order theories are attractive for explaining why some mental states are conscious and others are not, and they provide a natural account of introspective access, but they face the challenge of explaining how higher-order representations generate phenomenal properties rather than merely indexing functional states.

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**Thomas Nagel** articulated the subjective character of experience in "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974). Nagel argued that the objective methods of natural science — which abstract away from any particular point of view — are constitutively incapable of capturing what it is like to be a bat, or what it is like to be anything. The fact that there is something it is like to be a bat is what makes bats conscious, and this fact is not capturable by third-person description. Nagel was not committed to dualism, but he argued that we lack the conceptual resources for an adequate physicalist account.

**Frank Jackson** proposed the knowledge argument in "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982). Mary the color scientist knows all physical facts but learns something new when she first sees red. Jackson concluded that physicalism is false. In later work, Jackson retracted this conclusion, endorsing the phenomenal concepts strategy and arguing that Mary gains a new concept of an old property. His retraction is itself philosophically instructive about the difficulty of the argument's evaluation.

**David Chalmers** gave the field its current shape through his articulation of the hard problem in "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1994) and *The Conscious Mind* (1996). Chalmers argues that phenomenal consciousness cannot be functionally analyzed or physically reduced, and that the right response is property dualism. His later work develops panpsychism and structural coherence theories.

**Ned Block** distinguished phenomenal consciousness from access consciousness in "On a Confusion About a Function of Consciousness" (1995). This distinction cuts across the standard debates: functionalism can explain access consciousness but arguably cannot explain phenomenal consciousness. Block's paper is essential for evaluating whether any given theory of consciousness is addressing the right target.

**Daniel Dennett** argues in *Consciousness Explained* (1991) that there is no Cartesian theater — no privileged point where information becomes conscious. Consciousness is multiple simultaneous drafts of narrative, with no fact of the matter about exactly when or where something became conscious. Dennett's eliminativism about qualia is the most sustained attempt to dissolve the hard problem.

**Keith Frankish** develops illusionism in "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness" (2016): phenomenal properties are not real properties of experience but properties that our introspection *represents* experience as having. This is not a denial that experience exists but a claim about its nature: experiences have functional properties, not phenomenal ones in the philosophically loaded sense.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1994) / *The Conscious Mind* (1996)**
The central text for understanding the hard problem and the case for property dualism. The 1994 paper is the essential reading for the hard problem specifically; the 1996 book develops the full position. Every serious student of consciousness must engage with Chalmers' argument, whether to accept or reject it.

**2. Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974)**
A short, beautiful paper that states the subjective character of experience with unmatched clarity. Nagel does not solve the problem; he articulates it. The paper is essential both as an entry point into the hard problem and as a model of philosophical writing — a difficult idea made accessible through a carefully chosen example.

**3. Block, "On a Confusion About a Function of Consciousness" (1995)**
The distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness is now part of the standard toolkit of consciousness research, and Block's paper is where it was clearly defined. Essential for evaluating theories and for understanding why one theory's success at explaining access consciousness does not vindicate it as a theory of phenomenal consciousness.

**4. Dennett, *Consciousness Explained* (1991)**
The most ambitious attempt to dissolve the hard problem rather than solve it. Whatever one thinks of Dennett's conclusion, the book exemplifies how to apply a thorough-going functionalism to the phenomenology of consciousness — how to explain away, rather than explain, the intuitions that generate the hard problem. A necessary foil for property dualists.

**5. Dehaene, *Consciousness and the Brain* (2014)**
The most accessible and comprehensive presentation of Global Workspace Theory and the neuroscientific evidence for it. Dehaene describes the experimental paradigms (masking, inattentional blindness, binocular rivalry) that constitute the empirical program of consciousness science and explains how GWT integrates them. Essential for understanding the relationship between philosophical theorizing and empirical consciousness research.

## Open Questions

**1. Is the hard problem a genuine problem, a pseudo-problem, or something else?**
The debate between Chalmers and Dennett is ultimately a dispute about whether there is a hard problem at all. Dennett thinks the intuitions that generate the hard problem rest on cognitive illusions about the nature of consciousness; Chalmers thinks those intuitions track something real. This dispute cannot be resolved by further empirical work alone — it requires philosophical clarification of what the intuitions are and why they have the force they do.

**2. Can IIT's predictions be empirically tested?**
IIT makes specific predictions: that feedforward networks should be less conscious than recurrent ones; that cerebellum should be less conscious than cortex (it is more feedforward); that integrated information can be measured with TMS-EEG protocols. The *perturbational complexity index* (PCI) is a proposed proxy for Φ that correlates with states of consciousness in anesthetized and brain-injured patients. But whether PCI measures what IIT says it measures is contested. Can a theory of consciousness be tested empirically without presupposing a prior account of consciousness?

**3. Does the phenomenal/access distinction hold up?**
Block's distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness is widely accepted, but Block himself argues that the two can come apart: rich phenomenal consciousness can exceed access (the overflow argument). Critics argue that consciousness without access is unknowable and so empirically idle. The debate between Block and Cohen/Dennett about whether consciousness "overflows" cognitive access remains unresolved and has significant theoretical implications.

**4. What is the relationship between attention and consciousness?**
It is widely assumed that attention is required for consciousness — that only attended stimuli become conscious. But there is evidence for conscious perception of unattended stimuli. The relationship between attention, consciousness, and access is a major open empirical and philosophical question.

**5. Could a theory of consciousness be complete?**
Even if we had a perfect functional/neural account of all the easy problems, and even if we had a theory that correctly predicted all facts about which states are conscious and what they are like, would we have *explained* consciousness? Or would there be a residual question — why does this particular physical/functional organization give rise to *this* phenomenal character? The question of whether the hard problem could ever be fully answered may itself be a philosophical problem without a scientific resolution.
