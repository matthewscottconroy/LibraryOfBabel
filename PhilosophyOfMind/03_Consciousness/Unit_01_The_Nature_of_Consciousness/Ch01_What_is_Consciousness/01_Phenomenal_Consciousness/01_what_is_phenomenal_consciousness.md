# What Is Phenomenal Consciousness?

Phenomenal consciousness is the feature of mental life that makes it, in Thomas Nagel's famous phrase, like something to have it. When you see a red apple, there is something it is like for you to see that redness — a specific qualitative character to your experience that is not captured by any description of the physical processes in your visual cortex. This qualitative character — the felt quality of experience — is what philosophers call phenomenal consciousness, or qualia.

**The Basic Concept**

The term "phenomenal" comes from the Greek *phainomenon*, meaning appearance. Phenomenal consciousness refers to the way things appear to a conscious subject — not the physics of how they are, but the experience of what they are like. The painfulness of a pain, the itchiness of an itch, the blueness of the sky as seen, the taste of coffee, the sound of music — these are phenomenal properties of mental states.

Phenomenal consciousness is sometimes contrasted with purely informational or functional states. A thermostat processes information about temperature and responds accordingly — but there is presumably nothing it is like to be a thermostat. A chess-playing computer evaluates millions of positions — but there is presumably nothing it is like to do this. What would need to be added for there to be a phenomenal dimension — for it to be like something to be the thermostat or the chess computer? That question is the hard problem of consciousness.

**Qualia**

Philosophers use the term "qualia" (singular: quale) for the intrinsic, qualitative properties of phenomenal experience. Qualia are the specific feel of experiences — the redness of seeing red, the specific phenomenal character of tasting a lemon versus tasting honey, the particular quality of the ache of a muscle versus the sharp pain of a cut.

Several features are attributed to qualia in the philosophical literature:

*Intrinsicality*: Qualia are intrinsic properties of mental states, not relational or functional properties. The redness of seeing red is a feature of the experience itself, not a feature of how the experience is connected to other states.

*Ineffability*: Qualia are difficult or impossible to fully convey in language. You can describe the physics of red light, describe the neural processes it triggers, describe the behavioral dispositions it produces — but none of this captures what seeing red is like for someone who has never seen it.

*Privacy*: Qualia are directly accessible only to their subject. You cannot know what it is like for me to see red by observing my behavior, my brain states, or any third-person data. The phenomenal character of experience is, in some sense, essentially first-personal.

*Immediacy*: Qualia are known directly, without inference. I do not infer that I am in pain; I feel it. The phenomenal character of experience is given immediately to the subject who has it.

**Why Phenomenal Consciousness Matters**

Phenomenal consciousness is philosophically significant for several reasons.

It is what makes the mind-body problem hard. If mental states were purely functional — defined entirely by their causal roles — they could in principle be explained by physics and information theory. The hard problem arises precisely because phenomenal consciousness seems to be something over and above functional organization: it seems that you could have all the functional organization without the phenomenal character. The zombie argument (discussed elsewhere) makes this intuition precise.

It is what makes personal identity matter morally. If future-you has all the same memories, beliefs, and character traits but no phenomenal consciousness — if there is nothing it is like to be future-you — then arguably nothing that matters morally about you has survived. Questions about personal identity over time, about what makes your life yours, are deeply connected to questions about phenomenal consciousness.

It is the explanandum of neuroscience's most ambitious project: the neural correlates of consciousness. Neuroscientists seek the brain states that are correlated with conscious experience — but specifying what they are looking for requires a grasp of what phenomenal consciousness is.

**The Spectrum of Views**

Philosophers disagree about the nature of phenomenal consciousness.

Some hold that qualia are genuine, irreducible features of reality — properties that cannot be captured by functional or physical descriptions. This is the position of philosophers like Chalmers, Nagel, and Jackson (before his later recantation).

Some hold that qualia are real but reducible — they are physical or functional properties under a different mode of presentation. Mary in the knowledge argument learns something new because she gains a new way of representing an old physical property, not because she encounters a new property.

Some hold that qualia are illusory — that introspection gives us a systematically distorted picture of our own mental states, and that the supposedly irreducible qualitative character of experience is an artifact of how we represent our own mental processes, not a genuine feature of those processes. This is illusionism, defended by Keith Frankish and Daniel Dennett.

These positions will be explored in detail in the chapters on the hard problem and its responses. For now, phenomenal consciousness — whatever its ultimate nature — is the central phenomenon that any adequate philosophy of mind must account for.
