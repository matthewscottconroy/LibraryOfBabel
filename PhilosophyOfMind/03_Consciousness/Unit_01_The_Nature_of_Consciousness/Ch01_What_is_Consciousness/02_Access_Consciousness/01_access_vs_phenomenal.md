# Access Consciousness vs. Phenomenal Consciousness

One of the most influential distinctions in contemporary philosophy of mind is Ned Block's distinction between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness. Block introduced this distinction in 1995 to clarify the different questions that philosophers of mind are actually asking when they speak of "consciousness," and to prevent conflation of problems that require different solutions.

**Block's Two Concepts**

*Phenomenal consciousness* (P-consciousness) is experience — the subjective, qualitative aspect of mental states. A mental state is phenomenally conscious when there is something it is like to be in that state. P-consciousness is the redness of seeing red, the painfulness of pain, the specific feel of tasting wine. It is defined entirely by its intrinsic phenomenal character.

*Access consciousness* (A-consciousness) is availability for use. A mental state is access-conscious when its content is available for reasoning, reporting, and the rational control of action. A-consciousness is about functional role: a state is A-conscious if it is "poised" to be used by central systems — if it can be reported, thought about, integrated with other states, and used to guide behavior.

Block's key claim is that these are genuinely different concepts and can come apart. Phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness are not the same thing, and conflating them causes serious errors in the philosophy of mind.

**The Conceptual Independence of the Two Notions**

The independence of P-consciousness and A-consciousness can be seen by considering cases where they come apart — at least in principle, if not in fact.

*A-consciousness without P-consciousness*: A state could be available for reasoning and report without having any phenomenal character. A thermostat's internal states are accessible to its control mechanisms but have no phenomenal character. If there are "zombies" — beings physically identical to humans but lacking phenomenal experience — then zombies have A-conscious states without P-conscious ones.

*P-consciousness without A-consciousness*: Perhaps more interesting, there may be cases of phenomenal experience that is not available for cognitive report. Block argues that peripheral visual experience — the vague awareness of what is at the edges of the visual field — may be phenomenally present without being fully accessible to attention and verbal report. Similarly, certain emotional states might be phenomenally felt without being available to full deliberative reasoning.

Block cites the phenomenon of "blindsight" as relevant: patients with lesions to visual cortex can correctly guess about visual stimuli they report not seeing. On one interpretation, they have some access to the stimuli without phenomenal experience of them — A-consciousness without P-consciousness.

**Why the Distinction Matters**

The distinction is important because the hard problem of consciousness is specifically about P-consciousness, not A-consciousness. A-consciousness is, Block argues, relatively tractable: explaining why certain information is accessible to reasoning and report is a matter of functional organization, and in principle this can be explained in terms of information processing, neural architecture, and cognitive science.

P-consciousness, by contrast, is where the hard problem bites. Even after we explain everything about accessibility, integration, and report, we have not explained why there is something it is like — why the information processing is accompanied by experience.

Block worries that some philosophers of mind, especially those inclined toward functionalism, conflate the two notions and thereby treat the hard problem as already solved when it has merely been explained away. If you define consciousness in terms of access and report, then explaining consciousness is just explaining information availability — and you have changed the subject.

**Complications and Critiques**

The distinction has been challenged from several directions.

*The inference problem*: All our evidence for P-consciousness comes from A-consciousness — from reports, behavior, and inference. We can only study P-consciousness through its A-conscious effects. This means that claims about P-consciousness without A-consciousness — like the claim that peripheral vision is phenomenally rich — are always somewhat speculative.

*The integration objection*: Some philosophers hold that P-consciousness and A-consciousness are not genuinely separable. On higher-order theories of consciousness, a state is phenomenally conscious only if it is represented by a higher-order state — which is a form of accessibility. On global workspace theory, phenomenal consciousness just is availability to global broadcasting. On these views, Block's distinction is a distinction without a real difference.

*Ned Block's response*: Block maintains that even if P-consciousness and A-consciousness always co-occur in normal human beings, they are conceptually distinct and can be separated in principle. The question of whether they can be separated is empirical; the question of whether they are the same concept is conceptual. And conceptually, they are different: you can understand what P-consciousness is without understanding anything about access, and vice versa.

**The Significance for Theories of Consciousness**

Much of the debate about theories of consciousness — global workspace theory, higher-order theories, integrated information theory — can be framed in terms of Block's distinction. These theories typically explain A-consciousness: they explain why certain information is globally available, why certain states can be reported, why certain information is integrated across systems.

The question of whether they explain P-consciousness — whether explaining access thereby explains experience — is precisely the question at the heart of the hard problem. Block's distinction keeps this question sharp and prevents it from being dissolved by a change of subject.
