# Creature Consciousness vs. State Consciousness

When we say that something is conscious, we might mean two different things. We might mean that the thing itself is conscious — that it is awake, alert, and experiencing the world. Or we might mean that one of its mental states is conscious — that a particular perception, thought, or feeling has phenomenal character or is available for report. This distinction between creature consciousness and state consciousness is important for understanding the different questions that arise in the philosophy of mind.

**Creature Consciousness**

Creature consciousness is a property of organisms or systems as a whole. To say a creature is conscious is to say it is a subject of experience — that there is something it is like to be it. We attribute creature consciousness to human beings during waking life and deny it (or regard it as uncertain) during dreamless sleep or under deep anesthesia.

Creature consciousness comes in degrees and kinds. A sleeping human is unconscious, but only temporarily. A comatose patient may be in a minimally conscious state. Patients in a persistent vegetative state may have no conscious experience at all, or may retain some. Animals of different species may be conscious to varying degrees.

Several dimensions of creature consciousness are distinguishable:

*Wakefulness*: The contrast between being awake and being asleep or unconscious. This is the most basic dimension of creature consciousness and is often measured in terms of arousal level.

*Sentience*: The capacity to have phenomenal experiences — to feel pain and pleasure, to have qualitative experiences. This is the property that grounds moral concern for an organism.

*Self-awareness*: The capacity to be aware of oneself as a subject — to have some form of representation of one's own mental states and existence. This is a higher-level form of consciousness that may be present only in beings with sophisticated cognitive capacities.

*Reflexive consciousness*: The capacity to think about one's own thinking — the metacognitive dimension of mind that characterizes human beings and perhaps some non-human primates.

**State Consciousness**

State consciousness is a property of individual mental states. A particular perception, belief, desire, or emotion may be conscious or unconscious — it may or may not be phenomenally experienced, it may or may not be available for report and reflection.

State consciousness has been analyzed in several ways:

*Phenomenal state consciousness*: A mental state is phenomenally conscious if there is something it is like to be in that state — if it has phenomenal character. Visual experiences, pains, and emotional feelings are paradigmatically phenomenally conscious.

*Access-conscious states*: A mental state is access-conscious if its content is available to reasoning, report, and the rational control of behavior. This is Block's A-consciousness applied to states.

*Higher-order consciousness*: On higher-order theories, a mental state is conscious if there is a higher-order representation of it — if the subject is (or is disposed to be) aware of being in that state. On this view, state consciousness involves a kind of reflective or meta-representational dimension.

**The Relationship Between Creature and State Consciousness**

The two notions are related but not reducible to each other. A creature is conscious (in the most basic sense) if and only if it has at least some conscious mental states. But having conscious states involves more than just being a creature; the states themselves have to have certain properties.

The relationship becomes complicated when we consider borderline cases. A patient who is vegetative may show neural responses to stimuli that look like access consciousness without any phenomenal character — or with phenomenal character but no access. It may be that the creature is, in some attenuated sense, conscious while also being unable to access or report any of what is happening.

**Transient and Dispositional Consciousness**

A refinement of state consciousness distinguishes between occurrently conscious states (states that are currently conscious) and dispositionally conscious states (states that are not currently conscious but could become so). My belief that Paris is the capital of France is not currently occurrently conscious — I am not thinking about it — but it is accessible and could become conscious if I directed attention to it.

This distinction is important for understanding mental states that influence behavior without being phenomenally present. Implicit memories, background beliefs, and automatic skills may be dispositionally accessible without being phenomenally active.

**Why This Matters**

The distinction between creature and state consciousness helps carve up the explanatory targets of a theory of consciousness. A theory like global workspace theory primarily explains what makes certain states A-accessible — a theory of state consciousness. A theory of animal consciousness asks whether certain creatures have phenomenal experience — a theory of creature consciousness.

Conflating the two can lead to errors. A creature might be a conscious organism (a subject of experience) even when most of its mental states are unconscious. And a system might process information in ways that resemble state consciousness while falling short of the kind of unified subjectivity characteristic of creature consciousness.
