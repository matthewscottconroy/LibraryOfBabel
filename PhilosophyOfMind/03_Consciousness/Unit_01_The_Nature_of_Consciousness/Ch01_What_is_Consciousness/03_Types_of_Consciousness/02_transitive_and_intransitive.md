# Transitive and Intransitive Consciousness

Another important distinction in the taxonomy of consciousness is between transitive and intransitive consciousness. Like the creature/state distinction, this division helps philosophers be precise about what they are asking when they ask about consciousness.

**Intransitive Consciousness**

Intransitive consciousness is the kind of consciousness expressed by sentences of the form "X is conscious" without any object. A person waking from sleep becomes conscious — they are conscious, period, without necessarily being conscious of any particular thing. An organism is conscious in this intransitive sense if it has the capacity for experience, regardless of what specific experiences it is currently having.

Intransitive consciousness is roughly equivalent to creature consciousness. It answers the question: is this thing a subject of experience? Is there something it is like to be it? The intransitive use describes a general capacity or condition rather than a relation to a particular object.

**Transitive Consciousness**

Transitive consciousness is expressed by sentences of the form "X is conscious of Y." I am conscious of the coffee cup on my desk; I am conscious of the headache I have; I am conscious that it is Tuesday. In each case, consciousness is directed toward a particular object, fact, or state.

Transitive consciousness is a relation between a subject and something of which the subject is aware. It is connected to the philosophical concept of intentionality — the "aboutness" of mental states. To be conscious of something is to have a mental state that is directed toward or represents that thing.

**How the Distinction Generates Problems**

The distinction generates philosophical problems precisely because the two kinds of consciousness are not straightforwardly related.

Can there be intransitive consciousness without transitive consciousness? Can there be a subject of experience who is not conscious of anything in particular? Some philosophers argue that consciousness always requires an object — even the most diffuse and undirected form of consciousness is consciousness of something (a vague field, a background hum, the passing of time). But others argue that there can be pure awareness without any specific content — a kind of "empty" or "bare" consciousness that is not directed at any particular object.

This question connects to debates in phenomenology. Husserl and his followers held that consciousness is always intentional — always a consciousness-of-something — and that the attempt to describe "pure consciousness" abstracted from all content is misguided. But the question of whether there is a "pure witness" behind all content — a subject that is not itself any particular content — remains philosophically active, particularly in cross-cultural philosophy of mind drawing on contemplative traditions.

Can there be transitive consciousness without intransitive consciousness? Could a non-conscious system be conscious of something? This sounds paradoxical: how can a system represent or be aware of something without being a conscious subject? But it depends on how "conscious" is being used. A system could respond differentially to stimuli in ways that mimic awareness without being a subject of experience. Many cognitive scientists and AI researchers talk about systems being "aware of" features of their environment without attributing phenomenal consciousness.

**Phenomenology's Contribution**

The phenomenological tradition has developed rich accounts of transitive consciousness under the heading of intentionality. Husserl analyzed the structure of intentional acts — the way consciousness is directed toward objects — in terms of the distinction between the act (noesis) and the object as intended (noema). Every conscious act has an intentional structure: it represents its object in a particular way, from a particular perspective, with particular features highlighted.

This detailed structural analysis of transitive consciousness complements but does not replace the question of intransitive consciousness. Even after characterizing all the intentional structures of experience — how objects are represented, how time is experienced, how the self is implied in all experience — there remains the question of why any of this is accompanied by experience at all. This is precisely the hard problem: why is there a subject for whom all this intentional structure has the character it does?

**Practical Significance**

In clinical contexts, the distinction between transitive and intransitive consciousness is practically important. A patient may be awake and responsive (intransitively conscious) without showing clear evidence of being conscious of specific features of their environment (transitively conscious). Conversely, through non-behavioral measures, patients in vegetative states have shown neural evidence of being conscious of specific stimuli — apparently transitively conscious of something — in ways that suggest some preserved intransitive consciousness.

Understanding the relationship between these two forms of consciousness is one of the central challenges of clinical consciousness research and has direct implications for how we evaluate and treat patients with disorders of consciousness.
