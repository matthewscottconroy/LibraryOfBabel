# Further Reading: What is Consciousness?

## Primary Sources

**Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974)** — Nagel's landmark paper introduced the idea that consciousness essentially involves a subjective point of view — something it is like to be a particular creature. It remains the touchstone for discussions of phenomenal consciousness and the limits of objective, third-person explanation.

**Ned Block, "On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness" (1995)** — Block distinguishes phenomenal consciousness (P-consciousness, the felt quality of experience) from access consciousness (A-consciousness, availability to cognitive processes). This distinction has structured almost all subsequent debate about the nature and function of conscious states.

**David Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind* (1996)** — Chalmers argues that phenomenal consciousness cannot be explained purely in functional or physical terms, coining the phrase "the hard problem." The book offers a systematic treatment of dualist and non-reductive alternatives and remains a central reference point.

**Francis Crick, *The Astonishing Hypothesis* (1994)** — Crick argues that consciousness is the product of neural activity, specifically proposing that 40 Hz oscillations in thalamocortical circuits are the neural correlates of consciousness. A landmark attempt to bring consciousness into empirical neuroscience.

## Secondary Literature

**Daniel Dennett, *Consciousness Explained* (1991)** — Dennett's heterophenomenology approach denies that there is a single "Cartesian theater" where experience is unified. He defends a multiple-drafts model, arguing that the intuitions driving dualist views are systematically misleading.

**Christof Koch, *The Feeling of Life Itself* (2019)** — Koch surveys the landscape of consciousness science and defends Integrated Information Theory, while engaging accessibly with rival approaches including Global Workspace Theory. Valuable for students seeking a neuroscientist's perspective.

**Bernard Baars, *A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness* (1988)** — Baars introduces the global workspace framework, arguing that conscious contents are those broadcast widely across specialized brain systems. Foundational for cognitive and neuroscientific research programs.

**David Rosenthal, *Consciousness and Mind* (2005)** — A systematic development of higher-order thought theory, arguing that a mental state is conscious when it is accompanied by a suitable higher-order thought representing it. Represents the most developed version of the HOT program.

**Susan Schneider and Max Velmans (eds.), *The Blackwell Companion to Consciousness* (2007)** — A comprehensive reference volume covering all major theories, methodologies, and debates. Indispensable for graduate students and researchers.

**Antonio Damasio, *The Feeling of What Happens* (1999)** — Damasio draws on neuroscientific evidence from patients with brain lesions to argue that consciousness is intimately tied to the body's self-representation. Bridges philosophy of mind and cognitive neuroscience.
