# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: What is Consciousness?

## Key Arguments

**Block's Argument for a Principled P/A Distinction**
Ned Block argues that "consciousness" is ambiguous between phenomenal consciousness (P-consciousness: what it is like, the qualitative feel) and access consciousness (A-consciousness: availability for reasoning, reporting, and behavioral control). These can come apart: a state can be A-conscious without being P-conscious (blindsight: the patient correctly guesses visual stimuli they claim not to see), and possibly P-conscious without being A-conscious (Block's overflow hypothesis). The distinction matters because conflating them generates pseudo-solutions to the hard problem by explaining A-consciousness and claiming to have explained P-consciousness.

**The What-It's-Like Criterion (Nagel)**
Thomas Nagel argues that a conscious experience is one that has a subjective character—there is something it is like to have it, from the creature's point of view. The existence of this subjective character is precisely what any objective, third-person account of the brain cannot capture. Nagel does not claim the subjective is non-physical but that our current conception of the physical (objective, structural, third-person) is inadequate to accommodate the subjective. This criterion provides the most influential characterization of phenomenal consciousness.

**Dennett's Heterophenomenology**
Dennett argues that consciousness should be studied through "heterophenomenology": the systematic study of subjects' verbal reports about their experience, treated as data to be explained, without the assumption that reports are directly accurate descriptions of inner states. Consciousness is not an inner theater with a Cartesian observer; the apparent unity and richness of experience is a construction explained by cognitive mechanisms. Heterophenomenology aims to be neutral between dualist and physicalist accounts while preserving the data of first-person reports.

**The Multiple Drafts Model**
Dennett's multiple drafts model holds that the brain runs many content-generating processes simultaneously; there is no single "Cartesian theater" where consciousness is located. Rather, mental contents compete for influence in various behavioral and cognitive processes, with no single moment or place where they "become conscious." The apparent temporal unity and definiteness of conscious experience is a retrospective construction, not a real-time presentation in a unified medium. This directly challenges the traditional phenomenological view of a unified stream of consciousness.

## Core Concepts

**Phenomenal Consciousness (P-Consciousness)**
Phenomenal consciousness refers to the subjective, qualitative aspect of experience—the way experiences feel from the inside, or "what it is like" to have them. P-consciousness is Block's term for this property, contrasted with access consciousness. The hallmark of P-consciousness is irreducibility to any functional description: two functionally identical systems might differ in their P-consciousness (the zombie possibility). P-consciousness is the main target of the hard problem of consciousness and the focus of most philosophical debate about mind.

**Access Consciousness (A-Consciousness)**
Access consciousness refers to the availability of a mental state's content for use in reasoning, verbal report, and the control of behavior. A state is A-conscious if its content is poised to serve as a premise in inference, as input to speech, and as a basis for intentional action. A-consciousness is functionally definable and is what most cognitive science studies under the label "consciousness." Block's point is that A-consciousness and P-consciousness are distinct: explaining the former does not explain the latter.

**The What-It's-Like Character**
Nagel's phrase "what it is like" identifies the defining feature of conscious experience: every conscious state has a particular subjective character from the subject's perspective. There is something it is like to see blue, to taste wine, to feel anxious—a qualitative character that cannot be captured in purely functional or physical terms. The concept grounds the distinction between genuine consciousness and mere information processing and is the target of functionalist, representationalist, and eliminativist responses.

**Transitive vs. Intransitive Consciousness**
Consciousness can be construed as intransitive (a creature is simply conscious, awake, aware) or transitive (a creature is conscious of something—an object or state). Phenomenological accounts of consciousness as always intentionally directed focus on transitive consciousness; accounts of minimal wakefulness focus on intransitive consciousness. The distinction is important because different theories may handle each better: global workspace theory naturally explains access to specific contents (transitive), while biological naturalism tries to ground the minimal subjective quality (intransitive).

**The Cartesian Theater**
Dennett's critical concept of the "Cartesian theater" refers to the implicit model in which all the brain's processes send their outputs to a central "arena" where a Cartesian self views the unified, definite contents of consciousness. Dennett argues this model is incoherent—it merely relocates the problem of consciousness to a homunculus within the brain—and that the unity and definiteness of consciousness are cognitive constructions, not features of any literal inner stage. The concept is important because it diagnoses a pervasive confusion in both folk and scientific thinking about mind.

## Thought Experiments

**Nagel's Bat**
Nagel asks what it is like to be a bat—to navigate by echolocation and experience the world through ultrasonic echoes. He argues that even a complete neurophysiological description of bat sonar would not tell us what the bat's experience is like from the inside. The subjective character of bat experience is accessible only from the bat's own point of view, which we cannot occupy. The thought experiment establishes the irreducibility of subjective experience to objective physical descriptions and supports the claim that phenomenal consciousness is not captured by any third-person account.

**Blindsight**
Weiskrantz's blindsight patients have cortical damage in visual areas but retain some visual discrimination—they correctly "guess" the location or orientation of stimuli in their blind field, yet report no visual experience. The case illustrates the dissociation between A-consciousness (the information is available for response) and P-consciousness (there is nothing it is like for the patient to see it). Blindsight is important philosophical evidence for Block's distinction and challenges accounts that equate consciousness with functional access.

**The Superblindsight Thought Experiment (Block)**
Block imagines a "super-blindsighter"—a person who has learned to spontaneously report and act on visual information in their blind field, without any phenomenal experience. This person would be A-conscious of the visual stimuli without being P-conscious. The scenario is designed to make vivid that A-consciousness and P-consciousness are genuinely distinct: the superblindsighter has everything needed for access consciousness but lacks any phenomenal feel. The thought experiment directly tests whether functionalist accounts of consciousness conflate A and P consciousness.
