# Key Thinkers: What is Consciousness?

---

**Thomas Nagel** (b. 1937)

Nagel's central contribution to consciousness studies is his argument that any adequate account of mind must preserve the subjective, first-person character of experience. His 1974 paper "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" argues that even complete physical knowledge of a bat's brain would fail to capture what it is like to experience echolocation. This subjective character — the "what it's like" — cannot be accommodated by objective, third-person scientific description. Nagel does not endorse dualism straightforwardly but insists that physicalism as currently conceived is inadequate.

*Key works:* "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974); *Mortal Questions* (1979); *The View from Nowhere* (1986)

---

**Ned Block** (b. 1942)

Block is best known for drawing a sharp distinction between phenomenal consciousness (P-consciousness) and access consciousness (A-consciousness). P-consciousness refers to the qualitative, felt character of experience; A-consciousness refers to information being available for use in reasoning, reporting, and guiding behavior. Block argues these come apart — a state can be P-conscious without being A-conscious, and vice versa — and that much philosophical and empirical confusion results from failing to keep them separate.

*Key works:* "On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness" (1995); "Troubles with Functionalism" (1978); *Consciousness, Function, and Representation* (2007)

---

**David Chalmers** (b. 1966)

Chalmers distinguishes the "easy problems" of consciousness (explaining cognitive functions, attention, reportability) from the "hard problem" — explaining why any physical process gives rise to subjective experience at all. He argues that functional and physical facts leave an explanatory gap for phenomenal consciousness, and that this gap is in principle unbridgeable by any reductive strategy. He has explored property dualism, panpsychism, and "virtual reality" accounts as alternatives.

*Key works:* *The Conscious Mind* (1996); "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1995); *The Character of Consciousness* (2010)

---

**Daniel Dennett** (b. 1942)

Dennett is the most prominent contemporary defender of a thoroughly physicalist, deflationary account of consciousness. He argues that the intuitions driving the "hard problem" are the result of a mistaken "Cartesian theater" picture of the mind. His multiple drafts model holds that there is no single place in the brain where experience "happens" — cognitive processing occurs in parallel streams with no privileged moment of conscious presentation. He has consistently argued that properly explaining cognitive functions leaves nothing further to explain.

*Key works:* *Consciousness Explained* (1991); *Brainstorms* (1978); *From Bacteria to Bach and Back* (2017)

---

**Francis Crick** (1916–2004) **and Christof Koch** (b. 1956)

Crick and Koch pioneered the neural correlates of consciousness (NCC) research program, seeking to identify the minimal neural mechanisms sufficient for a given conscious state. They proposed that synchronized 40 Hz oscillations in thalamocortical circuits are strong candidates for NCCs. Their collaboration moved consciousness from a topic philosophers discussed to a mainstream empirical research program within neuroscience.

*Key works:* Crick & Koch, "Towards a Neurobiological Theory of Consciousness" (1990); Koch, *The Feeling of Life Itself* (2019)
