# Type-B Physicalism: The A Posteriori Necessary Identity

Type-B physicalism is perhaps the most sophisticated and widely held contemporary form of physicalism about consciousness. It holds that consciousness is identical with physical properties — but that this identity, unlike mathematical truths, is knowable only a posteriori, through empirical investigation rather than conceptual analysis. The classic analogy is the identity of water with H₂O: "water is H₂O" is a necessary truth, but one we could not have known from the armchair.

**Kripke's Framework and Its Application**

The framework for Type-B physicalism comes from Saul Kripke's work on rigid designators and necessary a posteriori truths. Kripke argued that natural kind terms like "water" and "gold" and proper names like "Aristotle" and "Napoleon" are rigid designators — they refer to the same thing in every possible world.

Given this, "water is H₂O" is necessarily true: in any possible world, whatever water is, it is H₂O. But the truth is not known a priori — we discovered it empirically. The necessity is not conceptual but metaphysical.

Type-B physicalists apply the same framework to consciousness: "pain is C-fiber firing" (or whatever the correct neural identification turns out to be) is necessarily true — there is no possible world in which pain occurs without the corresponding neural activity — but this necessity is discovered empirically, not by conceptual analysis.

**How This Responds to the Zombie Argument**

The zombie argument held that phenomenal consciousness and physical properties are not necessarily connected because zombies — beings with all the physical properties of conscious humans but without consciousness — are conceivable. Type-B physicalism responds: conceivability does not imply possibility. The apparent conceivability of zombies reflects our epistemic situation — our lack of a priori access to the connection between physical and phenomenal properties — not a genuine metaphysical possibility.

Just as someone ignorant of chemistry might conceive of watery-stuff that is not H₂O (it looks, tastes, and behaves like water but has different molecules), someone who lacks a priori knowledge of the mind-brain identity might conceive of a zombie. But just as the former conceivability does not make non-H₂O water metaphysically possible, the latter conceivability does not make zombies metaphysically possible.

The zombie intuition, on this view, is an illusion arising from the way our concepts pick out their references. We have two different ways of picking out the same property: "pain" picks it out by its phenomenal character, and "C-fiber firing" picks it out by its physical character. Because these are different modes of presentation of the same property, there seems to be a gap between them — but this is an epistemic gap, not a metaphysical one.

**Chalmers' Response: The Two-Dimensional Argument**

Chalmers has developed an extensive response to Type-B physicalism using two-dimensional semantics. The core of his response is that the phenomenal-physical identity cannot be genuinely necessary in the relevant sense.

For the water/H₂O case, the necessity derives from the fact that "water" picks out its reference rigidly — whatever H₂O is in the actual world, "water" refers to it in all possible worlds. So the necessity comes from the semantics of "water," not from something special about water itself.

But for consciousness, Chalmers argues, the phenomenal character of pain is not a matter of which properties "pain" refers to by description. The phenomenal character is directly and essentially involved in what pain is — not just in how we pick it out. Pain is not just whatever-property-plays-the-pain-role; it is a property that has the specific phenomenal character it has essentially. So the two-dimensional maneuver that works for water does not work for consciousness.

**The Explanatory Gap Problem**

Even if Type-B physicalism is right that consciousness is identical with some physical property, there remains what Joseph Levine calls the "explanatory gap." Even after we know that pain = C-fiber firing (for the sake of argument), we still find it impossible to understand why C-fiber firing should be painful — why it should have the specific phenomenal character it has. The explanation does not go through: we cannot derive the phenomenal character from the physical description.

For the water/H₂O case, once we know the identity, we can explain why water has its properties from the properties of H₂O — why it boils at 100°C, why it dissolves salt, why it conducts electricity. The explanation runs through. For consciousness, the explanation does not run through: we cannot explain why neural activity of a certain type is accompanied by experience of a certain character.

Type-B physicalists respond that the explanatory gap is epistemological, not metaphysical — a reflection of the special way we access phenomenal properties, not evidence that they are distinct from physical ones. But critics find this response unsatisfying.

**The Phenomenal Concept Strategy**

A related and influential development within Type-B physicalism is the phenomenal concept strategy. The strategy holds that our phenomenal concepts — the concepts we use to think about our own qualitative states — have a special character that explains why they seem to resist reduction even if their referents are physical.

Phenomenal concepts work by direct acquaintance: when I think about my pain, I deploy a concept that refers to the property directly, without any description or reference-fixing condition. This direct acquaintance is what makes it seem as though phenomenal properties are something over and above functional or physical ones. But the seeming reflects the nature of our concepts, not the nature of the properties themselves.

The phenomenal concept strategy is one of the more productive recent developments in physicalist responses to the hard problem, and it connects to debates about the knowledge argument and the nature of a posteriori necessity. Whether it fully closes the explanatory gap is still contested.
