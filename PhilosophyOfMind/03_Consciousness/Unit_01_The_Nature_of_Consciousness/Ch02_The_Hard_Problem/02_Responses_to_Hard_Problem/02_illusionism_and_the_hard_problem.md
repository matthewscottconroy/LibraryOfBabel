# Illusionism and the Hard Problem

Illusionism is the view that phenomenal consciousness — in the specific sense that generates the hard problem — does not exist. Our introspective reports represent our mental states as having intrinsic, non-functional qualitative properties (qualia), but this representation is systematically mistaken. There is no "what it is like" over and above the functional organization of our mental states; the appearance of such a thing is itself a product of how our cognitive systems represent their own states.

This is a radical position, and it is important to understand precisely what it denies. Illusionists do not deny that we have experiences, that we are conscious, or that our mental states differ in their qualitative character in some sense. They deny that our experiences have the specific intrinsic, ineffable, non-functional properties that philosophers call qualia. The "hard problem" is generated by treating this representation as accurate; illusionists argue the representation is inaccurate, and with it the hard problem dissolves.

**Dennett's Heterophenomenology**

Daniel Dennett has long been the most prominent defender of a view in this vicinity, though he resists the label "illusionism" for various reasons. His "heterophenomenology" treats first-person reports about experience as data about what subjects believe or represent about their experience, not as infallible reports about the nature of experience itself.

For Dennett, the hard problem arises from taking introspective reports too seriously — from assuming that when subjects report vivid qualitative experience, they are accurately tracking an inner reality of irreducible qualia. Dennett argues that introspection is a cognitive process that constructs representations of mental states, and these representations can be systematically misleading.

The famous case of the "color phi phenomenon" — where two rapidly alternating colored spots appear to produce a smoothly moving spot that changes color in mid-flight — illustrates this for Dennett: the brain constructs a retrospective narrative about experience that does not accurately represent the underlying processing. If this can happen for the color of a spot in apparent motion, perhaps the apparent intrinsic character of all experience is similarly constructed.

**Frankish's Illusionism**

Keith Frankish has more explicitly defended illusionism as the right response to the hard problem. Frankish distinguishes between what he calls "real seemings" — the appearance of having qualia — and actual qualia. On Frankish's view, when we introspect and it seems that we have experiences with irreducible qualitative character, this seeming is real. But the seeming does not accurately represent what is actually going on: there are no qualia with the intrinsic, non-functional properties that seem to be there.

The "hard problem" would be the problem of explaining why physical processes produce qualia. Frankish argues: they don't produce qualia, because there are no qualia in the relevant sense. What they produce is the representation of qualia — introspective representations that misrepresent the nature of mental states. The "easy problem" of explaining these representations remains, but it is indeed an easy problem.

Frankish compares his position to the treatment of secondary qualities in Galileo and Locke: colors as we experience them are not "in" objects in the way they seem to be; what is in objects is just reflectance properties, wavelength responses, and so on. Similarly, qualia as we experience them — intrinsic, ineffable, and non-functional — are not properties of experiences in the way they seem to be; what experiences have are functional properties that we misrepresent as qualitative.

**Objections to Illusionism**

The most pressing objection to illusionism is what Frankish himself calls "the meta-problem": how can the appearance of consciousness be illusory if the appearance is itself a form of consciousness? If it seems to me that I have qualia, this seeming is itself a conscious state — and this conscious state is exactly what needs to be explained. You cannot escape the hard problem by declaring that the hard problem is an illusion, because the illusion itself involves experience.

Frankish responds that the meta-problem is the real problem to solve — and it is a form of the easy problem. The question of why we have the introspective representations we do — why it seems to us that we have qualia — is an empirical question about cognitive architecture, and in principle it can be answered by a functional account of introspection.

A second objection: illusionism is self-undermining because the "illusory" qualia would have to be experienced as illusory qualia. An illusion is something that appears one way when it really is another way — but something must "appear" for the illusion to occur. If my experience of redness is an illusion, there must be some phenomenal character to the illusion. And now we face the same hard problem for that phenomenal character.

Frankish responds that the "appearing" in question need not involve phenomenal consciousness in the hard sense. The brain constructs representations of states as having qualitative character, and these representations influence behavior and verbal report, without there needing to be any genuinely phenomenal appearing in the philosophically loaded sense.

**The Cost of Illusionism**

Many philosophers, including some physicalists, find illusionism too costly. It requires that our introspective judgments — among our most certain and immediate forms of knowledge — are systematically wrong in a deep way. The certainty that I am now having an experience seems to be as certain as anything can be. Illusionism asks us to give this up, and many find the price too high.

The alternative response is to accept that we have experiences with genuine qualitative character and find a way to account for them within a broadly physicalist framework — perhaps through the phenomenal concept strategy, perhaps through a more liberal conception of what "physical" means, perhaps through some form of panpsychism. Illusionism takes the harder road of denying the datum rather than the harder road of explaining it.
