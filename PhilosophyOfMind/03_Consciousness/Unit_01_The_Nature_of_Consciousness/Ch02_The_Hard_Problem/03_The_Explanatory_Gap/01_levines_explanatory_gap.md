# Levine's Explanatory Gap

Joseph Levine introduced the concept of the "explanatory gap" in his 1983 paper "Materialism and Qualia: The Explanatory Gap." While Levine's argument is often discussed alongside Chalmers' hard problem, Levine's claim is more modest: he argues that there is an explanatory gap between physical and phenomenal descriptions — an inability to explain the qualitative character of experience in physical terms — but he does not draw the metaphysical conclusion that this gap reveals a genuine ontological distinction.

**The Nature of the Gap**

Levine asks us to consider two kinds of identity claim. The first is a paradigm case of successful reduction: temperature is mean molecular kinetic energy. This is not just a correlation claim; it is an explanation. We understand why heat — the property of being hot — is mean molecular kinetic energy: we can explain what heat does, why it has its causal profile, and how it is produced and transmitted, all in terms of molecular motion. The explanation goes through.

The second is an apparent identity claim: pain is C-fiber firing (or some more accurate neural description). Even if this identity claim is true, Levine argues, the explanation does not go through. We can learn that pain correlates with C-fiber firing, but we cannot understand why C-fiber firing should be painful. We cannot derive the painfulness of pain from any description of the neural process. There is a gap in our understanding even if there is no gap in the underlying reality.

This is the explanatory gap: not a gap in the world but a gap in our ability to explain. We cannot see how physical properties necessitate phenomenal properties — how any neural description of a brain state would lead us to expect that state to be experienced in the specific way it is.

**The Explanatory Gap vs. The Hard Problem**

Levine's explanatory gap is related to but distinct from Chalmers' hard problem.

The hard problem makes a metaphysical claim: phenomenal properties are not physical properties. Zombies are possible; consciousness goes beyond the physical. The explanatory gap, as Levine originally formulated it, makes an epistemological claim: even if phenomenal properties are physical, we cannot explain them in terms of physical descriptions.

This distinction is philosophically significant. Levine allows that physicalism might be true — that pain might be identical with some neural state — while insisting that even in that case, our understanding of mind would be incomplete. We would know that pain is a neural state without understanding why it has the phenomenal character it does.

**The Knowledge Argument Connection**

The explanatory gap connects naturally to Frank Jackson's knowledge argument. Mary the color scientist knows all the physical facts about color vision but lacks knowledge of what seeing red is like. Jackson uses this to argue that phenomenal facts are not physical facts.

Levine's gap offers a more cautious reading: Mary's limitation reveals an explanatory gap — she cannot derive what seeing red is like from the physical facts — but this might be because of the nature of our epistemic access to phenomenal properties, not because phenomenal properties are genuinely non-physical.

**Can the Gap Be Closed?**

Levine himself takes the explanatory gap seriously but does not conclude from it that physicalism is false. He argues that we might not be able to close the gap with our current conceptual resources, but that this is an epistemic limitation rather than a metaphysical fact.

Several proposals for closing the gap have been offered:

*Better neuroscience*: Perhaps when neuroscience is complete enough, we will see explanatory connections between neural properties and phenomenal properties that are currently invisible. This optimistic view holds that the gap is a function of our current ignorance.

*Conceptual revision*: Perhaps we need new concepts — concepts that bridge the physical and phenomenal — that our current conceptual framework does not contain. The gap is not evidence of a real distinction but evidence that our concepts are not yet adequate to the phenomena.

*The phenomenal concept strategy*: Our phenomenal concepts work differently from our physical concepts — they pick out their referents by direct acquaintance rather than by description. The explanatory gap arises because we cannot "translate" between these two kinds of concepts, not because their referents are distinct.

*Acceptance*: Some philosophers — including Levine in his more pessimistic moods — suggest that the explanatory gap might be ineliminable. Consciousness might be a domain where our ordinary explanatory resources break down, not because it is supernatural but because our cognitive architecture is not suited to explaining the connection between objective physical description and subjective phenomenal character.

**The Gap's Role in the Broader Debate**

The explanatory gap has proven to be a more durable concept than many initially expected. Even physicalists who are confident that consciousness is a physical phenomenon must grapple with the fact that we cannot explain it in the way we explain other physical phenomena. Whether this reflects a genuine feature of consciousness or merely our cognitive limitations is one of the central open questions in the philosophy of mind.

Levine's contribution was to isolate this epistemological issue from the metaphysical debates about zombies and dualism, allowing it to be discussed on its own terms. Even philosophers who reject Chalmers' zombie argument and are confident in physicalism often accept that there is something to be explained about why the explanatory gap appears to exist.
