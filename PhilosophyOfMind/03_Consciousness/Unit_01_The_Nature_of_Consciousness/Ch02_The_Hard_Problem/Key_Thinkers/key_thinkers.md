# Key Thinkers: The Hard Problem of Consciousness

---

**David Chalmers** (b. 1966)

Chalmers coined the term "the hard problem of consciousness" to designate the question of why and how physical processes give rise to subjective experience. He contrasts this with "easy problems" such as explaining attention, memory integration, and verbal reportability — which, however difficult empirically, are in principle tractable by functional or computational explanation. Chalmers argues that no such explanation can account for why there is something it is like to be in a physical state, and has defended property dualism and, more recently, panpsychism as live responses.

*Key works:* "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1995); *The Conscious Mind* (1996); *The Character of Consciousness* (2010)

---

**Joseph Levine** (b. 1952)

Levine introduced the concept of the "explanatory gap" in a 1983 paper that anticipated much of Chalmers' later work. He argued that even if psychophysical identity claims are true, they resist a priori derivation from physical facts in a way that ordinary scientific identities do not. Levine distinguishes the metaphysical question (is consciousness physical?) from the epistemological one (can we explain why a physical state has the properties it does?), suggesting the gap may be epistemic rather than ontological.

*Key works:* "Materialism and Qualia: The Explanatory Gap" (1983); *Purple Haze: The Puzzle of Consciousness* (2001)

---

**Frank Jackson** (b. 1943)

Jackson's knowledge argument uses the thought experiment of Mary, a scientist raised in a black-and-white room who knows all physical facts about color vision, to argue that she learns something new — what it is like to see red — upon leaving the room. If physicalism were true, Mary's complete physical knowledge should leave nothing to learn. Jackson originally took this to refute physicalism, though he later revised his view. The argument has spawned an enormous secondary literature.

*Key works:* "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982); "What Mary Didn't Know" (1986); *From Metaphysics to Ethics* (1998)

---

**Philip Goff** (b. 1978)

Goff is among the most prominent contemporary defenders of panpsychism as a response to the hard problem. He argues that the causal-structural picture of physical science leaves phenomenal properties unaccounted for and that the most parsimonious solution is to treat consciousness as a fundamental feature of reality present in some form throughout nature. His "combination problem" — explaining how micro-level phenomenal properties combine into unified macro-level experiences — is a key challenge for his own view.

*Key works:* *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality* (2017); *Galileo's Error* (2019); *Why? The Purpose of the Universe* (2023)

---

**Daniel Dennett** (b. 1942)

Dennett is the leading critic of the hard problem, arguing that Chalmers and others have been seduced by a misleading "Cartesian theater" model of the mind. He insists that once all functional, cognitive, and behavioral phenomena are explained, there is nothing further to explain — what philosophers call "qualia" are not genuine phenomena that escape functional characterization. His heterophenomenological method treats subjects' reports as data without assuming their accuracy about inner states.

*Key works:* *Consciousness Explained* (1991); "Quining Qualia" (1988); *Sweet Dreams* (2005)
