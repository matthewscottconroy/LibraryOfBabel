# Rosenthal's Higher-Order Thought Theory

David Rosenthal's Higher-Order Thought (HOT) theory is one of the most carefully developed accounts of what makes a mental state conscious. The central claim is simple: a mental state is conscious if and only if it is accompanied by a higher-order thought — a thought about that state — to the effect that one is in it. Without a higher-order thought, the mental state is unconscious.

**The Basic Idea**

The motivation for higher-order theories comes from reflecting on the difference between conscious and unconscious mental states. Both conscious and unconscious states can influence behavior, have content, and play functional roles. What distinguishes conscious from unconscious states is not their functional role but whether they are being experienced — whether the subject is, in some sense, aware of being in them.

Rosenthal's account of this awareness is in terms of higher-order representations. To be conscious of a mental state is to have a (higher-order) representation of it. Specifically, a mental state M is conscious if and only if the subject has a roughly contemporaneous, non-inferential, higher-order thought to the effect that one is in M.

This is a third-person, functionalist account of first-person experience. It does not appeal to anything mysterious or irreducible; it analyzes consciousness in terms of representational structure. A conscious pain is a pain that is accompanied by a thought "I am in pain." An unconscious pain — perhaps a mild ache that influences behavior without being noticed — lacks this accompanying higher-order representation.

**The Transitivity Principle**

The HOT theory is grounded in what Rosenthal calls the "transitivity principle": a mental state is conscious if we are conscious of it. Being conscious of a state is what makes it a conscious state. And our awareness of our own mental states consists in our representing them through higher-order thoughts.

This principle explains the asymmetry between conscious and unconscious mental states: what distinguishes them is not a difference in first-order content but a difference in whether there is a higher-order representation of the state.

**Dispositional vs. Occurrent HOTs**

Rosenthal distinguishes between occurrent higher-order thoughts and dispositional ones. For a mental state to be conscious, is it enough to be disposed to think about it (dispositional), or must one actually be thinking about it (occurrent)?

Rosenthal holds that occurrent HOTs are required. A conscious state is one that is actually accompanied by a higher-order thought, not merely one that could be. This means that consciousness requires a certain kind of cognitive activity — the actual production of a higher-order representation.

Some critics find this too demanding: it seems to imply that we are constantly producing a stream of higher-order thoughts about our experiences, which does not match our intuitive picture of consciousness as flowing along without constant metacognitive activity.

**Rosenthal's Account of Qualitative Character**

One challenge for HOT theories is explaining the qualitative character of conscious experience — the redness of seeing red, the painfulness of pain. On Rosenthal's view, qualitative character must be explained in terms of the representational content of mental states.

Rosenthal holds that sensory qualities are properties that mental states have, and they are represented in the higher-order thought as properties of those states. The redness of a visual experience is a property of the mental state that the higher-order thought represents the subject as being in. Qualitative character is thus part of the content of higher-order representation.

This is a somewhat deflationary account of qualia: instead of being irreducible intrinsic properties of experience, qualia are representational properties that can in principle be functionally characterized. Critics who take qualia to be irreducible will find this unsatisfying; Rosenthal accepts this consequence and takes it as a virtue of the theory — it integrates consciousness into a naturalistic, representational picture of the mind.

**Problems for HOT Theory**

**The absent qualia problem**: Could a system have all the right higher-order thoughts without the accompanying qualitative experience? Rosenthal's theory defines consciousness in terms of higher-order representation, but a system might represent itself as being in a certain state without there being any phenomenal character to that state. The higher-order thought seems insufficient for genuine qualitative consciousness.

**The mistaken HOT**: What if someone has a higher-order thought that they are in a certain mental state, but they are actually not? On Rosenthal's view, this would make the state conscious — but what conscious state would it be? If there is no first-order state, there is nothing to be conscious of. Rosenthal addresses this by appealing to the idea that the HOT would still make the subject conscious of something — perhaps a state with "zero intensity."

**The self-consciousness problem**: HOT theories require that subjects have thoughts about their own mental states — but this requires a capacity for self-reference and representation of mental states as one's own. Critics argue that this cognitive capacity is itself in need of explanation and that the HOT theory, rather than explaining consciousness, presupposes a sophisticated form of self-consciousness.

**The relevance of higher-order awareness**: Why should the presence of a higher-order thought about a state make the state conscious? What is the connection between being represented and having phenomenal character? HOT theories have been criticized for failing to explain why higher-order representation should produce or constitute subjective experience, rather than merely tracking it.

Despite these challenges, HOT theory remains one of the most worked-out naturalistic accounts of consciousness, and Rosenthal has developed responses to each of these objections in his extensive published work.
