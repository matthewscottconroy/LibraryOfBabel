# Lycan's Higher-Order Perception Theory

William Lycan defends a version of higher-order theory that differs from Rosenthal's in an important respect: where Rosenthal holds that the higher-order representation of a conscious state takes the form of a thought, Lycan holds that it takes the form of perception — or more precisely, internal monitoring. Lycan's theory is often called "Higher-Order Perception" (HOP) theory or the "inner sense" theory of consciousness.

**The Inner Sense Model**

Lycan draws on a long tradition of thinking about introspection as a kind of inner perception — an analogy between our awareness of the external world through the senses and our awareness of our own mental states through introspective monitoring. On this view, we have internal monitoring systems that track our first-order mental states, and the activation of these monitoring systems is what makes a first-order state conscious.

The key difference from Rosenthal's view is that Lycan does not require that the higher-order representation take the form of a propositional thought. Instead, the higher-order representation can be a more primitive form of scanning or monitoring. Inner sense is to thought as perception is to judgment: it is a form of awareness that does not necessarily involve conceptualization or propositional structure.

This makes Lycan's theory, in some ways, more applicable to non-human animals and to pre-reflective forms of consciousness. An organism might have an inner monitoring system without having the conceptual resources to form full-blown higher-order thoughts about its own mental states.

**Functional Roles and Monitoring**

Lycan's functionalism is thoroughgoing: mental states, including conscious states, are individuated by their functional roles — their causal relations to inputs, outputs, and other mental states. Inner sense systems are functional systems that have the monitoring of first-order states as their functional role.

This gives a decidedly mechanistic flavor to the account of consciousness. Consciousness is what happens when inner monitoring systems do their job — when they scan and represent the current states of first-order processing systems. There is nothing mysterious about consciousness on this view; it is a functional capacity that evolved because it was useful for flexible, adaptive behavior.

**Advantages of HOP Over HOT**

Lycan argues that the perceptual model has several advantages over the thought-based model.

First, it accommodates pre-conceptual consciousness. Many animals and infants seem to be conscious without having the conceptual resources to form thoughts about their own mental states. Inner monitoring does not require conceptual sophistication.

Second, it aligns better with cognitive neuroscience, where "monitoring" and "feedback" are familiar concepts that refer to real neural processes. The inner sense model makes consciousness more continuous with neuroscientific description.

Third, it captures the passive, immediate character of inner awareness. When I notice that I am in pain, this does not feel like the product of active cognition — forming a thought — but like a kind of noticing or detection. The perceptual metaphor fits this phenomenology better than the thought metaphor.

**Problems for HOP**

**The regress problem**: If consciousness requires inner monitoring, does inner monitoring require meta-monitoring? If I must be aware of my first-order states to be conscious, must I be aware of my monitoring of those states to be genuinely aware of my first-order states? This threatens an infinite regress of monitoring systems. Lycan responds that monitoring systems can operate without themselves being monitored — they do their job at a sub-personal level — but critics wonder whether this sufficiently distinguishes HOP from an account on which inner monitoring is itself unconscious.

**The same qualitative character problem**: Why should monitoring a first-order state produce a conscious state with the specific qualitative character of the first-order state? If I monitor a visual experience of redness, the monitoring seems to inherit the redness of what it monitors. But it is unclear how a monitoring system — qua monitoring system — determines what the resulting conscious experience is like.

**The biological plausibility question**: Is there actually a distinct inner monitoring system that operates over first-order mental states? Or is this a philosopher's fiction without clear neural correlates? The success of the HOP theory is partly an empirical question about how introspection works neurologically.

**Lycan's Representationalism**

Lycan's HOT theory is embedded within a broader representationalist account of mind. On his view, mental states are representations, and their intentional and qualitative properties are explained in terms of what they represent and how. Consciousness, for Lycan, is a feature of how representations are organized — specifically, when they are represented by inner monitoring systems.

This representationalism extends to qualia: qualitative character is a representational property, not an irreducible intrinsic property. Seeing red represents redness — a property of surfaces in the world — and the phenomenal character of seeing red is determined by this representational content.

This "intentionalism" or "representationalism" about qualia is shared by many higher-order theorists and connects the theory of consciousness to the broader theory of intentionality and mental content.
