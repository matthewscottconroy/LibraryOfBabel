# First-Order vs. Higher-Order Theories of Consciousness

The debate between first-order and higher-order theories of consciousness is one of the central structural debates in the field. It concerns what is required for a mental state to be conscious: is it enough for the state to have the right kind of first-order representational content, or must there also be a second-level representation of that state?

**First-Order Theories**

First-order theories hold that a mental state is conscious in virtue of the kind of content it has, without any requirement for a higher-order representation. The most prominent first-order theory is "first-order representationalism" or "one-level representationalism," associated with Fred Dretske and Michael Tye.

On first-order representationalism, a mental state is phenomenally conscious in virtue of representing the world in a certain way. The redness of seeing red is a matter of representing redness — a property of surfaces — in the appropriate way. The specific phenomenal character of an experience is determined entirely by what it represents.

For Tye and Dretske, the relevant kind of representation is "poised, abstract, non-conceptual representation" (Tye's PANIC theory): experiences are conscious when they represent states of the organism's environment in a form that is poised to impact on the cognitive systems responsible for conceptual processing and reasoning.

The advantage of first-order theories is parsimony: consciousness requires only the right kind of first-order content, not an additional layer of higher-order representation. This makes the theory simpler and avoids the regress problems that higher-order theories face.

The challenge for first-order theories is explaining how a first-order representation — a visual experience, say — acquires phenomenal character. What distinguishes a phenomenally conscious visual representation from an unconscious one? First-order theorists point to differences in the kind of content (abstract, non-conceptual) and its functional role (poised to influence cognition), but critics argue these differences do not explain why some representations are experienced and others are not.

**Higher-Order Theories**

Higher-order theories hold that a first-order state becomes conscious only when it is represented by a higher-order state — a state about that state. The two main variants are higher-order thought (HOT) theories (Rosenthal) and higher-order perception or inner sense theories (Lycan, Armstrong).

The advantage of higher-order theories is that they explain the intuitive connection between consciousness and self-awareness: to be conscious of something is to be aware of it, and to be aware of a mental state is to represent it. The transitivity principle — that conscious states are states we are conscious of — motivates the higher-order approach.

Higher-order theories also explain how the same first-order state can be conscious at one time and unconscious at another: it depends on whether a higher-order representation is present.

**The Debate**

The central dispute between first-order and higher-order theories concerns the relation between consciousness and self-representation. Higher-order theorists hold that all genuine phenomenal consciousness involves a form of self-representation — being aware of oneself as being in a certain state. First-order theorists deny this: phenomenal consciousness can be entirely "outward-looking," representing features of the world without any implicit self-representation.

The empirical debate is hard to adjudicate because both theories are consistent with most behavioral and neurological data. The key difference is phenomenological: does conscious experience necessarily involve a "for-me-ness" — an implicit self-reference — or can there be purely object-directed experience without any implicit subject?

Phenomenologists in the Husserlian tradition have argued that all experience involves what Husserl called "pre-reflective self-consciousness" — a form of self-awareness that is not a higher-order thought but is built into the structure of experience itself. This position attempts to honor the intuitions behind higher-order theories (consciousness involves self-awareness) without adopting the specific higher-order machinery (a separate second-level representation).

**Conscious vs. Unconscious Representations**

One of the most productive implications of the first-order/higher-order debate concerns unconscious mental representations. Both sides agree that there are unconscious representations — representations that influence behavior but are not experienced. The question is what distinguishes these from conscious representations.

For first-order theorists: unconscious representations differ in their functional properties (they are not poised to influence central cognition in the right way) or their content-type (they are conceptual rather than non-conceptual, or they represent the wrong kinds of properties).

For higher-order theorists: unconscious representations lack a higher-order representation of them. The neural states underlying unconscious visual processing produce a first-order representation of the visual scene, but there is no higher-order system that represents the subject as being in that first-order state.

These different accounts make different predictions about cases where first-order and higher-order systems dissociate — as they do in certain neurological conditions and in laboratory experiments involving masking, inattentional blindness, and change blindness.
