# Further Reading: Higher-Order Theories of Consciousness

## Primary Sources

**David Rosenthal, "Two Concepts of Consciousness" (1986)** — Rosenthal introduces the core higher-order thought (HOT) thesis: a mental state is conscious if and only if it is accompanied by a suitable higher-order thought representing it as one's own state. This paper sets the framework for the HOT tradition.

**David Rosenthal, *Consciousness and Mind* (2005)** — A systematic book-length development of HOT theory, addressing objections and distinguishing the view from rivals. Rosenthal argues that consciousness is a relational property — a state has it by virtue of its relations to other states.

**William Lycan, *Consciousness* (1987)** — Lycan defends a higher-order perception (HOP) theory: a mental state is conscious when it is the target of an inner perceptual faculty (inner sense). He argues HOP is more psychologically plausible than HOT because monitoring is an ongoing process rather than a discrete thought.

**Peter Carruthers, *The Opacity of Mind* (2011)** — Carruthers defends a "dispositional" higher-order theory, arguing that conscious states are those that are available to, rather than actually targeted by, higher-order representations. He integrates this with a broader theory of mindreading and self-knowledge.

## Secondary Literature

**Ned Block, "Mental Paint" (2003)** — Block argues against higher-order theories by contending that phenomenal properties cannot be fully captured by the relational, representational facts posited by HOT and HOP views. A useful source of objections.

**Uriah Kriegel, *Subjective Consciousness: A Self-Representational Theory* (2009)** — Kriegel develops a "same-order" alternative to standard HOT theories: a state is conscious when it represents itself, rather than being represented by a distinct higher-order state. Useful for contrasting HOT with related self-representational approaches.

**Josh Weisberg, "Abusing the Notion of What-It's-Likeness: A Response to Block" (2011)** — Defends Rosenthal's HOT theory against Block's objections, clarifying the relationship between higher-order representation and phenomenal consciousness.

**Sydney Shoemaker, "Self-Knowledge and Inner Sense" (1994)** — A critical examination of inner sense theories (HOP), raising the "aiming problem": it is unclear how inner sense reliably targets conscious states rather than producing representations independently.
