# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Higher-Order Theories

## Key Arguments

**Rosenthal's Higher-Order Thought Thesis**
David Rosenthal argues that a mental state is conscious if and only if it is accompanied by a suitable higher-order thought (HOT) to the effect that one is in that state. The HOT need not itself be conscious—it can be a dispositional or occurrent but non-conscious thought. The argument is that without such a higher-order awareness, the mental state would be in no way present to the subject; it would be functionally identical to an unconscious state. This thesis locates consciousness in meta-representation rather than in first-order phenomenal properties.

**The Transitivity Principle**
Rosenthal invokes the transitivity principle: a mental state is conscious only if we are conscious of it. Unconscious mental states are states we have no awareness of; conscious states are states we are aware of. This principle is nearly definitional but has the implication that consciousness requires a second-order state directed at the first-order state. The transitivity principle motivates HOT theory by making the relevant upward-directed representation constitutive of, rather than merely correlated with, consciousness.

**The Argument Against First-Order Theories**
HOT theorists argue that first-order theories of consciousness—which hold that a mental state's consciousness is intrinsic to it or constituted entirely by its first-order representational properties—cannot explain the difference between conscious and unconscious states of the same functional type. Two states with identical causal roles can differ in whether they are conscious (if one is HOT-accompanied and the other is not). Only a second-order condition can explain why states of the same first-order type sometimes reach consciousness and sometimes don't.

**Lycan's Inner Sense Theory**
William Lycan proposes a higher-order perception (HOP) theory: consciousness involves an "inner sense" that monitors first-order mental states, analogous to a perception faculty that takes mental states rather than external objects as its objects. On this view, consciousness is a form of inner perception rather than inner thought. The advantage over Rosenthal's HOT view is that inner sense is more naturalistically motivated—perception is already well understood—and it avoids some of the conceptual apparatus of "thoughts." The disadvantage is that it generates a regress: is the inner sense itself conscious? And if so, is there an inner sense of the inner sense?

**The Problem of Targetless Thoughts**
A serious objection to HOT theory is that it seems possible for a HOT to occur without a corresponding first-order state: I could have a higher-order thought that I am in pain when in fact I have no pain state. In this case, HOT theory implies that there is a conscious pain—there is something it is like to be in pain—without any pain state. But this seems incoherent: a conscious experience without a first-order state is a ghost HOT. Rosenthal's response is that in this case, the subject undergoes a "mental state" that consists in there seeming to be a pain, even without a genuine pain, but this response is contested.

## Core Concepts

**Higher-Order Thought (HOT) Theory**
HOT theory holds that a first-order mental state is conscious if and only if it is accompanied by an appropriate higher-order thought—a thought to the effect that one is currently in that first-order state—where this HOT is non-inferential and non-observational. The HOT need not itself be phenomenally conscious: its role is to make the first-order state present to the subject as a mental state. Rosenthal distinguishes dispositional HOTs (a standing disposition to have such thoughts) from occurrent HOTs (an actually occurring thought), endorsing occurrent HOTs for genuine conscious states.

**Higher-Order Perception (HOP) Theory**
Lycan's HOP theory replaces Rosenthal's higher-order thought with a higher-order perception: inner sense monitors first-order states much as outer sense monitors physical objects. This captures the idea that consciousness has a quasi-perceptual phenomenology—it seems to involve a kind of awareness—while grounding it in a naturalistic faculty. HOP theory differs from HOT theory in requiring that the higher-order state be perceptual rather than propositional and in allowing for phenomenal properties of the inner sense itself.

**Self-Representationalism**
Uriah Kriegel's self-representationalist theory holds that conscious states are not conscious by virtue of a separate higher-order state but because every conscious state partly represents itself—a conscious perception of red is simultaneously a representation of red and a representation of itself as representing red. This "same-order" variant of higher-order theory preserves the insight that consciousness involves meta-representation without requiring a distinct higher-order state. It avoids some problems for HOT theory (such as targetless HOTs) but faces questions about the coherence of genuine self-representation.

**The Transitivity Principle**
The transitivity principle states that a mental state is conscious only if the subject is conscious of it. It is the core insight motivating higher-order theories: consciousness requires a subject's awareness of their own states, not merely the states themselves. The principle is relatively uncontroversial as a claim about conscious states in ordinary language, but higher-order theorists take it as a principled, constitutive truth: having a HOT directed at a first-order state is what makes that state conscious, not merely what accompanies or follows consciousness.

**Dispositional vs. Occurrent HOT**
Rosenthal distinguishes between a standing disposition to form a HOT about a state (which he thinks is insufficient for consciousness) and an actually occurring HOT (which he takes to be necessary). Some critics (like Carruthers) favor dispositional HOTs—states are conscious if they are available to be the objects of higher-order representations—which may better accommodate unconscious but available states. The distinction matters because dispositional versions imply that states can be conscious even when no higher-order thought is actually occurring, which may be more faithful to common experience.

## Thought Experiments

**The Unconscious Pain Case**
Imagine a person undergoing surgery under anesthesia whose pain pathways are active (pain information is processed, encoded, and influencing physiological responses) but who has no higher-order representation of being in pain. HOT theory implies this person has no conscious pain—there is nothing it is like to be in pain for them—because there is no HOT. This seems correct: we don't say anesthetized patients consciously suffer (normally). But if the same processing occurs in a patient with incomplete anesthesia and a malfunctioning HOT system, the intuitions may conflict. The case tests whether HOT is truly necessary and sufficient for conscious experience.

**Rosenthal's Targetless Thought**
A subject has a higher-order thought "I am currently seeing red" but is in fact in total darkness with no visual first-order state. What does HOT theory predict? It seems committed to saying the subject has a conscious "seeing of red" despite the absence of any first-order visual state. This is paradoxical: a conscious experience without any corresponding experience type. Rosenthal responds that such cases involve an "apparent" or "seeming" pain/experience without a genuine one, but critics argue this response preserves HOT theory only by undermining its constitutive claim.

**The Red-All-Over Inversion**
Consider a subject whose inner sense (on Lycan's theory) systematically misidentifies first-order states: every time the subject has a state of seeing red, the inner sense reports "seeing green." The subject will confidently report seeing green, believe they are seeing green, and act on this belief. Higher-order theories that make consciousness constituted by the higher-order state are committed to saying the subject consciously sees green. But first-order theorists insist the subject consciously sees red (because that is the first-order state's phenomenal property). The case tests which level—first-order or higher-order—determines phenomenal character.
