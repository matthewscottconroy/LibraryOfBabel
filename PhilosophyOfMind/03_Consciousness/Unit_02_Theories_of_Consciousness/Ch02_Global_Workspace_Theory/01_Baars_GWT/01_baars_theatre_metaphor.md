# Baars and the Theatre Metaphor

Bernard Baars developed Global Workspace Theory (GWT) in his 1988 book *A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness*, drawing on cognitive psychology and earlier work on cognitive architectures. GWT has become one of the most influential scientific theories of consciousness, combining psychological plausibility with some degree of empirical testability.

**The Theatrical Metaphor**

Baars presents GWT through a vivid metaphor: consciousness is like a spotlight on a theatre stage. The stage is the mind — holding multiple, competing processes that operate in parallel, largely in the dark. Only what falls within the spotlight — only what is in the focus of conscious attention — is made available to the whole cognitive system. Everything else continues to operate, but locally, without being broadcast to the rest of the system.

The "global workspace" is the metaphorical stage: a limited-capacity workspace that allows information from different brain systems to be broadcast globally. Information that enters the global workspace becomes available to many specialized processors — for reporting, reasoning, memory storage, action planning, and so on.

The key distinction is between:
- **Local processes**: numerous, fast, parallel processors that handle specialized tasks (visual processing, speech perception, motor control) without making their results widely available.
- **Global processes**: the broadcast of selected information to the entire system, making it available for a wide range of cognitive uses.

Conscious experience corresponds to the global broadcast. What you are currently conscious of is the information that is currently in the global workspace, available for system-wide use.

**The Functional Architecture**

In more technical terms, GWT proposes the following architecture:

Incoming information is first processed by specialized, "modular" processors that operate in parallel and without mutual access. These processors compete for access to the global workspace. Winning the competition — having information reach the global workspace — involves a kind of bidding process based on factors like salience, emotional significance, and the demands of current tasks.

Once information reaches the global workspace, it is broadcast globally to all processors. This global availability is what constitutes consciousness. The specialized processors can then receive this information and use it in their own processing.

This architecture explains several features of consciousness:

*Its serial character*: Although the brain processes information in parallel, consciousness seems serial — we can only consciously attend to one thing at a time (or a small number of things). GWT explains this by the bottleneck created by global workspace capacity: only a limited amount of information can occupy the workspace at any time.

*Its unifying function*: Consciousness seems to integrate information from different domains — seeing a red apple involves coordinating color, shape, memory, and expectations. The global broadcast provides this integration.

*Its reportability*: Whatever is in the global workspace can, in principle, be reported. This explains why conscious states are typically reportable.

**Consciousness vs. Cognitive Processing**

A central virtue of GWT is that it explains the difference between conscious and unconscious cognitive processing in functional terms. Unconscious processes are those that operate locally, without accessing the global workspace. They can influence behavior (subliminal perception, unconscious priming) but do not participate in the globally coordinated behavior characteristic of conscious processing.

This distinction maps onto many psychological phenomena:
- Automatic vs. controlled processing
- Implicit vs. explicit memory
- Subliminal vs. supraliminal perception
- Overlearned skills that can be performed "automatically" without conscious attention

In each case, GWT predicts that the unconscious/automatic version of the process involves local, non-broadcast processing, while the conscious/controlled version involves the global workspace.

**Baars' GWT and the Hard Problem**

Baars is a cognitive psychologist, not primarily a philosopher, and GWT is primarily a functional/computational theory. It explains the functional role of consciousness — what consciousness does — rather than why it has phenomenal character.

This means that GWT is primarily a theory of access consciousness, in Block's terminology. It explains why certain information is available for global use, report, and reasoning. It does not directly address why the information that reaches the global workspace is experienced — why global broadcasting is accompanied by phenomenal consciousness rather than occurring "in the dark."

Some philosophers and scientists have argued that this gap means GWT leaves the hard problem entirely untouched. Others argue that GWT, by identifying the neural mechanisms of access consciousness, provides the best available scientific foundation for eventually understanding phenomenal consciousness as well — even if the connection is not yet understood.

**Legacy**

Baars' GWT has been extremely influential in cognitive science and cognitive neuroscience. It provided one of the first systematic accounts of how to test predictions about consciousness empirically — asking not just what consciousness feels like but what functional role it plays, and designing experiments to test whether that role is present.

The theory was later developed in more neural terms by Dehaene and colleagues, who sought to identify the neural implementation of the global workspace. This neural version of GWT is now one of the most widely cited theories in consciousness research.
