# Further Reading: Global Workspace Theory

## Primary Sources

**Bernard Baars, *A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness* (1988)** — The foundational text of global workspace theory (GWT). Baars argues that consciousness corresponds to information being broadcast from a "global workspace" to multiple specialized processors. The theater metaphor and the "spotlight of attention" are introduced here.

**Bernard Baars, "In the Theater of Consciousness: The Workspace of the Mind" (1997)** — A more accessible treatment of GWT, elaborating the theater metaphor and connecting it to attention, working memory, and cognitive control.

**Stanislas Dehaene, *Consciousness and the Brain* (2014)** — Dehaene's rigorous synthesis of experimental evidence (masking, inattentional blindness, anesthesia studies) with the "global neuronal workspace" model, showing how conscious access correlates with ignition of long-range cortical networks.

**Stanislas Dehaene and Jean-Pierre Changeux, "Experimental and Theoretical Approaches to Conscious Processing" (2011)** — Reviews the neural global workspace model, distinguishing between subliminal, preconscious, and conscious states. Outlines neural signatures of conscious access including P3 components and late cortical amplification.

## Secondary Literature

**Bernard Baars and Stan Franklin, "An Architectural Model of Conscious and Unconscious Brain Functions: Global Workspace Theory and IDA" (2007)** — Extends GWT into a computational architecture and discusses its relationship to artificial intelligence and cognitive systems.

**Ned Block, "Perceptual Consciousness Overflows Cognitive Access" (2011)** — Block challenges GWT by arguing that phenomenal consciousness is richer than what is globally broadcast, appealing to Sperling-style experiments on iconic memory as evidence that we consciously experience more than we can report.

**Hakwan Lau and David Rosenthal, "Empirical Support for Higher-Order Theories of Conscious Awareness" (2011)** — Argues that neuroimaging evidence often cited in favor of GWT is equally compatible with higher-order theories, raising challenges for GWT's empirical distinctiveness.

**Victor Lamme, "Why Visual Attention and Awareness Are Different" (2003)** — Argues from neuroscience that recurrent local processing (not broadcast) is sufficient for phenomenal consciousness, challenging GWT's equation of consciousness with global broadcast.
