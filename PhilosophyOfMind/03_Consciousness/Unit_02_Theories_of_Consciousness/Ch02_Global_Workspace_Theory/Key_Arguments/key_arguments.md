# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Global Workspace Theory

## Key Arguments

**Baars' Global Broadcast Argument**
Bernard Baars argues that the functional architecture of consciousness consists of a "global workspace"—a limited-capacity broadcast system that makes information widely available to multiple specialized, encapsulated processors throughout the brain. When information gains access to the global workspace, it is broadcast to all processors simultaneously (ignition); it becomes conscious in virtue of this global availability. Unconscious processes, by contrast, operate locally within specialized modules without access to this broadcast channel. The argument connects consciousness to a specific functional architecture rather than to any specific neural substrate.

**The Theater Metaphor and Its Limitations**
Baars' original "global workspace" metaphor compares consciousness to a spotlight in a theater: what falls within the spotlight (the workspace) is visible to all spectators (processes); what is outside remains in the dark. This metaphor is useful but potentially misleading if taken to imply a Cartesian theater with a homuncular audience. Dehaene and Changeux's neural version (neuronal global workspace theory) replaces the metaphor with specific predictions about neural ignition, long-range cortical synchrony, and the non-linear transition from non-conscious to conscious processing, which can be empirically tested.

**The Argument That GWT Explains Access Consciousness**
GWT provides a principled functional account of access consciousness: a state is access-conscious if its content is broadcast in the global workspace and thus available for verbal report, reasoning, and voluntary control. This explains empirical findings about masking, inattentional blindness, and the limits of working memory capacity: these are all cases where information fails to ignite the global workspace. GWT's advocates argue it is the best available functional theory of consciousness because it is empirically constrained and makes specific, testable predictions.

**The Objection That GWT Only Addresses Easy Problems**
Critics (most notably Chalmers) argue that GWT explains access consciousness but leaves the hard problem untouched: even if we fully understand the neural mechanism of global broadcast, we still face the question of why there is something it is like to have information in the global workspace. GWT's proponents (Baars, Dehaene) typically respond that solving the easy problems is solving all the solvable problems—the "hard problem" may not be a genuine scientific problem but a conceptual illusion—or that the question of why broadcast is conscious rather than non-conscious is not a separate question from understanding the functional architecture.

## Core Concepts

**Global Workspace**
The global workspace is the cognitive and neural architecture that makes information widely available throughout the brain by broadcasting it across a network of specialized processors. In Baars' original psychological model, it corresponds to the contents of attention and working memory. In Dehaene and Changeux's neural version, it corresponds to a long-distance fronto-parietal network that, when activated, creates a self-amplifying ignition and broadcasts information to multiple posterior cortical areas. Access to the global workspace is the functional correlate of consciousness on GWT.

**Ignition**
Ignition is the non-linear transition from unconscious to conscious processing in the neuronal global workspace theory (NGWT). When sensory evidence crosses a threshold, a cascade of activation spreads across frontal and parietal areas, creating a sustained, self-amplifying state in which information becomes widely available. Ignition is characterized by all-or-none dynamics (consciousness is an attractor state), long-range synchrony, and late-onset (P3 wave in EEG). The ignition concept grounds GWT's empirical predictions and distinguishes it from continuous processing models.

**Broadcast**
Broadcast is the global workspace mechanism by which information, once admitted to the workspace, becomes simultaneously available to multiple specialized subsystems—motor, memory, attention, language, executive. This availability is what distinguishes conscious from unconscious processing: conscious information can be used flexibly and widely; unconscious information remains local and encapsulated. The broadcast metaphor emphasizes the informational (not spatial) character of the global workspace and its function in enabling flexible, goal-directed behavior.

**Pre-Conscious vs. Conscious Processing**
GWT draws a sharp distinction between pre-conscious processing (information is processed locally in specialized modules, inaccessible to report and flexible use) and conscious processing (information is broadcast in the global workspace and available for flexible, cross-domain use). This distinction maps onto the distinction between fast, automatic processing and slow, deliberate, attention-dependent processing. Empirical support comes from studies of masked priming (pre-conscious) versus attended, unmasked stimuli (conscious), and from unconscious numerical processing in masked priming paradigms.

**Neuronal Global Workspace Theory (Dehaene and Changeux)**
The neuronal global workspace theory specifies the neural implementation of Baars' functional theory. The global workspace is identified with a distributed network of cortical neurons with long-range axons, especially in prefrontal and parietal cortex. When input activates these neurons strongly enough, ignition occurs: a self-sustained, top-down activation that maintains and broadcasts the information. The theory predicts the P3 ERP component, the non-linear masking curve, and patterns of neural activation distinguishing conscious from non-conscious processing in experimental paradigms.

## Thought Experiments

**The Spotlight of Attention**
Baars invites us to consider the experience of hearing a phone ringing in a noisy room: initially we may process it pre-consciously without noticing it, but when attention is directed to it, it "pops" into consciousness—it gains access to the global workspace. The sudden availability of the ringing for report, memory, and action illustrates the all-or-none character of workspace access. The thought experiment supports the distinction between pre-conscious and conscious processing and the role of attention in gating workspace access.

**Inattentional Blindness**
Simons and Chabris' gorilla experiment (an instance of inattentional blindness) demonstrates that subjects focused on one task can fail to notice a highly salient stimulus—even one as conspicuous as a gorilla walking through a scene. GWT explains this as a failure of the gorilla stimulus to gain workspace access: attention was occupied by the primary task, preventing the gorilla from igniting the global workspace. The experiment illustrates that consciousness depends on more than sensory processing; it requires competitive access to a limited-capacity broadcast system.

**The Binocular Rivalry Case**
In binocular rivalry, different images are presented to each eye; subjects experience alternating perception of one image or the other, never a fusion. Neural imaging shows that the physically constant stimulus generates varying neural activation patterns corresponding to which image is currently conscious. GWT predicts that whichever image "wins" workspace access at a given moment is the one that is experienced, with the losing image suppressed to unconscious processing. The case is used to test GWT's claim that consciousness is determined by workspace access rather than by sensory input per se.
