# Tononi's IIT: Phi and Information Integration

Integrated Information Theory (IIT) was developed by neuroscientist Giulio Tononi and has been elaborated in collaboration with Christof Koch, Marcello Massimini, and others. IIT is distinctive among theories of consciousness for several reasons: it starts from phenomenology rather than neuroscience, it proposes a precise mathematical measure of consciousness (phi), and it makes counterintuitive predictions — including that simple transistor networks could be conscious and that the cerebellum, despite its enormous number of neurons, contributes little to consciousness.

**Starting from Phenomenology**

Most theories of consciousness begin with neuroscience or cognitive psychology and try to identify neural or functional correlates of experience. IIT reverses the direction: it starts with the phenomenology of consciousness and asks what physical properties a system must have to generate consciousness with those phenomenological features.

Tononi identifies five axiomatic properties of consciousness — properties that every conscious experience necessarily has:

1. *Intrinsic existence*: Consciousness exists; it is an intrinsic, irreducible fact. Each experience exists from the intrinsic perspective of the subject.

2. *Composition*: Each experience is structured — it is composed of phenomenal distinctions. Seeing a red square involves both seeing redness and seeing a square shape, as distinguishable components.

3. *Information*: Each experience is informative — it is one experience rather than another, and this specificity comes from the fact that it excludes alternatives. Seeing a red square excludes seeing a green circle, and so on.

4. *Integration*: Each experience is unified — it cannot be reduced to independent, non-overlapping parts. The redness and the squareness of the experience are experienced together, not separately.

5. *Exclusion*: Consciousness is definite — it has the content it has, and it has a definite grain, duration, and spatial extent.

From these axioms, IIT derives its core claims: consciousness is identical with integrated information, and the amount of consciousness a system has is measured by phi.

**Phi: The Measure of Consciousness**

Phi (Φ) is a mathematical measure of integrated information. Intuitively, a system has high phi if its whole contains more information than the sum of its parts — if the system, considered as a whole, generates information that cannot be explained by examining its parts independently.

To compute phi, one examines the system's causal structure: how each state of the system causally constrains the past and future states, and whether this causal structure can be partitioned into independent sub-systems without information loss. A system with high phi cannot be broken down into parts that explain the whole — the parts are tightly integrated in a way that generates irreducible information.

A high-phi system is one in which the state of the whole is highly informative in a way that the states of the parts cannot individually account for. Such a system generates "intrinsic causal power" — it makes a difference to itself in a way that transcends the sum of its components' effects.

**IIT's Identity Claim**

IIT makes the strong metaphysical claim that consciousness just is integrated information. This is not merely a correlation claim — not "consciousness correlates with phi" — but an identity claim: phenomenal consciousness is the experience of integrated information, and phi is a quantitative measure of how much consciousness is present.

This identity claim has a panpsychist implication: any system with non-zero phi has some degree of consciousness. Even very simple systems — a single transistor, a thermostat — have some minimal phi and therefore some minimal consciousness. Human brains, with their extremely high phi, have rich phenomenal consciousness. But consciousness is not unique to biological organisms; any integrated system generates it to some degree.

**Why the Cerebellum**

IIT's most striking neuroscientific prediction concerns the cerebellum. The cerebellum has more neurons than the rest of the brain and is highly active in many tasks, yet lesions to the cerebellum do not produce the kind of profound disruptions to conscious experience that equivalent cerebral cortex lesions produce.

IIT explains this: the cerebellum's architecture, with its highly regular, modular organization, has relatively low phi. Each module of the cerebellum processes information independently of the others; the architecture does not support the kind of irreducible integration that generates high phi. The cerebral cortex, by contrast, has a less regular, more interconnected architecture that supports much higher phi.

This prediction distinguishes IIT from GWT: global workspace theory predicts that the prefrontal cortex and its connections are most important for consciousness; IIT predicts that what matters is the integration architecture, not the broadcasting function.

**The Mathematical Framework: IIT 3.0**

Tononi's most developed formulation, IIT 3.0, provides a complete mathematical framework. The key quantities are:

- The "cause-effect structure" of a system in a given state: how the system's current state constrains past causes and future effects.
- "Integrated information" Φ: how much the cause-effect structure of the whole differs from the sum of the cause-effect structures of its parts when optimally partitioned.
- The "maximally integrated cause-effect structure" (MICS): the set of concepts that specify the cause-effect structure of the whole over its parts.

The MICS is identified with conscious experience: every aspect of conscious experience corresponds to an element of the MICS, and the system's overall consciousness corresponds to the Φ of the MICS.

This mathematical framework is precise enough to be testable in principle, though in practice computing phi for biologically realistic neural networks is computationally intractable with current methods.
