# The Axioms of IIT and Their Philosophical Significance

IIT's methodology is unusual in philosophy and science: it begins from axiomatic phenomenology and derives ontological claims from it. Understanding the axioms and the derivation is essential to evaluating the theory.

**The Methodological Reversal**

Standard science moves from mechanism to phenomenon: we identify physical mechanisms and explain how they produce observable phenomena. IIT reverses this: it starts with the undeniable fact of consciousness and its phenomenological structure, derives what physical properties any system generating such consciousness must have, and then asks which physical systems have those properties.

The motivation for this reversal is Tononi's view that consciousness is the one thing we know with certainty — more certain, in some sense, than our knowledge of the external world. The phenomenological structure of consciousness is given to us immediately and directly. Therefore, it makes sense to start there and ask what the physical substrate of consciousness must be.

This Cartesian starting point has attracted both admiration and criticism. Admirers find the phenomenological grounding of the axioms a virtue: IIT is explaining consciousness in terms of what consciousness actually is, not in terms of what it does. Critics worry that the axioms reflect contingent features of human phenomenology rather than necessary features of consciousness as such, and that deriving ontological claims from phenomenological axioms involves a problematic leap.

**The Five Axioms in Detail**

*Intrinsic existence*: Consciousness is real and intrinsic — it exists from the system's own perspective, not merely as described from outside. This axiom grounds IIT's "intrinsicalist" commitments: consciousness cannot be explained purely in relational or functional terms. It must be something the system has from the inside.

The ontological correlate: any substrate of consciousness must have "intrinsic causal power" — it must make a difference to itself, not merely to external observers.

*Composition*: Experience is structured — it is composed of multiple, distinguishable phenomenal elements. When I see a red triangle, my experience is not a single undifferentiated blob but an experience with structure: redness, triangularity, a particular location, a particular size, and so on.

The ontological correlate: the substrate of consciousness must be a system of elements with distinct causal contributions.

*Information*: Experience is informative — each experience specifies one state rather than others. Seeing red excludes seeing blue; seeing a circle excludes seeing a square. The informational character of experience is what gives it its specific content.

The ontological correlate: the substrate must be a system that specifies a definite state — that discriminates among possible states.

*Integration*: Experience is unified — it is a single, integrated whole, not a collection of independent parts. The visual field is experienced as a unified whole, not as a collection of independently experienced patches. This unity is not just the result of attention bundling separate experiences; it is a feature of experience itself.

The ontological correlate: the substrate must be an irreducibly integrated system — one whose causal power cannot be reduced to the causal power of its parts operating independently.

*Exclusion*: Experience is definite — it has the content it has, to the grain it has, for the duration it has. My visual experience right now has determinate content: a specific scene, at a specific level of resolution, during a specific temporal window. Experience does not include all possible levels of spatial and temporal resolution simultaneously.

The ontological correlate: the substrate of consciousness must have a definite grain and extent — the maximally irreducible cause-effect structure at a given level of description.

**Philosophical Significance**

The exclusion axiom has been particularly controversial. It implies that only the maximally integrated system at a given level can be conscious — subsystems of the maximally integrated system are not independently conscious, and suprasystems that include the maximally integrated system as a part are also not independently conscious.

This has the counterintuitive implication that if a human brain is the maximally integrated system, then neither individual neurons nor the brain-plus-body (or brain-plus-smartphone) can be independently conscious. Consciousness "belongs" to the maximally integrated system, not to sub- or super-systems.

This conflicts with intuitions about nested consciousness: can two neurons each be conscious while also participating in a more conscious whole? IIT says no — the exclusion axiom prevents this kind of nested consciousness.

**The Axioms as Contestable**

Philosophers have challenged each of the five axioms. Is experience really intrinsic, or is its character constituted partly by relational properties? Is experience really composed of distinct elements, or is it fundamentally holistic? Is the unity of consciousness really built into experience itself, or is it a feature of how we represent our experience?

These are not idle challenges. The axioms capture real features of phenomenology, but phenomenology is itself theory-laden — our descriptions of experience are shaped by our theoretical commitments. If the axioms are mistaken about what experience is essentially like, the theory built on them may be mistaken as well.
