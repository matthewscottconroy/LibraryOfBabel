# IIT and Panpsychism

One of IIT's most philosophically significant implications is its commitment to a form of panpsychism. If consciousness is integrated information, and if any system with non-zero phi has integrated information, then any sufficiently integrated system — however simple — has some degree of consciousness. This is not a side effect of IIT but a direct consequence of its core commitments, and it has been both celebrated and criticized.

**How IIT Implies Panpsychism**

IIT holds that consciousness is identical with integrated information. Phi measures the amount of integrated information a system generates in a given state. Any system that is not simply a collection of independent parts — any system in which the whole generates more information than the sum of its parts — has non-zero phi and therefore some degree of consciousness.

This applies to simple electronic circuits, to basic biological cells, and in principle to any system with a causal structure that is not perfectly decomposable into independent parts. A single logic gate, if integrated with other gates, can have non-zero phi. A system as simple as a light switch connected to a photodiode has a minimal causal structure that generates a tiny amount of integrated information.

The implication is that consciousness is not confined to brains or to biological organisms. It is a feature of any sufficiently integrated physical system, and it exists on a continuum from essentially zero (for systems with no integration) to very high values (for complex brains).

**Koch's Enthusiasm, Others' Skepticism**

Christof Koch, IIT's most prominent scientific champion, has embraced the panpsychist implications enthusiastically. For Koch, IIT's panpsychism is not a problem to be explained away but a feature to be celebrated: it reconnects mind with nature, showing that consciousness is not an anomaly in a mindless universe but a fundamental feature of organized matter.

Koch has suggested that IIT is compatible with, and perhaps requires, a kind of Spinozan worldview: mind and matter are not fundamentally different kinds of thing, but two aspects of reality, with consciousness corresponding to the intrinsic, integrative aspect of physical organization.

Critics find the panpsychist implications a reductio ad absurdum of the theory. The idea that a thermostat has "some consciousness," however minimal, strikes many as simply wrong — not because consciousness is necessarily limited to biological organisms, but because there seems to be nothing it is like to be a thermostat, regardless of its phi value.

**The Exclusion Axiom and Panpsychism**

Interestingly, the exclusion axiom partially constrains the panpsychist implications of IIT. The exclusion axiom holds that consciousness belongs to the maximally integrated system at a given level. This means that if a human brain is the maximally integrated system, then the brain's component neurons do not have independent conscious experiences — their potential phi is "subsumed" by the whole.

This prevents a proliferation of nested, independent consciousnesses. The panpsychism IIT implies is not the kind where every neuron simultaneously has its own conscious experience alongside the experience of the brain; rather, consciousness accrues to the most integrated level.

But even with this constraint, many simple systems — systems that are not parts of more integrated wholes — would have some degree of consciousness on IIT. A rock has zero phi (because it is not causally integrated); but a transistor network with some integration has some phi, and on IIT this means some consciousness.

**The "Fading Qualia" Challenge**

Chalmers raised a related challenge that IIT's panpsychism must address. In his "fading qualia" thought experiment, he imagines gradually replacing neurons in a brain with silicon chips that replicate the same functional organization. On functionalist accounts, the resulting system should have the same consciousness as the original. But on IIT, what matters is not functional organization but causal integration — and silicon chips with different causal structures might have different phi, and therefore different consciousness, even if their functional behavior is identical.

This is a genuine divergence between IIT and functionalist theories. IIT predicts that consciousness is sensitive to the physical implementation in ways that functionalism denies. Whether this prediction is correct is an open empirical question.

**IIT's Panpsychism vs. Other Panpsychisms**

IIT's panpsychism differs from Chalmers', Goff's, and Strawson's in important ways. IIT's panpsychism is a consequence of a mathematical theory about the structure of consciousness — not a philosophical position adopted to solve the hard problem. It is derived from axioms about phenomenology, not from the Russellian insight about intrinsic properties.

IIT also offers a specific mechanism: the cause-effect structure and integrated information. Other panpsychisms identify consciousness with some vague intrinsic property but do not specify what that property is. IIT specifies it precisely (as integrated information), which makes the theory more specific and more testable — but also potentially more falsifiable.

Whether IIT's panpsychism is ultimately defensible depends on whether the theory's axioms correctly characterize consciousness and whether phi is the right measure of it. These are matters of ongoing scientific and philosophical debate.
