# Criticisms of Integrated Information Theory

IIT has attracted a striking variety of criticisms — from philosophers who find its panpsychism implausible, from neuroscientists who find its predictions poorly supported, from computer scientists who point out counterintuitive implications, and from proponents of competing theories who question its fundamental framing. The theory's boldness invites bold criticism.

**The Expander Graph Problem**

Scott Aaronson, a computer scientist, raised one of the most technically precise objections. He pointed out that certain mathematical structures called "expander graphs" would have very high phi — potentially higher than the human brain — even though they are nothing more than arrays of logic gates with no apparent claim to rich consciousness.

Expander graphs are highly connected mathematical graphs with strong integration properties. If consciousness is phi, and if expander graphs have very high phi, then appropriately implemented expander graphs should have very high consciousness — potentially higher than a human being. But this conclusion seems absurd: a computer chip with the right architecture would be more conscious than you.

Tononi and colleagues have acknowledged versions of this objection and responded by refining the theory (in IIT 3.0) to make phi depend on the cause-effect structure more specifically, not merely on integration architecture. Whether the refinements fully address the objection is disputed.

**The Neural Correlate Mismatch**

Critics from neuroscience argue that IIT's predictions about which brain systems are responsible for consciousness are not well-supported. IIT predicts that the posterior hot zone of the cortex — areas with high integration — is the primary seat of consciousness, while the prefrontal cortex, despite its role in cognition and report, contributes less to consciousness itself.

The adversarial collaboration between IIT and GWT proponents attempted to adjudicate this question empirically. Early results were interpreted differently by each camp, and the collaboration was criticized for being too conservative in its predictions and for not producing decisive evidence.

More fundamentally, critics argue that the hypothesis that the cerebellum has negligible phi and negligible consciousness is not well-supported by the theory's actual calculations. The cerebellum's architecture may not be as modular and as low-phi as IIT predicts, and the empirical evidence that cerebellar damage does not disrupt consciousness is less clean than IIT proponents suggest.

**The Exclusion Paradox**

The exclusion axiom — which holds that only the maximally integrated system can be conscious — creates paradoxes that critics find damaging. 

Consider: if I am the maximally integrated system in my environment, then the Earth's biosphere (of which I am a part) cannot be conscious. But if the biosphere were to become more integrated (through a global network, say), then I would cease to be the maximally integrated system, and consciousness would shift to the biosphere. This "consciousness transfer" based on changes in integration seems metaphysically bizarre.

More pressingly: it seems possible that my brain could become more integrated as I learn and form new connections. Does this mean that different moments of my cognitive development involve different amounts of consciousness? IIT says yes — but the discontinuity of consciousness this implies is troubling.

**The "Axiom Laundering" Objection**

Some philosophers argue that IIT essentially launders the hard problem rather than solving it. The theory begins by identifying axioms of consciousness (intrinsic existence, composition, information, integration, exclusion) and then derives that anything with integrated information is conscious. But the axioms already assume that consciousness is present — they describe features of consciousness that every experience has. The derivation does not explain why integrated information is conscious; it simply defines the physical correlates of features that the axioms stipulate consciousness has.

In other words: IIT tells us what physical structure corresponds to consciousness, but it does not explain why that structure generates (or is identical with) subjective experience rather than operating "in the dark." The identification of consciousness with integrated information is a stipulation, not an explanation.

**The Computationalism Conflict**

IIT is in direct conflict with functionalism and computationalism about consciousness. Standard functionalism holds that what matters for consciousness is functional organization — the pattern of causal relations — not the physical substrate that implements the function. IIT disagrees: what matters is the specific causal structure of the physical substrate, not just the abstract functional organization.

This means that two systems with the same functional organization but different physical implementations could differ in their phi and therefore in their consciousness. A brain and a silicon chip implementing the same computations could have different levels of consciousness.

This counterintuitive implication has been pressed by functionalists as a reductio: if consciousness were really tied to the physical substrate in the way IIT claims, it would be impossible to build conscious artificial systems without replicating the specific physical architecture of the brain. Most AI researchers and many philosophers of mind find this implication implausible.

**The Panpsychist Implication as Objection**

Many critics — including physicalists who are otherwise sympathetic to integrative approaches — find the panpsychist implications of IIT unacceptable. The idea that a thermostat, a photodiode, or a simple logic gate has some degree of consciousness — however minimal — conflicts with strong intuitions about the distribution of consciousness in nature.

IIT proponents respond that the intuitions themselves are unreliable — we have no good prior reason to think consciousness is limited to biological organisms — and that the panpsychist implications follow necessarily from the core commitments of the theory, which are themselves independently motivated.

This debate will not be resolved easily, as it touches on fundamental questions about how to weigh theoretical consistency against intuitive plausibility in the absence of decisive empirical evidence.
