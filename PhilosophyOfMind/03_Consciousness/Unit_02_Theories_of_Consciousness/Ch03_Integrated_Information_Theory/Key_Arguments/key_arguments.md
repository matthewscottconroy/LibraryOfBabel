# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Integrated Information Theory

## Key Arguments

**The Phenomenological Axioms as Starting Points**
Tononi argues that any theory of consciousness must begin from the undeniable phenomenological facts: consciousness exists, it has intrinsic existence (from its own point of view), it is structured (has parts that relate to each other), it is specific (this experience and not another), it is unified (cannot be subdivided into independent experiences), and it is definite (excludes everything that is not part of it). These five axioms (existence, intrinsic existence, information, integration, exclusion) are the postulates of IIT, and the theory derives the properties of physical consciousness-substrates by identifying the physical correlates of each axiom.

**The Phi Argument: Consciousness as Integrated Information**
Tononi argues that consciousness is identical to the quantity of integrated information (Φ) generated by a system above and beyond the sum of the information generated by its parts. A system with high Φ generates more information as a whole than its parts do independently—it has causal power not reducible to its parts. Consciousness just is this integrated information; there is no further question of why high-Φ systems are conscious because the identity is constitutive, not contingent. The argument avoids the hard problem by identifying phenomenal properties with informational properties that can be objectively measured.

**The Exclusion Postulate and Its Implications**
IIT's exclusion postulate holds that consciousness cannot exist at multiple scales simultaneously: at any given spatiotemporal grain, only the system with the maximum Φ is conscious. This postulate prevents the proliferation of overlapping conscious subjects (the cerebral cortex and each of its sub-networks would all be conscious on a naive reading) but generates counterintuitive results: adding inputs that increase integration can change which system is the conscious one. The exclusion postulate is one of the most contested elements of IIT.

**The Feed-Forward Network Problem**
IIT implies that feed-forward networks—however computationally powerful—have zero integrated information (Φ = 0) because they have no feedback loops and hence no system-level integration above the sum of their parts. This means that a feed-forward network processing a visual scene would have no consciousness of it at all, even if its outputs were indistinguishable from those of a conscious system. For critics, this is a decisive objection: IIT seems to imply that some computationally sophisticated AI systems would lack consciousness entirely, which may or may not be the right verdict.

**The Argument Against "Zombie" Integrated Systems**
IIT makes specific predictions that challenge both physicalist and dualist positions. Against eliminativism: consciousness is real and measurable (via Φ). Against classical computationalism: it's not just computation that matters but the specific causal architecture. Against zombism: there is no conceivable physical duplicate with zero Φ that also has all the same causal powers, because Φ is itself a causal property of the system. IIT thus claims to resolve the hard problem not by dissolving it but by identifying what physical property consciousness is identical with.

## Core Concepts

**Phi (Φ)**
Phi is IIT's measure of integrated information—the amount of information generated by a system as a whole above and beyond the information generated by its parts when disconnected. A system with high Φ is one whose parts are strongly integrated: the whole "knows more" than the parts summed. Phi quantifies how much the system constrains its possible states through internal causal interactions, and it is offered as both a measure of the quantity of consciousness and (in IIT 3.0) as characterizing its quality through the "shape" of the informational structure in a high-dimensional space.

**Intrinsic Causal Power**
IIT holds that consciousness exists from an intrinsic point of view—not as seen from the outside but as characterized by the system's own causal structure. Intrinsic causal power is a system's ability to constrain its own past and future states; a system with high intrinsic causal power is not merely described by its state transitions but genuinely determines them from within. This concept grounds IIT's claim that consciousness is an objective, natural property (not a subjective add-on) while also being irreducibly first-personal.

**The Five Axioms (IIT 3.0)**
IIT is derived from five phenomenological axioms: (1) existence—experience exists; (2) composition—experience is structured; (3) information—experience is specific and different from other experiences; (4) integration—experience is unified and cannot be decomposed into independent parts; (5) exclusion—experience is definite, having a specific content at a specific level. These axioms ground five corresponding postulates about the physical substrate of consciousness, and the theory is derived as the unique set of physical properties satisfying all five postulates simultaneously.

**The Quale Space**
IIT 3.0 proposes that the quality of consciousness—what an experience is like—is determined by the specific shape of the system's cause-effect structure in a high-dimensional "quale space." Different experiences (seeing red vs. blue, feeling pain vs. pleasure) correspond to different shapes in this space; experiences with similar shapes are phenomenologically similar. This ambitious program attempts to explain not just the existence but the specific character of conscious experiences in physical-informational terms.

**Panpsychist Implications of IIT**
Because Φ is a property of any system with causal integration—including very simple systems like single neurons or even simple physical devices—IIT implies that consciousness is present (to varying degrees) throughout nature. This is not a commitment Tononi is reluctant to make; he embraces the panpsychist implication. IIT's panpsychism is unusual because it is grounded in a mathematical measure rather than an intuitive appeal to the continuity of evolution, and it predicts specific rankings of consciousness across different systems.

## Thought Experiments

**The Photodiode vs. the Brain**
Tononi asks us to compare a simple photodiode (which detects light and generates a binary signal) with a human brain (which integrates vast amounts of sensory, memorial, and conceptual information). Both "process information," but the brain generates vastly more integrated information—its state depends on inputs from an enormous number of internal sources, whereas the photodiode's state depends only on light intensity. IIT uses this comparison to argue that what matters for consciousness is not just information processing but integration: the photodiode has near-zero Φ, so it has (near-)no consciousness.

**The Cerebellum vs. the Cortex**
IIT explains why the cerebellum—which has more neurons than the cortex—contributes little to consciousness: its architecture is largely feed-forward and modular, generating low Φ. The cortex, with its dense, recursive, long-range connections, generates high Φ. Damage to the cerebellum affects motor control but not consciousness; damage to cortical areas directly affects conscious experience. IIT predicts this architectural difference will correlate with differences in Φ, and the comparison is used to support IIT's architectural account of what makes a brain region conscious.

**The Sleeping Brain and IIT**
During dreamless sleep, TMS/EEG studies show that cortical complexity drops dramatically: a pulse sent to one area of cortex fails to produce the widespread, differentiated response seen in waking. Tononi interprets this as a reduction in Φ: the sleeping brain is less integrated, less differentiated, less conscious. During dreaming, cortical complexity recovers, consistent with the richness of dream experience. This natural variation in consciousness maps onto IIT's predictions and provides empirical support for the theory's core claims about integration and consciousness.
