# Friston's Free Energy Principle

Karl Friston's "free energy principle" (FEP) is the most ambitious and mathematically elaborate version of the predictive processing framework. It is not merely a theory of perception or cognition but a proposed fundamental principle governing all biological systems: adaptive organisms minimize their free energy — a quantity related to surprise — by maintaining an internal model of the world and acting to bring their sensory states into accord with that model.

**The Basic Idea**

Friston's framework is built on the concept of "surprise" in an information-theoretic sense. A sensory state is surprising to a system if it has low probability under the system's model of the world — if the system did not expect it. Surprise, in this technical sense, is measured by the negative log probability of the sensory state under the system's model.

Biological organisms, Friston argues, have implicit or explicit models of their environments. These models specify what sensory states the organism expects to encounter. The organism "wants" (in a functional, not intentional, sense) to minimize surprise — to be in sensory states that its model expects.

But computing surprise directly is intractable for complex systems. The free energy is a tractable upper bound on surprise that can be minimized by adjusting either the system's internal model (perception and learning) or its sensory states (action). The organism can reduce surprise either by updating its model to better predict sensory input, or by acting to make the world match its model.

**Perception as Inference**

On the FEP account, perception is Bayesian inference: the brain maintains a probabilistic model of the world, receives sensory data, and updates the model to minimize free energy (maximize the fit between predictions and data). The result of this inference is the perceived world — the brain's best estimate of the causes of its sensory states.

This is the Helmholtz-Bayesian account of perception in its most rigorous form. Perception is not passive registration but active inference. The brain is always generating predictions, always comparing predictions to sensory input, always updating predictions in response to discrepancies.

**Action as Inference**

A distinctive feature of Friston's account is how it treats action. On a standard view, the sensorimotor system receives goals from higher cognition and executes them. On Friston's view, action is itself a form of inference: it is the organism's way of making the world match its predictions.

When the organism wants to move its hand, it generates a prediction that the proprioceptive state of the hand being in a new position is the expected state. This predicted state differs from the current sensory state, creating a prediction error. The motor system acts to fulfill the prediction — to bring the actual sensory state into accord with the predicted one.

This "active inference" account of action treats perception and action as two aspects of a single free energy minimization process. There is no sharp boundary between "top-down" inference about the world and "top-down" commands to the motor system; both are ways of minimizing surprise.

**Hierarchical Generative Models**

Friston's framework is hierarchical: the brain implements a deep hierarchy of generative models, with higher levels encoding more abstract, temporally extended predictions, and lower levels encoding more concrete, immediate predictions. Prediction errors propagate upward through the hierarchy, and predictions propagate downward.

This hierarchical architecture explains the richness of perception and cognition. Higher levels can encode predictions about sequences of events (expectations about narrative structure, causal sequences, social scripts) while lower levels encode predictions about sensory features (edges, colors, acoustic frequencies). The interaction between levels via prediction errors and predictions constitutes the complex dynamics of perception and cognition.

**Implications for Understanding the Brain**

The FEP has generated a research program for understanding brain organization. If the brain minimizes free energy, we expect:

- Cortical hierarchies to implement hierarchical generative models, with feedback connections carrying predictions and feedforward connections carrying prediction errors.
- Attention to modulate the precision weighting of prediction errors — to amplify the influence of reliable signals and dampen the influence of noisy ones.
- Learning to update the synaptic weights of the generative model to better predict future sensory input.
- Disorders of perception and cognition to arise from impairments in the generative model — from incorrect prior beliefs that are difficult to update, or from miscalibrated precision weighting.

**Criticisms of the FEP**

The most fundamental criticism of the FEP is that it is not falsifiable in any straightforward sense. The framework is formulated at a very high level of abstraction, and it is not clear what evidence would count against it. Proponents of the FEP argue that this is a virtue: the FEP is a general mathematical principle, like the principle of least action in physics, not an empirical hypothesis.

A second criticism concerns the move from free energy minimization to consciousness. Friston's framework explains perception and action but not the phenomenal character of experience. Why does free energy minimization produce anything it is like to be? This question is left open.

A third criticism concerns the interpretation of "surprise" and "prediction." Are these genuinely cognitive notions (involving representations of expected states) or merely information-theoretic abstractions that can be applied to any physical system? If the latter, then the FEP does not specifically characterize cognitive or perceptual systems — it applies to thermostats and self-organizing physical systems generally.

Despite these criticisms, the FEP has been tremendously productive, generating new hypotheses about perception, attention, motor control, learning, and psychopathology.
