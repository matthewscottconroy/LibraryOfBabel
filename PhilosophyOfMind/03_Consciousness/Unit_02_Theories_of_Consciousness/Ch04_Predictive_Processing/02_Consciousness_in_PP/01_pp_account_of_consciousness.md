# A Predictive Processing Account of Consciousness

Several philosophers and cognitive scientists have argued that predictive processing provides not just a theory of perception and cognition but a theory of consciousness itself. Andy Clark has been the most prominent advocate of this view, arguing that predictive processing gives us a unified account of both the nature of perceptual experience and its relationship to cognition, action, and self.

**Perception as Controlled Hallucination**

One of the most striking formulations of the PP account of consciousness comes from Anil Seth, who describes conscious experience as "a controlled hallucination." We are always hallucinating — always generating internal models of the world from the inside, based on prior expectations and highly processed sensory signals. What distinguishes veridical perception from hallucination proper is that in normal perception, the hallucination is constrained and corrected by incoming sensory information. The world "presses back" against our models and keeps them roughly accurate.

On this view, the rich, three-dimensional, colorful, stable world of normal perception is not "out there" waiting to be registered; it is constructed inside the brain, using sensory information as a constraint on an internally generated model. The experience of seeing a red apple is the brain's best predictive model of what would account for the current pattern of retinal excitation, given everything the brain knows about the world, apples, and colors.

This constructivist account of perception has significant consequences for the phenomenology of experience. The specific character of our experiences — the particular way things look and feel — is not simply determined by sensory input but is shaped by prior expectations, by emotional and motivational states, by cultural learning, and by the history of prediction errors that have shaped the brain's generative models.

**PP and the Hard Problem**

Andy Clark and Jakob Hohwy have offered different assessments of what predictive processing implies for the hard problem.

Clark is cautious: predictive processing, he argues, does not directly solve the hard problem. It explains the structure and content of experience — why we have the experiences we do, how they are shaped by priors and sensory input, how they relate to action and cognition. But it does not explain why there is experience at all — why the prediction-error minimization process is accompanied by phenomenal character.

Hohwy has been more ambitious, suggesting that the hierarchical, Bayesian structure of the generative model explains something important about the unity, presence, and "first-person feel" of experience. If the brain models itself as a Bayesian agent predicting sensory input, this self-modeling might account for the first-person character of experience — the sense that experiences happen to a subject.

Seth has pushed this further with his concept of the "predictive self-model." The sense of being a self — an embodied subject of experience — is itself a form of predictive processing: the brain generates and updates a model of itself as a bodily agent embedded in an environment. The self, on this view, is the brain's best prediction of what it takes to be itself.

**PP and Phenomenal Richness**

Predictive processing makes a specific prediction about the richness of conscious experience: experience should be richer in central areas of attention (where prediction errors are amplified and precisely weighted) and should exhibit characteristic distortions in the direction of prior expectations at the periphery.

This prediction connects to the debate about phenomenal overflow: does experience extend beyond what is attentionally focused? PP proponents like Seth tend to argue that the phenomenal richness of experience is largely constructed from sparse sensory input plus rich priors — and that the sense of rich peripheral awareness is itself a product of the generative model rather than evidence of rich unconscious representation.

**The "Presence" of Experience**

Hohwy has developed an account of what he calls the "presence" or "immediacy" of phenomenal experience within the PP framework. Experience seems to be immediately present — not inferred or constructed, but directly given. Yet on PP, experience is always a construction.

Hohwy argues that the sense of presence is itself explained by the PP framework: the brain's model of its own perceptual situation represents current sensory causes as maximally probable (they are the best explanation of current data), and this maximal probability is represented phenomenologically as presence and immediacy. The constructed world "feels" immediately present because the brain is confident that its current model is correct.

**Active Inference and the Phenomenology of Agency**

One of the most fruitful applications of PP to consciousness is to the phenomenology of agency and action. Active inference — the PP account of action as prediction fulfillment — implies a specific phenomenological structure for volitional action.

When I act, I am fulfilling a prediction — making my sensory state conform to a desired predicted state. The phenomenology of voluntary action, on this account, is the sense of being the cause of sensory change, generated by the match between predicted and actual sensory consequences of action. The "sense of agency" — the feeling that I am in control of my actions — is a form of prediction accuracy monitoring.

This account connects naturally to disorders of agency (delusions of control in schizophrenia, anosognosia) where the monitoring of action predictions goes wrong.
