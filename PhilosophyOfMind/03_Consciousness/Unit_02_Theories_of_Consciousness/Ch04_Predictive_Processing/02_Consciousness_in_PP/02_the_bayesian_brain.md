# The Bayesian Brain

The "Bayesian brain" hypothesis holds that the brain performs probabilistic inference, approximating Bayesian computation to combine prior beliefs with sensory evidence and generate posterior estimates of the state of the world. This hypothesis has become central to computational neuroscience and has implications for perception, cognition, action, and — through predictive processing — for the philosophy of mind.

**Bayes' Theorem and the Brain**

Bayes' theorem provides the normatively correct rule for updating beliefs in the light of evidence. If P(H) is the prior probability of a hypothesis, P(E|H) is the likelihood of observing evidence E given H, and P(E) is the marginal probability of the evidence, then Bayes' theorem states:

P(H|E) = P(H) × P(E|H) / P(E)

The posterior probability P(H|E) — the probability of H after observing E — is proportional to the prior probability P(H) multiplied by the likelihood P(E|H).

The Bayesian brain hypothesis applies this framework to perception: the brain computes a posterior estimate of the state of the world by combining prior beliefs about what the world is like with likelihoods derived from sensory data. The resulting posterior is the perceptual experience.

**Bayesian Perceptual Phenomena**

Several well-known perceptual phenomena can be given Bayesian explanations.

*The "light from above" assumption*: We tend to perceive convex shapes as convex and concave shapes as concave when lit from above, and we reverse this perception when the image is flipped. This reflects a strong prior belief that light comes from above.

*Multisensory integration*: When visual and auditory information both specify the location of an event, the brain's estimate of location is a weighted average of the two cues, with weights proportional to the reliability (inverse variance) of each cue. This is the statistically optimal (Bayesian) way to combine uncertain information.

*Cue combination in depth perception*: The brain combines binocular disparity, motion parallax, texture gradients, and shading to estimate depth. These cues are combined in approximately Bayesian fashion, with more reliable cues receiving greater weight.

*Perceptual learning*: As we gain experience with a visual environment, our priors over that environment become more accurate, and our perception becomes more efficient. Experienced radiologists "see" features in X-rays that novices miss, because they have better priors about what to expect.

**Critiques of the Bayesian Brain**

The Bayesian brain hypothesis is computationally attractive but faces several challenges.

*Intractability*: Full Bayesian inference over high-dimensional sensory spaces is computationally intractable. The brain cannot literally be computing full posterior distributions over all possible world states. Proponents respond that the brain implements approximate Bayesian inference — perhaps variational Bayes or sampling methods — that is tractable while remaining approximately optimal.

*The prior problem*: Bayesian inference requires prior probabilities. Where do the brain's priors come from? The answer must be some combination of evolutionary endowment, developmental learning, and adult experience. But specifying these priors is an open empirical challenge, and without knowing the priors, the framework is hard to test precisely.

*Flexibility vs. optimality*: If the brain were an optimal Bayesian reasoner, it would avoid well-documented cognitive biases (availability heuristic, confirmation bias, base rate neglect). But humans are not optimal Bayesian reasoners. Proponents respond that the Bayesian framework applies to low-level perceptual processing, not to high-level reasoning where biases are documented, and that biases can be explained as consequences of misapplied priors or computational shortcuts.

**The Bayesian Brain and Consciousness**

The connection between the Bayesian brain hypothesis and consciousness is made most explicitly in the predictive processing literature. If perception is Bayesian inference, and if conscious experience is the result of perception, then the content of consciousness reflects the brain's best Bayesian estimate of the state of the world.

This has implications for the phenomenology of experience:

The stability and coherence of normal experience reflects the brain's confidence in its current model. When the model is well-calibrated and sensory input is consistent with predictions, experience is stable and coherent.

Perceptual illusions and hallucinations reflect cases where the prior dominates — where the model is so confident that it overrides sensory correction. Certain psychedelic-induced perceptual alterations might reflect changes in the precision-weighting of priors vs. sensory signals, making the generative model less constrained by sensory input.

Disorders like schizophrenia have been interpreted within the Bayesian framework as involving miscalibrated priors or impaired precision-weighting — leading to perceptions (hallucinations) and beliefs (delusions) that are not adequately corrected by sensory evidence.

**The Limits of the Framework**

Like predictive processing more generally, the Bayesian brain framework is a theory of the structure and content of experience, not of why experience has phenomenal character. It explains what experiences are like — their content, stability, reliability, and relationship to sensory evidence — without explaining why the inferential processes involved are accompanied by any experience at all.

This limitation is acknowledged by most Bayesian brain proponents. The framework is a major contribution to understanding how the brain works and how experience is structured, but it leaves the hard problem of consciousness essentially untouched.
