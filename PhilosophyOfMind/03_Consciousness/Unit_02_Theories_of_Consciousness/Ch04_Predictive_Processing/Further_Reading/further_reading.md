# Further Reading: Predictive Processing and Consciousness

## Primary Sources

**Andy Clark, *Surfing Uncertainty: Prediction, Action, and the Embodied Mind* (2016)** — The most thorough philosophical treatment of predictive processing (PP). Clark argues that the brain is fundamentally a prediction machine that minimizes prediction error, and explores the implications for perception, action, cognition, and the self.

**Karl Friston, "The Free-Energy Principle: A Unified Brain Theory?" (2010)** — Friston presents the free-energy principle as a unifying framework for understanding brain function: biological systems minimize surprise (free energy) by improving predictive models or by acting on the world to make it conform to predictions. Central to understanding PP's broader theoretical context.

**Jakob Hohwy, *The Predictive Mind* (2013)** — A philosophical elaboration of predictive processing, arguing that the brain creates a model of hidden causes of sensory input. Hohwy explores the theory's implications for consciousness, perception, and psychopathology.

## Secondary Literature

**Andy Clark, "Whatever Next? Predictive Brains, Situated Agents, and the Future of Cognitive Science" (2013)** — A widely read review paper synthesizing the predictive processing framework and its significance for cognitive science, accessible for readers new to the field.

**Anil Seth, *Being You: A New Science of Consciousness* (2021)** — Seth develops the "controlled hallucination" view of perception based on predictive processing, arguing that conscious experience is the brain's best guess about the causes of sensory signals. Accessibly written and philosophically rich.

**Jakob Hohwy and Anil Seth (eds.), *Philosophy and Predictive Processing* (2020)** — A collection of essays applying PP to a range of philosophical problems including consciousness, self, mental illness, and the boundaries of cognition.

**Karl Friston and Karl Frith, "A Duet for One" (2015)** — Extends the predictive processing framework to interpersonal interaction, proposing that we model others as generative models coupled to our own, with implications for social cognition and consciousness.

**Lisa Feldman Barrett, *How Emotions Are Made* (2017)** — Barrett applies a predictive processing framework to emotion, arguing against basic emotion theories and in favor of a constructivist account in which the brain actively generates emotional experiences. Relevant for connecting PP to affective science.
