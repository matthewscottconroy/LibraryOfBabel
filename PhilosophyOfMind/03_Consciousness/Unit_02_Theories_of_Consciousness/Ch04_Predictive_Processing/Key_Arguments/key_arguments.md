# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Predictive Processing

## Key Arguments

**The Free Energy Principle as a Unifying Account**
Karl Friston argues that any self-organizing system that maintains itself over time must minimize "free energy"—a measure of the surprise or unpredictability of sensory signals relative to the system's internal model of the world. The brain does this by generating top-down predictions about sensory inputs and updating its generative models based on prediction errors. This principle claims to unify perception, action, attention, learning, and even consciousness under a single computational framework, offering an explanatory scope that no previous theory of mind achieved.

**Active Inference as an Account of Action**
In predictive processing, action is not merely the output of a command issued by the brain; it is a form of prediction error minimization. The brain generates predictions about proprioceptive and sensory states (what my limbs will feel like in position X) and issues motor commands that bring the body into a state that matches those predictions. This means perception and action are fundamentally the same kind of process—both minimize prediction error—which dissolves the traditional distinction between a perception system that takes in information and an action system that outputs commands.

**The Argument for Perception as Controlled Hallucination**
Clark and Friston argue that what we perceive is not a direct read-out of sensory signals but a prediction generated by the brain's generative model, modulated by bottom-up prediction errors. When the generative model is accurate, the prediction error is small, and what we experience closely tracks the world. When the model is inaccurate (as in hallucination or illusion), the prediction dominates. Perception is therefore "controlled hallucination"—the brain's best guess at the causes of its sensory signals—corrected by incoming evidence. This dissolves the distinction between perception and imagination.

**Precision-Weighting and Attention**
In predictive processing, attention is explained as precision-weighting: the brain assigns higher precision (reliability weighting) to prediction errors from sources that are treated as trustworthy, amplifying their influence on belief updating. This accounts for the role of attention in consciousness: attended stimuli have their prediction errors amplified, making them more influential in shaping the brain's model. Disorders of attention (neglect, schizophrenia) are reinterpreted as disorders of precision-weighting—systematic failures to assign the correct relative reliability to different channels.

**The Relationship to the Hard Problem**
Advocates of PP argue that it provides resources for approaching the hard problem: conscious experience is the content of the brain's highest-level predictive model—a model of itself as an agent with a body in the world. Phenomenal properties (the redness of red) are the brain's best model of the causes of its sensory signals, not properties of external objects or of neural states per se. Critics (including Chalmers) argue that PP, however sophisticated, still only explains access consciousness—the functional availability of predictive model contents—without explaining why any of this processing is accompanied by phenomenal experience.

## Core Concepts

**Prediction Error Minimization**
Prediction error minimization (PEM) is the core computational principle of predictive processing: the brain's generative models at every level of a hierarchy generate predictions of input from the level below, and receive back prediction errors (the difference between prediction and actual input). The system updates its models to reduce these errors, thereby improving the accuracy of its internal model of the world. PEM is claimed to be the organizing principle of all neural computation, not just perceptual processing.

**The Generative Model**
A generative model is an internal probabilistic model that specifies how hidden causes (states of the world) generate observable effects (sensory signals). The brain's generative model is a hierarchical structure in which each level models the hidden causes of the level below. By inverting the generative model—inferring causes from effects—the brain performs perception as inference. The generative model is updated by learning (adjusting model parameters) and can be used for imagination and planning by running the model in the absence of sensory input.

**Free Energy**
In Friston's formulation, free energy is a mathematical quantity that bounds the surprisal—the negative log probability—of sensory signals given the brain's model. Minimizing free energy is equivalent to maximizing the evidence for the brain's model of the world (model evidence), which is the same as minimizing surprise (unexpected sensory input). The free energy framework is taken from statistical thermodynamics and information theory and is claimed to provide a fundamental account of why living systems maintain their organization.

**Active Inference**
Active inference is the PP account of action: rather than issuing motor commands as outputs, the brain generates proprioceptive predictions (expected sensory states) and relies on reflex arcs to bring about the bodily states that fulfill these predictions. Action thus becomes a form of prediction error minimization in the proprioceptive domain—the body acts so as to make the world conform to the brain's predictions. This framework reframes agency as a form of perception, making the perception-action loop a unified process of inference.

**Hierarchical Predictive Coding**
Hierarchical predictive coding is the neural implementation of PP: the cortex is organized as a hierarchy of areas in which each level sends predictions down to the level below and receives prediction errors back up. Top-down connections carry predictions; bottom-up connections carry prediction errors. This maps onto known anatomical features of cortex (e.g., the distinction between superficial and deep cortical layers). The framework predicts that sensory cortex is more active for novel or unexpected inputs and less active for predicted inputs.

## Thought Experiments

**The Hollow Face Illusion**
The hollow face illusion: a hollow mask viewed from outside appears to have a normal protruding face. Despite bottom-up sensory information about the concavity, the brain's strong prior model of faces as convex overrides the evidence. The illusion persists even when observers know the mask is hollow. PP explains this as a case where the prior (expectation of convex faces) is so strong that the prediction error from the bottom-up signal is insufficient to override it. The illusion illustrates the top-down dominance of high-level priors in shaping perceptual experience.

**The Rubber Hand Illusion as Active Inference**
In the rubber hand illusion, synchronous touching of a rubber hand and a hidden real hand causes subjects to feel that the rubber hand is their own. PP explains this as a case where the brain's prediction of proprioceptive input (based on its model of its own body) is updated by the correlated visual-tactile information: the rubber hand is incorporated into the body model because it generates smaller prediction errors when treated as one's own than when treated as external. The illusion supports PP's account of body ownership as an inference rather than a direct perception.

**The Locked-In Patient (Precision and Consciousness)**
Patients with locked-in syndrome have intact consciousness but severely impaired motor output. PP would predict that their generative models are intact (rich prediction and updating) even without the ability to act to minimize sensory prediction error. This scenario tests the PP account of consciousness: if consciousness is the content of the generative model, locked-in patients should have full phenomenal experience; if it depends on the active inference loop (acting to change the world), their experience should be impoverished. Current evidence suggests the former, but the case highlights the need for PP to be precise about which aspect of the predictive hierarchy is constitutive of consciousness.
