# Biological Naturalism

Biological naturalism is John Searle's positive theory of consciousness, developed to replace both Cartesian dualism and standard physicalism with a view that takes seriously both the reality of consciousness and its dependence on the brain.

**The Central Claim**

Biological naturalism holds that consciousness is a biological phenomenon — a higher-level feature of the brain that is caused by lower-level neuronal processes. Consciousness is real, subjective, qualitative, and irreducible to any third-person functional or computational description. But it is not a separate substance or a separate kind of property; it is caused by and realized in the biological brain.

The analogy Searle draws repeatedly is to other biological phenomena: liquidity, solidity, digestion, photosynthesis. Liquidity is a higher-level feature of water that is causally produced by and realized in the behavior of water molecules. It is real — water really is liquid — but it is not a separate substance or non-physical property. Similarly, consciousness is a higher-level feature of the brain that is causally produced by and realized in neuronal processes. It is real — we really are conscious — but it need not be a separate substance.

**Consciousness as Emergent from Neural Activity**

Searle uses the term "emergent" carefully. He distinguishes between "weak emergence" (a higher-level property that is nothing over and above its micro-level base) and something like strong emergence (a higher-level property that is causally produced by but irreducible to its base). He wants to say consciousness is emergent in the latter sense: it is causally produced by neural processes but has features — subjectivity, qualitative character — that cannot be fully captured in third-person terms.

This is different from standard physicalism (which tries to reduce or eliminate subjective properties) and from property dualism (which makes mental properties genuinely separate from physical ones). Biological naturalism aims for a middle ground: consciousness is real and qualitative, but it is a natural biological phenomenon, not a metaphysical oddity.

**The Intrinsic Subjectivity of Consciousness**

Searle insists that consciousness is irreducibly subjective in the following sense: it is essentially characterized from the first-person point of view. There is no purely third-person, objective description that captures what consciousness is.

This does not mean consciousness is unknowable or that it cannot be studied scientifically. It means that any third-person scientific description of consciousness — in terms of neural activity, information processing, functional organization — describes consciousness from the outside and necessarily omits the first-person dimension.

The asymmetry is this: I can describe the neuronal correlates of pain in entirely third-person terms. But no third-person description tells you what pain is like. The first-person dimension — the "what it is like" — is not captured by any third-person description, however complete.

**Objections to Biological Naturalism**

*The causal completeness objection*: If the brain is causally complete with respect to behavior, how can consciousness be causally efficacious? If every behavioral event is causally determined by neuronal processes, there is no room for consciousness to add anything. This is a version of the causal exclusion problem. Searle's response is that consciousness is realized in the very neural processes that cause behavior — it is not an extra cause but an aspect of the neural cause itself. The first-person description and the third-person description describe the same causal process.

*The problem of privileging biology*: Why does consciousness require specifically biological neurons? Why couldn't silicon chips, if they implemented the same functional organization, give rise to consciousness? Searle's answer (derived from the Chinese Room argument) is that it is not the functional organization but the specific causal powers of the biological substrate that matter. But critics find this appeal to specific causal powers mysterious — what exactly is it about carbon-based neurons that silicon chips lack?

*The explanatory gap objection*: Biological naturalism promises to dissolve the explanatory gap (consciousness is just a biological phenomenon, like digestion) but does not actually bridge it. Saying that consciousness is caused by and realized in neural processes does not explain why neural processes give rise to subjective experience, any more than saying that digestion is "emergent" from digestive enzyme activity explains the specific phenomenology of hunger. The analogy to digestion and liquidity may obscure rather than illuminate the problem.

**Biological Naturalism and the Chinese Room**

The Chinese Room argument and biological naturalism are complementary aspects of Searle's philosophy of mind. The Chinese Room argues negatively: computation is not sufficient for understanding. Biological naturalism argues positively: the brain's specific causal powers are what generate consciousness.

Together, they define a distinctive position: consciousness is real, irreducibly subjective, and specifically biological — it arises from the causal powers of biological neural tissue, not from any abstract computational organization that could be implemented in different substrates.

The view remains controversial. It is broadly correct in insisting on the reality of consciousness and its causal efficacy; it may be too strong in tying consciousness specifically to biology; and it may not fully engage with the depth of the explanatory gap it claims to dissolve.
