# Further Reading: Biological Naturalism

## Primary Sources

**John Searle, *The Rediscovery of the Mind* (1992)** — Searle's most systematic statement of biological naturalism, arguing that consciousness is a real biological phenomenon produced by the brain but irreducible to computational or functional description. He attacks eliminativism, functionalism, and strong AI.

**John Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980)** — The original Chinese Room paper, arguing that syntactic manipulation of symbols (computation) is neither necessary nor sufficient for semantic content and genuine understanding. An argument against strong artificial intelligence and, by extension, computational functionalism.

**John Searle, *Intentionality: An Essay in the Philosophy of Mind* (1983)** — Develops the positive account: intentionality is a biological phenomenon with "conditions of satisfaction" determined by intrinsic causal properties of brain states, not by functional or relational properties.

## Secondary Literature

**John Searle, "Is the Brain a Digital Computer?" (1990)** — Searle argues that computation is observer-relative and that the mind is not a digital computer in any intrinsic sense. Extends the Chinese Room argument to the brain sciences.

**Andy Clark, "Connectionism, Competence, and Explanation" (1990)** — Provides a counterpoint from a computationalist perspective, arguing that Searle's biological naturalism fails to explain how biological processes give rise to semantic content any better than computational accounts.

**Patricia Churchland, "Is the Brain a Computer?" (1997)** — Presents a neurophilosophical critique of both strong AI and Searle's biological naturalism, arguing that understanding cognition requires looking at neural mechanisms without assuming either purely computational or purely biological explanatory strategies are privileged.

**Margaret Boden, *Mind as Machine* (2006)** — A comprehensive historical and philosophical account of artificial intelligence and cognitive science that situates biological naturalism within broader debates about machine minds and the nature of computation.

**Daniel Dennett, "Fast Thinking" (1994)** — Includes responses to Searle's Chinese Room from the perspective of Dennett's heterophenomenology, arguing that Searle's intuitions about "original" vs. "derived" intentionality beg the question.
