# Narrative Theories of the Self

Narrative theories hold that the self is not a substantial entity but a kind of story — an ongoing narrative that integrates past, present, and projected future into a coherent identity. On this view, what makes you the person you are is not a special metaphysical substance or even a distinctive set of psychological states, but the narrative pattern that organizes your experiences and presents them as aspects of a single life.

**Ricoeur's Narrative Identity**

Paul Ricoeur is the philosopher most associated with a systematic narrative theory of the self. In his *Oneself as Another* (1992), Ricoeur distinguishes between two senses of "identity":

*Idem-identity* (sameness): the kind of identity that remains constant over time, like the identity of a physical object or a substance. This corresponds to what remains the same — character, dispositions, habits.

*Ipse-identity* (selfhood): the identity constituted by commitment, promise-keeping, and self-narrative. I identify as the person who made a certain promise; my identity involves a kind of narrative continuity that I maintain through action and reflection.

For Ricoeur, narrative mediates between these two kinds of identity. The story we tell about our lives integrates our changing character (idem) with our maintained commitments (ipse) into a coherent narrative identity. We understand ourselves through the stories we tell about ourselves.

**MacIntyre and the Narrative Unity of a Life**

Alasdair MacIntyre, in *After Virtue* (1981), argues that human action is only intelligible within a narrative context. To ask "what is this person doing?" requires placing the action within a story — a shorter narrative of intention and context, embedded in a longer narrative of the agent's life, embedded in still longer narratives of community and tradition.

A human life, MacIntyre argues, has narrative unity: it is intelligible as a whole only when the parts are understood in relation to each other and to a larger telos — an orientation toward some conception of the good that gives the life its direction.

This narrative unity is not merely retrospective. We live by stories as much as we tell them; the narrative framework through which we understand our situation shapes our choices and our identity in the present.

**Dennett's "Center of Narrative Gravity"**

Daniel Dennett offers a more deflationary version of narrative theory. For Dennett, the self is a "center of narrative gravity" — an abstract point postulated to make sense of the complex flow of behavior and experience. Just as the center of gravity of a physical object is not a real physical thing but a useful theoretical posit, the self is not a real psychological thing but a useful posit for making sense of a person's behavior.

We are, Dennett argues, essentially storytellers about ourselves. The self is "spun" by the brain's narrative activity — the ongoing construction of a story that gives coherence to the organism's behavior. This story is not simply fabricated; it is constrained by the actual states of the organism and by the responses of others. But it is a construction, not a discovery of a pre-existing entity.

This deflationary view has consequences for personal identity: there is no fact of the matter about personal identity beyond what is captured by the narrative framework. The question "is this the same person as before?" is always a question about the continuity and coherence of the narrative, not about the persistence of an underlying substantial self.

**The Buddhist Connection**

Narrative theories of the self have been connected to Buddhist teachings about no-self (anatta). The Buddhist doctrine holds that there is no permanent, unchanging self — what we call "the self" is a process or pattern of interacting constituents, not a substantial entity. The narrative theory is sometimes presented as a Western philosophical expression of this insight.

Mark Siderits and other philosophers have explored this connection, arguing that Buddhist no-self and Humean bundle theory both anticipate the narrative account by denying a substantial self while acknowledging the reality of psychological continuity.

**Critiques of Narrative Theory**

*The minimal self objection*: Narrative theories seem to leave out the minimal self — the immediate, pre-reflective first-person character of experience. Even in severe amnesia (where narrative continuity is broken), there is still a subject having experiences. The minimal self cannot be identified with narrative continuity.

*The construction paradox*: If the self is a narrative construction, who constructs it? The narrative theory needs a constructor — a self that does the telling. But this constructor self cannot itself be a narrative construction, or we face an infinite regress.

*The biological organism*: Some philosophers argue that both narrative and minimal self theories fail to acknowledge the biological organism as the primary locus of personal identity. Psychological continuity theories (narrative or not) must be grounded in the biological continuity of the organism.

*The heterogeneity of self-narratives*: The narratives we tell about ourselves are often fragmentary, contradictory, and culturally specific. If the self is identical with the narrative, then a self with a fragmented narrative is a fragmented self — and many people with fragmented narratives live coherent lives. The connection between narrative and identity may be less tight than narrative theories suggest.

Despite these challenges, narrative theories have been enormously productive in philosophy, cognitive science, psychology, and psychotherapy. They have emphasized the temporal, interpretive, and social dimensions of selfhood that purely individualistic or neurobiological accounts tend to neglect.
