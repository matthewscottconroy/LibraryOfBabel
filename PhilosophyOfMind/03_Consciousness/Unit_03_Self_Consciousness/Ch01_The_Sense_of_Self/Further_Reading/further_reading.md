# Further Reading: The Sense of Self

## Primary Sources

**William James, *The Principles of Psychology*, Chapter 10: "The Consciousness of Self" (1890)** — James's foundational analysis of the self distinguishes the "I" (the knowing self, the stream of thought) from the "Me" (the empirical self — body, social roles, and psychological properties). His analysis of the "passing thought" as the self remains a touchstone.

**Antonio Damasio, *The Feeling of What Happens* (1999)** — Damasio argues for a multilevel account of self: the proto-self (automatic body-state representation), core self (momentary first-person perspective), and autobiographical self (extended narrative). Brain damage studies support the dissociation of these levels.

**Thomas Metzinger, *Being No One* (2003)** — Metzinger argues that the phenomenal self-model (PSM) is a dynamic, transparent representation in which the system models itself as an agent. The sense of self is a virtual representation, not a metaphysical entity. A demanding but central text.

## Secondary Literature

**Thomas Metzinger, *The Ego Tunnel* (2009)** — A more accessible version of the PSM theory, arguing that the self is a tunnel through which a model of reality is constructed. Discusses implications for ethics and the nature of consciousness.

**Shaun Gallagher, *How the Body Shapes the Mind* (2005)** — Gallagher argues that bodily experience — particularly proprioception and movement — is constitutive of the minimal sense of self, supporting a phenomenological and enactivist approach against purely representationalist accounts.

**Dan Zahavi, *Subjectivity and Selfhood* (2005)** — From a phenomenological tradition, Zahavi argues that the minimal self is not constructed but is a primitive feature of pre-reflective consciousness — the "ipseity" present in any conscious experience. Engages critically with Metzinger.

**Marya Schechtman, *The Constitution of Selves* (1996)** — Defends a narrative theory of personal identity, arguing that persons constitute themselves through self-narratives. Relevant for understanding how the autobiographical self relates to moment-to-moment experience.

**Chris Frith, *Making Up the Mind* (2007)** — Frith argues from neuroscience that the self is a model the brain constructs of itself as an agent, connecting this to predictive processing and the study of schizophrenia as a disruption of self-modeling.
