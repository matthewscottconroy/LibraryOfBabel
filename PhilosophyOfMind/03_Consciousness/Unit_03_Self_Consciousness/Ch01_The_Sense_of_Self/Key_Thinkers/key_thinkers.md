# Key Thinkers: The Sense of Self

---

**William James** (1842–1910)

James's psychology of the self distinguishes the "I" — the pure subject, the stream of consciousness as knower — from the "Me" — the empirical self as known, comprising bodily, social, and spiritual components. His analysis of personal identity in terms of the continuity of the stream of thought, without a persisting soul or Cartesian ego, anticipates modern deflationary accounts. James emphasized the "warmth and intimacy" of past experiences as the basis for feeling that they are one's own.

*Key works:* *The Principles of Psychology* (1890); *Psychology: Briefer Course* (1892); *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902)

---

**Antonio Damasio** (b. 1944)

Damasio argues that the self is not a single unified entity but a multilevel biological construction. The proto-self is a set of neural patterns mapping the organism's body state; the core self is a transient, moment-by-moment sense of agency arising from the encounter between the proto-self and an object of consciousness; the autobiographical self is an extended narrative representation dependent on memory and language. Neurological cases in which components of self are selectively damaged support this hierarchical account.

*Key works:* *The Feeling of What Happens* (1999); *Self Comes to Mind* (2010); *Descartes' Error* (1994)

---

**Thomas Metzinger** (b. 1958)

Metzinger argues that there is no such thing as a self — rather, there is a "phenomenal self-model" (PSM), a transparent representation the brain constructs of itself as an agent in the world. "Transparent" means the system cannot recognize it as a representation; it is experienced as direct self-knowledge. The sense of being a self is therefore an illusion in a specific sense: no entity corresponds to the model. Metzinger connects this to out-of-body experiences, depersonalization, and the neuroscience of body ownership (rubber hand illusion, etc.).

*Key works:* *Being No One* (2003); *The Ego Tunnel* (2009); "Minimal Phenomenal Selfhood" (2021)

---

**Dan Zahavi** (b. 1967)

Zahavi defends a phenomenological approach to selfhood, arguing against both Metzinger's eliminativism and bundle theories (Parfit, Hume). He argues that a minimal self — "ipseity" or first-person givenness — is a primitive feature of every conscious experience: experience is always given as mine. This minimal self is not a narrative or representational construction but an inherent feature of pre-reflective consciousness. His work draws on Husserl, Heidegger, and Sartre.

*Key works:* *Subjectivity and Selfhood* (2005); *Self and Other* (2014); *Phenomenology: The Basics* (2019)

---

**Shaun Gallagher** (b. 1948)

Gallagher draws on phenomenology and developmental research to argue that the most fundamental sense of self — what he calls the "minimal self" — is bodily and non-narrative. He distinguishes the sense of ownership (this body is mine) from the sense of agency (I am the author of this action) and argues that both are grounded in proprioception and motor control. His work connects phenomenological insights to empirical psychology and cognitive science.

*Key works:* *How the Body Shapes the Mind* (2005); *The Phenomenological Mind* (2012, with Zahavi); *Enactivist Interventions* (2017)
