# Immunity to Error Through Misidentification

"Immunity to error through misidentification" (IEM) is a technical concept introduced by Wittgenstein and developed by Shoemaker, Evans, and others. It identifies a distinctive feature of first-person self-ascriptions: certain reports about oneself cannot be wrong through misidentification of the subject. This property illuminates the unique character of first-person reference and has implications for theories of self-consciousness and personal identity.

**The Basic Observation**

Wittgenstein made the observation in the *Blue and Brown Books*: there are two uses of "I" — one as object, one as subject. "I" as object: "My arm is broken"; "I am six feet tall"; "My face is red." These reports are based on observation and can be wrong through misidentification — I might mistake someone else's arm for my own in a confusing scene. "I" as subject: "I have a toothache"; "I see a red circle"; "I think it will rain." These reports are not based on observation and cannot be wrong through misidentification.

For "I have a toothache," there is no further question: "Yes, there is a toothache here — but is it mine?" The toothache is not something I detect and then attribute to myself; it is simply felt. There is no identification procedure that could go wrong.

**Shoemaker's Analysis**

Sydney Shoemaker developed the concept of IEM into a systematic thesis about self-consciousness. A self-ascription is immune to error through misidentification (with respect to a predicate F) when:

1. The ascription is made on a basis that guarantees its predicate is correctly applied to something.
2. There is no possibility of misidentification: the ascription cannot be false because the right predicate is applied to the wrong subject.

For "I am in pain," condition 2 is satisfied: the report is not based on identifying the subject of the pain and then attributing pain to it. It is the direct expression of a pain state; the first-person reference is not mediated by identification.

Compare "He is in pain." This can be false in two ways: because the predicate "in pain" is misapplied (the person is not actually in pain) or because the subject reference "he" misidentifies (the person identified is not the one who is in pain). First-person bodily reports lack the second source of error.

**IEM and Bodily Self-Consciousness**

Bodily self-consciousness is a more complex case. Proprioceptive reports — "my leg is crossed," "I am standing" — are typically immune to error through misidentification: there is no possibility of correctly perceiving that someone is standing and incorrectly concluding it is me. The proprioceptive sense gives me information directly about my own body, without mediation.

However, some bodily reports can be misidentified. If I see a leg and think "that leg is crossed," and then infer "I am the person with the crossed leg," this inference could be wrong. But normal proprioceptive reports do not have this inferential structure; they are direct and IEM.

Rubber hand illusion and related experimental paradigms are interesting here: they can cause subjects to attribute sensations in an artificial hand to their own body, creating something like misidentification in bodily self-consciousness. These cases put pressure on the scope of IEM for bodily self-awareness.

**IEM and Mental Self-Consciousness**

Higher-order theories of consciousness (Rosenthal, Lycan) analyze consciousness of one's own mental states in terms of higher-order representations. If I am conscious of my pain because I have a higher-order representation "I am in pain," does the higher-order representation involve identification of the subject of the pain?

Shoemaker argued that genuine self-consciousness must be IEM — it cannot involve a separate identification step that could go wrong. On higher-order theories, the higher-order thought represents a first-order state as mine — and this representation could, in principle, get the attribution wrong (attribute the first-order state to the wrong subject). This seems to violate IEM.

Higher-order theorists respond that the relevant higher-order representation does not involve identification in the relevant sense — it does not identify the subject by description and could not misidentify. The debate turns on the details of how higher-order representations refer to their subjects.

**IEM and Personal Identity**

IEM has implications for debates about personal identity. If certain first-person judgments cannot be wrong through misidentification, this suggests that personal identity is not constituted by any re-identifiable feature that could in principle be misidentified. The self is not an object I identify and then refer to; it is the subject from which all identification proceeds.

This connects to the phenomenological tradition: Husserl's insistence that the "I" of experience is not an object of experience but the pole of all objectification, and Heidegger's insistence that Dasein is always already its own. The IEM property of first-person reports is a linguistic expression of the non-objectifiable character of subjectivity.
