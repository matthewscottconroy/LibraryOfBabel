# Further Reading: Subjectivity

## Primary Sources

**Thomas Nagel, *The View from Nowhere* (1986)** — Nagel argues that the objective standpoint, which abstracts from any particular perspective, is in tension with the irreducibly subjective character of experience. The book traces the consequences of this tension for ethics, epistemology, and philosophy of mind.

**Edmund Husserl, *Ideas: General Introduction to Pure Phenomenology* (1913, trans. 1931)** — Husserl's foundational phenomenological text argues that consciousness is always intentional — directed toward objects — and that the first-person perspective is the irreducible starting point for philosophical inquiry. The "epoché" (bracketing of natural attitude) and "reduction" are introduced here.

**Jean-Paul Sartre, *Being and Nothingness* (1943, trans. 1956)** — Sartre's analysis of pre-reflective and reflective consciousness, the "for-itself" (consciousness) and the "in-itself" (things), remains a central phenomenological treatment of subjectivity and its relation to self-awareness.

## Secondary Literature

**Dan Zahavi, *Subjectivity and Selfhood* (2005)** — Zahavi argues that phenomenological accounts of subjectivity — particularly Husserl's concept of "inner time-consciousness" and the "for-me-ness" of experience — provide a minimal account of the self that avoids both Cartesian ego theories and Humean bundle theories.

**Sydney Shoemaker, "Self-Reference and Self-Awareness" (1968)** — Shoemaker argues that first-person reference is immune to error through misidentification — one cannot mistakenly believe one is in pain when feeling pain. This "IEM" property of first-person reports is important for understanding the special status of subjective states.

**Alva Noë, *Strange Tools: Art and Human Nature* (2015)** — Noë argues that subjectivity is not a Cartesian inner theater but is constituted through embodied, world-engaging practices. Accessible treatment connecting phenomenology to enactivism.

**Galen Strawson, "The Self" (1997)** — Strawson defends the existence of a minimal, momentary "thin subject" that is the basis of each conscious episode, against narrative accounts of self and against eliminativist denials of selfhood.

**Evan Thompson, *Mind in Life* (2007)** — Thompson develops a phenomenological and biological account of subjectivity, arguing that the roots of selfhood lie in the self-organizing autopoietic processes of living organisms. Bridges Husserlian phenomenology and enactivism.
