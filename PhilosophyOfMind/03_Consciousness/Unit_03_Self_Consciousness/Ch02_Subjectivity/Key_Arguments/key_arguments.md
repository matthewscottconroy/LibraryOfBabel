# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Subjectivity

## Key Arguments

**Nagel's Argument for the Irreducibility of the Subjective**
Thomas Nagel argues in "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" that objective, third-person physical description necessarily excludes what is specific to a subject's perspective. Any objective account of the brain's processes is an account from no particular point of view; experience, by contrast, is inherently perspectival—it exists only from the subject's own point of view. No amount of objective information can reconstruct the subjective character of experience, not because we lack information but because the subjective and the objective are fundamentally different kinds of thing. Nagel does not conclude dualism but urges the development of a new kind of "objective phenomenology."

**Searle's First-Person Ontology**
Searle argues that phenomenal consciousness has a first-person ontology: it exists only as experienced by a subject, and its mode of existence is inherently first-personal. Physical objects have a third-person ontology—they exist independently of any subject's experience of them. This ontological asymmetry means that consciousness cannot be fully described in purely third-person terms without a remainder: there will always be something about consciousness that third-person descriptions leave out. Searle claims this is ontological, not merely epistemic: the phenomenon itself is constitutively subjective.

**The View from Nowhere vs. the View from Somewhere**
Nagel argues in The View from Nowhere that objective understanding aspires to a "view from nowhere"—a description of reality that is not relative to any particular perspective. But this ideal is incomplete: some facts (facts about the subjective character of experience, facts about values, facts about personal identity) cannot be captured from no particular perspective. A complete account of the world must include both objective and subjective standpoints, and the challenge is to understand how they relate without reducing one to the other.

**The Indexical Argument for the Irreducibility of the "I"**
Perry, Castañeda, and others argue that first-person indexical thoughts (thoughts involving "I," "here," "now") are irreducible to third-person descriptions. If I believe "I am in danger," this cannot be replaced without loss by "John is in danger"—even if I am John. First-person reference has a special cognitive role that no third-person description can play: only "I" directly guides self-regarding actions. This argument has implications for the philosophy of mind: the subjective perspective is partly constituted by indexical self-reference that resists reduction.

**The Explanatory Asymmetry Between First- and Third-Person**
Even complete knowledge of a subject's neural states does not entail what that subject's experience is like—there is an explanatory asymmetry between the third-person (objective, neural) and first-person (subjective, phenomenal) perspectives. This asymmetry is evidence that the first-person perspective introduces a mode of description that is not derivable from third-person facts. The asymmetry is the epistemic counterpart of the hard problem and motivates accounts of consciousness that take first-person ontology seriously.

## Core Concepts

**First-Person Ontology**
First-person ontology (Searle) is the claim that some entities—specifically, phenomenal mental states—have a mode of existence that is constitutively subjective: they can only exist as experienced by a subject from the inside. Contrast third-person ontology (mountains, molecules), which is objective in that these entities exist independently of any subject's perspective. First-person ontology does not imply dualism—conscious states can be biologically natural—but it does imply that third-person descriptions are incomplete as descriptions of consciousness.

**The Subjective Character of Experience**
The subjective character of experience (Nagel's "what it is like") is the property by virtue of which a state is a conscious experience: it is its being-for-the-subject, its phenomenal quality viewed from the subject's own perspective. The subjective character is not an additional property alongside the functional properties of a state but a different mode of access to the state—first-person rather than third-person. Nagel's claim is that this mode of access cannot be reconstructed from any third-person account, however complete.

**The Perspectival Character of Experience**
All experience is from a perspective: experiences have a point of view, a location in space and time, a set of salient features and absent ones. The perspectival character of experience is not an incidental feature but constitutive of what experience is—there is no experience without a perspective from which it is had. This perspectivalism is compatible with both naturalism (perspectives are biological facts about organisms) and phenomenology (perspectives are the transcendental condition of any world-disclosure).

**The Indexical "I"**
The indexical "I" is a term whose reference is determined by the context of utterance: whoever produces the token "I" in a given context is its referent. But the cognitive significance of "I" is not exhausted by its reference: a thinker's first-person thoughts have a unique role in self-consciousness and action that third-person references to the same person cannot replicate. Castañeda's quasi-indicators and Perry's "essential indexicals" demonstrate that first-person self-reference is cognitively and practically irreducible, which bears on the philosophy of self-consciousness.

**The View from Nowhere**
Nagel's "view from nowhere" is the ideal of objective knowledge: a description of reality that is not relative to any particular observer's perspective, that transcends all particular viewpoints and captures the world as it is "in itself." Nagel argues that this ideal is valuable and genuinely illuminating but inherently incomplete: some facts—subjective facts, moral facts, personal facts—can only be understood from a perspective, not from nowhere. This means no purely objective science can be a complete account of reality; a place must be made for irreducibly subjective descriptions.

## Thought Experiments

**Nagel's Bat**
Thomas Nagel asks what it is like to experience the world through echolocation—to navigate and discriminate objects through high-frequency sound bouncing off surfaces. Even with complete neurophysiological knowledge of bat sonar, we cannot reconstruct what bat experience is like from the inside. The thought experiment establishes that subjective experience is irreducibly perspectival and that objective knowledge, however complete, cannot close the gap to first-person phenomenal knowledge. It motivates the demand for an "objective phenomenology" that can, without eliminating the first-person character, make subjective experience an object of investigation.

**Perry's "I" and the Supermarket**
Perry imagines a shopper following a trail of sugar in a supermarket, trying to find the person who is making a mess. The shopper is in fact the person making the mess—the sugar trail is coming from a bag in his own cart. He eventually realizes "I am the one making the mess"—and immediately stops. No amount of third-person information (the person with the broken bag, the man in aisle 3) would have produced this result; only the first-person thought "I am making the mess" initiates the relevant action. The case demonstrates the irreducibility of first-person indexical thought to any third-person description.

**Jackson's Knowledge Argument as an Argument about Subjectivity**
Mary's case can be read not primarily as an argument about physical vs. non-physical facts but about objective vs. subjective knowledge. Mary has all objective (third-person) knowledge about color experience; when she sees red, she gains subjective (first-person) knowledge of what it is like. The asymmetry between these two kinds of knowledge—which are about the same states—illustrates the irreducibility of the first-person perspective. Whether this asymmetry is metaphysical (different facts) or epistemological (different modes of knowing the same fact) is the central question of the debate.
