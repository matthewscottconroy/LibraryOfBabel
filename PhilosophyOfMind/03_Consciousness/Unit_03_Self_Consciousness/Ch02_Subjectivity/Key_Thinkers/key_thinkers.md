# Key Thinkers: Subjectivity

---

**Edmund Husserl** (1859–1938)

Husserl founded phenomenology as the systematic study of the first-person perspective. He argued that all experience has an intentional structure — it is always "of" or "about" something — and that the task of philosophy is to describe the essential structures of consciousness from within the first-person standpoint, after "bracketing" assumptions about the external world. His analyses of inner time-consciousness, the "living present," and the "for-me-ness" of experience provide the most detailed account of subjectivity in the Western tradition.

*Key works:* *Logical Investigations* (1900–01); *Ideas I* (1913); *Cartesian Meditations* (1931); *The Phenomenology of Internal Time-Consciousness* (1928)

---

**Thomas Nagel** (b. 1937)

Nagel's philosophy of mind is organized around the tension between the objective and subjective standpoints. The objective standpoint abstracts from any particular perspective; the subjective standpoint is what it is like to be this particular creature at this particular time. Nagel argues that no expansion of objective knowledge can capture the subjective character of experience, and that this presents a permanent challenge for physicalism and for any science of the mind that models itself on physics.

*Key works:* "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974); *The View from Nowhere* (1986); *Mortal Questions* (1979)

---

**Jean-Paul Sartre** (1905–1980)

Sartre's phenomenology of consciousness distinguishes pre-reflective consciousness (non-positional awareness of oneself while engaged in the world) from reflective consciousness (thematic self-awareness). He argues that consciousness is always "nothing" — defined by what it is not — and that the attempt to grasp oneself as an object generates "bad faith." His analysis of intersubjectivity, particularly the "look" of the other (through which I become an object for another), remains a central contribution to the philosophy of subjectivity.

*Key works:* *Being and Nothingness* (1943); *The Transcendence of the Ego* (1937); *Sketch for a Theory of the Emotions* (1939)

---

**Dan Zahavi** (b. 1967)

Zahavi is the foremost contemporary interpreter of phenomenological accounts of subjectivity. Against deflationary and eliminativist accounts, he argues that subjectivity — the "mine-ness" or "for-me-ness" of experience — is not reducible to higher-order representation or cognitive access but is an intrinsic feature of any conscious experience. His engagement with cognitive science and philosophy of mind has helped phenomenological analyses of selfhood enter mainstream philosophical debate.

*Key works:* *Subjectivity and Selfhood* (2005); *Self-Awareness and Alterity* (1999); *Phenomenology of Spirit* (2005)

---

**Sydney Shoemaker** (b. 1931)

Shoemaker's work on self-knowledge and first-person authority is central to debates about subjectivity. His argument that first-person psychological reports are immune to error through misidentification (IEM) captures something distinctive about the subjective standpoint: when I say "I am in pain," I cannot be mistaken that it is I who is in pain. Shoemaker's work on self-knowledge has been influential in both analytic philosophy and empirical debates about introspection.

*Key works:* "Self-Reference and Self-Awareness" (1968); *Self-Knowledge and Self-Identity* (1963); *The First-Person Perspective and Other Essays* (1996)
