# Further Reading: Sleep, Dreams, and Hypnosis

## Primary Sources

**Sigmund Freud, *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1900, trans. 1913)** — Freud's foundational work arguing that dreams are the "royal road to the unconscious," disguised fulfillments of repressed wishes. Though his specific theory is contested, the work set the terms for all subsequent discussion of dreaming and the unconscious.

**J. Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley, "The Brain as a Dream State Generator" (1977)** — The activation-synthesis hypothesis, proposing that dreams are the cortex's attempt to make sense of random activation generated by the brainstem during REM sleep. A landmark challenge to Freudian theory.

**Antti Revonsuo, "The Reinterpretation of Dreams: An Evolutionary Hypothesis" (2000)** — Revonsuo proposes the threat simulation theory of dreaming: dreams evolved to simulate threatening events, allowing rehearsal of escape behaviors. Argues that the content of nightmares supports this evolutionary account.

## Secondary Literature

**Owen Flanagan, *Dreaming Souls: Sleep, Dreams, and the Evolution of the Conscious Mind* (2000)** — An accessible philosophical examination of theories of dreaming, consciousness during sleep, and the evolutionary function of REM sleep. Engages with Freud, Hobson, and neuroscience.

**David Foulkes, *Children's Dreaming and the Development of Consciousness* (1999)** — Foulkes presents longitudinal research on children's dreams, arguing that dreaming requires developed cognitive capacities including mental imagery and narrative ability, with implications for theories of dream consciousness.

**Ernest Hilgard, *Divided Consciousness: Multiple Controls in Human Thought and Action* (1977)** — Hilgard's "neodissociation theory" of hypnosis, proposing that hypnosis involves a division of consciousness into executive and hidden observer systems. A foundational text in hypnosis research.

**Irving Kirsch and Steven Lynn, "Hypnotic Involuntariness and the Automaticity of Suggested Behaviors" (1997)** — Argues against dissociation theories, proposing that hypnotic effects can be explained by expectancy, suggestion, and social role, without positing altered states. Useful for contrasting perspectives.

**Matthew Walker, *Why We Sleep* (2017)** — A neuroscientific synthesis arguing for the importance of sleep for memory consolidation, emotional regulation, and health. Connects REM and non-REM sleep to specific cognitive functions and provides context for philosophical discussions.
