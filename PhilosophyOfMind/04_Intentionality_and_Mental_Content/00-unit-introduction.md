# Unit Introduction: Intentionality and Mental Content

## Central Questions

Intentionality is the mind's power to be *about* things — to represent, refer to, or be directed toward objects and states of affairs. When you think about Paris, your thought is about Paris; when you fear a snake, your fear is directed toward the snake; when you want a glass of water, your desire is for water. This directedness toward objects — what Franz Brentano called *intentional inexistence*, meaning the in-existence (within-existence) of an object in a mental state — is arguably the defining feature of mentality. The central questions of this unit are: What is intentionality, and how does it arise? Can the intentionality of mental states be naturalized — explained in terms of purely physical or functional relations? How does mental content relate to the environment? And what is the relationship between derived intentionality (the intentionality of linguistic symbols, pictures, and other artifacts, which derive their aboutness from the minds that use them) and original intentionality (the intentionality of mental states themselves)?

A closely related set of questions concerns the nature of mental *content*: what determines the content of a mental state? If I believe that water is wet, the content of my belief is (roughly) the proposition that water is wet. But what determines this content? Is it fixed by internal factors — the functional role of the belief within my cognitive system, its relations to other beliefs and to sensory inputs? Or is it partly fixed by external factors — the environment in which I live, the kind of stuff my word "water" was originally used to pick out? The debate between *internalism* and *externalism* about content is one of the deepest in philosophy of mind, with ramifications for theories of meaning, self-knowledge, and the nature of mental representation.

## Major Positions and Debates

### Brentano's Thesis and Its Legacy

Brentano claimed in *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874) that intentionality is the mark of the mental: every mental phenomenon is directed toward an object, and no physical phenomenon is. This *Brentano thesis* established intentionality as the central topic of philosophy of mind (at least for the phenomenological tradition) and raised the question of how intentionality could be natural. Brentano himself held that intentional relations are sui generis — they cannot be reduced to physical relations — which led him toward a form of mentalist ontology.

Chisholm extended Brentano's thesis into a criterion for the irreducibility of the mental: sentences that are intentional (in his technical sense — referring to objects that may not exist, not subject to substitution salva veritate, not captured by extensional logic) cannot be paraphrased into purely physical terms. This gave a logical argument for the irreducibility of the mental that influenced Davidson's anomalous monism.

### Teleological Theories of Content

Teleological theories (Ruth Millikan, Fred Dretske, David Papineau) hold that the content of a mental state is determined by its *biological function* — by what the state is *supposed to* indicate or produce, where "supposed to" is cashed out in evolutionary or learning-theoretic terms. Millikan's *biosemantics* holds that a mental state has the content it does because states of that type were selected for (in evolutionary history) because they tracked a certain feature of the environment. A frog's fly-detector has content [fly] because the frog's visual system was selected for tracking flies.

The main virtue of teleological theories is that they explain *misrepresentation*: a state with content [fly] can be tokened by a BB pellet, and this counts as a misrepresentation because BB pellets are not what the state is *for*. The main objections concern the problem of *indeterminacy*: it is unclear whether evolutionary history selects for content [fly] rather than [small moving thing] or [food-shaped stimulus] — the teleological facts underdetermine content. And it is unclear whether the theory can explain the content of distinctively human mental states, which are only remotely connected to evolutionary pressures.

### Informational Theories and Dretske

Fred Dretske's *Knowledge and the Flow of Information* (1981) and *Explaining Behavior* (1988) develop an informational account of mental content: a state has the content that it carries information about in the relevant circumstances. A thermometer carries information about temperature; a bee's dance carries information about the direction of food. Mental states are biological devices for carrying information, and their content is determined by what they reliably co-vary with.

Dretske's account faces the disjunction problem: if a frog's visual system fires for both flies and BB pellets, does it have the content [fly or BB pellet]? The teleological elements in Dretske's later work are designed to address this problem, but they introduce the same indeterminacy problems as full teleological theories.

### Externalism and Twin Earth

Hilary Putnam's *Twin Earth* thought experiment (1975) argued that meaning ain't in the head. Twin Earth is a world exactly like Earth except that the substance called "water" is XYZ, not H₂O. Earthlings and their Twin Earth counterparts are *mentally* identical (same brain states, same functional organization) but their terms "water" refer to different things — H₂O and XYZ respectively. Mental content is therefore not fixed by what is in the head.

Tyler Burge extended this argument to social externalism: what we mean by "arthritis" is partly determined by the social-linguistic practices of our community, not purely by what is in our heads. Even if a subject's internal states were unchanged, they could have different mental contents in a different linguistic community.

Externalism raises the question of what relevance mental content has for psychological explanation. If content is partially constituted by the environment, how can it be a proper psychological kind? This is the *relevance problem* for externalism. Some argue for a *two-factor* theory of content: content has both a narrow component (what is in the head, relevant for psychology) and a wide component (which includes environmental factors, relevant for semantics and reference).

### Derived vs. Original Intentionality

John Searle distinguishes *derived intentionality* (the intentionality of words, pictures, and other representations, which are about things only because users take them to be about things) from *original intentionality* (the intentionality of mental states themselves, which is intrinsic and not derived from any more fundamental source of aboutness). On Searle's view, computers have only derived intentionality: their symbol manipulations are about things only because humans interpret them as being about things.

This distinction is central to the debate about artificial minds: can a computer or AI system have genuine (original) intentionality, or only the derived intentionality of its symbols? Functionalists deny the significance of the distinction; Searle's Chinese Room argument is designed to show that syntactic symbol manipulation is insufficient for genuine semantic content.

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**Franz Brentano** established intentionality as the central topic of philosophy of mind. His claim that every mental phenomenon is intentionally directed toward an object, and that intentionality marks the boundary between mental and physical, set the agenda for the phenomenological tradition and influenced analytic philosophers of mind.

**Roderick Chisholm** formalized Brentano's thesis in terms of the logical features of intentional sentences — their failure to satisfy extensionality principles — and argued that this shows the irreducibility of psychological to physical language.

**Hilary Putnam** argued in "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975) that meaning is not in the head. The Twin Earth argument shows that mental content is partly determined by environmental factors (the nature of the substance picked out) that go beyond what is in the subject's brain.

**Tyler Burge** extended externalism to *social* factors in "Individualism and the Mental" (1979). Burge's arthritis thought experiment shows that the content of a subject's mental states depends on the social-linguistic community in which the subject is embedded.

**Fred Dretske** developed an information-theoretic account of mental content, holding that mental states carry information about the world and that this information relationship grounds intentionality. His distinction between *analog* and *digital* information is particularly illuminating for understanding the relationship between perception and thought.

**Ruth Millikan** developed biosemantics in *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories* (1984), the most systematic attempt to naturalize intentionality by grounding it in biological function. Millikan's account connects intentionality to the proper functions of mental systems, determined by evolutionary history.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Brentano, *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874, esp. Book 2, Chapter 1)**
The founding text for the philosophy of intentionality. Brentano's characterization of mental phenomena as essentially directed toward objects, and his claim that this distinguishes them from physical phenomena, established the research program. The writing is accessible despite its age.

**2. Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975)**
The paper that introduced Twin Earth, semantic externalism, and the thesis that "meanings just ain't in the head." One of the most influential papers in twentieth-century philosophy. Available in *Mind, Language and Reality: Philosophical Papers, Vol. 2*.

**3. Burge, "Individualism and the Mental" (1979)**
The paper that extended externalism beyond reference to the content of beliefs, using social rather than natural kinds. Burge's thought experiments about the arthritis-sufferer and related cases are among the most effective in the literature.

**4. Millikan, *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories* (1984)**
The most systematic naturalistic account of intentionality. Dense and difficult, but rewarding for understanding what a genuinely biological account of content would look like. Millikan's distinction between *direct* and *derived* proper functions is illuminating for understanding original intentionality.

**5. Dretske, *Explaining Behavior: Reasons in a World of Causes* (1988)**
Dretske's mature account of how reasons explain behavior, connecting his information-theoretic account of content to a teleological view of the functions of mental states. More accessible than Millikan, and provides an excellent example of how to think about intentionality within a naturalistic framework.

## Open Questions

**1. Can intentionality be naturalized?**
Teleological and informational theories are the most developed attempts to explain intentionality in purely natural terms. But both face serious objections: indeterminacy (teleological theories underdetermine content), the disjunction problem (informational theories), and the problem of genuinely creative or abstract thought (neither theory handles mathematical or counterfactual content well). Whether a fully satisfactory naturalization of intentionality is possible remains open.

**2. Does the distinction between original and derived intentionality hold up?**
Searle claims that mental states have original intentionality while linguistic and computational symbols have only derived intentionality. But what exactly is the difference? Is there a principled account of original intentionality that does not itself appeal to mental states (which would make the distinction circular)? Some argue that all intentionality is derived from something more primitive — biological function, or dispositions to use — which would dissolve the distinction.

**3. What is the relationship between externalism and self-knowledge?**
If the content of my beliefs is partly determined by my environment, then I may not know (from the first-person perspective alone) what I believe. Burge argues that I can have authoritative self-knowledge even given externalism, because the concepts I deploy in self-knowledge are the same concepts I use in object-level thought. Critics argue that this makes self-knowledge too easy. The debate about the compatibility of externalism and authoritative self-knowledge remains unresolved.

**4. How does intentionality relate to phenomenal consciousness?**
Some philosophers (Searle, Strawson) hold that genuine intentionality requires phenomenal consciousness — the aboutness of mental states is inseparable from what it is like to be in them. Others (Dennett, functionalists generally) hold that intentionality is independent of phenomenal consciousness, since functional states can have content without phenomenal properties. This dispute has implications for artificial minds and for the relationship between the cognitive and phenomenal aspects of mind.

**5. Is there a coherent notion of narrow content?**
The internalist ideal of a notion of content that supervenes on the brain — sometimes called "narrow content" — has proven elusive. Every attempt to specify it (Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory, two-factor theories, functional role semantics) faces objections. Yet the intuition that there must be some sense in which psychological explanation is not hostage to environmental facts retains force. Whether narrow content is theoretically coherent and scientifically useful is genuinely open.
