# Intentionality and Aboutness

Intentionality is the property of mental states of being "about" something, of being directed at an object or state of affairs. When you think about Paris, your thought is about Paris. When you see a red apple, your visual experience is of a red apple. When you desire a cup of coffee, your desire is directed at coffee. This "aboutness" — this directedness of mental states toward objects and states of affairs — is what philosophers call intentionality.

Franz Brentano, writing in the 1870s, proposed intentionality as the mark of the mental: what distinguishes mental phenomena from physical phenomena is precisely this property of being "about" something. Physical processes — the motion of a rock, the rusting of iron — are not about anything. They just happen. Mental processes are different: thoughts, perceptions, desires, beliefs are all about things; they have content that represents or is directed at objects and states of affairs.

**The Structure of Intentional States**

Intentional states have two aspects: an intentional content (what the state is about) and an intentional mode (the attitude taken toward that content). When I believe that it will rain, the content is the proposition "it will rain" and the mode is belief. When I fear that it will rain, the same content is held under the mode of fear. When I desire that it will rain, the mode is desire.

This content-mode structure allows for a fine-grained taxonomy of mental states. Different states can have the same content with different modes (believing vs. hoping vs. fearing that P). And the same mode can take different contents (believing that P vs. believing that Q). The content is what individuates different states with the same mode; the mode is what distinguishes different attitudes toward the same content.

**Intentional Objects**

A peculiarity of intentional states is that they can be directed at objects that do not exist. I can think about Santa Claus, long for the return of a deceased person, fear a non-existent monster. In each case, the mental state is directed at an object — but that object does not exist.

Brentano tried to capture this with his concept of "intentional inexistence": the object of an intentional state exists "in" the state, as an intentional object, even if it does not exist in the external world. This is a puzzling notion — what does it mean to exist "in" a mental state?

The problem of intentional objects has generated several responses:

*Meinongian ontology*: Meinong proposed that there are objects of several kinds — existing objects, subsisting objects (like numbers and propositions), and "absurd" objects (like round squares). The non-existing objects of intentional states are real in the sense of having properties (Santa Claus has a white beard), even though they do not exist. This ontology is highly controversial but has been developed with care by contemporary philosophers.

*Eliminativist responses*: We can re-describe apparently object-directed intentional states without quantifying over non-existent objects. "I think about Santa Claus" can be paraphrased as "I have a thought with the content 'Santa Claus...'" without implying that Santa Claus exists.

*Internalist content*: Perhaps intentional states are directed at objects via mental representations — concepts or images that constitute the intentional content. The mental state is directed at the representation, which in turn represents (or purports to represent) the external object. Non-existent objects are handled by noting that the representation fails to refer.

**Intentionality and Consciousness**

Intentionality and consciousness are sometimes treated as two separate marks of the mental that need to be kept distinct. There are arguments for thinking that:

*There can be intentionality without consciousness*: Unconscious beliefs, desires, and perceptions — states that represent or are directed at things without being phenomenally conscious — seem to exist. Subliminal perception involves processing visual information in ways that are directed at the stimulus (affecting behavior) without any conscious experience of the stimulus.

*There can be consciousness without intentionality (or with minimal intentionality)*: Pure phenomenal states — like a diffuse feeling of unease or a background sensory tone — may have phenomenal character without being clearly directed at any specific object. Some argue that a completely undirected awareness is possible.

Whether these claims are correct is debated. Phenomenologists in the Husserlian tradition insist that all consciousness is intentional — all consciousness is consciousness-of-something. Philosophers of mind sympathetic to phenomenal consciousness as a basic category argue that phenomenal experience can come apart from intentional directedness.

The relationship between intentionality and consciousness — whether one is reducible to the other, or whether they are independent properties that happen to often co-occur — is one of the central problems at the intersection of philosophy of mind and phenomenology.
