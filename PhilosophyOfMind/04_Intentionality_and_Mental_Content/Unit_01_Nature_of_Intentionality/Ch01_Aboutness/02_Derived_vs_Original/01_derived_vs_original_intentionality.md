# Derived vs. Original Intentionality

One of the most fundamental questions about intentionality is whether mental intentionality is original — intrinsic to the mental state — or whether it is derived from something else, as the intentionality of words and symbols is derived from the mental states of language users. This distinction has profound implications for the Chinese Room argument, for the prospects of artificial intelligence, and for theories of mental content.

**The Distinction**

The word "cat" is about cats. But it is about cats only because speakers of English use it to mean cats. The word itself — the marks on paper or the sounds in air — has no intrinsic aboutness. Its intentionality is derived from the intentions of the language users who employ it. Call this derived intentionality.

Mental states, by contrast, seem to have intentionality that is not derived from anything more fundamental. My thought about cats is about cats not because I use my thought to mean cats — rather, being about cats is what makes my thought the thought it is. The thought's intentionality is not conferred by anything outside the thought; it is original, or intrinsic.

John Searle, who introduced this distinction (or at least made it prominent in analytic philosophy of mind), draws a sharp line: intentionality is either original (mental, intrinsic) or derived (linguistic, symbolic). Physical syntax — the manipulation of symbols — can never on its own give rise to original intentionality. This is the core of the Chinese Room argument.

**Why This Matters for AI**

If intentionality comes in original and derived forms, and if only original intentionality constitutes genuine mental states, then the question for artificial intelligence becomes: can a computational system have original intentionality, or only derived?

On Searle's view, the answer is clear: computational systems manipulate symbols according to formal rules; the symbols have only derived intentionality, conferred by the programmers and users who assign meanings to them. No amount of computational sophistication can give a system original intentionality — because original intentionality requires specific causal powers of the biological brain.

Critics of Searle argue that this picture is wrong in several ways. Some argue that the distinction between original and derived intentionality is not as sharp as Searle assumes — that all intentionality is, in some sense, "derived" from physical causal processes. Others argue that original intentionality, properly understood, is not uniquely biological and that sufficiently complex artificial systems could have it.

**The Grounding Problem**

Related to the derived/original distinction is the "symbol grounding problem" formulated by Stevan Harnad. The problem is that symbols in a symbolic AI system get their meanings from other symbols — the meaning of "cat" is given by its connections to other words, which get their meanings from other words, in an endless regress. Symbols never make contact with the world; they are connected to other symbols all the way down.

Harnad argues that to ground symbols — to give them genuine reference — there must be some non-symbolic representations: perceptual and motor representations that make contact with the world without the mediation of language. Iconic representations (images) and categorical perception are his candidates for the non-symbolic base.

The symbol grounding problem is the computational version of the derived/original intentionality distinction. Symbolic AI systems have derived intentionality; systems with sensorimotor grounding might have something closer to original intentionality.

**Naturalistic Accounts and the Collapse of the Distinction**

Many naturalistic philosophers of mind deny that the derived/original distinction is as fundamental as Searle supposes. On causal-informational theories of content (Dretske, Fodor), mental states have original intentionality in virtue of causal relations to the world. These causal relations are physical relations — there is nothing "special" about mental intentionality beyond its physical causal connections.

On teleological theories (Millikan, Papineau), mental states have original intentionality in virtue of their evolutionary function — what they were designed to represent. Again, this is a natural, physical property, not something requiring a special biological substance.

On these views, the distinction between original and derived intentionality is not a distinction between mental and non-mental intentionality, but between intentionality grounded in the right kind of causal or functional relations and intentionality that is merely stipulated. In principle, an artificial system could have the right kind of causal relations or functions to have original intentionality — it would just need the right kind of physical connections to the world and the right kind of history.

**The Irreducibility Claim**

Searle's position requires not just that there is a distinction between original and derived intentionality, but that original intentionality is irreducible to any physical or functional properties. It is this irreducibility that the Chinese Room argument is designed to establish: no amount of formal symbol manipulation, no matter how complex, constitutes genuine original intentionality.

Whether original intentionality is irreducible to physical or functional properties is the deep question. Those who deny the irreducibility claim accept that intentionality can be naturalized — explained in terms of physical or functional relations. Those who affirm it accept Searle's conclusion that original intentionality is a biological phenomenon that cannot be replicated in artificial systems that merely simulate the functional behavior of minded beings.
