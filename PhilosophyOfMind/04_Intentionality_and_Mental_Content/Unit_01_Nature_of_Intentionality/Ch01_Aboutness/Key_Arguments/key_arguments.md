# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Aboutness

## Key Arguments

**Brentano's Argument That Intentionality Marks the Mental**
Franz Brentano argued in Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint that all and only mental phenomena are characterized by intentional inexistence—the immanent directedness of acts toward an object. Physical phenomena (colors, sounds, shapes as presented) lack this directedness; only psychic acts (presentations, judgments, emotions) have it. The identification of intentionality as the mark of the mental provides a criterion for distinguishing the mental from the physical that does not depend on substance dualism and set the agenda for all subsequent attempts to naturalize mental content.

**The Problem of Non-Existent Objects (Meinong)**
Alexius Meinong argued that intentional directedness toward fictional or impossible objects (golden mountains, round squares, Sherlock Holmes) requires that such objects have some form of being, even if they do not exist. His "theory of objects" (Gegenstandstheorie) posits a domain of "subsistent" objects that are the targets of thoughts even when nothing corresponds to them in reality. While widely rejected, Meinong's analysis forces any theory of intentionality to explain how mental states can be about non-existent objects without presupposing a special ontology of non-existent entities.

**Quine's Argument for Intensional Contexts and Their Problems**
Quine argued that intentional or "intensional" contexts (belief-reports, desire-ascriptions) create opacity: co-referring terms cannot be substituted salva veritate (saving truth value). Believing Cicero is an orator does not entail believing Tully is an orator, even though Cicero = Tully. This opacity shows that intentional contexts track more than reference—they track something like sense or mode of presentation. Quine treated this opacity as a reason to regard intentional vocabulary with suspicion; Fodor and others treated it as a reason to posit fine-grained mental representations.

**The Argument That Intentionality Cannot Be Reduced to Physical Relations**
Searle argues that intentionality is a biological phenomenon that cannot be fully explained by any physical or functional relation alone. Any physical relation (causal, structural, informational) can hold between a representation and its target without constituting genuine aboutness: a thermostat is causally responsive to temperature but is not genuinely about temperature in the way that a human thought is. The additional ingredient—genuine intentionality—is a feature that biological brains have but physical and computational systems do not automatically possess.

**The Naturalization Project**
Fodor, Dretske, Millikan, and others argue that intentionality can be naturalized—explained in terms of physical relations (causal, information-theoretic, evolutionary) without residue. The project is motivated by the conviction that if intentionality is a real property of mental states, it must have a naturalistic account; a property that resists naturalization would be suspiciously sui generis. The main strategies are causal/informational (content is determined by what reliably causes a representation), teleological (content is determined by what a representation was selected to represent), and inferential role (content is determined by the role a representation plays in inference).

## Core Concepts

**Intentionality**
Intentionality is the property of mental states whereby they are about, represent, or are directed toward objects, properties, or states of affairs. Coined by Brentano as the distinguishing mark of the mental, intentionality encompasses the full range of mental directedness: perceiving red, believing that it is raining, desiring a cup of coffee, fearing heights. Intentionality is puzzling because: (1) it can be directed toward non-existent objects; (2) it is semantically fine-grained (two thoughts can be about the same thing under different descriptions); and (3) it is normative (there can be misrepresentation).

**Intentional Inexistence**
Intentional inexistence is Brentano's term for the mode in which an object is "contained" in a mental act: the object of a thought "exists" in the thought as an intentional object, but this existence within the thought is not the same as real existence. When I think of a unicorn, the unicorn has intentional inexistence in my thought; but there is no real unicorn. The concept attempts to explain how thoughts can be about objects without presupposing those objects' real existence, by positing a special kind of immanent existence within mental acts.

**Intentional Object**
An intentional object is the object toward which a mental act is directed—the thing the act is about. Intentional objects have a special status: they need not exist (I can wish for immortality) and their identity depends on how the subject represents them (I can think of Hesperus without thinking of Phosphorus, even though they are the same planet). Contemporary philosophy of mind distinguishes between the intentional object (determined by representational content) and the real object (what actually satisfies the representation, if anything).

**Opacity (Referential Opacity)**
Referential opacity is the property of a context in which co-referring terms cannot be substituted salva veritate. Intentional contexts (belief reports, desire ascriptions) are typically opaque: "John believes that Hesperus is visible" does not entail "John believes that Phosphorus is visible," even though Hesperus = Phosphorus. Opacity shows that intentional content is more fine-grained than reference: what matters is not only what a mental state is about (its reference) but how it represents its object (its mode of presentation or content). Opacity is a central data point for theories of mental content.

**Misrepresentation**
Misrepresentation is the capacity of a mental state to represent something incorrectly—to be about X but to predicate false properties of it, or to represent X when Y is the actual cause. Any adequate theory of intentionality must explain misrepresentation: a purely causal account (content is whatever causes the representation) cannot allow for misrepresentation, because the actual cause of the representation is always the "right" cause. Dretske's information-theoretic account, Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory, and Millikan's teleological account are designed to handle misrepresentation while remaining naturalistic.

## Thought Experiments

**Meinong's Golden Mountain**
Meinong observes that we can think about a golden mountain, wonder whether it exists, and conclude it does not. But thinking requires an object; there must be something we are thinking about. Yet there is no real golden mountain. Meinong concludes that the golden mountain has a kind of being (subsistence) without existing. Russell famously criticized this as violating Occam's razor: the description "the golden mountain" can be analyzed as a disguised quantification without positing any subsistent entity. The thought experiment is the foundational case for the problem of non-existent intentional objects.

**The Thought About Sherlock Holmes**
When I think "Sherlock Holmes is a detective," I am thinking about a fictional character who does not exist. The intentional structure of my thought has content (being a detective) directed at an object (Holmes) that has no real-world counterpart. Theories of intentionality must explain this: the object theory posits subsistent fictional objects; the pretense theory holds that thoughts about fictions are plays of make-believe; the description theory identifies Holmes with a description. The case tests any theory's ability to handle non-existent intentional objects without ontological bloat.

**The Thermometer and Temperature (Misrepresentation)**
A thermometer reads 98.6°F when the actual temperature is 100°F—has the thermometer misrepresented the temperature? In one sense yes (the display is wrong); in another sense no (the thermometer is just a physical device that lacks genuine intentionality). The case tests the line between genuine intentionality (with the normative notion of correct and incorrect representation) and mere causal covariation (where there is no genuine aboutness). Any naturalistic account of intentionality must explain why human thoughts about temperature involve genuine representation while thermometers do not—or bite the bullet and say thermometers have proto-intentional states.
