# Propositional Attitudes

Propositional attitudes are mental states that are about propositions — states of believing, wanting, hoping, fearing, knowing, and so on, all of which have a propositional content. When I believe that it will rain tomorrow, the proposition "it will rain tomorrow" is the object of my belief. The term "propositional attitude" was introduced by Bertrand Russell to describe this structure: the subject has an attitude (belief, desire, hope...) toward a proposition (a complete thought, the kind of thing that can be true or false).

**The Structure: Mode and Content**

Every propositional attitude has two components: a mode (the type of attitude) and a content (the proposition toward which the attitude is directed).

The mode determines what kind of psychological relation the subject has toward the content. Believing that P and desiring that P are different psychological states even when P is the same. The mode is sometimes called the "propositional attitude operator" — it operates on the content proposition to produce the specific mental state.

The content is a proposition — typically a complete declarative sentence that expresses a state of affairs that can be true or false. "It will rain tomorrow," "there are nine planets," "pain is unpleasant" — these are all the kinds of propositions that can serve as the content of propositional attitudes.

**The Role of Propositional Attitudes in Folk Psychology**

Propositional attitudes are the central posits of "folk psychology" — the common-sense framework we all use to explain and predict each other's behavior. I explain why you reached for an umbrella by attributing to you the belief that it will rain and the desire to stay dry. I predict that you will leave early by attributing to you the belief that the roads will be busy and the desire to arrive on time.

This folk-psychological explanation is so natural and automatic that we rarely notice we are doing it. But it involves substantive commitments: it assumes that other people have beliefs, desires, and other propositional attitudes that causally explain their behavior, and that these attitudes can be attributed by interpreting behavior in the light of the assumption of rationality.

Jerry Fodor argued that folk psychology is a genuine scientific theory — the "intentional stance" (to use Dennett's phrase) — that makes predictively powerful claims about the causal structure of behavior. If folk psychology is correct, propositional attitudes are real mental states that cause behavior.

**Propositional Attitudes and the Language of Thought**

One influential account of the physical implementation of propositional attitudes is the "language of thought" (LOT) hypothesis, associated with Fodor. On this view, propositional attitudes are relations to mental representations — sentences in a "language of thought" or "Mentalese" that have compositional syntactic and semantic structure.

To believe that P is to have a mental representation with the content P in the "belief box" — a functional storage structure dedicated to belief. To desire that P is to have a representation with content P in the "desire box." The propositional structure of attitudes is mirrored by the compositional structure of mental representations.

The LOT hypothesis explains several features of thought:

*Productivity*: We can think an infinite number of distinct thoughts (any proposition can be a thought content) — which requires that thought be compositional, capable of generating complex representations from simpler parts.

*Systematicity*: If you can think that John loves Mary, you can also think that Mary loves John — different combinations of the same conceptual components.

*Inferential coherence*: Thoughts combine in systematic inferential patterns that reflect their compositional structure.

**Problems for Propositional Attitude Psychology**

*The non-conceptual content problem*: Some mental states seem to have content that is not propositional — perceptions, for instance, seem to represent properties and objects without having the full propositional structure of beliefs. How do propositional attitude psychology and theories of non-conceptual representation fit together?

*Eliminativist challenges*: Eliminative materialists (Churchland) argue that folk psychology is a radically mistaken theory that will be displaced by neuroscience. If folk psychology is false, propositional attitudes are not real mental states. Whether folk psychology is vindicated or eliminated by cognitive neuroscience is an open question.

*Holism*: The content of propositional attitudes seems to depend on their connections to other attitudes — the meaning of believing that a cat is on the mat depends on what else one believes (about cats, mats, spatial relations). If content is holistic, individuating specific propositional attitudes is difficult, and the atomistic picture implied by LOT may be wrong.

**The Intentionality of Propositions**

Propositional attitudes inherit the puzzles of intentionality generally. The proposition that P is typically explained in terms of possible states of affairs, structured facts, or sentence meanings. The attitude toward P is a relation between a subject and a proposition. Both the nature of propositions and the nature of the relation (how a mind can be "about" a proposition) raise deep metaphysical questions that folk psychology does not answer on its own.
