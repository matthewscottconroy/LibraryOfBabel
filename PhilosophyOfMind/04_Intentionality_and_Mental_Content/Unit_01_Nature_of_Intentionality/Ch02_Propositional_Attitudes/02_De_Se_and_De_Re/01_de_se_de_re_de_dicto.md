# De Se, De Re, and De Dicto Attitudes

Philosophers of language and philosophy of mind have distinguished several ways in which a mental state can be about something. The distinctions between de dicto, de re, and de se attitudes — literally "about the word," "about the thing," and "about oneself" — capture important differences in how propositional attitudes are specified and what exactly they represent.

**De Dicto vs. De Re**

The most fundamental distinction is between de dicto and de re readings of attitude attributions.

Consider: "Alice wants to marry the richest person in the world." This sentence has two readings:

*De dicto*: Alice wants it to be the case that she marries whoever is the richest person in the world. She has a desire whose content involves the description "the richest person in the world." She may not know who this person is, and she would be satisfied by marrying whoever turns out to be richest.

*De re*: There is some person — the richest person in the world — and Alice wants to marry him/her specifically. Alice's desire is about a specific individual, not about whoever satisfies a description. The desire is directed at the person, not the description.

The difference matters when identity conditions are at stake. Suppose the richest person in the world is someone Alice actively dislikes. On the de dicto reading, Alice's desire is satisfied by marrying this person (since they are the richest). On the de re reading, Alice's desire is definitely not satisfied by marrying this person — it is directed at the specific person (under whatever description Alice uses to think about them), and that person is not who she has in mind.

**Opacity and Transparency**

These distinctions connect to the Fregean notion of referential opacity. In opaque contexts (de dicto readings), co-referring terms cannot be substituted salva veritate. If the richest person in the world is Bill Gates, we cannot substitute "Bill Gates" for "the richest person in the world" in the de dicto reading without potentially changing the truth value. Alice wants to marry the richest person in the world (de dicto), but Alice may not want to marry Bill Gates — if she doesn't know that Bill Gates is the richest person.

Transparent contexts (de re readings) allow substitution of co-referring terms. If Alice's desire is directed at Bill Gates (de re), then she wants to marry Bill Gates, whatever she calls him.

**De Se Attitudes**

The de se reading — attitudes about oneself — was identified by Hector-Neri Castañeda and John Perry as a special and philosophically important class. De se attitudes are mental states in which the subject is represented in a specifically first-personal way — in the way one represents oneself as oneself.

John Perry's famous "messy shopper" example illustrates this: A shopper in a grocery store notices a trail of sugar leaking from someone's cart. She follows the trail, trying to identify the source, until she realizes — the trail is coming from her own cart. At this moment, she forms a belief: "I am making a mess." This belief is de se — it represents the subject as herself, in the first-person way.

Before she realized the trail was hers, she might have had the belief "that woman is making a mess" (de re, about herself) — but this belief, while also about her, does not have the right kind of self-referential character to produce the appropriate behavior. The de se belief "I am making a mess" is action-guiding in a way the de re belief is not.

Perry argues that de se beliefs are essential to explaining why people act in ways that require them to represent themselves specifically as themselves. Any action that involves moving one's body toward a goal requires the agent to represent themselves as the one who is in a certain location relative to the goal — a de se self-representation.

**Centered Worlds and De Se Content**

Lewis analyzed de se attitudes in terms of "centered worlds." A centered world is a possible world plus a designated "center" — a particular individual at a particular time and place within that world. Beliefs locate the subject within a centered world: they specify not just what the world is like (what propositions are true) but where the subject is within it.

On Lewis's account, to believe that one is the shortest person in the room is not to believe a proposition (which would be either true or false independently of who entertains it) but to self-ascribe a property — the property of being the shortest person in the room. Belief is self-ascription of properties rather than acceptance of propositions.

This framework handles de se attitudes naturally: de se beliefs are beliefs that essentially involve self-ascription, and they cannot be reduced to beliefs about the world without a center.

**Implications for Action Theory**

The de se/de re/de dicto distinction has implications beyond philosophy of language. For theories of action and rationality, de se representations play a crucial role: any agent that acts must represent itself as the agent — must have de se knowledge of its own location, state, and capacities. The integration of de se self-knowledge with world knowledge is required for rational action.

This connection to action has also been important for debates in artificial intelligence and robotics: building systems that can act effectively requires building systems that have genuine de se representations of their own states and positions, not merely de re or de dicto beliefs about what objects are where.
