# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Propositional Attitudes

## Key Arguments

**Frege's Puzzle About Cognitive Value**
Frege observed that "Hesperus = Hesperus" is trivially true (knowable a priori), while "Hesperus = Phosphorus" is informative (knowable only empirically). Yet if both names refer to the same planet, these sentences express the same proposition on a purely referential account. Frege concluded that names have senses (modes of presentation) in addition to references, and that the cognitive value of a sentence is determined by its sense. For propositional attitudes, this implies that beliefs individuated only by their truth conditions (what they are about) cannot explain the differences in informational value between co-referring beliefs.

**The Problem of De Re vs. De Dicto Beliefs**
A de dicto belief is a belief under a description: "Mary believes that the tallest spy is dangerous" describes what Mary believes about someone as described. A de re belief is a belief about a specific individual: "Mary believes of John (who is the tallest spy) that he is dangerous." These can come apart: Mary may have the de dicto belief without knowing that John is the tallest spy. The distinction matters for attitude ascription, legal contexts, and the individuation of mental content, because de re and de dicto beliefs have different truth conditions and different implications for rationality.

**The Substitution Failure Argument**
In intentional (belief, desire) contexts, co-referring terms cannot generally be substituted salva veritate. "Oedipus wants to marry Jocasta" is true; "Oedipus wants to marry his mother" is false (or at least fails to report the same attitude), even though Jocasta is his mother. This failure of substitution shows that propositional attitude reports are sensitive to the mode of presentation of the object, not just to the object itself. Any theory of propositional attitudes must explain this substitution failure.

**Kripke's Puzzle About Belief**
Kripke describes Pierre, who learns "Londres est jolie" (London is pretty) in French and believes it, then moves to London and learns "London is not pretty" in English without realizing London = Londres. Pierre seems to believe both that London is pretty and that London is not pretty—he is not irrational, yet he holds contradictory beliefs. Kripke argues this puzzle arises even without description theories and suggests our normal principles for belief attribution (disquotation, translation) are incoherent in complex cases. The puzzle shows that the semantics of belief reports is deeply problematic.

**Fodor's Language of Thought Hypothesis for Propositional Attitudes**
Fodor argues that propositional attitudes are relations between people and mental representations with syntactic and semantic properties: to believe that P is to stand in a functional relation to a mental sentence in the language of thought that means P. This account explains the productivity and systematicity of thought (the same mental words can combine in many ways), the opacity of belief reports (the relation is to a representation, not just to a truth-condition), and the fine-grained individuation of attitudes (different representations with the same truth-conditions can be believed or disbelieved independently).

## Core Concepts

**Propositional Attitude**
A propositional attitude is a mental state involving a relation between a subject and a proposition—a structured content that is either true or false. Examples: believing that it will rain, desiring that the team wins, fearing that the bridge will collapse. The term was introduced by Russell. Propositional attitudes are individuated by both their attitude type (belief vs. desire vs. fear) and their content (which proposition is the object). The main debates concern how finely to individuate content (Fregean senses vs. Russellian propositions), whether content is "wide" or "narrow," and how attitudes are realized in the brain.

**De Re and De Dicto**
De dicto (from the saying) attitudes are characterized from inside the subject's perspective—by how the subject represents the object. De re (from the thing) attitudes are characterized from outside—as relations to specific objects regardless of how the subject represents them. "Lois believes that Superman can fly" is de dicto (reporting her belief under the Superman description); "Lois believes of Clark Kent that he can fly" is de re (reporting her relation to Clark Kent regardless of description). The distinction affects attributions of knowledge, rationality, and moral responsibility.

**Opacity and Transparency**
An intentional context is opaque if co-referring terms cannot be substituted salva veritate; it is transparent (referentially transparent) if they can. Standard logical contexts are transparent: "If A = B and F(A), then F(B)." But belief contexts are opaque: "John believes A is F" does not entail "John believes B is F" even if A = B, because John may not know that A = B. The opacity of belief contexts motivates Fregean sense theory and the language of thought hypothesis, both of which explain opacity by positing fine-grained mental representations that beliefs are relations to.

**Narrow and Wide Content**
Narrow content is content determined entirely by the subject's internal physical or functional states—what two individuals share when they are functional duplicates, regardless of their environments. Wide content is determined partly by the subject's environment and history—it can differ between functional duplicates in different environments (Putnam's Earth and Twin Earth). The question of whether propositional attitude content is narrow or wide is a central debate in philosophy of mind: wide content is needed for semantic externalism, while narrow content is needed for explanation of behavior by content that is accessible to the agent.

**The Language of Thought (LOT)**
Fodor's language of thought hypothesis holds that thinking is the manipulation of mental representations that have a combinatorial syntactic structure and compositional semantic properties. Mental representations are sentences in a "Mentalese"—an internal language in which thoughts are tokened. This account makes propositional attitudes relations to mental sentences and explains the productivity and systematicity of thought. Propositional attitude psychology (folk psychology) is vindicated if it tracks these computational relations to representations.

## Thought Experiments

**Frege's Hesperus/Phosphorus Case**
"Hesperus is Phosphorus" was an empirical discovery (both are Venus), even though "Hesperus is Hesperus" is a trivial tautology. If propositional attitudes were individuated only by truth-conditions (what they are about), believing "Hesperus is Hesperus" and believing "Hesperus is Phosphorus" would be the same belief. But they clearly are not: a rational person can believe one without believing the other. The case motivates Fregean sense-based individuation of propositional attitudes and reveals that content must be more fine-grained than reference.

**Kripke's Pierre**
Pierre, a French speaker, sincerely assents to "Londres est jolie" (London is beautiful). He is then transported to London, learns English, and sincerely assents to "London is not pretty"—without realizing that London and Londres are the same city. By the disquotational principle, Pierre believes London is pretty (from his French belief) and believes London is not pretty (from his English belief). But Pierre is perfectly rational. Kripke argues no theory—Fregean, Russellian, or functional—cleanly resolves this puzzle without undermining our ordinary principles for belief attribution.

**Oedipus and His Mother**
Oedipus wants to marry Jocasta and in fact does so. He does not want to marry his mother (he would be horrified). Yet Jocasta is his mother. Does Oedipus want to marry his mother? De dicto: no (under the description "my mother," he does not want to marry her). De re: it seems we can say yes (of that woman, who is his mother, he does want to marry her). The case illustrates the ambiguity of attitude reports and shows that propositional attitudes depend essentially on how the subject represents their objects—not just on what the objects are.
