# Key Thinkers: Propositional Attitudes

---

**Bertrand Russell** (1872–1970)

Russell introduced and systematized the concept of propositional attitudes in philosophy. He argued that believing, desiring, hoping, and related mental states are relations between a mind and a proposition — an abstract entity that can be true or false. Russell's multiple-relation theory of judgment (in the *Logical Investigations*) and his later accounts of propositions provided the logical framework within which the philosophy of propositional attitudes has developed.

*Key works:* *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912); "On Propositions" (1919); *An Inquiry into Meaning and Truth* (1940)

---

**Gottlob Frege** (1848–1925)

Frege's distinction between sense and reference is indispensable for understanding propositional attitudes. The same object can be thought about under different senses, which explains why "Hesperus is Hesperus" and "Hesperus is Phosphorus" are not interchangeable in belief contexts. Frege recognized that in "oratio obliqua" (indirect discourse about propositional attitudes), expressions refer to their ordinary senses rather than their ordinary referents — a crucial observation for the logic of belief reports.

*Key works:* "On Sense and Reference" (1892); "The Thought: A Logical Inquiry" (1918); *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* (1893)

---

**Jerry Fodor** (1935–2017)

Fodor is the most important twentieth-century defender of propositional attitude psychology. He argued that beliefs, desires, and other propositional attitudes are scientifically genuine, causally efficacious states and that they are best understood as computational relations to mental representations (sentences in the "Language of Thought"). He opposed both behaviorism (which eliminates propositional attitudes) and eliminative materialism (which replaces them), insisting that cognitive science presupposes and vindicates folk psychology's propositional attitude framework.

*Key works:* "Propositional Attitudes" (1978); *Psychosemantics* (1987); *The Elm and the Expert* (1994)

---

**Donald Davidson** (1917–2003)

Davidson's anomalous monism holds that mental events, including propositional attitude states, are identical to physical brain events, but that there are no strict psychophysical laws connecting mental descriptions to physical ones. The propositional attitudes are characterized by their normative role in a system of rationality: attributing beliefs and desires requires making the subject maximally rational, which means propositional attitude explanations are not reducible to physical causal laws.

*Key works:* "Mental Events" (1970); "Radical Interpretation" (1973); *Essays on Actions and Events* (1980)

---

**Stephen Stich** (b. 1943)

Stich challenged the scientific legitimacy of propositional attitude psychology in *From Folk Psychology to Cognitive Science* (1983), arguing that cognitive science processes information syntactically and that content-involving descriptions (beliefs, desires) will not correspond to natural computational kinds. His later work, including *The Fragmentation of Reason* (1990), explores whether there are multiple incommensurable standards of rationality and questions the normative foundations of propositional attitude attribution.

*Key works:* *From Folk Psychology to Cognitive Science* (1983); *The Fragmentation of Reason* (1990); *Deconstructing the Mind* (1996)
