# Causal Theories of Mental Content

Causal theories of mental content hold that mental representations get their content — their aboutness — from causal relations between the representation and the things it represents. The thought "water" is about water because water causes that type of thought. This approach tries to naturalize intentionality: to explain mental content in terms of natural, physical causal relations, without appealing to any spooky intrinsic properties of mental states.

**The Basic Idea**

The earliest causal theories drew on the analogy of photographs. A photograph of the Eiffel Tower represents the Eiffel Tower because the Eiffel Tower caused the photograph. The causal history of the photograph determines what it is about.

Applied to mental states: the mental representation "cat" represents cats because cats cause the tokening of "cat" representations in the mind. When you see a cat and form the belief "that's a cat," the causal chain running from the cat through your visual system to the mental state determines that the mental state is about cats.

Fred Dretske and Jerry Fodor developed this basic idea in more sophisticated directions, attempting to handle various problems while preserving the naturalistic, causal framework.

**Dretske's Informational Theory**

Fred Dretske's approach in *Knowledge and the Flow of Information* (1981) grounds meaning in informational relations. A mental state carries information about an external state if the occurrence of the mental state is nomically (law-governedly) connected to the external state — if the mental state would not occur unless the external state did.

Dretske further distinguishes the natural meaning of a signal (smoke naturally means fire, because smoke would not occur without fire) from the non-natural meaning of a mental representation (the belief that there is fire). Mental content is a form of natural meaning that has been "digitized" — extracted from its original informational context and made context-independent.

The key move is that the content of a representation is determined not by the actual causes of its tokening but by the ideal conditions under which it would serve as a reliable detector. In optimal conditions, the representation "cat" would occur if and only if there is a cat. The content is what would reliably cause the representation under ideal conditions.

**Fodor's Asymmetric Dependence Theory**

Jerry Fodor developed a more sophisticated causal theory designed to handle the "error problem" — the fact that our mental representations can misrepresent. If I look at a dog in bad light and form the belief "cat," the dog has caused my "cat" representation. But this does not mean that "cat" is about dogs — my "cat" representation is misrepresenting.

Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory holds that what makes "cat" mean cat rather than dog is the asymmetry between two causal relations: "cat" causes "cat" representations (appropriately caused); "dog" sometimes causes "cat" representations (mistaken cases). The second relation asymmetrically depends on the first — cows cause "cat" representations only because cats do. If cats stopped causing "cat" representations, cows would stop doing so too (since the misidentification would no longer be possible). But if cows stopped causing "cat" representations, cats would not thereby stop doing so.

This asymmetric dependence constitutes the normative claim implicit in content attribution: "cat" means cat because cat is what it is supposed to be caused by; other things cause it only derivatively, in cases of error.

**Advantages of Causal Theories**

Causal theories have the significant advantage of naturalizing intentionality. They explain what mental states represent in terms of physical causal relations — no mysterious intrinsic properties, no appeal to teleology or functional norms, just causation.

They are empirically tractable: the causal histories of mental states are in principle investigable by science, connecting philosophy of mind to cognitive neuroscience and psychology.

They handle the distinction between de re and de dicto representations naturally: what a representation is (de re) about is determined by what it is causally connected to in the world.
