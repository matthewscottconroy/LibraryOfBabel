# Problems for Causal Theories of Content

Despite their appeal, causal theories of mental content face several serious problems. These problems have generated an extensive literature and have led to the development of more sophisticated alternatives — including teleological theories and inferential role semantics.

**The Disjunction Problem**

The most discussed objection is the "disjunction problem" or "misrepresentation problem." A simple causal theory says: "X" means X because X causes "X" tokens. But "X" tokens can be caused by things that are not X — mistakes happen. If I misidentify a fox as a cat, the fox has caused my "cat" representation. Does this mean "cat" means cat-or-fox?

A simple causal theory implies yes: since both cats and foxes cause "cat" tokens, "cat" means cat-or-fox. But this is wrong — "cat" does not mean cat-or-fox. When I misidentify a fox as a cat, my belief is false, not a true belief about a disjunctive property.

Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory is designed to handle this: foxes cause "cat" tokens only because cats do (since the misidentification depends on the cat/cat connection being in place). But critics argue that the asymmetric dependence theory has its own difficulties:

What exactly does "asymmetric dependence" amount to? The claim that if cats stopped causing "cat" tokens, foxes would too, is a counterfactual. Counterfactuals are notoriously difficult to evaluate. For many representations, it is hard to know whether the relevant counterfactual dependencies hold.

The theory may be circular: the notion of "mistaken application" that defines the asymmetric dependence may itself presuppose the very notion of correctness that the theory is trying to explain.

**The Frege Problem**

A related problem concerns the fine-grained individuation of content. If Mary thought about Hesperus (the Morning Star) and believes "Hesperus rises in the morning," she also thereby thinks about Phosphorus (the Evening Star) — since Hesperus = Phosphorus. But she might not believe "Phosphorus rises in the morning" — she might not know that Hesperus is Phosphorus.

Causal theories individuate content by what the representation is causally connected to. Since Hesperus = Phosphorus, both names are causally connected to the same object. So a simple causal theory implies that "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" have the same mental content — which seems wrong, since they are cognitively distinct.

Dretske and Fodor attempt to handle this by distinguishing modes of presentation — the way the content is accessed — from the content itself. But specifying modes of presentation in causal terms is difficult.

**The Distality Problem**

When I form the representation "dog," what exactly does it causally respond to? There is a chain of proximate and distal causes: the dog's surface features → light reflected from the dog → retinal stimulation → neural processing → the mental representation. At which level does the content get fixed?

Simple causal theories say the most distal cause — the dog — is what the representation is about. But why prefer the most distal cause? All the intermediate causes are also causal antecedents of the representation. Dretske addresses this with the notion of information-channel conditions: the content is fixed by what the representation carries information about, given background conditions.

But specifying the relevant "background conditions" that determine information channels requires appealing to something like functional or teleological norms — bringing in resources that go beyond pure causation.

**The Prehistory Problem**

Causal theories face a problem with the content of novel representations — representations formed in creatures that never had the right causal history. If a creature evolved in an environment containing only purple berries (but not the concept of "purple") and then encounters a blue sky for the first time, what does its new representation of blueness represent? There is no causal history connecting the representation to blue things generally; there is only the current causal connection.

This problem is especially acute for representations of abstract or theoretical entities. My representation of "quark" was not causally produced by quarks (they are too small to interact with me directly). Its content is fixed by some more indirect causal story. Specifying these indirect stories without collapsing into teleological or inferential role approaches is difficult.

**The Role of These Problems**

The problems for causal theories are not knockdown refutations — sophisticated versions of causal theory have responses to each. But they motivate looking at alternative approaches: teleological theories ground content in evolutionary function rather than causal history; inferential role semantics grounds content in inferential relations within a conceptual system rather than causal relations to the world. The debate among these approaches remains active.
