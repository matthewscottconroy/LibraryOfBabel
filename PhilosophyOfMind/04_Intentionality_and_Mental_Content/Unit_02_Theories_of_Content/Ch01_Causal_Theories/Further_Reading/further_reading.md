# Further Reading: Causal Theories of Mental Content

## Primary Sources

**Fred Dretske, *Knowledge and the Flow of Information* (1981)** — Dretske develops an information-theoretic account of mental content: a mental state represents X if it carries information about X in the sense of Shannon information. Foundational for causal-informational approaches to content.

**Fred Dretske, *Explaining Behavior: Reasons in a World of Causes* (1988)** — Dretske refines his earlier view by addressing the "misrepresentation problem" — how states can represent things they are not caused by. He introduces a "learning period" during which the representational function is established.

**Jerry Fodor, "A Theory of Content, I and II" (1990)** — Fodor develops his "asymmetric dependence" account of mental content: a symbol means X if (i) X causes tokens of that symbol, and (ii) non-X tokens of that symbol (errors) depend asymmetrically on the X-caused tokens. Published in *A Theory of Content and Other Essays* (1990).

## Secondary Literature

**Ruth Millikan, "In Defense of Proper Functions" (1989)** — While primarily a teleological account, Millikan argues here against purely causal accounts because they cannot distinguish content from mere causal correlates — the "disjunction problem." Essential contrast to Dretske and Fodor.

**Barry Loewer and Georges Rey (eds.), *Meaning in Mind: Fodor and His Critics* (1991)** — A collection of critical responses to Fodor's causal-informational semantics, covering the asymmetric dependence account, the language of thought, and mental content broadly.

**David Papineau, "Representation and Explanation" (1984)** — An early and clear statement of a causal-informational approach that anticipates several subsequent developments, distinguishing representational from non-representational causal relations.

**Robert Stalnaker, *Our Knowledge of the Internal World* (2008)** — Argues for an internalist, two-dimensional semantic framework that accommodates the intuitions driving causal theories while preserving the rational accessibility of content. A sophisticated alternative perspective.
