# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Causal Theories of Content

## Key Arguments

**Dretske's Information-Theoretic Semantics**
Fred Dretske argues that mental content can be naturalized using information theory: a state carries information about X if and only if the probability of that state given X is 1 (in the learning period when the system is calibrated to its environment). The semantic content of a mental state is the information it is "designed" to carry—information that it was recruited to carry through a history of learning or evolution. This information-theoretic account handles co-variation while avoiding pure causal covariance accounts, but it faces difficulties with error and the distinction between information and meaning.

**Fodor's Asymmetric Dependence Theory**
Fodor argues that a mental symbol M means X if and only if: (1) X-caused M-tokens and other-things-caused M-tokens, and (2) X-caused M-tokens do not depend on other-things-caused M-tokens, but other-things-caused M-tokens do asymmetrically depend on X-caused M-tokens. Cows cause "horse" tokens (when I mistake a cow for a horse), but this causal relation depends on there being horse-caused "horse" tokens; the reverse is not true. This asymmetric dependence is what makes "horse" mean horse rather than horse-or-cow, and it handles the disjunction problem.

**The Disjunction Problem**
The simplest causal covariance account of content says that "horse" means whatever reliably causes "horse" tokens. But sometimes cows (in bad light) cause "horse" tokens—so "horse" means horse-or-cow? No: "horse" means horse, and the cow tokens are misrepresentations. But the causal account cannot distinguish between horses being the content and horses-or-cows being the content if both cause the representation. Solving the disjunction problem is the central challenge for causal theories: the solution must specify which cause is the "right" one without circularity.

**The Teleological Solution to the Disjunction Problem**
Millikan's teleological account and Dretske's learning-period account both solve the disjunction problem by appealing to function: the content of a representation is what it was selected (biologically or through learning) to co-vary with, not what it currently co-varies with. Error occurs when the system operates outside the conditions under which it was calibrated. This grounds the normative notion of correct representation in natural selection or learning history, avoiding the disjunction problem without circularity.

**The Challenge from Counterfactual Situations**
Causal theories must specify not just what actually causes M-tokens but what would cause them in counterfactual situations—this determines what M means. A "horse" token has horse content because, in the right conditions, only horses cause it. But specifying the "right conditions" without circularity (without already invoking semantic notions) is notoriously difficult. Different causal theories give different accounts of the counterfactual—Dretske's information theory, Fodor's asymmetric dependence, and Stampe's causal theory each handle this differently.

## Core Concepts

**Causal Theory of Content**
A causal theory of content holds that the content of a mental representation is determined by causal relations between the representation type and the things it is about: what M means is determined by what causes M-tokens. The most basic version (pure causal covariance) faces the disjunction problem and the problem of error. More sophisticated versions (Dretske's information-theoretic account, Fodor's asymmetric dependence) add constraints on the causal relation to allow for misrepresentation. Causal theories are naturalistic: they explain content in terms of relations that can be described without using semantic or intentional concepts.

**Informational Semantics (Dretske)**
Dretske's informational semantics holds that a signal carries information about X if and only if the conditional probability of the signal given X is 1—the signal is an infallible indicator of X in the circumstances. During the learning period, when a system is being calibrated to its environment, the representations it forms acquire informational content; after the learning period, the system can misrepresent because conditions have changed. The account naturalizes content using Shannon information theory, identifying meaning with the information a state was designed to carry.

**Asymmetric Dependence (Fodor)**
Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory specifies that M means X when the law connecting X to M is not asymmetrically dependent on any other content-determining law: all other things that cause M-tokens do so only because X causes M-tokens, but not vice versa. This captures the intuition that M means horse (not horse-or-cow) because cow-caused-M depends on there being horse-caused-M (if I had no "horse" concept, I would not mistake cows for horses), while the reverse is false. The theory is a sophisticated response to the disjunction problem.

**The Disjunction Problem**
The disjunction problem arises for any causal or information-theoretic account of content: if both X and Y cause M-tokens (because Y is sometimes mistaken for X), then the pure causal account implies M means X-or-Y, not X. But this is the wrong result—M means X, and Y-caused M-tokens are errors. The problem shows that causal covariance is insufficient for content: the theory must specify which of the causes is the "correct" one without using semantic notions (which would be circular). Solutions include asymmetric dependence, teleological function, and causal-historical chains.

**Naturalizing Intentionality**
The project of naturalizing intentionality aims to give a sufficient account of mental content in purely physical-causal terms, without using semantic or intentional vocabulary in the analysans. This project is motivated by physicalism: if mental states have content, and content is a real property, it must be explainable in naturalistic terms. The main naturalistic proposals are causal-informational (Dretske, Fodor), teleological (Millikan, Papineau), and inferential-role theories. Searle argues that no such naturalization is possible; critics of naturalizing intentionality typically hold that normativity (the correctness conditions of representation) cannot be reduced to non-normative physical relations.

## Thought Experiments

**The Cow-in-the-Dark Case**
A farmer's visual system reliably indicates horses in normal conditions, but one dark night it produces a "horse" representation in response to a cow. If pure causal covariance determines content, then "horse" means horse-or-whatever-causes-horse-representations, including cows at night. This seems wrong: the farmer has misrepresented the cow as a horse. The disjunction problem shows that we need a theory that privileges horses as the "right" cause of horse-representations while allowing that cows sometimes cause misrepresentations. The case is used to motivate asymmetric dependence and teleological accounts.

**The Swampman's Representations (Davidson)**
Davidson's swampman is created by chance from a perfect molecular replica of a human being. The swampman has no causal history—no learning period, no evolutionary history, no past encounters with the world. On causal theories of content, the swampman's mental representations have no content (or the wrong content) because they have not been calibrated by the right causal history. This challenges the intuition that functional duplicates have the same mental content and shows that causal theories are committed to historical individuation of content.

**Dretske's Thermometer Analogy**
Dretske uses the analogy of a thermometer: the mercury column carries information about temperature in an ideal thermometer (there is a perfect correlation). If we build a device to use this information—to classify temperatures by the mercury level—the device has a proto-intentional state (the mercury level "means" a given temperature). Extending this to brains: neural states carry information about environmental features and were recruited by learning/evolution to carry that information. The analogy is meant to show how genuine content can arise from physical causal relations, but critics argue thermometers still lack genuine intentionality.
