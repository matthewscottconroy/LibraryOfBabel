# Key Thinkers: Causal Theories of Mental Content

---

**Fred Dretske** (1932–2013)

Dretske pioneered the information-theoretic approach to mental content. His 1981 book argued that a mental state carries information about an external state of affairs — and thereby represents it — in the sense in which thermometers carry information about temperature. In *Explaining Behavior* (1988), he refined this into a learning-period account to handle misrepresentation: a state acquires its representational function during a period of reliable correlation, after which errors are possible. Dretske's framework influenced both causal and teleological successors.

*Key works:* *Knowledge and the Flow of Information* (1981); *Explaining Behavior* (1988); *Naturalizing the Mind* (1995)

---

**Jerry Fodor** (1935–2017)

Fodor's "asymmetric dependence" theory of content is the most developed purely causal account. It holds that a symbol means X when there is a causal law linking X to the symbol, and when the symbol's being caused by non-X things asymmetrically depends on the X-to-symbol law — errors happen because the basic X-representation mechanism exists, but not vice versa. Fodor intended this to naturalize content while preserving the systematicity and productivity of mental representation required by the language of thought hypothesis.

*Key works:* "A Theory of Content, I and II" (1990); *A Theory of Content and Other Essays* (1990); *Psychosemantics* (1987)

---

**Saul Kripke** (b. 1940)

While Kripke is not a philosopher of mind in the narrow sense, his causal theory of reference (for natural kind terms and proper names) directly influenced causal theories of mental content. He argued that names and natural kind terms refer not via descriptive content but via causal-historical chains anchored to initial baptisms. This causal picture of reference was adapted by Dretske, Fodor, and others into accounts of how mental representations acquire their content.

*Key works:* *Naming and Necessity* (1980); "A Puzzle about Belief" (1979)

---

**Hilary Putnam** (1926–2016)

Putnam's twin earth thought experiment established that mental content is not "in the head" — the meaning of natural kind terms is determined by causal-historical relations to the environment, not by internal cognitive states. Though Putnam drew externalist rather than narrowly causal conclusions, his argument is the most influential starting point for understanding why causal-historical factors must figure in content determination.

*Key works:* "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975); *Reason, Truth and History* (1981); *Representation and Reality* (1988)
