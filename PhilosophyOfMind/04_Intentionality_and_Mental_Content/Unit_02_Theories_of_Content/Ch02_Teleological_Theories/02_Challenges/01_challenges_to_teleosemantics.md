# Challenges to Teleological Theories of Content

Teleosemantics has attracted influential objections from various directions. While proponents have developed responses to many of these objections, the challenges have also clarified the limits of the teleosemantic approach and motivated alternatives.

**The "Swampman" Objection**

Donald Davidson's "Swampman" thought experiment challenges any theory that grounds content in causal history. Suppose a bolt of lightning strikes a swamp, and by cosmic coincidence, the atoms rearrange themselves into a perfect physical duplicate of Davidson. This Swampman would behave exactly like Davidson, have the same internal organization, and would seem to form the same beliefs and desires. But the Swampman has no relevant history — no evolutionary history, no developmental history.

Does the Swampman have mental representations with content? Millikan must say no (or say only that the Swampman has states that are identical to representations but have no content) — because it has no evolutionary history and therefore no proper functions.

Many philosophers find this counterintuitive. The Swampman seems to think and perceive and believe things, and it seems arbitrary to deny content to its states simply because of the unusual circumstances of its creation.

Millikan and defenders respond: the Swampman might go through the motions of representation without actually representing anything. The intuition that the Swampman has genuine mental content is itself a product of our tendency to attribute content on the basis of functional behavior — and that tendency is precisely what teleosemantics challenges.

**The "Color of Grass" Problem: What Did Evolution Select For?**

Teleosemantics requires specifying what the ancestral selection was for — what the representation was designed to indicate. But for many representations, this is genuinely unclear.

Suppose our visual system evolved to respond to the color green as a cue for vegetation. Does our "green" representation represent green things (the property of the objects) or vegetation (the biologically relevant feature) or chlorophyll (the physical cause)? The selection story can be told at multiple levels, and there is no obvious way to pick the right level.

This problem is especially acute for abstract representations. What exactly were our ancestral belief-forming mechanisms selected to track? The question may be unanswerable for many complex human representations.

**The "New Fact" Problem**

Teleosemantics grounds content in evolutionary history — what the system was designed to respond to in the ancestral environment. But what happens when a new kind of object, not present in the evolutionary environment, matches the conditions that used to reliably indicate something else?

Suppose the cat-detector was selected to respond to cats. Now cats go extinct but a new animal (call it a "scat") evolves that perfectly matches all the features cats had. Does the cat-detector now represent scats? Millikan's framework says no — it represents cats, because that is what it was selected for. But cats no longer exist; the representation is always empty.

This seems counterintuitive: surely a functioning perceptual system that responds to the actual features of the environment should have its content updated to match what it is actually tracking. Teleosemantics, by tying content to history rather than current function, seems insufficiently sensitive to present reality.

**The Frege Problem**

Like causal theories, teleosemantics faces challenges from Frege-style puzzles. If Hesperus = Phosphorus, and the "Hesperus-detector" and "Phosphorus-detector" were selected to track the same object (the planet Venus), then the two representations have the same content — which seems wrong.

Teleosemantics can appeal to different modes of presentation or different perceptual conditions under which the representations are produced. But specifying these modes of presentation in purely teleosemantic terms, without smuggling in intensional notions, is challenging.

**The Normativity Problem**

A more fundamental challenge: teleosemantics grounds normativity — what representations are supposed to represent — in evolutionary history. But evolutionary history is itself a natural, causal process. Does the derivation of normativity from natural history actually capture the kind of normativity involved in mental representation?

Some philosophers argue that there is a "is-ought gap" here: the fact that evolution selected a mechanism for a certain function does not by itself entail that the mechanism ought to be used in that way or that representations produced by it are correct in the normatively relevant sense. The biological "ought" — what the system was designed to do — may be different from the semantic "ought" — what the representation is supposed to represent.

This objection connects to broader debates about whether any naturalistic theory can fully capture intentional normativity, or whether normativity is irreducibly non-natural in a way that resists causal-historical explanation.
