# Further Reading: Teleological Theories of Mental Content

## Primary Sources

**Ruth Millikan, *Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories* (1984)** — Millikan's foundational work introducing teleosemantics. She argues that the content of mental representations is determined by the biological function for which they evolved — the conditions under which the cognitive systems that produce them historically succeeded in their biological purpose.

**Ruth Millikan, "Biosemantics" (1989)** — A concise statement of Millikan's view, arguing that the content of a representation is determined by what it was supposed to indicate — its "Normal conditions" of proper function — in the evolutionary history of the systems that produce and consume it.

**David Papineau, *Reality and Representation* (1987)** — Papineau defends a teleological account of mental content grounded in biological function, arguing that desires represent their satisfaction conditions because those conditions are what the desires were selected to produce.

## Secondary Literature

**Karen Neander, *A Mark of the Mental: In Defense of Informational Teleosemantics* (2017)** — Neander develops a sophisticated version of teleosemantics that integrates causal-informational and teleological considerations. She defends the view against objections concerning swampmen and misrepresentation.

**Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa, "Fodor on Teleosemantics" (2010)** — A critical discussion of the relationship between Fodor's causal-informational account and Millikan's teleosemantics, examining whether teleological considerations can resolve the disjunction problem.

**Jerry Fodor, "A Theory of Content, II: The Theory" (1990)** — Includes Fodor's critique of teleological theories, arguing they cannot account for intentional content independently of appeal to the very causal-informational relations he seeks to naturalize.

**Peter Godfrey-Smith, *Philosophy of Biology* (2014)** — Provides a clear introduction to theories of biological function (selected effects vs. causal role), essential background for evaluating teleosemantic claims about the role of evolutionary function in content determination.

**Fiona Cowie, "Why Isn't Anyone Afraid of Ruthenium?" (2009)** — A careful critical evaluation of teleosemantics, arguing that the account has difficulties with the content of beliefs about novel or abstract objects not present in the evolutionary environment.
