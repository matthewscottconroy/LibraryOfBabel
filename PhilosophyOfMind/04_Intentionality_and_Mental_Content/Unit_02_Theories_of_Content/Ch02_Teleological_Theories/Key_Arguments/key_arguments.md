# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Teleological Theories

## Key Arguments

**Millikan's Argument That Content Is Determined by Proper Function**
Ruth Millikan argues that the content of a mental representation is determined by its proper function—the function it has in virtue of the history of selection that produced it. A frog's fly-detector has the proper function of detecting flies because ancestors who had such detectors and successfully caught flies reproduced; the proper function is what the device was "designed" to do. Misrepresentation occurs when the device fails to fulfill its proper function—fires for a BB pellet instead of a fly. The appeal to proper function grounds content in natural history without invoking semantic notions in the definition.

**The Argument from Misrepresentation**
Teleological theories handle misrepresentation better than pure causal theories: a representation misrepresents when it fails to fulfill its proper function. On a pure causal account, if a "fly" representation fires in response to a BB pellet, the representation represents fly-or-BB-pellets (no misrepresentation). On the teleological account, the representation has the proper function of indicating flies (because it was selected for this function), so firing for a BB pellet is a malfunction—a genuine misrepresentation. This is taken to show that teleology is required for a naturalistic account of intentional error.

**Papineau's Biological Account of Mental Content**
David Papineau develops a biological teleosemantics: mental representations acquire their content through the natural selection of behaviors that used those representations. The content of "water" representation is water because responses to water were selected for—the ancestors whose representations tracked water survived better. This account is more explicitly evolutionary than Millikan's and ties content directly to adaptive behavior rather than to proper function in general. The biological account makes content partly historical and partly environmental.

**The Swampman Objection to Teleological Theories**
Davidson's swampman—a molecule-for-molecule replica of a person, created by chance—would have no evolutionary history and no personal learning history. Teleological theories therefore imply the swampman has no mental representations with content, or has different content from the original person. This conflicts with the intuition that the swampman, being internally identical to a normal person, would have the same mental states. The objection puts pressure on any account that makes content historical: it either bites the bullet (swampman has no genuine mental content) or revises its account of the relevant history.

**The Challenge from Derived Intentionality**
Teleological theories face the charge that they can at best account for biological or derived intentionality—the intentionality of systems selected by evolution—but not for the full range of human intentionality, which includes thoughts about abstract objects, impossible entities, and genuinely novel ideas for which no selection history exists. A theory that works for the frog's fly-detector may be inadequate for human concepts of justice, the square root of minus one, or quarks. Advocates respond by extending the relevant selection to cultural and individual learning histories.

## Core Concepts

**Proper Function**
A proper function (Millikan) is the function something has in virtue of the history of selection that produced it—what it was designed to do. Proper functions can be biological (the heart's proper function is to pump blood—not to make sounds—even though it does both) or cultural (a dollar bill's proper function is to serve as currency, in virtue of the social-institutional history that produced it). Mental representations have proper functions in virtue of the selection pressures (evolutionary and developmental) that shaped the systems that produce them. The normativity of representation (correctness vs. error) is grounded in proper function.

**Teleosemantics**
Teleosemantics is the approach to mental content that grounds semantic properties (content, misrepresentation) in teleological properties (proper functions, biological/learned functions). The main variants are Millikan's biosemantics, Papineau's biological theory, and Dretske's later indicator function theory. All share the core idea that the content of a representation is determined by what it was selected (by natural selection or learning) to indicate or track, rather than by what it currently or typically co-varies with. Teleosemantics is motivated by the need to account for misrepresentation without circularity.

**Biosemantics**
Millikan's biosemantics is the specific version of teleosemantics that grounds content in biological proper functions—the functions that natural selection directly produced. A mental representation has the content X if the function for which it was selected (in the evolutionary sense) is to co-vary with X. This makes content partly a historical matter: two organisms with identical current functional states but different evolutionary histories could have different semantic contents. Biosemantics has been influential in animal cognition research and in cognitive science debates about the origins of intentionality.

**Normal Conditions**
Teleological theories typically invoke "normal conditions" to handle the disjunction problem: a representation has content X because, under the conditions that obtained when the system was selected for, it co-varies with X. Under abnormal conditions (BB pellets, misleading environments), the representation fires incorrectly. The notion of normal conditions is crucial but contested: what counts as the relevant historical conditions? If the system was selected in a world with BB pellets, do they become part of the normal environment? This difficulty threatens to make the account circular or arbitrary.

**Derived vs. Intrinsic Intentionality (Searle)**
Searle distinguishes intrinsic intentionality (genuine aboutness, inherent in mental states) from derived intentionality (assigned by users—words and signs have content only because minds interpret them as having it). Teleological theories are criticized for providing only derived intentionality: they explain how a physical system came to have certain functional properties, but the normativity of representation (the distinction between correct and incorrect function) is still a matter of external attribution from the perspective of natural selection, not genuine self-directed aboutness. Defenders argue proper functions are objective, not attributed.

## Thought Experiments

**Millikan's Frog Tongue**
A frog's tongue snaps at small moving dark objects in its visual field. It was selected to snap at flies; flies are the proper objects of this behavior. If a BB pellet moves through its field, the frog snaps—but this is an error, not a case of the tongue "detecting BB pellets" because BB pellets are not what the device was selected to detect. The thought experiment establishes the teleological account: the frog misrepresents the BB pellet as a fly. Critics point out that the description "was selected to detect flies" is itself interpretive and depends on a human perspective on the frog's history.

**The Swampman's Semantic Poverty**
Imagine the swampman, just created, looking at a tree. His visual system produces the same neural activation as a normal human's "tree" representation. But on the teleological account, this state has no proper function (it has no selection history) and therefore no semantic content—there is nothing it represents. The swampman's "beliefs" and "perceptions" are not genuine mental states because they lack the right history. The thought experiment is taken to be either an unacceptable bullet to bite (surely the swampman does have experiences) or evidence that content individuation should not be purely historical.

**The Chameleon's Color**
A chameleon's skin changes color to match its background; this is typically a defensive function (camouflage). Now suppose we consider whether the chameleon's skin "represents" the background color. Teleologically, if the color change was selected for because it matched the background, it has the function of matching the background—a proto-representational proper function. This thought experiment tests the range of the teleological account: does it over-generate representational content (by applying to simple biological mechanisms that seem to lack genuine intentionality) or is it the right level of description for biological intentionality?
