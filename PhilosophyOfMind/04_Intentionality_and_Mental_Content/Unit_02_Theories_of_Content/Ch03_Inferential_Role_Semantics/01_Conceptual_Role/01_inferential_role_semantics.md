# Inferential Role Semantics

Inferential Role Semantics (IRS) — also called conceptual role semantics or functional role semantics — grounds the content of mental representations in their functional or inferential role within the system of mental states. A representation means what it means in virtue of its inferential connections to other representations: what it follows from, what follows from it, under what conditions it is appropriately applied, and how it interacts with other concepts.

**The Core Claim**

The meaning of a concept, on the IRS view, is constituted by its role in thought — specifically, its inferential role: what other concepts and beliefs it connects to, what inferences it licenses, what experiences justify its application. The concept BACHELOR means what it means because it is connected to UNMARRIED, MALE, ADULT, and because it licenses certain inferences (bachelors are unmarried; therefore, if John is a bachelor, John is unmarried) and not others.

This approach traces back to Sellars' "inferentialist" picture of meaning and has been developed in contemporary philosophy by philosophers including Ned Block (who calls his version "conceptual role semantics") and Robert Brandom (whose inferentialism is more focused on social-discursive practices than on mental representations per se).

**The Two-Factor Theory**

Ned Block distinguishes between "conceptual role" (the inferential relations of a representation within the cognitive system — its "narrow" content) and "referential role" (the relation of the representation to external objects — its "wide" content). He defends a two-factor theory of meaning that combines both.

The conceptual role factor accounts for the cognitive significance of a representation — why two representations with the same referent (Hesperus/Phosphorus) can differ in their epistemic properties and inferential connections. Hesperus and Phosphorus have the same referential role (both refer to Venus) but different conceptual roles (they are connected to different bodies of information and different inferential networks).

The referential role factor accounts for the aboutness of a representation — what in the world it is about. This factor is grounded in causal-informational connections to the world, as in causal theories.

The two-factor theory is an attempt to capture both the cognitive significance (narrow content) and the representational content (wide content) of mental representations without reducing one to the other.

**Holism and Its Consequences**

IRS is naturally holistic: the meaning of a concept is determined by its connections to other concepts, and changing those connections changes the meaning. This has a striking consequence: if every change in your belief system changes the meaning of your concepts, then you and I may never share exactly the same mental content, since we have different belief systems.

This holism threatens to make content attribution of limited usefulness: if concepts mean different things to different people (because they have different inferential roles), how can we ever truly share a thought? How can we meaningfully say that you and I both believe that water is wet, if your concept WATER has different inferential connections from mine?

There are two responses. One is to accept a degree of content-relativism: exact content sharing is an idealization; real content attribution is approximate. The other is to restrict meaning to a "core" inferential role — the analytically true inferences that constitute the concept — while treating the rest as epistemically contingent.

**Brandom's Social Inferentialism**

Robert Brandom extends inferentialism from mental representations to linguistic meaning and social practice. For Brandom, the meanings of concepts are constituted by the "game of giving and asking for reasons" — the social practices of offering inferences, challenging them, and vindicating them.

On Brandom's view, there is no sharp line between the conceptual and the empirical — between what is true by virtue of meaning and what is true by virtue of fact. Meaning is constituted by the whole inferential practice of a community, and this practice includes both analytic and synthetic connections.

Brandom's version connects philosophy of language and philosophy of mind to social philosophy and epistemology — making clear that conceptual meaning is not a property of individual mental representations but a feature of social-discursive practices.

**Advantages of IRS**

IRS explains the "cognitive significance" of content — why different representations of the same object can matter differently to rational agents. It gives a natural account of concept possession: to have a concept is to be able to use it inferentially, to license appropriate inferences and refuse inappropriate ones.

It accommodates the holistic character of meaning without the implausible atomism of some causal theories: no concept stands alone; each concept is embedded in a network.

It connects to practices of rational justification: the inferential connections of a concept determine what counts as evidence for beliefs involving it.

**Problems**

The holism of IRS threatens the possibility of content sharing and translation. If two minds with different belief systems cannot share the same content, translation and communication become mysterious.

IRS has difficulty accounting for the "wide" content of representations — their aboutness. Inferential roles are internal, cognitive relations; they do not obviously connect representations to the external world. IRS needs supplementation with a theory of reference to explain how representations represent external objects.

Whether IRS can be genuinely naturalistic — whether inferential roles can be characterized without appeal to normative notions (what inferences a system ought to draw) — is also questioned.
