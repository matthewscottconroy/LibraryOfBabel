# Problems and Prospects for Inferential Role Semantics

Despite its appeal, inferential role semantics faces a set of structural difficulties that have occupied philosophers since the view was first seriously developed. Understanding these difficulties — and the responses to them — illuminates the theoretical landscape of theories of mental content.

**The Holism Problem in Depth**

The central problem for IRS is semantic holism: if the meaning of a concept is constituted by its inferential role — its connections to other concepts — then any change in those connections changes the meaning. Two people who differ in any of their beliefs, no matter how peripheral, thereby differ in the meaning of at least some of their concepts.

This generates what is sometimes called the "radical translation problem" for mental content: it becomes unclear whether two people ever mean exactly the same thing by their words and thoughts. W.V.O. Quine drew this consequence and embraced it — he argued for the "indeterminacy of translation" and the impossibility of individuating propositional attitudes precisely.

But most philosophers of mind want to resist this conclusion. When I say "water is H₂O" and you say "water is H₂O," it seems clear that we mean the same thing and hold the same belief. If holism implies otherwise, there is something wrong with holism.

The standard response distinguishes "meaning-constituting" inferential connections from mere contingent connections. The inference "bachelors are unmarried" is part of what constitutes the concept BACHELOR; the inference "bachelors tend to be lonely" is a contingent belief that does not affect what BACHELOR means. If only the "core" inferential connections constitute meaning, the holism problem is mitigated.

But drawing the line between meaning-constituting and contingently believed connections is difficult — it requires something like the analytic/synthetic distinction, which Quine himself attacked in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." IRS thus faces a challenge in explaining what makes some inferential connections meaning-constituting and others merely contingent.

**The Extensional Adequacy Problem**

IRS grounds meaning in inferential roles. But two concepts could in principle have identical inferential roles while differing in their extension (the class of things they apply to). Conversely, two concepts could have the same extension while differing in their inferential roles.

If meaning is inferential role, concepts with the same inferential role have the same meaning — and concepts with different inferential roles have different meanings — even when their extension matches or diverges. This violates the standard constraint that meaning determines extension (that "water" and "H₂O" have the same extension is part of what makes them synonymous — or at least co-extensional).

IRS needs to explain how inferential roles constrain extension — how the internal, cognitive connections of a concept determine what in the world it applies to. Without such an explanation, IRS gives a purely internal account of meaning that loses the connection to the world.

**The Connection to the World**

This last point generalizes: IRS in its pure form is an "internalist" or "narrow" theory of content. It individuates mental contents by what is inside the head — by the inferential connections within the cognitive system. But as externalist arguments (Putnam's Twin Earth, Burge's social externalism) show, mental content is partly constituted by factors outside the head.

Most IRS theorists accept that the "wide" content of representations — their connection to external objects — is not captured by inferential roles alone. Hence the two-factor theories: inferential role gives narrow content (cognitive significance), causal-informational relations give wide content (reference).

But two-factor theories face their own problems: it is unclear how the two factors combine, whether the narrow factor is coherent on its own, and whether wide content can be fully explained in causal terms.

**Brandom's Normative Inferentialism and its Challenges**

Brandom's social inferentialism avoids some problems of individual-level IRS by locating inferential norms in social practices. But it faces its own challenges. If the norms governing inferences are constituted by social practices, then conceptual content is essentially social — available only to beings embedded in language-using communities. This seems to exclude non-linguistic animals and pre-linguistic infants from having concepts in the full sense, which many find too restrictive.

Brandom also needs to explain how social inferential norms connect to the world — how the game of giving and asking for reasons is anchored in non-inferential, perceptual engagement with the environment. His answer involves primitive "reliable differential responses" to perceptual input, but the connection between these responses and fully conceptual, inferentially articulated content is a point of ongoing discussion.

**The Prospects**

IRS continues to be developed as an account of the "narrow" or cognitive-significance dimension of mental content, often in combination with causal or teleological accounts of reference. The dual-factor strategy captures something genuine: there is both an internal, cognitive-significance component to meaning and an external, referential component, and neither reduces to the other. How to integrate these components into a unified theory of mental content remains one of the open problems in philosophy of mind.
