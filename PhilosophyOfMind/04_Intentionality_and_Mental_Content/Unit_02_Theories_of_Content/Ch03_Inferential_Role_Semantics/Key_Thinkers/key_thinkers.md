# Key Thinkers: Inferential Role Semantics

---

**Wilfrid Sellars** (1912–1989)

Sellars' critique of the "Myth of the Given" established the framework within which inferential role semantics developed. He argued that there is no bedrock of non-conceptually given mental content — all mental and epistemic items are embedded in a web of inferential relations. Meaning is constituted by role in what he called the "logical space of reasons," not by direct causal connection to the world. Sellars' influence on Brandom, McDowell, and contemporary inferentialism is profound.

*Key works:* "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" (1956); *Science, Perception and Reality* (1963); *Naturalism and Ontology* (1980)

---

**Ned Block** (b. 1942)

Block gave inferential role semantics its canonical formulation in contemporary philosophy of mind. He proposed "conceptual role semantics" as the account of narrow content — the component of mental content that supervenes on the physical states of the brain — which is then combined with a causal-informational component to yield wide content. Block's two-factor theory was an attempt to honor both the holistic, inferential constitution of content and the environmental anchoring provided by causal theories.

*Key works:* "Advertisement for a Semantics for Psychology" (1986); "Conceptual Role Semantics" (1998); *Consciousness, Function, and Representation* (2007)

---

**Robert Brandom** (b. 1950)

Brandom is the most ambitious contemporary inferentialist. In *Making It Explicit* (1994), he argued that all semantic content — in language and mind — is inferentially articulated: to possess a concept is to be committed to certain inferences and to be entitled to others. Drawing on Sellars, Hegel, and Wittgenstein, Brandom presents a "normative pragmatism" in which meaning is constituted by social, norm-governed inferential practice. His work is the most thorough anti-representationalist, anti-causal account of content available.

*Key works:* *Making It Explicit* (1994); *Articulating Reasons* (2000); *Between Saying and Doing* (2008)

---

**Christopher Peacocke** (b. 1950)

Peacocke defends a more moderate conceptual role theory, arguing that concepts have "possession conditions" — the inferential dispositions a thinker must have to count as possessing a given concept. This approach is "individualist" (content supervenes on internal states) but not holistic, because possession conditions are local and can be stated without reference to the thinker's entire belief system. Peacocke's account attempts to combine the insights of conceptual role theories with the need for determinate, stable content.

*Key works:* *A Study of Concepts* (1992); *Being Known* (1999); *The Realm of Reason* (2004)
