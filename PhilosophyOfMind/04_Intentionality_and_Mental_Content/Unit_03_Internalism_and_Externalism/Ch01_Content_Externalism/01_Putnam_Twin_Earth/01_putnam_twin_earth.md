# Putnam's Twin Earth and Semantic Externalism

Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment, published in "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), is one of the most influential arguments in twentieth-century philosophy of language and philosophy of mind. It argues that the meaning of at least some mental representations — natural kind concepts like WATER, GOLD, or TIGER — is not fully determined by what is "in the head." The environment plays an essential role in fixing semantic content.

**The Thought Experiment**

Imagine a planet, Twin Earth, that is exactly like Earth in almost every respect. The people on Twin Earth speak what sounds like English, have the same physical constitutions as Earth humans, and have the same experiences. There is, however, one difference: what flows in rivers on Twin Earth, fills glasses, and is called "water" by Twin Earthlings is not H₂O — it is a different substance, XYZ, that looks, smells, tastes, and behaves just like H₂O under normal conditions. The difference is not detectable by the senses.

Now consider two people: Oscar, an English speaker on Earth, and Twin Oscar, his physical duplicate on Twin Earth. When Oscar says "water," he refers to H₂O. When Twin Oscar says "water," he refers to XYZ. Their utterances have different truth conditions: "water is falling from the sky" is made true by different stuff in their respective mouths.

But Oscar and Twin Oscar are molecule-for-molecule identical. Everything that is "in Oscar's head" is the same as what is "in Twin Oscar's head." Their psychological states, as narrowly construed, are identical.

**The Moral: Meanings Ain't in the Head**

Putnam's conclusion: meanings are not in the head. If Oscar and Twin Oscar have exactly the same brain states, and if their word "water" has different meanings (refers to different substances), then the meaning of "water" cannot be fully constituted by their psychological states.

This is "semantic externalism" or "content externalism": the content of mental representations is partly constituted by the external environment, not solely by what is inside the individual's skin.

Putnam expressed this with the slogan: "Cut the pie any way you like, meanings just ain't in the head!"

**The Extension to Mental Content**

Putnam's original argument was primarily about the meaning of natural language terms. But philosophers of mind quickly extended it to mental content: if the meaning of "water" is partly determined by the environment, then the content of one's thoughts about water is also partly determined by the environment.

Oscar's thought "water is refreshing" is about H₂O; Twin Oscar's qualitatively identical thought is about XYZ. Their thoughts have different contents — different truth conditions — even though their psychological states are identical.

This means that mental content — the content of beliefs, desires, and other propositional attitudes — is not a purely psychological matter. It depends on the environment.

**Implications for Psychology**

This has striking implications for scientific psychology. If mental content is partly environmentally determined, then two physically identical people could have different mental content. This seems to undermine the idea that psychological laws — laws relating mental states to behavior — can be specified in terms of content.

If Oscar and Twin Oscar have different beliefs about water (one about H₂O, one about XYZ) but respond behaviorally the same way (both drink freely from rivers, both avoid heavily polluted-looking liquids), then behavioral psychology cannot distinguish them. But their states have different contents — they are about different things.

This motivates Fodor's defense of "narrow content" — a form of psychological state that is the same for Oscar and Twin Oscar, determined solely by what is in their heads, and that is the relevant basis for psychological explanation.

**Kripke's Complementary Argument**

Saul Kripke's arguments in *Naming and Necessity* (1972) complement Putnam's: natural kind terms and proper names are rigid designators — they refer to the same thing in every possible world, determined by the actual referent, not by any description the speaker associates with the term. The reference of "water" was fixed when people first dubbed the watery stuff they encountered; subsequent uses of the term refer to whatever that original stuff was.

Together, Kripke and Putnam established the view that natural kind terms have their reference fixed by causal history and the nature of the referent, not by the mental states of users. This grounds semantic externalism in philosophy of language as well as philosophy of mind.

**The Role of Experts**

Putnam also introduced the concept of the "division of linguistic labor": not every speaker needs to know the full chemical constitution of water to use "water" correctly. We defer to experts — chemists, in the case of water. The reference of "water" is fixed partly by the community of experts who know what the substance really is.

This social dimension of reference extends semantic externalism to a social domain: the content of my thoughts is partly constituted by the practices and knowledge of my linguistic community, not just by my individual psychological states. Burge developed this social dimension into an independent argument for externalism.
