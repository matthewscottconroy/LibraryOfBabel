# Burge's Social Externalism

Tyler Burge's social externalism extends the externalist argument beyond natural kinds to the contents of ordinary mental states. While Putnam's Twin Earth argument required a far-off hypothetical planet and exotic chemistry, Burge's argument works with entirely mundane cases involving ordinary misunderstanding of ordinary terms — and it arrives at the same externalist conclusion.

**The Arthritis Thought Experiment**

Burge's most influential example, from "Individualism and the Mind" (1979), concerns a person who has a partial misunderstanding of the word "arthritis." Alf believes he has arthritis in his thigh. In fact, arthritis is by definition a condition of the joints; it cannot affect muscle tissue. Alf's belief is false — not just about whether he has arthritis, but about what arthritis is.

Now consider a counterfactual situation: a community in which "arthritis" refers to a condition that can affect muscles as well as joints. In this community, what Alf calls "arthritis" (and what his doctors call "arthritis") includes thigh conditions. In this counterfactual community, Alf's belief "I have arthritis in my thigh" is true.

The key point: Alf in the actual world and Alf in the counterfactual world could have exactly the same physical states — the same brain activity, the same dispositions, the same qualitative experiences. What differs between them is their social environment: the community in which "arthritis" is used with different extension.

But their beliefs have different contents: actual Alf believes that he has a disease of the joints in his thigh (incorrectly); counterfactual Alf believes he has a broader condition that can affect muscles (correctly). Same physical states, different belief contents.

**The Social Dimension**

Burge's argument differs from Putnam's in an important way. Putnam's argument requires that the external environment contain the relevant kind (H₂O, XYZ), and the Twin Earth must differ in its chemistry. Burge's argument works within a single physical environment — the same world — by varying only the social-linguistic practices of the community.

The content of Alf's beliefs about arthritis is partly constituted by the social practices of the linguistic community in which he participates. He defers to the linguistic community for the extension of "arthritis," even if he doesn't know the full extension himself. This social deference means that his beliefs have the content fixed by the community's usage, not just by his individual psychological states.

**Individualism vs. Anti-Individualism**

Burge calls the view that mental content is fully determined by what is inside the individual "individualism" — and he argues that individualism is false. The content of mental states is partly constituted by external factors: for Putnam, environmental factors (the nature of the substances); for Burge, social factors (the practices of the linguistic community).

Anti-individualism (or "social externalism") holds that what you believe, want, and mean is not determined solely by what is going on inside you — it depends on your community and environment.

**Implications for Cognitive Science**

Burge pressed these results against what he called "inner content" theories in cognitive science. If mental content is anti-individualistic, then the contents of mental states in cognitive explanations are not fixed by the individual's psychology alone. Psychological generalizations — laws that connect mental states with inputs and outputs — must either be stated in terms of narrow, purely individualistic states (which may not correspond to any natural psychological kind) or must recognize that the objects of psychological explanation are individuals-in-environments.

This has consequences for how cognitive science is conceived. If psychological explanation must be anti-individualistic, then the "inside the skin" focus of much cognitive science — on algorithms, representations, and processing mechanisms — misses something essential about mental states.

**McKinsey's Paradox**

An influential objection to Burge's anti-individualism comes from Michael McKinsey's "McKinsey's paradox." The argument runs:

1. I can know a priori (by introspection) that I think that arthritis can affect joints.
2. If I think that arthritis can affect joints, then my community uses "arthritis" to refer to a joint disease. (This follows from Burge's anti-individualism.)
3. Therefore, I can know a priori that my community uses "arthritis" to refer to a joint disease.

But conclusion 3 seems wrong: I cannot know a priori facts about my linguistic community; these are empirical facts about the world. So something in the argument must be wrong.

The standard response challenges premise 1: we do not have the kind of a priori self-knowledge that the paradox assumes. Burge himself argues that authoritative self-knowledge and anti-individualism are compatible, but the detailed argument is complex and disputed. The paradox has generated an extensive literature on the relationship between self-knowledge and mental content.
