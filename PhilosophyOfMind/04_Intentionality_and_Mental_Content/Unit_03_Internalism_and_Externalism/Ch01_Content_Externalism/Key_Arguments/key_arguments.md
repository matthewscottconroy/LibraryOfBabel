# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Content Externalism

## Key Arguments

**Putnam's Twin Earth Argument**
Hilary Putnam argues that mental content is not "in the head." On Twin Earth, there is a liquid that is indistinguishable from water in every observable respect but has a different chemical composition (XYZ). A pre-1750 Earthling and their Twin Earth counterpart have identical brain states (neither knows about H₂O or XYZ), yet their terms "water" pick out different substances. Since their brain states are identical but their content differs, content is not determined by brain states alone—it is determined partly by features of the environment. This is the founding argument of semantic externalism.

**Burge's Arthritis Case**
Tyler Burge argues that the content of a subject's thoughts depends on the linguistic community's practices, not just on the subject's internal states. Suppose John has a number of true beliefs about arthritis but also falsely believes he has arthritis in his thigh (arthritis can only occur in joints). In a counterfactual community where "arthritis" applies to thigh ailments, John would have a different concept—different content—despite being in the same internal state. This "social externalism" extends Putnam's natural kind externalism to all concepts, including concepts for which there is no natural kind individuating them.

**The Boundary Argument**
Externalism implies that content supervenes on more than the skin of the organism: it includes the physical environment (Putnam) and the social-linguistic community (Burge). This "boundary" argument shifts the unit of content individuation from the individual brain to the individual-in-environment. Internalists respond by arguing that only internal states can explain behavior (since the agent acts on what's in the head), and that externalist content is not causally relevant in the right way.

**The Argument from Deference and Linguistic Division of Labor**
Putnam argues that ordinary speakers defer to experts for the content of natural kind terms: the content of "gold" in my speech is partly fixed by what metallurgists say gold is, not by my own limited ability to identify gold. This "linguistic division of labor" shows that individual speakers' mental contents can outrun their individual epistemic capacities. The content of "gold" is partly constituted by the social-institutional context that includes expert knowledge, not by any feature of the individual's psychology.

**The Incompatibility of Externalism and First-Person Authority**
A serious consequence of externalism is that subjects may not have infallible or even privileged access to the contents of their own mental states. On Burge's view, John doesn't know (without conceptual analysis of his community's practices) exactly what his "arthritis" concept refers to. This conflicts with the intuition that we have special authority about our own mental states. Burge responds by distinguishing the concept itself (about which there may be ignorance) from the fact that one is thinking with that concept (about which first-person authority remains).

## Core Concepts

**Semantic Externalism**
Semantic externalism holds that the meaning or content of mental and linguistic representations is determined partly by factors external to the individual—by the physical environment (Putnam) or by the social-linguistic community (Burge). Externalism contrasts with internalism, which holds that meaning is entirely determined by what is "in the head." Semantic externalism is now widely accepted in the philosophy of language and mind, though its implications for mental causation, self-knowledge, and cognitive science remain contested.

**Wide Content**
Wide content is content that is not determined solely by the subject's internal states—it can differ between functional duplicates in different environments or communities. Wide content is the content that is affected by Twin Earth: my "water" thought has different wide content than my Twin Earth counterpart's "water" thought, even though we are functionally identical. Wide content is semantically important for capturing what a thought is about in the actual world, but it is contested as a basis for explaining behavior (since behavior is driven by internal states, not by the distant environment).

**Narrow Content**
Narrow content is content that supervenes entirely on the subject's internal physical or functional states—the content that functional duplicates share. Internalists argue that narrow content is what matters for explaining behavior: the agent acts on the basis of what's in the head, not on the basis of distant environmental or social facts. Advocates of narrow content (Fodor, Jackson) propose various accounts of what narrow content is: Fodor's "two-factor" theory, Jackson's functional description, or the phenomenal intentionality thesis.

**Twin Earth**
Twin Earth is Putnam's hypothetical planet that is qualitatively indistinguishable from Earth except that its "water" is XYZ rather than H₂O. Pre-1750 Earthlings and their Twin Earth counterparts would have identical internal states (neither has the concepts of H₂O or XYZ) yet would mean different things by "water"—one refers to H₂O, the other to XYZ. Twin Earth is the canonical thought experiment for semantic externalism and has been used to generate twin-cases for virtually every natural kind concept and many others.

**Linguistic Division of Labor**
Putnam's linguistic division of labor holds that the content of natural kind terms in a community is partly fixed by experts who can identify the relevant natural kind, while ordinary speakers defer to these experts. I can use "gold" to refer to gold even if I cannot reliably distinguish gold from fool's gold, because my use is backed up by the community's experts. This shows that semantic content is a social phenomenon that cannot be individuated at the level of the individual speaker's psychology.

## Thought Experiments

**Twin Earth and "Water"**
Putnam asks us to imagine that pre-1750 Oscar on Earth and his physical duplicate "Oscar" on Twin Earth both use the word "water" for a liquid they experience identically. On Earth, water is H₂O; on Twin Earth, "water" is XYZ. Despite the twins being physically identical, their "water" refers to different substances. The thought experiment establishes that reference (and hence content) is determined by the physical environment, not by the individual's internal states. This motivates the slogan "meanings just ain't in the head."

**Burge's Arthritis Counterfactual**
John has many true beliefs about arthritis and also believes he has arthritis in his thigh. He is wrong—arthritis is a disease of the joints. Now imagine a counterfactual community in which "arthritis" applies to any inflammation, including of muscles. In that community, John's word "arthritis" refers to a different disease concept, and his thigh belief would be true. Yet John's intrinsic physical state is the same in both cases. The thought experiment shows that the content of a thought depends on the social-linguistic practices of one's community, not on internal states alone—social externalism.

**The Brain-in-a-Vat Revisited**
A brain-in-a-vat (BIV) is stimulated by a computer to have experiences identical to those of a normal person. On externalist accounts, the BIV's "water" thoughts do not refer to water but to the computer's stimulations—because those are the BIV's actual causal environment. This has the counterintuitive consequence that the BIV cannot truly think "I am a brain in a vat"—because its "water" refers to a different substance. Putnam uses this to argue for a self-refutation of the BIV hypothesis. The case shows how externalism has unexpected implications for global skepticism.
