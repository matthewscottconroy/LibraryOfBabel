# Narrow Content

Narrow content is the putative inner, environment-independent component of mental content — the component that is fully determined by the individual's internal psychological states, without contribution from the external environment. The concept is motivated by the internalist intuition that there must be something psychologically real about mental states that is determined by what is inside the head, independent of how the environment happens to be.

**The Motivation**

Putnam and Burge established that mental content is partly determined by the external environment. Oscar's and Twin Oscar's thoughts about water have different contents — different truth conditions — even though their internal psychological states are identical. This is "wide content" or "broad content."

But many philosophers of mind have the strong intuition that there must also be a sense in which Oscar's and Twin Oscar's mental states are the same — a sense that captures the cognitive significance of their thoughts, the inferential roles their states play, and the way their thoughts guide their behavior. If we want to explain why both Oscar and Twin Oscar drink from rivers, reach for glasses of clear liquid, and form similar expectations about rain, we seem to need a notion of content that is common to both.

This common component — the part of content that is the same for Oscar and Twin Oscar — is narrow content.

**Two-Dimensional Semantics and Narrow Content**

David Chalmers has developed a sophisticated framework for understanding narrow content using two-dimensional semantics. On his account, every expression (or mental representation) has two "intensions" — two functions from possible worlds to extensions.

The "primary intension" picks out what the expression applies to in any world considered as actual. For "water," the primary intension picks out whatever watery stuff is present in the relevant world — H₂O on Earth, XYZ on Twin Earth. The primary intension is the same for Oscar and Twin Oscar.

The "secondary intension" picks out what the expression applies to in other worlds, given that the actual referent has been fixed. For "water," once we know that water = H₂O (because that's what the actual watery stuff is), the secondary intension picks out H₂O in every possible world. This is narrow content in one sense: it is the functional sense in which "water" picks out whatever plays the water-role in the actual world.

**Fodor's Narrow Content**

Jerry Fodor defended narrow content as the psychologically relevant content for cognitive science. His argument: cognitive science explains behavior in terms of mental states — beliefs, desires, and their interactions. For these explanations to work, the relevant mental states must be the same for behaviorally equivalent people. If Oscar and Twin Oscar behave identically, their psychologically relevant states must be identical. Therefore, the content that is relevant to cognitive explanation must be narrow.

Fodor described narrow content as "content that is not individuated by the environment" — content determined solely by what is inside the organism. In his technical framework, narrow content is a function from contexts to truth conditions: given a particular environment (a context), the narrow content of a mental state determines its wide content (truth conditions) in that environment.

**Challenges to Narrow Content**

*The existence problem*: Is there any coherent notion of narrow content, or is narrow content merely an abstraction with no psychological reality? Some philosophers argue that the only genuinely contentful mental states are broad states — that talk of "narrow content" is only talk of what would be true if the environment were such-and-such, not about any actual property of mental states.

*The explanatory power problem*: If narrow content is just a function from contexts to wide content, does it have any explanatory power of its own? Wide content is what connects mental states to the world and makes them true or false. Narrow content, on some views, is just the pattern of wide-content assignments across contexts — a second-level abstraction rather than a substantive psychological property.

*The characterization problem*: How exactly should narrow content be specified? One option is in terms of functional or inferential role (what inferences the state licenses, independent of environmental factors). Another is in terms of phenomenal character (what the states feel like). Neither option is unproblematic. Functional role faces holism problems; phenomenal character is only relevant to conscious states and may not generalize.

*Burge's critique*: Burge argues that narrow content is not the right level for psychological explanation — that genuine psychological explanation requires the intentional, content-involving description of mental states, and that intentional descriptions are inherently anti-individualistic. Retreating to narrow content to preserve individualism sacrifices what makes psychological explanation genuinely intentional.

The debate between narrow and wide content is one of the most productive in the philosophy of mind, because it touches on fundamental questions about the proper methodology of cognitive science and the relationship between commonsense psychological explanation and scientific psychology.
