# Fodor's Naturalism and Asymmetric Dependence

Jerry Fodor's career-long project was to develop a scientifically respectable account of intentional mental states. His naturalistic theory of content — developed in *Psychosemantics* (1987) and *A Theory of Content* (1990) — attempts to specify, in purely causal-physical terms, what it is for a mental representation to have a particular content. The cornerstone of his mature theory is the "asymmetric dependence" account.

**Fodor's Desiderata for a Theory of Content**

Fodor set out what he took to be the desiderata any adequate theory of mental content must satisfy:

1. *Naturalism*: Content should be specified in non-intentional, non-semantic terms. A theory that explains intentionality in terms of more intentionality is viciously circular.

2. *Atomism*: Concepts should be able to be individuated one at a time, without being defined in terms of other concepts. Fodor resisted holism: the meaning of WATER should not depend on the meanings of all other concepts.

3. *Misrepresentation*: The theory must allow for misrepresentation — for false beliefs and incorrect perceptions.

4. *Narrow content*: For cognitive science, the psychologically relevant content must be narrow — fully determined by the individual's internal states.

**The Asymmetric Dependence Theory**

Fodor's theory of content — his answer to conditions 1-3 — is the asymmetric dependence theory, or "information-theoretic" theory of meaning. The core claim:

"X" means X if and only if:
(a) X causes "X" tokens
(b) Non-X causes of "X" tokens asymmetrically depend on the X → "X" law

Condition (b) handles misrepresentation: sometimes cows cause "horse" representations (when I misidentify a cow as a horse in bad lighting). The question is: why doesn't "horse" thereby mean horse-or-cow?

Fodor's answer: the non-X causes of "horse" representations (the cows) asymmetrically depend on the X causes (the horses). Specifically: if horses stopped causing "horse" representations, cows would stop causing them too (since the misidentification depends on the cow-looks-like-horse connection to the horse → "horse" law). But if cows stopped causing "horse" representations, horses would continue to do so. The cow → "horse" connection depends on the horse → "horse" connection, but not vice versa. This asymmetric dependence is what makes "horse" mean horse rather than horse-or-cow.

**The Relationship to LOT**

Asymmetric dependence theory was designed to complement the Language of Thought (LOT) hypothesis. On LOT, mental representations are the constituents of thought — sentences in Mentalese. Asymmetric dependence theory specifies what these mental symbols mean.

Together, LOT and asymmetric dependence theory provide a naturalistic picture of intentionality: mental representations are physical symbols (brain states) that have content in virtue of their causal relations to external kinds. Thought is the manipulation of these symbols according to syntax-respecting rules.

**Objections and Responses**

*The problem of "intended" causes*: The asymmetric dependence theory requires that the relevant causal law be the "intended" one — the one that is supposed to be triggered. But specifying which causal law is the "intended" one seems to require appealing to the very intentional notions that the theory is supposed to explain.

Fodor responds that the asymmetric dependence structure itself, without any appeal to intentions, picks out the relevant law. The asymmetry is a purely causal-logical relation, not an intentional one.

*The co-extension problem*: If "water" and "H₂O" are co-extensive, why do they mean different things? Both are caused by the same stuff. Fodor's response is that "water" and "H₂O" have different causal-informational relations to their referents: "water" is connected to superficial properties (clear, watery), while "H₂O" is connected to chemical properties. These different causal modes of connection give them different cognitive significance.

*Fodor and narrow content*: Fodor was committed to narrow content as the basis for cognitive science. But asymmetric dependence theory is a theory of wide content — content that is sensitive to the external environment. He needed a way to derive narrow content from his wide-content theory. His solution was to treat narrow content as a "function from environments to wide contents" — a way of characterizing what a state contributes to wide content as a function of context.

**Fodor's Legacy**

Whatever the ultimate fate of Fodor's specific proposals, his contributions to philosophy of mind are enormous. He forced clarity about the requirements for a naturalistic theory of content, articulated the LOT hypothesis with precision, and developed the asymmetric dependence theory as a serious attempt to solve the disjunction problem. The debates his work generated — about holism, narrow content, naturalism, and the prospects for cognitive science — continue to shape the field.
