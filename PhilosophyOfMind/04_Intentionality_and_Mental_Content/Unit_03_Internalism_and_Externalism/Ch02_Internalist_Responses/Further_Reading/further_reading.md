# Further Reading: Internalist Responses to Externalism

## Primary Sources

**Jerry Fodor, "Methodological Solipsism Considered as a Research Strategy in Cognitive Psychology" (1980)** — Fodor argues that cognitive psychology must proceed by attributing states with "narrow content" — content that supervenes on internal physical states — because cognitive processes are computational and run over internal representations, indifferent to external facts.

**Gabriel Segal, *A Slim Book about Narrow Content* (2000)** — Segal defends the notion of narrow content against externalist objections, arguing that there is a coherent sense in which mental content supervenes on internal physical states and that this notion is scientifically and explanatorily useful.

**Michael McKinsey, "Anti-Individualism and Privileged Access" (1991)** — McKinsey's "McKinsey paradox": if externalism is true and we have a priori access to our own thoughts, then we could know a priori facts about the external world. This apparent consequence is used to argue against externalism (or against privileged access).

## Secondary Literature

**Paul Boghossian, "Content and Self-Knowledge" (1989)** — Examines whether content externalism is compatible with ordinary self-knowledge, arguing that the combination generates a problematic entailment about empirical knowledge being available a priori.

**Anthony Brueckner, "What an Anti-Individualist Knows A Priori" (1992)** — Responds to the McKinsey paradox, arguing that the apparent a priori knowledge of external facts is not genuinely a priori because privileged access is itself dependent on the external world.

**Christopher Peacocke, *Sense and Content* (1983)** — Defends an account of perceptual content that is sensitive to internal phenomenal states rather than entirely externally determined, preserving a role for internal character in determining representational content.

**Brian Loar, "Phenomenal States" (1990)** — Defends a phenomenal concepts strategy according to which subjects have a special, internally constituted mode of presentation of their own mental states that does not require environmental or social anchoring.

**John Gibbons, *The Norm of Truth* (2013)** — Defends a form of internalism about justification and belief rationality while acknowledging the force of externalist arguments about semantic content, distinguishing the epistemic and semantic dimensions of the internalism/externalism debate.
