# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Internalist Responses

## Key Arguments

**The Autonomy Argument for Narrow Content**
Internalists argue that the causal explanation of behavior must appeal to intrinsic properties of the agent's internal states, not to extrinsic relations to distant environments or communities. Two agents in identical internal states would behave identically; since behavior is explained by internal states, the content relevant to behavioral explanation must supervene on internal states. This "autonomy argument" (associated with Fodor's two-factor theory) supports the positing of narrow content as the explanatorily relevant content for cognitive science.

**The Phenomenal Intentionality Thesis**
Horgan and Tienson, and independently Strawson, argue that phenomenal consciousness is itself intrinsically intentional: what an experience is about is determined by its phenomenal character, not by its external causal history or relational properties. If so, there is a kind of content—phenomenal intentional content—that is entirely internal and that cannot be "Twinned" away. The phenomenal intentionality thesis supports internalism by grounding content in phenomenal experience, which is typically held to supervene on internal states.

**The Causal Relevance Argument Against Wide Content**
Wide content is determined partly by external factors (distant chemical composition, community practices) that are causally isolated from the agent's brain at the time of action. If what the agent actually does is determined by internal states, then wide content—which can differ while internal states remain fixed—cannot be what explains or causes the behavior. Only narrow content, determined by internal processing architecture, is causally relevant in the way needed for cognitive explanation. This argument is used to motivate narrow content as the proper explanandum of cognitive science.

**The Descriptive Approach to Narrow Content**
Jackson's Canberra Plan approach proposes to define narrow content using functional descriptions: the narrow content of a belief is what someone believes when they have the internal state that typically plays the belief-in-water role in their cognitive economy. This functional description picks out H₂O on Earth and XYZ on Twin Earth, yielding different wide contents while the narrow content (the description) remains the same. This preserves internalism for the purpose of behavioral explanation while acknowledging that reference is fixed externally.

**The Conceptual Role Approach to Narrow Content**
Fodor's two-factor theory and Block's two-component approach propose that concepts have both a narrow component (inferential/conceptual role, determining what the concept contributes to reasoning) and a wide component (reference, fixed by the environment). The narrow component is entirely internal and is what cognitive science should study; the wide component is needed to connect content to truth-conditions. This approach preserves internalism for psychological explanation while accepting that semantic content (in the truth-theoretic sense) is externally fixed.

## Core Concepts

**Narrow Content**
Narrow content is the component of mental content that supervenes entirely on the subject's internal physical or functional states—the content that functional duplicates in different environments necessarily share. Internalists argue that narrow content is what is needed to explain behavior, since behavior is produced by internal states. The challenge for narrow content theorists is to specify what narrow content is in a way that makes it genuinely contentful (capable of being true or false) while remaining entirely internal. Proposals include functional descriptions, Fregean senses, and phenomenal character.

**Phenomenal Intentionality**
Phenomenal intentionality is the view that intentionality—the aboutness of mental states—is grounded in and constituted by phenomenal consciousness. On this view, what a thought or experience is about is determined by its phenomenal character, not by its causal-historical relations to the environment. Phenomenal intentional content is both internal (it supervenes on the subject's phenomenal states) and rich (it carries full representational content). Horgan, Tienson, and Loar defend phenomenal intentionality as a response to externalism; Strawson sees it as revealing that genuine intentionality is phenomenal through and through.

**The Two-Factor Theory**
Block's two-factor theory of content holds that mental representations have two kinds of content: conceptual or inferential role (the narrow component) and reference (the wide component). The inferential role determines what the concept contributes to reasoning independently of the environment; the reference is fixed by external relations to the world (via causal chains, natural kind membership, community practices). Two-factor theories attempt to capture both the epistemic-inferential dimension (explaining why knowing "water" includes knowing its inferential connections) and the truth-conditional dimension (explaining why "water" is true of H₂O, not XYZ) of content.

**Cognitive Science and Internalism**
Many cognitive scientists argue that theories in cognitive science—especially computational models of cognition—are individualist or internalist: they explain cognitive processing in terms of internal computational states, not in terms of wide relational content. The processes that implement reasoning, language parsing, and decision-making operate over internal symbols, not over the wide content those symbols pick out in the world. This gives cognitive science a methodological stake in internalism, and externalist critics must either show that wide content plays a role in cognitive explanation or accept that cognitive science is limited to a restricted domain.

**The Matching Problem**
Internalists face the "matching problem": they must explain how narrow content connects to wide content—how the same internal state picks out H₂O on Earth and XYZ on Twin Earth. The matching problem shows that narrow content cannot by itself fix reference; some further account of how internal states are hooked up to the world is needed. Solutions include Fodor's two-factor indexing, Jackson's functional descriptions, and Chalmers' two-dimensional semantics. Each proposal must avoid either collapsing narrow into wide content or leaving narrow content semantically vacuous.

## Thought Experiments

**Fodor's Duplicate Argument**
Imagine two agents in identical brain states—one on Earth, one on Twin Earth. They produce identical behaviors in response to identical (qualitative) experiences. Internalists argue that since the behavior is the same, the psychological explanation must be the same—and the explanatory content must therefore be narrow. If the wide content differs (H₂O vs. XYZ) but the behavior is identical, wide content cannot explain the behavior. The thought experiment is a direct application of the autonomy argument and is designed to show that cognitive science needs narrow, not wide, content.

**The Slow Switching Case (Burge)**
A subject is raised on Earth, uses "water" normally, then (without knowing it) is transported to Twin Earth and continues to use "water" for the XYZ-substance. According to externalism, the subject's "water" content gradually shifts from H₂O to XYZ—but the subject's internal states remain constant throughout. Internalists argue this is counterintuitive: the subject's mental state should be the same as before, since nothing relevant to them has changed. The slow-switching case tests whether content should track the individual's internal states or their external environment.

**The Migraine and the Arthritis Counterfactual**
Consider someone who in every relevant respect is like Burge's patient John: same internal state, same behavior, same patterns of inference. In one world, the community uses "arthritis" as we do; in another, it uses "arthritis" to include muscle inflammation. According to Burge, the same internal state constitutes different beliefs in the two worlds. Internalists argue that this is the wrong result: since the person's reasoning and action would be identical in both worlds, attributing different beliefs—with different contents—fails to explain the only thing that needs explaining.
