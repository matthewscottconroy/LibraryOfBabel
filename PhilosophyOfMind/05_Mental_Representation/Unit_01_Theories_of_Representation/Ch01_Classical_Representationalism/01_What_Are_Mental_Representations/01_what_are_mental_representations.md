# What Are Mental Representations?

Mental representations are internal states or structures that stand for, or represent, aspects of the world. The idea that the mind works by manipulating representations — internal stand-ins for external objects and properties — is one of the central assumptions of cognitive science and much of philosophy of mind. But what exactly mental representations are, whether they exist, and how they work are deep and contested questions.

**The Representational Theory of Mind**

The "representational theory of mind" (RTM) holds that mental processes are processes involving mental representations. To think, perceive, believe, and desire is to have mental states that contain representational content — states that are about something, that specify how things are (or how they should be).

RTM is the default assumption of most cognitive science. When cognitive scientists study memory, they study how representations of past events are encoded, stored, and retrieved. When they study language comprehension, they study how sentences are parsed and how linguistic representations are constructed and interpreted. When they study reasoning, they study how representations are combined and transformed according to inferential rules.

The central philosophical questions about RTM concern:

*What kind of things are mental representations?* Are they sentences in a language of thought? Pictures or images? Distributed activation patterns? Something else?

*How do mental representations have their content?* Why does a particular brain state represent a cat? This is the question of intentionality, addressed by the theories of content discussed in the previous chapter.

*How do mental representations interact?* How are they combined, transformed, and used in cognitive processes? This is largely a question for cognitive science and computational theory.

**The Language of Thought Hypothesis**

The most influential specific theory of mental representations is Fodor's Language of Thought (LOT) hypothesis. Fodor argues that mental representations are sentences in a formal language — "Mentalese" — that have compositional syntactic and semantic structure.

Sentences in Mentalese have parts (concepts) that combine according to syntactic rules to form complex representations. The semantic content of complex representations is determined compositionally: the meaning of the whole is determined by the meanings of the parts and the way they are combined.

The LOT hypothesis explains two structural features of thought:

*Productivity*: We can think infinitely many distinct thoughts. LOT explains this by the productivity of syntax: there are finitely many primitive concepts and finitely many syntactic rules, but they can be combined to generate infinitely many sentences.

*Systematicity*: If you can think the thought JOHN LOVES MARY, you can also think the thought MARY LOVES JOHN. This is not a coincidence: it follows from the fact that the thoughts share components (JOHN, MARY, LOVES) that can be recombined according to the same syntactic rules.

These structural features of thought provide powerful arguments for LOT, since any theory that cannot explain productivity and systematicity is incomplete.

**Connectionism and Distributed Representations**

Connectionism offers an alternative to LOT. In connectionist networks, information is represented not by discrete symbol structures but by patterns of activation across many interconnected nodes. There are no distinct symbols; instead, concepts and memories are distributed across the network as patterns of weights.

Connectionist networks are good at recognizing patterns, generalizing from experience, and handling noisy or ambiguous input. They are less clearly compositional: the representation of a complex whole is not straightforwardly constituted by the representations of its parts in the way LOT specifies.

The debate between LOT theorists and connectionists about the proper format of mental representations has been one of the most productive in cognitive science. The question is not just empirical (which kind of network does the brain actually use?) but partly conceptual (what kind of representation is required to explain the properties of thought?).

**Non-Representationalist Views**

Some philosophers and cognitive scientists deny that mental processes require representations in any robust sense. Hubert Dreyfus, drawing on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, argued that skilled human activity is characterized by "absorbed coping" that does not involve the manipulation of inner representations. When an expert chess player sees a good move, they do not first represent the board position and then apply rules; they respond directly to the affordances of the situation.

Dynamical systems theory (van Gelder) argues that some cognitive processes are better described by differential equations governing the evolution of a system's state over time, without any intermediate representations. The cognitive system's behavior is explained by its dynamics, not by the representations it manipulates.

These non-representationalist views are controversial, but they have productively challenged the default assumption that all cognition must be representational.
