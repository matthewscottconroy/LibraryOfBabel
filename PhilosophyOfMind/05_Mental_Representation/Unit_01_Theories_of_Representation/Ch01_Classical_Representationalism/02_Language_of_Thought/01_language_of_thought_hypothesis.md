# The Language of Thought Hypothesis

Jerry Fodor's Language of Thought (LOT) hypothesis is the most developed and defended account of the nature of mental representations. First presented in *The Language of Thought* (1975), and elaborated and defended throughout Fodor's career, LOT holds that thought is constituted by the manipulation of sentences in a formal language with compositional syntax and semantics.

**The Core Hypothesis**

Thought is computation over sentences in Mentalese. These "sentences" are physical structures in the brain that have both syntactic properties (formal properties that can be processed by physical mechanisms) and semantic properties (content that represents aspects of the world).

Mental processes — inference, planning, remembering, imagining — are processes in which sentences in Mentalese are combined, transformed, and stored according to rules that operate on their syntactic properties. The semantic validity of mental processes is explained by the syntactic structure of the representations involved: valid inferences are inferences where the syntactic processing preserves semantic truth.

**The Arguments for LOT**

*The Productivity Argument*: Human thought is productive — we can think infinitely many distinct thoughts. We have not thought the thought "there are exactly 17,342 grains of sand on my local beach right now" before, but we can think it now. This productivity requires a generative system: a finite set of primitive concepts combined by finitely many rules to generate an infinite set of complex thoughts. This is exactly what a formal language provides.

*The Systematicity Argument*: The capacity for thought comes in systematic packages. If you can think JOHN LOVES MARY, you can think MARY LOVES JOHN. If you can think IT IS RAINING AND IT IS COLD, you can think IT IS RAINING and IT IS COLD separately. These systematic relations are not coincidences; they require explanation.

Fodor argues that the best explanation of systematicity is that the thoughts that stand in systematic relations share component representations — the same concepts appearing in different configurations. This is the hallmark of a compositional formal language.

*The Compositionality of Content*: The content of complex thoughts is systematically determined by the content of simpler thoughts. The thought JOHN IS TALL AND MARY IS SHORT has the content it does because of the contents of JOHN IS TALL and MARY IS SHORT and the content of the logical connective AND. This compositional determination of content is explained by the compositional syntax of LOT.

*The Cognitive Science Argument*: Successful cognitive science explains cognition in computational terms, treating mental processes as computations over representations. The best-developed computational models assume that the representations involved have combinatorial structure — the structure of a formal language. LOT provides the theoretical foundation for this research program.

**The Format of Mentalese**

What does Mentalese look like? Fodor is somewhat reticent about the specific format, but the general picture involves:

*Primitive concepts*: Atomic symbols that refer to specific properties, kinds, and relations. These are the "lexical" items of Mentalese.

*Logical structure*: A compositional syntax that allows primitive concepts to be combined into complex structures, and that specifies the semantic contribution of logical operators (and, or, not, if-then, quantifiers).

*Phrase structure*: Mental representations have phrase structure analogous to sentences in natural language — subjects, predicates, nested clauses.

One question is whether the logical vocabulary of Mentalese is learned or innate. Fodor's nativist tendencies led him to suggest that most concepts — including seemingly learned concepts — are innate. This "concept nativism" is controversial; it implies that the concept AIRPLANE was in the mind prior to the invention of airplanes, waiting to be activated by the right stimulus.

**The Connectionist Challenge**

The most sustained challenge to LOT comes from connectionism. Connectionist networks represent information in distributed activation patterns rather than discrete symbol structures. They lack explicit, manipulable rules and symbols.

Fodor and Pylyshyn argued against connectionism in a famous 1988 paper: connectionist networks cannot explain the systematicity and productivity of thought unless they are "implementation-level" descriptions of LOT at a higher level of description. If connectionist networks implement LOT, then LOT is vindicated; if they replace LOT, they must explain systematicity by other means.

Connectionists have developed responses, arguing that systematicity is a contingent, learned property that need not be "hard-wired" into the architecture. The debate between LOT and connectionist approaches continues to be productive, driving both philosophical analysis and empirical research in cognitive science and neural networks.

**The Innateness of Primitive Concepts**

One of the most striking implications of LOT is Fodor's concept nativism: if complex concepts are constructed from primitive concepts by syntax, and if syntax does not add content, then all the content in complex concepts must be present in the primitives. If primitive concepts are learned by hypothesis formation and confirmation, then learning a new concept requires having the concept already available. This threatens a regress.

Fodor bites the bullet: most concepts are not learned from experience; they are triggered by experience. The experience of airplanes triggers the innate concept AIRPLANE; it does not teach it. This nativism is widely regarded as implausible, and has motivated some philosophers to reject the strong LOT picture in favor of a more developmental or ecological account of concept acquisition.
