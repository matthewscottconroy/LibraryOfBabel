# Further Reading: Classical Representationalism

## Primary Sources

**Jerry Fodor, *The Language of Thought* (1975)** — Fodor's central argument that thought requires a "language of thought" (Mentalese) — a system of mental symbols with combinatorial syntax and semantics. He argues that only if mental representations have constituent structure can cognitive processes be productive and systematic.

**Jerry Fodor, *Representations* (1981)** — A collection of essays developing the computational theory of mind and its implications, including discussions of intentionality, mental imagery, and the nature of mental representation.

**Zenon Pylyshyn, *Computation and Cognition* (1984)** — Pylyshyn argues that cognition requires "cognitive impenetrability" — that core perceptual and cognitive processes operate over discrete symbolic representations in a way that is encapsulated from general beliefs. Defends classical symbolic AI against connectionionist alternatives.

## Secondary Literature

**Stephen Stich, *From Folk Psychology to Cognitive Science* (1983)** — Argues that a scientific psychology will deal in syntactic computational states rather than content-bearing propositional attitudes, a "syntactic theory of mind" that challenges representationalism's commitment to semantically characterized mental states.

**William Ramsey, *Representation Reconsidered* (2007)** — A critical examination of whether any of the extant notions of mental representation employed in cognitive science — symbolic, sub-symbolic, or syntactic — satisfy a rigorous philosophical criterion. Argues that representation is used more loosely than theorists realize.

**Andy Clark and Annette Karmiloff-Smith, "The Cognizer's Innards" (1993)** — Examines the relationship between explicit symbolic representation and implicit connectionist representation in cognitive architecture, relevant for assessing the scope and limits of classical representationalism.

**David Marr, *Vision* (1982)** — Marr's three-level framework (computational, algorithmic, implementational) is among the most influential applications of the classical representational paradigm to a specific cognitive domain — early vision. Essential reading for understanding representationalism in cognitive neuroscience.
