# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Classical Representationalism

## Key Arguments

**Fodor's Argument for the Language of Thought**
Jerry Fodor argues that the productivity and systematicity of thought require that the mind have a compositional representational medium—a Language of Thought (LOT) in which complex thoughts are built from atomic mental symbols by combinatorial rules. Anyone who can think "John loves Mary" can think "Mary loves John"—this systematicity is not accidental but reflects a common, combinatorially structured representational medium. Without LOT, the systematicity of thought would be an inexplicable coincidence. LOT is Fodor's version of classical representationalism applied to cognition.

**The Representational Theory of Mind (RTM)**
The RTM holds that mental processes are causal processes over mental representations: thinking is the transformation of symbolic representations by computational processes that are sensitive to their formal (syntactic) properties. The cognitive revolution's central insight is that the same formal process can be physically realized in many ways—what matters is the representational level. RTM naturalizes mentality by showing how representations can have both causal properties (they can push other representations around) and semantic properties (they have content that can be true or false).

**The Argument from Structural Isomorphism**
An early argument for mental representations holds that perceptual states must represent their objects by sharing their structure: the representation of a scene mirrors the spatial arrangement of the scene's elements. This structural isomorphism between representation and represented is claimed to explain how representations can function as maps or models of the world. Critics argue that structural isomorphism faces the problem of multiple interpretations (the same structure can represent many different things) and that representation requires a system that interprets the structure, not just the structure itself.

**The Argument for Computation over Representations**
Classical AI and cognitive science argued that intelligent behavior requires computation: the application of formal rules to symbolic structures. Computation over representations explains how a finite system can produce an infinite range of behaviors (through combinatorial generation) and how it can exhibit rational, goal-directed behavior (through representation of goals and subgoals). This argument supports representationalism by making representations the vehicle of intelligent behavior and computation the process that exploits them.

## Core Concepts

**Mental Representation**
A mental representation is an internal state of a cognitive system that carries information about (or "stands for") something outside itself. Mental representations have both semantic properties (content, about which they can be correct or incorrect) and formal/syntactic properties (which determine how computational processes operate on them). The classical view holds that perception, thought, memory, and action all essentially involve mental representations of their respective objects. The nature of representation—whether it requires resemblance, causal covariance, functional role, or something else—is a central question of philosophy of mind.

**The Language of Thought (Mentalese)**
The Language of Thought (LOT) hypothesis holds that human thought is realized in a medium with the structure of a language: it has a vocabulary of mental symbols, a syntax governing their combination, and compositional semantics. Complex thoughts are built from atomic symbols by grammatical rules, just as complex sentences are built from words. LOT explains the productivity (infinitely many thoughts from finite symbols), systematicity (if you can think P, you can think related thoughts), and inferential coherence of thought. It is the foundation of Fodor's representationalist-computational theory of mind.

**Productivity**
Productivity is the property of a cognitive system whereby it can produce and understand an infinite number of distinct representations from a finite set of elements and combinatorial rules. The productivity of language (there are infinitely many sentences) is explained by the finite vocabulary and grammar of natural language. Fodor argues that thought is similarly productive (there are infinitely many thoughts) and that this productivity requires a compositional representational medium—LOT—in which thoughts are combinatorially generated.

**Systematicity**
Systematicity is the property that pairs of cognitive capacities are linked: anyone who can think a given thought can also think certain related thoughts. If you can think "John loves Mary," you can think "Mary loves John"—not by accident but because both thoughts share component representations (John, Mary, loves) that can be rearranged. Fodor and Pylyshyn argue that systematicity is best explained by a compositional representational system (LOT) and cannot be explained by purely associative or connectionist systems without an additional story about how components are encoded.

**Classical Computational Architecture**
Classical computational cognitive architecture (associated with Newell and Simon, Fodor, and early AI) holds that cognition is serial, explicit, symbol-based computation: the mind operates by applying explicit rules to discrete, type-distinct symbols stored in a symbolic memory. This architecture is contrasted with connectionist (neural network) architectures, which process information through patterns of activation across distributed networks. The debate between classical and connectionist architectures is partly about which better captures human cognitive architecture and partly about which is more explanatory.

## Thought Experiments

**Chomsky's Colorless Green Ideas**
Chomsky's sentence "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is grammatical but semantically anomalous—it has the syntactic structure of a well-formed sentence but violates semantic constraints. The sentence demonstrates that syntactic form and semantic content are dissociable: computations can operate on formal structure without regard to meaning. This thought experiment supports the classical representationalist claim that cognition is formal symbol manipulation—the system operates over representations in virtue of their syntactic properties, not their semantic content.

**Fodor's Thought Bubble Test**
Imagine being asked to think of a new thought that no one has ever thought before. You can do it easily—for instance, "The number of molecules in this room is divisible by the prime number 127." This thought is probably novel, yet you could produce it on demand by combining familiar concepts in a new way. The productivity of thought—demonstrated by this ability to generate novel thoughts—requires a combinatorial representational system. The thought bubble test is a pre-theoretical argument for LOT: thought is productive in the same way language is, and for the same structural reason.

**The Map and the Territory**
Consider a map of a city that accurately represents the streets, buildings, and spatial relations. The map represents the city through structural isomorphism: spatial relations in the map mirror spatial relations in the city. Now ask: what makes this relationship representational? The same physical structure could be used as wallpaper, a fire starter, or a source of origami paper—it would have the same spatial properties but no representational role. The thought experiment shows that representation requires more than isomorphism: it requires a system that uses the structure to guide behavior or cognition, raising the question of what a "using" system is.
