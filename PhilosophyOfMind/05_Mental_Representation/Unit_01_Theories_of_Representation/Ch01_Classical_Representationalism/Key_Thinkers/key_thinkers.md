# Key Thinkers: Classical Representationalism

---

**Jerry Fodor** (1935–2017)

Fodor is the architect of classical representationalism in cognitive science. His language of thought (LOT) hypothesis holds that thought occurs in a mental language — Mentalese — with discrete combinatorial symbols whose semantics is compositional. This structure explains the systematicity and productivity of thought: the ability to think any of an infinite range of propositions because complex thoughts are built from reused components. Fodor argued that the computational theory of mind (CTM) is the only scientifically tractable account of cognition, and defended LOT against connectionist and eliminativist challengers throughout his career.

*Key works:* *The Language of Thought* (1975); *Representations* (1981); *The Modularity of Mind* (1983); *LOT 2: The Language of Thought Revisited* (2008)

---

**Zenon Pylyshyn** (1937–2022)

Pylyshyn defended the classical symbolic paradigm in cognitive science against connectionist rivals. He argued that core cognitive processes operate over discrete syntactic structures and that such processes are "cognitively impenetrable" — they cannot be influenced by the subject's beliefs or intentions. His distinction between the computational level, the algorithmic level, and the neural implementation level (drawn from Marr) provided a framework for classical cognitive science and a basis for resisting assimilation of cognition to neural dynamics.

*Key works:* *Computation and Cognition* (1984); "Computation and Cognition: Issues in the Foundations of Cognitive Science" (1980); *Seeing and Visualizing* (2003)

---

**David Marr** (1945–1980)

Marr's analysis of vision — one of the most influential works in computational cognitive science — proceeds by distinguishing three levels: the computational level (what the system does and why), the algorithmic level (how it does it, what representations and processes are used), and the implementational level (how the algorithm is realized in neural hardware). His account of early vision as computing representations of intensity changes, edges, and surfaces exemplifies classical representationalism applied to perceptual processing.

*Key works:* *Vision* (1982); "Representing Visual Information" (1978, with Nishihara)

---

**Hilary Putnam** (1926–2016)

Putnam played a key role in establishing functionalism, which provides the theoretical context for classical representationalism. He argued that mental states are defined by their functional roles rather than by their physical constitution, which meant that the same computational-representational organization could be multiply realized. Putnam later became a prominent critic of computationalism, arguing in *Representation and Reality* that computational description is always observer-relative and cannot account for original intentionality.

*Key works:* "The Nature of Mental States" (1967); *Mind, Language and Reality* (1975); *Representation and Reality* (1988)
