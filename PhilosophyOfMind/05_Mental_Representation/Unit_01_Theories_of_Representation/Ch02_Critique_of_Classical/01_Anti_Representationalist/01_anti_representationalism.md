# Anti-Representationalism

Anti-representationalism challenges the core assumption of classical cognitive science: that cognition consists in the manipulation of internal representations. For anti-representationalists, the representational story gets something fundamentally wrong about how minds work — or at least about how skilled, embodied, embedded cognition works.

**Dreyfus's Phenomenological Critique**

Hubert Dreyfus developed the most philosophically sophisticated critique of classical AI and cognitive science from a phenomenological perspective. Drawing on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, Dreyfus argued that skilled human performance is characteristically non-representational.

The phenomenological starting point: when we act skillfully — driving, playing an instrument, engaging in conversation — we are not consulting internal representations and applying rules. We are directly responding to the situation, guided by practical know-how that is embodied in our skilled behavior rather than encoded in explicit representations.

Heidegger's notion of "ready-to-hand" equipment is key here: tools and things in our environment that we deal with skillfully are not represented as objects with properties; they are simply used, in a transparent, pre-reflective way. The hammer is not represented as having the property of being heavy; its weight is simply felt as I drive the nail. Only when the hammer breaks or I notice something unusual does explicit representation kick in.

Dreyfus argued that AI systems fail to achieve genuine intelligence partly because they try to represent the background of practical understanding that makes intelligent action possible. This background — what Dreyfus called "the background" or what Wittgenstein called the "bedrock" — cannot be fully made explicit; it is the pre-reflective, non-representational framework within which representation and explicit reasoning occur.

**The Frame Problem**

Dreyfus connected his critique to what AI researchers call the "frame problem": the difficulty of specifying what changes and what stays the same when an action is performed. When I move a glass from one side of the table to the other, the position of the glass changes, but the color of the walls, the temperature of the room, the number of people present — all stay the same. How does an AI system know what to update and what to leave unchanged?

For a representational system that explicitly represents everything, this requires updating an enormous database in a principled way — and no principled, general solution to the frame problem has been found. For an embodied, embedded agent, however, the frame problem does not arise in the same way: the agent is physically continuous with its environment, and most of the relevant stability is maintained automatically by the physics of the world, not by inference.

**Embodied and Enactive Cognition**

Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch developed an "enactive" approach to cognition that radicalized anti-representationalism. On their view, cognition is not the recovery of a pre-given world by means of inner representations; it is the "enactment" of a world through the organism's sensorimotor interaction with its environment.

There is no pre-given world to be represented; there is only the world as brought forth by the organism's particular mode of engagement. The visual world of a bee (structured around UV light and floral patterns) is fundamentally different from the visual world of a human — not because they represent the same world differently, but because their sensorimotor capacities enact different worlds.

On this view, the brain is not a representation-processing device; it is part of a larger sensorimotor system that couples the organism to its environment. Cognition happens in the loop between brain, body, and world — not inside the brain.

**Rodney Brooks and Behavior-Based Robotics**

From the engineering side, roboticist Rodney Brooks challenged the representational paradigm of AI through his behavior-based robotics program. Brooks argued that classical AI's approach — building rich world models and reasoning about them — was fundamentally misguided.

His "subsumption architecture" organized robot behavior into layers of simple behaviors (avoid obstacles, move toward light sources) that could be combined without any central world model or planner. The robot's "intelligence" emerged from the interaction between simple behaviors and the environment, without any explicit representation of the world.

Brooks' famous quip: "the world is its own best model." Instead of building an internal representation of the world, use the world itself as the representation — sense it directly and respond appropriately.

**What Anti-Representationalism Establishes**

Anti-representationalism has been most convincing as a critique of classical, symbol-based AI and cognitive science, particularly for domains of embodied, skilled performance. It has been less convincing as a general theory of cognition, since many cognitive tasks — mathematical reasoning, language comprehension, planning — seem to require something like explicit representation.

The productive response to anti-representationalism has been to develop "minimal" or "deflationary" notions of representation that can account for the flexible, environment-coupled character of cognition without requiring the rich, explicit, language-like representations of classical LOT.
