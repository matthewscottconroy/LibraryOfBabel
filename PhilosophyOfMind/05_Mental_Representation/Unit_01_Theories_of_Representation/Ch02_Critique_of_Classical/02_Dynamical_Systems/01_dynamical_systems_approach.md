# The Dynamical Systems Approach to Cognition

The dynamical systems approach to cognition challenges both classical, symbol-based models and many connectionist models by proposing that cognitive processes are best described as continuous dynamical systems — systems whose states evolve over time according to differential equations — rather than as sequences of discrete computational steps operating on representations.

**The Basic Approach**

A dynamical system is a system with a state space and rules governing how the state evolves over time. The state is a point in an n-dimensional state space (where n is the number of relevant variables); the dynamics specify how the state changes in time.

Dynamical systems theory provides a rich mathematical vocabulary for describing systems: attractors (stable states to which the system tends), bifurcations (qualitative changes in the system's behavior as parameters change), trajectories through state space, and so on.

Tim van Gelder argued in "What Might Cognition Be If Not Computation?" (1995) that cognition should be understood as the behavior of a dynamical system rather than as the manipulation of representations by a computational system. His famous example was the Watt governor — the centrifugal governor that regulates the speed of steam engines — which achieves its regulatory behavior through coupling between physical quantities (gear speed, valve position) rather than through any internal representation of the target speed.

**Cognition Without Representation?**

Van Gelder's radical claim was that some cognitive processes might be non-representational — that the state variables of the relevant dynamical systems need not be representations of anything. The coupling between the organism and its environment can achieve adaptive behavior without any internal encoding of information about the environment.

This is a version of anti-representationalism, but with more mathematical precision. Rather than arguing on phenomenological grounds that skilled behavior is non-representational, van Gelder argues on systems-theoretic grounds that the right description of some cognitive processes is in terms of state-space trajectories, not symbol manipulation.

**Embodied, Embedded, Extended Cognition**

The dynamical systems approach connects naturally to the extended mind thesis (Clark and Chalmers). If cognition is a dynamical process coupling organism and environment, it makes no principled sense to draw a sharp boundary at the skin. The cognitive system is the organism-environment coupling, not just what is inside the organism.

Similarly, the dynamical systems approach supports embodied cognition: the body is not merely a vehicle for carrying the brain around; it is an integral part of the cognitive system. The motor system, the proprioceptive system, and the environment are part of the dynamical system that constitutes cognition.

This gives a different picture of what "mental states" are: not discrete internal symbols but momentary positions in a high-dimensional state space, with the cognitive history of the organism encoded in the dynamical landscape (the shape of the attractors and trajectories) rather than in stored symbolic structures.

**The Representationalist Response**

Representationalists have pushed back on several fronts.

*The information processing argument*: Cognitive systems must process information about the environment in order to behave appropriately. Processing information requires representing information. The dynamical systems approach captures the dynamics of behavior but leaves out the question of why the behavior is appropriate — and appropriateness requires representation.

*The compositionality argument*: The productivity and systematicity of thought require a compositional representational system. Dynamical systems, described in terms of differential equations and state spaces, do not obviously have the right structure to explain compositionality.

*The "rescuing" move*: Perhaps the dynamical systems approach is correct at the level of implementation but not at the level of computational explanation. The Turing machine equivalent of a dynamical system might use representations at a higher level of description, even if the underlying implementation is a smooth dynamical system.

**Cognitive Science Applications**

The dynamical systems approach has been most productive in developmental psychology (Thelen and Smith's work on motor development), perception-action coupling (the study of how perception and action are continuously coupled in skilled performance), and language processing (connectionist models of lexical access and syntactic parsing that emphasize continuous, graded activation dynamics).

It has been less productive in domains where classical representational models have been most successful: formal reasoning, planning, problem-solving, and explicit language comprehension. The consensus view is that the dynamical systems approach captures important aspects of cognition — especially online, embodied, sensorimotor cognition — while classical representationalism captures important aspects of deliberate, reflective cognition.
