# Further Reading: Critique of Classical Representationalism

## Primary Sources

**Hubert Dreyfus, *What Computers Can't Do* (1972; revised as *What Computers Still Can't Do*, 1992)** — Dreyfus' sustained phenomenological critique of classical AI and symbolic representationalism, arguing that human cognition is not rule-governed manipulation of discrete symbols but is grounded in embodied, skilled, context-sensitive coping.

**Paul Churchland, "Eliminative Materialism and the Propositional Attitudes" (1981)** — Churchland argues that folk psychology — the framework of beliefs, desires, and propositional attitudes that classical representationalism formalized — is a radically false theory that will be replaced by mature neuroscience.

**Tim van Gelder, "What Might Cognition Be, If Not Computation?" (1995)** — Van Gelder proposes that cognition is better modeled as a dynamical system than as a computational process, using Watt's governor as a paradigm case to argue that representational computation is not necessary for intelligent behavior.

## Secondary Literature

**Andy Clark, *Being There: Putting Brain, Body, and World Together Again* (1997)** — Clark argues that classical representationalism misses the embodied, environmentally coupled character of cognition. Real cognitive systems off-load work onto the environment and exploit bodily dynamics rather than constructing rich internal models.

**Rodney Brooks, "Intelligence Without Representation" (1991)** — Brooks argues from robotics that intelligent behavior can be achieved through direct sensorimotor coupling without explicit internal representations, challenging the necessity of classical representational architecture.

**Mark Johnson, *The Body in the Mind* (1987)** — Drawing on cognitive linguistics, Johnson argues that the conceptual structures underlying thought are grounded in bodily experience (image schemas) rather than in arbitrary symbol systems, challenging the autonomy of classical representationalism.

**William Ramsey, *Representation Reconsidered* (2007)** — Argues that most uses of "representation" in cognitive science are either vacuous or fail to satisfy the conditions required by classical representationalism, raising a challenge from within the cognitive science tradition.
