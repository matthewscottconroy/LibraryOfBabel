# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Critique of Classical Representationalism

## Key Arguments

**Dreyfus's Phenomenological Critique**
Hubert Dreyfus argued from the 1960s onward that classical AI and representationalism misunderstand human cognition by treating it as explicit rule-following over symbolic representations. Human expertise is not rule-based but embodied skill: the expert chess player or surgeon acts from a background of practical know-how that is not stored as explicit representations and cannot be fully articulated as rules. Dreyfus drew on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty to argue that skilled action is absorbed, context-sensitive, and not mediated by any explicit representation of the situation.

**The Frame Problem**
The frame problem, first identified in formal AI, is the problem of specifying what does not change when an action is performed. Lifting a block changes its position but not the color of the walls, the number of planets, or the subject's social security number. A complete representational system would need to explicitly track all non-changes, which is computationally intractable. Dreyfus and other critics argued that the frame problem is not a technical detail but reveals that representationalist AI fundamentally misunderstands how human cognition deals with change—through a non-representational, situated sensitivity to relevance.

**The Relevance Problem**
Related to the frame problem, the relevance problem concerns how a cognitive system determines which features of its situation are relevant to a given task. A formal symbol system must check all its stored representations for relevance, but this check itself requires determining which representations are relevant—generating an infinite regress. Human cognition avoids this by being pre-reflectively sensitive to relevance through embodiment and practice, not through explicit search. This argument challenges classical representationalism's assumption that all cognition operates over explicit symbolic representations.

**The Dynamical Systems Critique**
Chemero, van Gelder, and others argue that cognition is better modeled by dynamical systems—systems characterized by coupled differential equations describing continuous change over time—than by discrete symbol manipulation. The Watt governor, which maintains a steam engine's speed through continuous mechanical feedback, is a paradigm case: it regulates behavior without representing target speed or current speed. Applied to cognition, this suggests that behavior can be generated by embodied dynamics without mental representation, undermining the classical assumption that representation is necessary for adaptive behavior.

**Enactivism: No Representation Needed**
Radical enactivists (Hutto and Myin) argue that basic forms of cognition—perception, action, motor control—do not involve any mental representations at all. The organism's engagement with the world is not mediated by internal models but constituted by sensorimotor patterns of interaction. Representation enters only with language and propositional thought. This challenges classical representationalism at its root by denying that the mind is a representational system and proposing that cognition is participation in the world rather than modeling of it.

## Core Concepts

**The Frame Problem**
The frame problem arises in any system that represents the world symbolically: when an action is performed, the system needs to update its representation of what has changed, but it also needs to know (and represent) what has not changed. In a world with many objects and properties, the list of non-changes is enormous, and any attempt to explicitly represent all of them leads to computational intractability. The problem illustrates the difficulty of implementing common-sense reasoning in formal systems and has been cited as a fundamental obstacle to strong AI.

**Situated Cognition**
Situated cognition is the view that cognitive processes are fundamentally shaped by, and inseparable from, the body and environment in which they occur. Rather than operating on context-free symbolic representations, cognition is responsive to and embedded in specific contexts. The desk is "workable" for writing, the doorknob is "graspable"—these affordances are perceived directly without the mediation of explicit representations. Situated cognition draws on Gibson's ecological psychology and Heidegger's phenomenology and is a precursor to 4E approaches.

**Affordances (Gibson)**
Affordances are the possibilities for action that an environment offers to an organism with specific body and motor capacities. A chair affords sitting for a human but not for a cat; a staircase affords climbing for a typical adult but not for an infant. Affordances are not properties of the environment alone or of the organism alone but of the organism-environment relation. Gibson argued that affordances are directly perceived—without the mediation of representations—challenging the classical picture of perception as constructing a world model.

**Dynamical Systems**
Dynamical systems theory models cognitive processes as continuous, coupled changes in state variables over time, governed by differential equations. On this view, behavior emerges from the dynamics of the organism-environment system rather than from the execution of a program over stored representations. The key advantage is that dynamical systems can model the continuous, context-sensitive, real-time aspects of behavior that classical symbolic systems struggle to capture. The challenge is to explain how higher cognition (language, reasoning) fits within this framework.

**Radical Enactivism**
Radical enactivism (Hutto and Myin's "Radical Enactive Cognition" or REC) holds that basic cognition—perception, action, sensorimotor skill—does not involve any mental content or representation. The organism's engagement with the world is content-free: there is interaction and response but no representation of states of affairs. Representational mental states (beliefs, desires with propositional content) arise only with language and cultural scaffolding. Radical enactivism challenges both classical computationalism and representational enactivism (Varela, Thompson, Rosch) which retain a minimal notion of representation.

## Thought Experiments

**Dreyfus's Novice-Expert Continuum**
Dreyfus describes the progression from novice to expert: a novice chess player follows explicit rules (if attacked, defend); an expert perceives the board as a gestalt and acts from an immediate, non-deliberate sense of what the situation calls for. The expert's skill cannot be captured as a set of explicit rules without losing something essential—the responsive, holistic, embodied character of expertise. The thought experiment challenges classical AI's assumption that expertise is stored explicit knowledge, suggesting that the representationalist framework is fundamentally inadequate for mature cognition.

**The Watt Governor (van Gelder)**
The Watt centrifugal governor regulates a steam engine's speed through a mechanical feedback loop: as the engine speeds up, spinning weights rise and close a valve, reducing steam supply. It maintains speed without representing "target speed" or "current speed" anywhere in its mechanism—it is a physical instantiation of coupled dynamics. Van Gelder argues that the Watt governor is a better model of cognitive regulation than a computer program: it achieves sophisticated, adaptive behavior through continuous dynamics, not through symbolic computation over representations.

**The Ant's Path (Dennett on Minsky)**
Dennett describes a thought experiment where an ant traces a complex path on a beach that looks like a caricature of Winston Churchill. Did the ant represent Churchill? Obviously not—the path is the product of a simple tropism, not a complex representation. The thought experiment generalizes: can we be confident that any system that produces complex, adaptive outputs does so by representing anything? The case motivates skepticism about attributing rich representations to systems based purely on behavioral complexity, supporting the enactivist suspicion of over-intellectualizing cognition.
