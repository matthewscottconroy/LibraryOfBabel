# Depictive vs. Propositional Representations

The distinction between depictive and propositional representations is fundamental to the imagery debate, but it is also philosophically important in its own right. Understanding the difference between these two formats — and why the difference matters for cognition — illuminates the nature of mental representation more broadly.

**What Makes a Representation Depictive**

A depictive representation represents by resemblance or structural isomorphism: the representational properties of the picture correspond to the represented properties of the scene in a systematic, analog way. A picture of a square has a square shape; a map of a city preserves the relative distances and directions between locations; a painting of a red apple has reddish regions where the apple's red surface is.

Kosslyn characterized depictive representations by two key properties:

*Spatial structure*: The representation is organized spatially, with parts of the representation corresponding to parts of what is represented. The upper-left region of a visual image represents the upper-left region of the represented scene.

*Intrinsic determination of represented properties*: In a depictive representation, the represented properties are determined by the intrinsic structure of the representation, not by any interpretive conventions. A picture of a square represents squareness by being square (having four equal sides at right angles), not by being labeled "square."

These properties distinguish depictive from propositional representations.

**What Makes a Representation Propositional**

A propositional representation represents by encoding a proposition — a content that is either true or false. It has semantic content that is not determined by its intrinsic structure but by conventional assignment of meaning.

Propositional representations are:
- Discrete: they consist of distinct symbols that either do or do not apply
- Arbitrary: the connection between symbol and referent is conventional, not structural
- Compositionally structured: complex representations are built from simpler parts according to explicit combinatorial rules
- Not intrinsically spatial: they can represent spatial properties by describing them, without themselves having spatial organization

**The Ambiguity About "Depictive"**

One subtle point in the debate is that "depictive" does not mean that a representation literally looks like what it represents. A heat map is depictive — it uses color to represent temperature variations in a spatially organized way — but it doesn't "look like" temperature.

The key feature of depictive representations is the analog preservation of the relevant structure. What matters for mental imagery is whether the representation preserves metric spatial structure — whether the relative positions, distances, and orientations of parts of the represented scene are reflected in the relative positions, distances, and orientations of parts of the representation.

**The Conceptual Analysis**

Ned Block introduced a useful distinction between "pictorial" representations (which encode information by analogy with perceptual experience) and "description" representations (which encode information by conventional rule). This distinction crosscuts the format of the representation: pictorial representations can be realized in non-pictorial media (a musical piece can depict a scene), and descriptions can be conveyed pictorially.

What is at issue in the imagery debate is not whether mental images are literally pictures but whether they encode spatial information in an analog, structure-preserving way or in a discrete, propositional way.

**Why the Format Matters**

The format of mental representations matters for cognition in several ways:

*Cognitive affordances*: Depictive representations support certain kinds of reasoning more naturally. Spatial reasoning, visualization, mental rotation — these seem to be supported by representations that directly encode spatial structure. Propositional representations support logical inference naturally.

*Cognitive costs*: Different formats have different processing costs. Depictive representations may require more "space" in working memory (since they encode continuous, metric information) but support faster spatial comparisons. Propositional representations may be more compact but require more inferential work to extract spatial information.

*Cognitive architecture*: If some representations are depictive, this implies a cognitive architecture that includes an analog format — not just a single universal propositional language. This complicates the LOT picture and suggests that cognition involves multiple representational formats, each suited to different cognitive tasks.

**Imagery and Perception**

One of the most important findings of the imagery debates is the overlap between imagery and perception at the neural level. If early visual areas are recruited during mental imagery, this suggests that the "depictive" quality of imagery is not just a metaphor or a feature of its phenomenology — it reflects the use of genuinely pictorial (in the relevant sense) neural structures.

This finding has implications for understanding perception itself: if the same neural machinery is used for both perception and imagery, the line between "perceiving" and "imagining" becomes less sharp. Perception may itself be partially constructive and imaginative, not purely receptive.
