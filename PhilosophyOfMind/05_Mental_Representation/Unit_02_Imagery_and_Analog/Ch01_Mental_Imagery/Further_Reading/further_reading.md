# Further Reading: Mental Imagery

## Primary Sources

**Stephen Kosslyn, *Image and Brain: The Resolution of the Imagery Debate* (1994)** — Kosslyn presents the definitive depictive (picture-like) account of mental imagery, arguing from behavioral chronometric data and neuroimaging that visual imagery involves quasi-pictorial representations in early visual cortex.

**Zenon Pylyshyn, *Seeing and Visualizing: It's Not What You Think* (2003)** — Pylyshyn defends the propositional/descriptionist view that mental imagery involves tacit knowledge encoded propositionally rather than picture-like representations. He argues that experiments on mental rotation and scanning can be explained without positing spatial displays.

**Roger Shepard and Jacqueline Metzler, "Mental Rotation of Three-Dimensional Objects" (1971)** — The landmark paper demonstrating that reaction time for judging two 3D shapes as identical rotates linearly with the angle of rotation, suggesting that mental rotation involves genuinely analog, spatial processing.

## Secondary Literature

**Ned Block (ed.), *Imagery* (1981)** — An important collection including papers by Kosslyn, Pylyshyn, Dennett, and others that defined the imagery debate. Dennett's "Two Approaches to Mental Images" offers a deflationary alternative to both depictivism and descriptionism.

**Bénédicte de Gelder, "Is Mental Imagery Really Visual?" (1986)** — Questions whether neuropsychological evidence supports the equation of visual imagery and visual perception, examining patients with cortical blindness and aphantasia-relevant conditions.

**Adam Zeman, Michaela Dewar, and Sergio Della Sala, "Lives Without Imagery — Congenital Aphantasia" (2015)** — The paper that introduced the term "aphantasia" for the condition of having no voluntary visual mental imagery, raising questions about whether imagery is necessary for cognition and what role it plays.

**Fabian Grabenhorst and Edmund Rolls, "Value, Pleasure and Choice in the Ventral Prefrontal Cortex" (2011)** — Contextualizes imagery in a wider theory of multimodal representation, relevant for understanding how imagery connects to memory, planning, and reward.
