# Key Thinkers: Mental Imagery

---

**Stephen Kosslyn** (b. 1948)

Kosslyn is the principal defender of the depictive (picture-like) theory of mental imagery. Through decades of behavioral and neuroimaging research, he argued that visual mental images are represented in a medium with spatial properties analogous to a visual display, and that early visual cortex (area V1) is reactivated during imagery. His chronometric experiments on mental scanning (the time to scan across a mental image increases with distance) and mental rotation support the claim that imagery involves genuinely spatial, analog representation.

*Key works:* *Image and Brain* (1994); *Ghosts in the Mind's Machine* (1983); *The Case for Mental Imagery* (2006, with Thompson and Ganis)

---

**Zenon Pylyshyn** (1937–2022)

Pylyshyn is the leading critic of depictive theories of imagery. He argues that apparent spatial properties of imagery can be explained without positing any picture-like medium: subjects tacitly know what images would look like and simulate spatial properties accordingly. He coined the term "tacit knowledge" to describe this propositional representation of image structure, and argued that mental rotation and scanning experiments are compatible with his descriptionist account. His critiques forced repeated refinements of the depictivist position.

*Key works:* *Seeing and Visualizing* (2003); "What the Mind's Eye Tells the Mind's Brain" (1973); *Computation and Cognition* (1984)

---

**Roger Shepard** (b. 1929)

Shepard's mental rotation experiments (with Metzler, 1971) provided the first and most striking evidence for analog mental representation. The linear relationship between rotation angle and reaction time suggested that mental rotation is a genuinely continuous, spatial process — not a series of discrete symbolic operations. Shepard later developed a broader theory of "second-order isomorphism" between mental representations and the world, arguing that the structure of mental space reflects the structure of physical space through evolutionary tuning.

*Key works:* "Mental Rotation of Three-Dimensional Objects" (1971, with Metzler); "Second-Order Isomorphism of Internal Representations" (1975); "The Mental Image" (1978)

---

**Daniel Dennett** (b. 1942)

Dennett argued against both depictive and strict propositional theories of imagery in "Two Approaches to Mental Images" (1979), proposing instead that imagery reports are data about functional states that need not involve any particular representational medium. He introduced the term "heterophenomenology" — treating imagery reports as evidence about functional states without assuming that the reported structures (pictures, colors) are literally present in the mind. His deflationary approach challenged both sides of the imagery debate.

*Key works:* "Two Approaches to Mental Images" (1979); *Content and Consciousness* (1969); *Consciousness Explained* (1991)
