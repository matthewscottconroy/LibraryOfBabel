# Cognitive Maps

The concept of a cognitive map — a mental representation of the spatial structure of an environment — was introduced by Edward Tolman in the 1940s and has since become one of the most productive concepts in cognitive science and neuroscience. It illustrates how mental representations can be analog and spatial rather than propositional, and has been central to debates about the nature of spatial cognition.

**Tolman's Original Experiments**

Edward Tolman challenged the behaviorist orthodoxy of his time with a series of ingenious maze experiments on rats. Classical behaviorism held that animals learn stimulus-response associations: in a maze, the rat learns a chain of responses (turn left here, turn right there) triggered by stimuli at each choice point.

Tolman showed that rats acquire something more flexible than stimulus-response chains. In his "latent learning" experiments, rats that explored a maze without reward performed much better once food was introduced than rats that had not explored the maze. This showed that the rats had learned something from mere exploration — a representation of the maze's spatial structure — even without any reinforcement.

More strikingly, in his "place learning" versus "response learning" experiments, Tolman showed that rats learn the place of a goal rather than the response required to reach it. If the maze is modified after training, rats take the shortest new path to the goal rather than the trained response — suggesting they have a spatial representation of the maze, not just a response habit.

Tolman proposed that rats — and by extension, all animals capable of spatial navigation — form "cognitive maps": internal representations of the spatial structure of the environment that allow flexible, purposive navigation.

**O'Keefe and Nadel: Place Cells**

The neurobiological basis of cognitive maps was discovered by John O'Keefe and Lynn Nadel, whose 1978 book *The Hippocampus as a Cognitive Map* proposed that the hippocampus is the neural substrate of spatial mapping.

O'Keefe and colleagues discovered "place cells" — hippocampal neurons that fire selectively when the animal occupies a particular location in the environment. Different place cells fire at different locations, collectively forming a neural representation of the environment's spatial structure.

In 2005, Edvard Moser and colleagues discovered "grid cells" in the entorhinal cortex — neurons whose firing fields form a regular hexagonal grid across the entire environment, providing a metric coordinate system for spatial representation.

The discovery of place cells and grid cells earned O'Keefe and the Mosers the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, vindicating the cognitive map hypothesis at the neural level.

**Cognitive Maps Beyond Spatial Navigation**

The hippocampus and its associated structures do more than spatial navigation: they are central to episodic memory (memory for specific past events) and to many forms of relational memory. This has led to the hypothesis that the "cognitive map" framework generalizes beyond space.

Eleanor Maguire and colleagues showed that London taxi drivers, who must memorize the layouts of thousands of streets, have enlarged posterior hippocampi compared to non-taxi drivers — and that the size of the enlargement correlates with years of experience. This provided striking human evidence for hippocampal involvement in spatial representation.

More recently, researchers have proposed that the hippocampus implements a general "relational mapping" function — not just mapping physical space but mapping conceptual and social spaces. "Concept cells" that respond to specific people or concepts have been found in human hippocampus and related areas, suggesting that cognitive maps may be a general mechanism for organizing experience.

**Cognitive Maps and the Propositional/Depictive Debate**

Cognitive maps are natural candidates for depictive, analog representations: they represent spatial structure by preserving the metric properties of the environment. The hippocampal place cell system provides a concrete neural implementation of something that genuinely looks like a map — a set of representations organized according to the spatial structure they represent.

But cognitive maps are not literally pictures. They are implemented in a pattern of neural activations rather than in a spatial array of pixels. What makes them "map-like" is the functional analog relationship between the representation and the spatial environment, not a literal resemblance.

This connects to broader debates about the representational format of cognition: cognitive maps illustrate that analog, structure-preserving representations are not merely hypothetical — they have clear neural realizations in the hippocampal-entorhinal system.
