# Simulation and Emulation in Cognition

Simulation and emulation are concepts that have become important in cognitive science and philosophy of mind for understanding both social cognition (our understanding of other minds) and the general question of how the brain models the world. The simulation theory of mind reading, the emulation theory of motor control, and predictive processing accounts all invoke something like "internal models" that the brain uses to represent and anticipate the world.

**Simulation Theory of Mind Reading**

The simulation theory of mind reading, associated with Robert Gordon and Alvin Goldman, holds that we understand other people's mental states by simulating them — by using our own cognitive machinery to replicate what the other person's mental processes would be like.

When I try to understand why my friend is upset, I do not apply a theory about the relationship between circumstances and emotional states. I put myself in their shoes — I use my own emotional processing system to simulate what I would feel in their circumstances, and I attribute that feeling to my friend.

Goldman distinguished "low-level simulation" (automatic, mirror-system-based resonance with others' actions and emotions) from "high-level simulation" (deliberate, introspective use of one's own mental resources to project into another's perspective).

The discovery of mirror neurons — cells that fire both when performing an action and when observing the same action — was initially taken as neural evidence for low-level simulation. The mirror system was seen as implementing automatic "resonance" with others' actions that forms the basis of social understanding.

This interpretation has been contested. Many neuroscientists now hold that mirror neurons play a role in action understanding but that their role in mind reading is more limited than initially thought. The relationship between mirror systems, simulation, and theory of mind remains actively debated.

**Emulation Theory of Motor Control**

In motor control, an "emulator" or "internal model" is a neural system that predicts the sensory consequences of motor commands. When you reach for a cup, your motor system sends commands to your arm muscles and simultaneously sends an "efference copy" of those commands to an emulator, which predicts what the sensory feedback from the movement should be.

This predicted feedback — rather than, or in addition to, the actual sensory feedback — is used to guide the movement in real time. Because neural transmission of actual sensory feedback takes time (tens of milliseconds), relying solely on actual feedback would make rapid, precise movement impossible. The emulator compensates by providing predicted feedback that can be used without delay.

Emulators are forward models: given a motor command and the current state, they predict the next state. They represent the body's dynamics — how it moves in response to commands — in an internal model that can be run in parallel with the actual movement.

**The Role of Simulation in Cognition More Broadly**

The simulation and emulation concepts have been generalized beyond social cognition and motor control. Lawrence Barsalou's "perceptual symbol systems" theory proposes that all cognition involves the simulation of perceptual and motor states. When we think about "red," we partially activate the visual states we would have when seeing red. When we think about "grasping," we partially activate the motor states we would have when grasping.

This "grounded cognition" view connects mental representation to bodily simulation: concepts are not arbitrary symbols but patterns of neural activation that simulate sensory and motor experience.

Predictive processing frameworks make a similar move: the brain's generative models continuously simulate expected sensory input, and cognition is the ongoing process of running and updating these internal simulations.

**Simulation and the Hard Problem**

The simulation framework has implications for the hard problem of consciousness. If mental representations are simulations of sensory experience, and if the hard problem concerns the phenomenal character of that experience, then phenomenal consciousness is intrinsically connected to the simulation process.

Some philosophers have argued that this connection is explanatory: the phenomenal character of experience arises from the brain's ongoing simulation of the organism's sensory situation. Anil Seth's account of consciousness as "controlled hallucination" is a version of this view.

Others argue that simulation merely relocates the hard problem: we still need to explain why the simulation is accompanied by any phenomenal character at all, rather than running "in the dark."
