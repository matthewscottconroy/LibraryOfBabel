# Further Reading: Maps, Models, and Simulations

## Primary Sources

**Edward Tolman, "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men" (1948)** — Tolman's landmark paper introducing the concept of the "cognitive map" — an internal, map-like representation of spatial environments used by rats (and humans) in flexible navigation. Challenged stimulus-response behaviorism and initiated the study of spatial cognition.

**Kenneth Craik, *The Nature of Explanation* (1943)** — Craik proposed that cognition involves constructing internal "models" of the world that simulate causal processes. This "model-based" view of mental representation anticipates later developments in mental simulation, planning, and analogical reasoning.

**Philip Johnson-Laird, *Mental Models* (1983)** — Johnson-Laird argues that reasoning proceeds not through formal logic but through constructing and inspecting mental models — internal analog representations of possibilities. Hugely influential in the psychology of reasoning and the theory of mental representation.

## Secondary Literature

**John O'Keefe and Lynn Nadel, *The Hippocampus as a Cognitive Map* (1978)** — Presents evidence for place cells and a neural basis for Tolman's cognitive map in hippocampal neurons. Essential background for the neuroscience of spatial cognition and map-like mental representations.

**Ruth Garrett Millikan, *Language: A Biological Model* (2005)** — Includes Millikan's analysis of "pushmi-pullyu" representations — representations that both describe how things are and direct action — which provides a framework for understanding the distinctive character of map-like mental models.

**Dedre Gentner and Albert Stevens (eds.), *Mental Models* (1983)** — An influential collection exploring the use of mental models in scientific reasoning, everyday cognition, and problem-solving. Connects Johnson-Laird's framework to psychological and cognitive science research.

**Andy Clark, *Being There* (1997)** — Discusses how cognitive systems exploit environmental structure rather than maintaining detailed internal maps, challenging the centrality of mental models in explaining intelligent behavior.

**Rolf Pfeifer and Christian Scheier, *Understanding Intelligence* (1999)** — A robotics-based treatment that examines when models and simulations are needed versus when direct coupling to the environment suffices, relevant for evaluating the scope of model-based accounts of cognition.
