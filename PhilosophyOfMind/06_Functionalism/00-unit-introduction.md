# Unit Introduction: Functionalism

## Central Questions

Functionalism is the most widely held position in contemporary philosophy of mind. Its central claim is that mental states are defined by their *functional roles* — by the causal-dispositional profile of a state rather than by what it is made of. Pain, for example, is not defined as C-fiber firing (the type identity theorist's answer) but as whatever state is caused by tissue damage, causes distress and avoidance behavior, and interacts with beliefs and desires in characteristic ways. Any physical system that instantiates the right functional organization has mental states, regardless of its physical composition.

The central questions for this unit are: How exactly should we characterize functional roles? What is the argument from multiple realizability to functionalism, and how compelling is it? Can functionalism account for the *phenomenal* properties of mental states — the feels and qualia that seem to be something over and above functional organization? And what are the limits of the functionalist framework — where does it succeed and where does it fail?

Functionalism occupies a peculiar dialectical position: it is the dominant theory, accepted (in some form) by most philosophers of mind, yet it faces devastating objections on its own terms. Block's absent qualia and inverted qualia objections show that functional organization seems insufficient for phenomenal consciousness. Searle's Chinese Room suggests that syntactic symbol manipulation is insufficient for semantic content, even if the functional organization is perfect. These objections have not been answered to everyone's satisfaction; they have instead driven a generation of philosophers to either refine functionalism or look for alternatives.

## Major Positions and Debates

### Multiple Realizability and the Case for Functionalism

Putnam's argument from multiple realizability is the central positive argument for functionalism. If pain can be realized in human neurons, octopus neurons, and — at least in principle — silicon circuits, then pain cannot be identical to any particular neural state type. But if there is something that all pains have in common across these diverse physical realizations, it must be something at a more abstract level — namely, functional organization. Pain is the functional state that plays a certain causal role, whatever the physical substrate.

Multiple realizability also provides the argument for the *special sciences*: geology, biology, psychology, and economics describe real patterns that crosscut physical descriptions. If every geological, biological, or psychological generalization required specifying the physical realizer, these sciences would be impossible. Functionalism explains how there can be irreducible special science generalizations: they capture functional patterns that abstract from physical implementation.

### Machine Functionalism and Turing Machine States

Putnam's early version of functionalism — machine functionalism — identified mental states with the abstract states of a Turing machine or probabilistic automaton. Mental state M is the state that, together with certain inputs, produces certain outputs and transitions to other states according to a machine table. This gave a precise formal account of what a functional state is.

Machine functionalism faced immediate objections. Ned Block's *combinatorial-state automaton* argument showed that any organism could be described as running any Turing machine table, so the theory fails to constrain what counts as having a mental state. Putnam himself abandoned machine functionalism, arguing that it cannot account for the holism of mental states and that the relevant notion of functional organization is more complex than any simple machine table.

### Psychofunctionalism vs. Analytical Functionalism

*Analytical functionalism* (or conceptual role functionalism) holds that mental state terms are implicitly defined by platitudes that specify their roles — claims like "pain causes distress," "beliefs are caused by the things they are about," "desires cause actions in conjunction with relevant beliefs." On this view, the concept of pain just is the concept of the state that plays the pain role. The content of mental concepts is exhausted by their functional specifications.

*Psychofunctionalism* holds that the functional roles that define mental states are not conceptual or a priori but empirical — they are whatever roles psychological theory identifies as the defining roles for mental states. On this view, what pain *is* is an empirical question, answered by the best psychology.

The distinction matters for multiple realizability: if functional roles are analytically defined, then any system that plays the relevant role counts as being in the mental state by definition; if they are empirically defined, there may be contingent facts about which functional organizations are the relevant ones.

### Block's Absent Qualia and Inverted Qualia

Ned Block's absent qualia argument holds that it is conceivable for a system to have perfect functional organization — to be functionally identical to a conscious being — without having any phenomenal experience. The *nation of China* thought experiment: suppose we wire up the entire population of China to replicate the functional organization of a human brain. There is surely nothing it is like to be this functionally organized system; yet according to functionalism, it would have mental states, including conscious experiences.

The *inverted qualia* objection holds that it is conceivable for two people to have the same functional organization but for the qualitative character of their experiences to be systematically inverted — what looks red to one looks green to the other and vice versa, yet both use "red" for red things and "green" for green things, so no behavioral difference is detectable. If inverted qualia are possible, then functional organization does not determine phenomenal character.

Functionalist responses to these objections include: (a) denying that absent or inverted qualia are genuinely conceivable when we think through all the functional details (the system-reply analogue); (b) accepting that functionalism does not account for phenomenal consciousness but arguing that phenomenal consciousness is not a proper subject of psychological theory (the deflationary response); (c) developing a richer notion of functional organization that is designed to capture phenomenal properties.

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**Hilary Putnam** is the originator of machine functionalism and the multiple realizability argument. His influential papers "Psychological Predicates" (1967) and "The Nature of Mental States" (1968) set the terms of the debate. Putnam's later rejection of his own functionalism — in *Representation and Reality* (1988) — is also important.

**Ned Block** has been the most important critic of functionalism from within a broadly sympathetic framework. His distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness (also discussed in Unit 03) is partly motivated by the inadequacy of functionalism for phenomenal consciousness. His absent qualia and inverted qualia arguments remain the most pressing challenges.

**Sydney Shoemaker** developed the causal theory of mental states and argued for a sophisticated version of functionalism that takes seriously the intrinsic properties of phenomenal states. Shoemaker's work on *Sydney's theory of qualia* — that qualia are functional properties that we are not fully able to introspect — is an important attempt to reconcile functionalism with the phenomenal.

**John Searle** is functionalism's most prominent opponent. His Chinese Room argument (1980) claims that intentionality and consciousness require more than functional organization — they require the right kind of *causal powers*, which only biological systems have. Searle's *biological naturalism* holds that consciousness is a biological phenomenon produced by specific kinds of physical processes.

**David Lewis** developed the analytical functionalist program with characteristic precision in "An Argument for the Identity Theory" (1966) and "Psychophysical and Theoretical Identifications" (1972). Lewis's use of Ramsey sentences to characterize functional roles is the most technically precise statement of analytical functionalism.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Putnam, "The Nature of Mental States" (1968)**
The foundational statement of functionalism, with the multiple realizability argument fully articulated. Putnam argues that mental states are not identical to brain states but to the abstract states of a Turing machine, individuated by their functional roles. Short, precise, and immensely influential.

**2. Block, "Troubles with Functionalism" (1978)**
The most influential critique of functionalism. Block introduces the absent qualia argument (the China brain), the inverted qualia argument, and distinguishes different versions of functionalism. This paper defined the problems that functionalists have been trying to solve ever since.

**3. Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980)**
The Chinese Room argument in its original form. Searle argues that a person who follows Chinese-language rules without understanding Chinese cannot thereby understand Chinese; similarly, a computer that manipulates symbols according to syntactic rules cannot thereby have semantics. The paper has generated an enormous literature of responses and counter-responses.

**4. Lewis, "Psychophysical and Theoretical Identifications" (1972)**
Lewis's account of how functional roles, specified by Ramsey sentences derived from folk-psychological platitudes, can ground mental state identifications. This is the most technically precise version of analytical functionalism and provides the clearest picture of how the identification of mental states with functional roles is supposed to work.

**5. Shoemaker, "Some Varieties of Functionalism" (1981) / *The First-Person Perspective* (1996)**
Shoemaker's work represents the most sustained attempt to develop functionalism in a way that takes phenomenal consciousness seriously. His argument that qualia are irreducibly first-personal but still functional is important for understanding the space of possible responses to Block's objections.

## Open Questions

**1. Can functionalism account for phenomenal consciousness?**
This is the central open question for functionalism. Block's absent qualia argument suggests that functional organization is sufficient for neither the presence nor the specific character of phenomenal experience. Functionalists have various responses, but none has been generally accepted. Whether a richer account of functional organization — or a different conception of phenomenal properties — can bridge the explanatory gap is genuinely unsettled.

**2. What is the right level of functional description for psychological explanation?**
Functionalism is compatible with many different levels of functional description: neuroscientific, computational, algorithmic, folk-psychological. Which level is the right one for psychological explanation? This is partly an empirical question (which level captures genuine patterns in behavior) and partly a philosophical one (what the point of psychological explanation is).

**3. Is multiple realizability as general as Putnam claimed?**
Some philosophers (Shapiro, Bechtel) have argued that the alleged multiple realizability of mental states is overstated: the physical differences between human pain and octopus pain may be correlated with behavioral and functional differences, so pain is not the same functional kind across species. If this is right, multiple realizability may support only a weaker conclusion than Putnam's argument requires.

**4. Does the Chinese Room show that syntax is insufficient for semantics?**
Searle's argument depends on the claim that symbol manipulation is essentially syntactic (formal) and that semantics (meaning) cannot be derived from syntax alone. Functionalists typically respond with the systems reply: it is not the person who has the mental states but the whole system that does. Whether the systems reply succeeds is deeply disputed, and the dispute connects to fundamental questions about the nature of intentionality.

**5. How should functionalism handle the normativity of the mental?**
Mental states are intrinsically normative: beliefs can be true or false, rational or irrational; desires can be appropriate or inappropriate. Can a purely functional account capture this normativity? Some philosophers argue that the normativity of the mental is irreducible to causal-functional facts and that this shows the limits of functionalism; others argue that normativity can be naturalized within a broadly functional framework.
