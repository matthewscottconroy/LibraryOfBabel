# Functionalism: Mental States as Functional Roles

Functionalism is the view that mental states are defined by their functional roles — by what they do rather than what they are made of. Pain is not a specific kind of brain state (as type identity theory holds); pain is whatever state plays the "pain role" — the state that is typically caused by tissue damage, that causes distress and withdrawal behavior, that motivates seeking relief, and that interacts with other mental states in characteristic ways. Any system whose states play these roles thereby has mental states — silicon or carbon, biological or artificial.

**The Historical Context**

Functionalism emerged in the late 1960s as a response to the multiple realizability problem for type identity theory. Type identity theory holds that each mental state type is identical with a neural state type: pain = C-fiber firing (or some neural state). But Hilary Putnam pointed out that pain seems to be multiply realizable: an octopus, a Martian, or a silicon robot might have pain without having C-fibers. Type identity theory cannot account for this, since it insists on a fixed neural type for each mental type.

Functionalism solves this by abstracting away from the physical substrate. Pain is whatever state plays the pain role in the relevant system. C-fiber firing plays this role in humans; some other state plays it in octopuses; silicon state S plays it in the robot. All are pain, because all play the same functional role.

**The Functional Role**

A mental state's functional role is specified by its causal-relational properties:

- *Input relations*: What kinds of external stimuli cause the state? Pain is caused by tissue damage, noxious stimuli, and intense sensory inputs.

- *Output relations*: What behavior does the state tend to produce? Pain tends to produce withdrawal, vocalization, and rest behaviors.

- *Internal relations*: How does the state interact with other mental states? Pain interacts with beliefs (about the source of pain), desires (to reduce pain), and attention (pain captures attention).

These three kinds of relations — to inputs, outputs, and other mental states — collectively define the functional role. A state that has this exact profile of causal relations IS pain, whatever its physical implementation.

**The Turing Machine Analogy**

Putnam's original functionalism drew on the Turing machine model of computation. A Turing machine is defined by its abstract computational organization — its states and transition function — not by the physical hardware that implements it. The same program can run on different hardware.

Similarly, minds are defined by their computational organization — the abstract functional relationships among states, inputs, and outputs. The same mind could in principle run on different hardware. This gives functionalism its characteristic independence from specific physical substrates.

**Multiple Realizability and the Identity Question**

Functionalism's central virtue is that it explains multiple realizability: the same mental state type can be realized by different physical types. Pain in a human is realized by C-fiber activity; pain in an octopus is realized by different neural states; pain in a silicon robot might be realized by different computational states.

This does not mean that mental states are not physical. Each individual token of a mental state is physical — it is realized by some specific physical state. But the mental state type is multiply realizable: different physical tokens can realize the same mental state type.

This "token identity" with "type non-identity" is the hallmark of non-reductive physicalism — the view that mental states are physical (at the token level) while being irreducible to physical types (at the type level).

**Varieties of Functionalism**

Several distinct versions of functionalism have been developed:

*Machine functionalism* (Putnam): Mental states are the computational states of an ideal Turing machine. The mind is defined by its program.

*Psychofunctionalism* (Fodor, Armstrong): Mental states are characterized by their causal roles in the actual psychology of creatures like us — as revealed by empirical psychology, not by a priori analysis.

*Analytic functionalism* (Lewis, Shoemaker): Mental states are defined by their roles in folk psychology. The content of mental concepts is given by the role they play in our common-sense psychological theory.

Each version faces different objections and advantages. The versions differ primarily in where they look for the specification of functional roles: in abstract computation theory, in empirical psychology, or in folk psychological theory.

**The Central Virtue and the Central Challenge**

The central virtue of functionalism is that it makes consciousness and intentionality principled features of any physical system with the right functional organization, rather than mysteriously tied to carbon-based biology. This is the right basis for theories of artificial intelligence: if a system has the right functional organization, it has mental states.

The central challenge is accounting for phenomenal consciousness. If mental states are defined by functional roles, then two systems with the same functional organization have the same mental states. But might not one system have the right functional organization while lacking any qualitative, phenomenal experience? This is the "absent qualia" problem — and it drives much of the opposition to functionalism.
