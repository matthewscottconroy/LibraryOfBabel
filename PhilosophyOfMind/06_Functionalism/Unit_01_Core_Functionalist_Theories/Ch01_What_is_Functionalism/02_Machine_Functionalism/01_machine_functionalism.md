# Machine Functionalism

Machine functionalism is the original version of functionalism developed by Hilary Putnam in the 1960s. It holds that a mind is a kind of Turing machine — a computational system defined by its abstract program — and that mental states are the computational states of that machine.

**The Turing Machine Model**

A Turing machine consists of:
- A finite set of states (the "machine table")
- A tape divided into cells, each containing a symbol
- A read-write head that scans the tape
- A transition function: for each combination of current state and tape symbol, specifying the next state, the symbol to write, and the direction to move the head

The Turing machine is a mathematical abstraction: it is defined entirely by its states and transition function, with no reference to any physical substrate. The same Turing machine can be implemented in vacuum tubes, transistors, biological neurons, or any other physical medium that can maintain discrete states and implement the transition function.

Putnam proposed that human minds are probabilistic automata — a more general kind of Turing machine in which the transitions are probabilistic rather than deterministic. A mental state, on this view, is a computational state of this automaton. To be in pain is to be in the computational state that mediates between nociceptive inputs and pain-behavior outputs in the appropriate probabilistic way.

**The Explanatory Appeal**

Machine functionalism has several explanatory virtues.

*Multiple realizability*: Since the machine is defined by its abstract organization, not its physical substrate, the same mental states can be realized in different physical materials. This explains why psychology is autonomous from neuroscience: the same psychological generalizations hold for any physical system with the right functional organization.

*Scientific legitimacy*: Turing machines are mathematically well-defined objects. Characterizing minds as Turing machines connects the philosophy of mind to computability theory and the foundations of computer science.

*Practical AI*: If minds are Turing machines, then building minds is a matter of building the right programs. This underpins the strong AI research program: machine intelligence is, in principle, achievable by finding and implementing the right computational organization.

**Putnam's Later Recantation**

Remarkably, Putnam himself later rejected machine functionalism, offering several arguments against it.

*The multiple realization of psychology*: Psychology is multiply realizable not only across physical substrates but also across computational architectures. The same psychological state can be realized by many different programs. If mental states are defined by computational states, then identical psychological states in different systems would have to be identical computational states — but this seems too restrictive.

*Holism and context-sensitivity*: Psychological states are not individuated in the simple, context-independent way that computational states are. The beliefs and desires of a creature are deeply contextual and holistic; there is no simple way to specify a discrete functional role for each psychological state.

*The semantic externalism problem*: Mental content is partly determined by the environment (as Putnam himself argued with Twin Earth). A purely computational characterization of mental states ignores the environmental determination of content.

Putnam's rejection of his own theory is philosophically instructive: it shows that even its inventor found machine functionalism too simple and too computationalistic to capture the genuine complexity of mental life.

**The Legacy of Machine Functionalism**

Despite Putnam's recantation, machine functionalism left a lasting legacy. It established the multiple realizability argument against type identity theory. It connected philosophy of mind to computer science and gave the AI research program a philosophical foundation. And it introduced the crucial idea that the relevant level of explanation for mental states is the functional-computational level, not the physical implementation level.

Later versions of functionalism — psychofunctionalism, analytic functionalism — address the limitations of machine functionalism while preserving its core insights: that mental states are defined by their roles, not their physical stuff, and that the same mental states can be realized in different physical systems.
