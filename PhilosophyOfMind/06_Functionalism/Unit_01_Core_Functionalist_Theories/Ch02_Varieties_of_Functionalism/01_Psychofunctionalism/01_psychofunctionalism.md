# Psychofunctionalism

Psychofunctionalism holds that the functional roles constituting mental states are specified by scientific psychology, not by a priori analysis or computational theory. Mental states are whatever states play certain causal roles in actual psychological processes — as revealed by empirical investigation of cognition, perception, emotion, and behavior.

**The Contrast with Other Functionalisms**

Machine functionalism characterizes mental states in terms of Turing machine states — an a priori, mathematical characterization. Analytic functionalism characterizes mental states in terms of folk psychological theory — another kind of a priori characterization. Psychofunctionalism, by contrast, is empirical: the relevant functional roles are those revealed by scientific psychology.

This makes psychofunctionalism an empirical research program rather than a philosophical analysis. The identity conditions for mental states are determined by what psychology finds, not by conceptual analysis alone.

**Fodor's Argument for Psychofunctionalism**

Jerry Fodor's defense of psychofunctionalism draws on his commitment to "special science" autonomy. Different sciences are irreducible to each other not because their subject matters are ontologically distinct, but because their generalizations are couched at different levels of description. Psychology's generalizations are not the same as neuroscience's generalizations, even if psychology is physically realized.

Psychological explanations proceed by attributing internal states with functional organization, where the organization is specified by psychological theory. The relevant theory is not folk psychology but scientific cognitive psychology — the theory of memory systems, attention, learning, language processing, and so on.

Psychofunctionalism connects to Fodor's Language of Thought hypothesis: the LOT provides the computational architecture through which psychological states are realized, and psychofunctionalism characterizes these states in terms of their roles in the LOT-implemented cognitive system.

**Ramsey-Lewis Functionalism vs. Psychofunctionalism**

David Lewis's "Ramsey-Lewis" version of analytic functionalism takes a different approach. Lewis proposes that mental concepts get their meaning from folk psychology — the common-sense theory of mind that we all implicitly accept. The content of mental concepts is given by "Ramsifying" folk psychological theory: replacing all mental terms with variables and specifying that the things satisfying the open sentences jointly constitute the mental states.

This is a priori in the sense that it doesn't require empirical psychology to specify the functional roles. Lewis's functionalism is faithful to common sense: pain is whatever folk psychology says is caused by injury, causes distress, motivates relief-seeking, and so on.

Psychofunctionalism departs from this: it allows that scientific psychology might revise or replace folk psychological categories. The psychological states that figure in scientific laws may not be the folk psychological categories we begin with.

**Problems for Psychofunctionalism**

*The problem of cross-species application*: Psychofunctionalism identifies mental states with whatever plays certain psychological roles according to empirical psychology. But empirical psychology is mostly done on humans. What counts as the "same mental state" in an octopus, an insect, or a robot? Different species may have very different cognitive architectures, making cross-species application of psychological categories uncertain.

*The underdetermination of functional roles*: Scientific psychology does not uniquely specify functional roles at the level of individual mental states. The cognitive scientific picture is of systems with many interacting components, and the individuation of specific states within this system is not straightforwardly determined by the science.

*The intentionality of psychological laws*: Psychological laws are couched in intentional terms (beliefs, desires, representations). If psychofunctionalism is to be a naturalistic reduction of the intentional, it cannot use intentional terms in specifying the functional roles it is identifying with mental states — but scientific psychology is deeply intentional.

**The Contribution of Psychofunctionalism**

Despite these problems, psychofunctionalism has been influential in grounding the philosophy of mind in empirical science. By tying the identity conditions for mental states to scientific psychology rather than folk wisdom or mathematical computation, it connects philosophical theory of mind to the ongoing research program of cognitive science — and opens the possibility that philosophical claims about mental states might be empirically falsifiable.
