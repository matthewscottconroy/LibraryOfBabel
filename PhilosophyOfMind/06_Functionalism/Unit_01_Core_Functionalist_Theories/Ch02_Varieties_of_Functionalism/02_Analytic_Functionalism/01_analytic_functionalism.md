# Analytic Functionalism

Analytic functionalism holds that the identity conditions for mental states are given by folk psychological theory — the common-sense framework of beliefs, desires, intentions, perceptions, and emotions that we use to explain and predict behavior. Mental states are defined by the roles they play in folk psychology, and this is an a priori, conceptual truth — it can be known without empirical investigation.

**Lewis's Ramsey-Lewis Framework**

David Lewis developed the most systematic version of analytic functionalism using the Ramsey-Lewis method for functional definition. The strategy:

1. Take folk psychology as a body of platitudes: "Pain is caused by tissue damage and typically causes withdrawal and avoidance behavior." "Beliefs combine with desires to cause actions." "Visual experiences represent the colors and shapes of objects." And so on.

2. Conjoin all these platitudes into a single "folk psychology theory" T.

3. Replace every mental term in T with a variable: replace "pain" with x, "belief" with y, and so on. This yields a "Ramsey sentence" — an open sentence in many variables.

4. The mental state types are then identified with whatever physical states satisfy the open sentence — whatever plays the functional roles specified by the Ramsey sentence.

This defines mental states in terms of their folk-psychological functional roles without any circular use of mental terms. "Pain" picks out whatever state plays the pain-role in our folk theory.

**The A Priori Character**

Analytic functionalism is a priori in the sense that the connection between mental state types and functional roles is a matter of conceptual analysis — of what our mental concepts mean, not what we discover empirically. Knowing the meaning of "pain" involves knowing (implicitly) the functional role that pain plays.

This is a strong claim, and it has been challenged. Are folk psychological platitudes really analytic? The list of platitudes is not easily specified — there is no canonical folk psychological theory. Different people might have different implicit folk psychological theories, leading to different functional role specifications for the same mental state term.

**The Relationship to Mental Causation**

One virtue of analytic functionalism is that it vindicates mental causation. If mental states are defined by their causal roles — by the causal relations they enter into with inputs, outputs, and other mental states — then mental states are essentially causal. They are not epiphenomenal properties that float above the causal order; they are constituted by their causal powers.

This gives analytic functionalism a natural account of how mental states can cause behavior: the belief and desire that rationalize an action do so because the action is the output associated with those states in folk psychological theory. There is no "ghost in the machine" — the causal story is the identity of the mental state.

**Shoemaker's Causal Powers Functionalism**

Sydney Shoemaker developed a version of analytic functionalism centered on the concept of causal powers. For Shoemaker, a property just is a set of causal powers — a way of contributing to the causal profile of the things that have it. Mental states are properties of systems; they are constituted by their causal powers, which are the causal relations they enter into.

This makes the connection between mental states and their causal roles not merely contingent (as it might be on some functional role theories) but constitutive: a mental state is its causal powers, not something separate that happens to have those causal powers.

Shoemaker's view connects analytic functionalism to the metaphysics of properties and causation more broadly, making explicit the commitment to a "powers" metaphysics.

**Objections to Analytic Functionalism**

*The liberalism objection*: If mental states are defined by functional roles, then any system with the right functional organization has mental states. This might be too liberal: it might count thermostats, economies, or other systems as having mental states if they implement the right functional organization. (This objection connects to the absent qualia problem.)

*The chauvinism objection*: If mental states are defined by specifically folk-psychological roles, then systems whose psychology diverges significantly from human folk psychology might be denied mental states — even if they have genuine internal states that function in similar ways. This might be too restrictive.

Lewis acknowledged both objections and argued that neither is fatal: folk psychology gives a loose enough specification to avoid excessive chauvinism, while the biological and behavioral constraints on what counts as "the right" functional organization prevent excessive liberalism. Whether Lewis's resolution is satisfying is debated.
