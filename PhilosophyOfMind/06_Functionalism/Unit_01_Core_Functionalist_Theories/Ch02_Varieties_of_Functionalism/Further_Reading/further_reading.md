# Further Reading: Varieties of Functionalism

## Primary Sources

**David Lewis, "Psychophysical and Theoretical Identifications" (1972)** — Develops analytic functionalism: mental states are whatever states realize the functional roles specified by commonsense psychology, making the identification of mental states with physical states a posteriori but derivable from a priori folk-psychological theory.

**Jerry Fodor, "Special Sciences (or: the Disunity of Science as a Working Hypothesis)" (1974)** — Argues for the autonomy of the special sciences (psychology, economics, biology) from physics, grounding the case for psychofunctionalism: mental states should be individuated at the level of psychology, not physics.

**Brian Loar, *Mind and Meaning* (1981)** — Defends a version of psychofunctionalism that is sensitive to the need for narrow content, arguing that functional roles can be specified without reference to environmental factors.

## Secondary Literature

**Ned Block, "Psychologism and Behaviorism" (1981)** — Distinguishes psychofunctionalism (states defined by roles in a scientific psychology) from analytic functionalism (states defined by folk-psychology theory) and from behaviorism, clarifying the logical geography of functionalist positions.

**Jaegwon Kim, "Multiple Realizability and the Metaphysics of Reduction" (1992)** — Questions the multiple realizability argument, arguing that multiple realizability at the level of organisms or species undermines local reduction but not the possibility of species-specific reductions. Challenges the typical motivation for functionalism.

**Georges Rey, *Contemporary Philosophy of Mind* (1997)** — A balanced survey of functionalism and its varieties, suitable for students, with critical discussions of machine functionalism, analytic functionalism, and psychofunctionalism.

**William Lycan, *Consciousness and Experience* (1996)** — Defends a "homuncular functionalism" — an account in which mental states are individuated by their functional roles within a hierarchical teleological system — and applies this to the problem of consciousness.
