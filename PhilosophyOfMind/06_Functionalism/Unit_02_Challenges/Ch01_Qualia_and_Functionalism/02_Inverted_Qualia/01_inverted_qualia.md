# Inverted Qualia

The inverted qualia thought experiment asks us to imagine a being who has the same functional organization as a normal human being but whose qualitative experiences are systematically inverted: what looks red to them looks green to us, what looks blue to them looks yellow to us, and so on. If inverted qualia are possible, this shows that functional organization does not determine phenomenal character — the same function can be realized by different qualia.

**The Thought Experiment**

Consider Qualia Inverter, a person who is functionally identical to you: they respond to ripe tomatoes by saying "red," to grass by saying "green," they prefer tomatoes over grass in the way you do, they learn color words in the same way, they pass all the behavioral tests for color discrimination correctly. But when they see a tomato, their inner experience is what you would call "green," and when they see grass, their inner experience is what you would call "red."

Crucially, the inversion is systematic and complete: since it is inverted everywhere, Qualia Inverter has no way of noticing that their qualia are inverted. All the inferential and behavioral connections are preserved. They just have different qualitative experiences than you do.

If this scenario is conceivable and possible, then functional organization does not determine qualia. The same function can be realized with either normal or inverted qualia — suggesting that qualia are something over and above functional organization.

**The Connection to the Hard Problem**

Inverted qualia is related to the zombie argument for the hard problem. Both arguments exploit the idea that the phenomenal and the functional can come apart. Where zombie arguments say they can come apart by having no phenomenal dimension (absent qualia), the inverted qualia argument says they can come apart by having a different phenomenal dimension (different qualia, same function).

Together, these arguments suggest that phenomenal consciousness is not logically determined by functional organization — that physical-functional descriptions leave open what the qualitative character of experience is like.

**Epistemic vs. Metaphysical Versions**

The inverted qualia argument can be stated in two ways:

*Epistemic version*: I can never know whether my color qualia are inverted relative to yours, even if we have the same functional organization. Our functional behavior is identical; the qualitative differences (if any) are invisible from the outside.

*Metaphysical version*: Not only can I not know whether your qualia are inverted, but inverted qualia are genuinely possible — we could have the same functional organization with different qualia.

The epistemic version is generally accepted: it is a genuine epistemological puzzle that we cannot directly compare qualitative experiences across subjects. The metaphysical version is more contested: it requires that qualia are genuinely separable from functional organization, which functionalists deny.

**Block's Qualia vs. Functional States**

Ned Block uses inverted qualia to motivate his distinction between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness. A system with inverted qualia has the same access consciousness as normal (the same information is globally available, the same reports are made). But the system has different phenomenal consciousness (different qualitative character).

This distinction is only possible if phenomenal and access consciousness can come apart — which they can, if inverted qualia are possible.

**Functionalist Responses**

*Denying the conceivability*: Some functionalists argue that inverted qualia are not genuinely conceivable. Color experience is not just a point in phenomenal space; it has rich inferential and affective connections (red is warm and urgent; blue is cool and calm) that are functionally significant. If you invert the qualia but preserve the function, you would have to invert all these affective and inferential connections too — and at that point, the "inversion" is not a simple transposition.

*The representationalist response*: If phenomenal character is determined by representational content, and representational content is functionally determined, then inverted qualia that preserve function are impossible. Two systems with the same functional organization represent the same properties — and therefore have the same qualia. This is Dretske's and Tye's representationalist response.

*The psychophysical laws response*: Perhaps the same qualia must accompany the same functional states as a matter of psychophysical law — not logical necessity, but natural necessity. Inverted qualia are not logically impossible, but they don't occur because the laws governing the mind-body connection happen to be such that the same function produces the same qualia.

The inverted qualia thought experiment continues to serve as a useful test case for theories of consciousness and for evaluating the claims of functionalism.
