# Further Reading: Qualia and Functionalism

## Primary Sources

**Ned Block, "Troubles with Functionalism" (1978)** — Block introduces the "absent qualia" and "inverted qualia" objections to functionalism. The absent qualia objection asks whether a system could implement the right functional organization without any phenomenal experience; the inverted qualia objection asks whether two systems could have the same functional organization but systematically different qualitative experiences.

**Frank Jackson, "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982)** — The knowledge argument (Mary's room) is used to argue that phenomenal properties are not captured by any functional description, because Mary can know all functional facts about color perception without knowing what it is like to see red.

**Joseph Levine, "Materialism and Qualia: The Explanatory Gap" (1983)** — Even if functionalist identifications are true, they leave unexplained why the physical/functional states have the qualitative character they do. This explanatory gap is treated as an epistemic, though not necessarily ontological, challenge to functionalism.

**Sydney Shoemaker, "Absent Qualia Are Impossible — A Reply to Block" (1981)** — Shoemaker argues that the absent qualia scenario is not genuinely conceivable because phenomenal properties are constitutively functional: a state's functional role partly constitutes its qualitative character.

## Secondary Literature

**David Chalmers, "Absent Qualia, Faded Qualia, Dancing Qualia" (1996)** — Chalmers develops a series of thought experiments designed to show that functional organization cannot suffice for specific qualitative character, even granting that some phenomenal experience accompanies it.

**Daniel Dennett, "Quining Qualia" (1988)** — Dennett argues that the concept of qualia is incoherent — no single notion can satisfy all the properties philosophers typically attribute to qualia — and that the apparent problems for functionalism dissolve once qualia are rejected.

**Brian Loar, "Phenomenal States" (1990)** — The phenomenal concepts strategy: argues that special first-person phenomenal concepts refer to the very physical/functional states picked out by scientific descriptions but through a different mode of presentation, explaining why there appears to be an explanatory gap.
