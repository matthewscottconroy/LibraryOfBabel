# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Qualia and Functionalism

## Key Arguments

**The Inverted Qualia Argument**
The inverted qualia scenario posits two individuals who are functionally identical—they respond to red stimuli in all the same ways, call them "red," associate them with fire engines, etc.—but whose phenomenal experiences are qualitatively inverted: what looks red to one looks green to the other, and vice versa. If inverted qualia are coherently possible, then phenomenal properties are not constituted by functional roles (since the functional roles are identical while the qualia differ). This is a direct argument against functionalism's claim to capture all mental properties in functional terms.

**Block's Absent Qualia Argument (China Brain)**
Block argues that a system can realize all the functional organization of a human mind without having any phenomenal consciousness at all—the system has absent qualia. The China Brain is Block's paradigm case: 1.4 billion people, each playing the role of a neuron, realize the functional organization of a human brain. Does the China Brain-system have a phenomenal experience of pain? Intuitively no—there is no pain that belongs to the aggregate system. If absent qualia are possible, functionalism cannot explain the existence of phenomenal consciousness.

**Block's Access/Phenomenal Distinction as Anti-Functionalist**
Block argues that functionalism can at best capture access consciousness (availability for reasoning and report) but not phenomenal consciousness (the qualitative, felt character of experience). Since phenomenal consciousness is not constituted by any functional role, functionalism is an incomplete theory of mind: it describes the cognitive-computational architecture but leaves out what is most important—the subjective feel of experience. This argument ties the challenge to functionalism to the broader hard problem of consciousness.

**Shoemaker's Objection: Qualia Are Causally Efficacious**
Sydney Shoemaker argues against the inverted qualia objection that phenomenal properties are not epiphenomenal appendages to functional states but are themselves constitutively connected to functional roles: the qualia of red are partly constituted by their typical causes (red objects) and effects (red-comparisons, aesthetic responses). If qualia really were inverted, they would over time lead to differences in functional role that would make the inversion detectable. This response defends functionalism by denying that purely qualitative inversion, holding functional roles fixed, is truly coherent.

**The Liberalism Problem**
The liberalism problem is that functionalism attributes phenomenal consciousness too widely: any system with the right functional organization has qualia, even if it is a simple thermostat, a Chinese-speaking room, or a population of people organized as a brain. If phenomenal consciousness requires something more specific than functional organization—something biological, something physical, something about first-person perspective—then functionalism is insufficient. The liberalism problem motivates Searle's biological naturalism, IIT's architectural specificity, and various restrictions on which functional organizations are sufficient for phenomenal consciousness.

## Core Concepts

**Inverted Qualia**
Inverted qualia is the scenario in which two subjects are functionally identical but phenomenally different: the qualitative character of their experiences is systematically inverted (e.g., what one sees as red, the other sees as green), while all their functional-behavioral properties remain the same. The possibility of inverted qualia (assuming it is coherent) would show that phenomenal properties are not determined by functional properties, directly refuting functionalism. The debate concerns whether spectral inversion is coherent, whether it would be empirically detectable, and whether the functional roles of color experience are rich enough to preclude inversion.

**Absent Qualia**
Absent qualia is the scenario in which a system has all the functional organization of a conscious being but has no phenomenal consciousness at all—there is nothing it is like to be the system. Block's China Brain and Ned Block's zombie are examples. If absent qualia are possible, functionalism is false as a theory of consciousness: functional organization is not sufficient for phenomenal properties. Functionalists respond either by denying the possibility of absent qualia (if the functional organization is right, qualia must be present) or by restricting the relevant functional organization (e.g., requiring the right implementation).

**Phenomenal Properties**
Phenomenal properties are the intrinsic, qualitative properties of conscious experience—the redness of a red experience, the painfulness of pain, the specific taste of coffee. Phenomenal properties are what consciousness has "over and above" its functional properties (if it has anything over and above). Block argues phenomenal properties cannot be captured by functional descriptions; Dennett argues that they are constituted by functional properties and the appearance of an additional "extra" is an artifact of a confused conception of qualia.

**The Liberalism Problem**
The liberalism problem for functionalism is that purely functional criteria for mental state attribution may be satisfied by systems that we have strong intuitions lack genuine mental states—especially genuine phenomenal states. A thermostat "detecting temperature," a simple feedback control system, or a very simple information-processing system all have some functional organization. Functionalists must specify which functional organization is sufficient for which mental properties, and the specification must be principled (not ad hoc) and must match our intuitions about which systems have which mental states.

**Functional Isomorphism**
Functional isomorphism is the relation of having the same functional organization—the same causal-functional structure of internal states and their interactions. Functionalism holds that functional isomorphs share all mental properties: the same beliefs, desires, phenomenal states. The challenge is that we seem able to conceive of functional isomorphs that differ phenomenally (inverted qualia) or that lack phenomenal properties altogether (absent qualia). Both challenges are arguments that functional isomorphism is insufficient for phenomenal identity.

## Thought Experiments

**Block's China Brain**
Block imagines the population of China organized so that each person plays the functional role of a neuron in a human brain. The aggregate "China Brain" system realizes all the functional organization of a human brain with, say, a pain state. Does the aggregate have a phenomenal experience of pain? The intuitive answer is no—there is no pain that belongs to the aggregate. The thought experiment is designed to show that functional organization, even when perfectly replicated, is insufficient for phenomenal consciousness.

**The Inverted Spectrum**
Two individuals, A and B, are raised in the same environment and learn color vocabulary together, reaching identical functional states for color discrimination and behavior. But A's experience of red is phenomenally identical to B's experience of green, and vice versa—their spectra are inverted. Since their functional states are identical, functionalism predicts they have the same mental properties. But they seem to have different phenomenal properties. The thought experiment challenges functionalism by showing that phenomenal properties might vary independently of functional properties.

**The Suddenly Sighted (von Senden)**
People born blind who gain sight through surgery describe their visual experience as radically different from that of sighted individuals—some experience depth as flat color patches, others report geometric forms that take time to be recognized as familiar objects. Whether these reports show that phenomenal properties depend on developmental-causal history (and are therefore not determined by current functional organization alone) is debated. The case is used to argue that the qualia of color experience are not simply fixed by functional-discriminative behavior but also by the developmental and experiential history of the subject.
