# Searle's Argument Against Functionalism

Searle's Chinese Room argument, discussed in detail in the section on consciousness theories, is also one of the most influential arguments against functionalism specifically. It targets the claim that the right kind of computational organization is sufficient for genuine mental states — the claim Searle calls "Strong AI."

**Functionalism's Computational Claim**

Functionalism, in its machine functionalism version, holds that a system has mental states if and only if it implements the right computational program. "Minds are programs," in the functionalist's slogan. The physical substrate is irrelevant; what matters is the abstract computational organization.

This claim has the attractive consequence that artificial systems could, in principle, have genuine minds — if they implement the right program. It grounds the strong AI research program and provides the philosophical underpinning for claims that sophisticated AI systems might be conscious.

**Searle's Counter**

The Chinese Room is specifically designed to counter this functionalist claim. The person in the room implements any program you like — they follow the rules of any program for responding to Chinese inputs with Chinese outputs. By the functionalist's own standard, the system implementing the program has the mental states that the program is designed to have (understanding Chinese, in the example).

But Searle argues that implementing the program is clearly not sufficient for understanding. The person in the room doesn't understand Chinese, no matter how sophisticated the program. Therefore, implementing the program is not sufficient for mental states — and functionalism's computational claim is false.

**The Syntax-Semantics Gap**

Searle's argument rests on a specific diagnosis of why computational programs are insufficient: they are purely syntactic — they specify operations on symbols defined by their formal properties — but genuine mental states require semantics — genuine content, genuine understanding of what the symbols mean.

Functionalism, on Searle's view, makes the mistake of thinking that syntax is sufficient for semantics. Implementing the right syntactic operations (the right program) will produce the right semantic content. Searle denies this: no amount of syntactic complexity produces semantics. Syntax is intrinsically without meaning; meaning requires the biological causal powers that neurons have and that silicon chips lack.

**Responses from Functionalists**

The major functionalist responses to Searle were discussed in the section on the Chinese Room. Here we focus on the specifically functionalist responses.

*The systems reply*: The Chinese room, as a whole system, does understand Chinese — even if the person inside does not. This is the most natural response from a functionalist standpoint: mental states are properties of systems with the right functional organization, not of any single component of such systems.

*The robot reply*: The program embedded in a robot with sensory and motor connections to the world would have genuine semantics through its causal connections to external reality. This responds to Searle's syntax-semantics gap: syntax connected to the world through appropriate causal connections produces semantics.

*The brain simulator reply*: A program that simulates the activity of every neuron in a Chinese speaker's brain, neuron by neuron, would have genuine understanding — since it implements the same functional organization as a system that genuinely understands. Functionalists argue that Searle's intuition that this system lacks understanding reflects an unjustified bias toward biological substrates.

**The Deeper Issue: Intrinsic vs. Derived Intentionality**

The debate between Searle and functionalists ultimately comes down to the question of intrinsic versus derived intentionality (discussed in the section on intentionality). Searle holds that only biological neurons have the causal powers required for original, intrinsic intentionality. Functionalists hold that intentionality is constituted by functional organization and can be instantiated in any physical medium with the right causal structure.

This disagreement is not resolved by the Chinese Room argument alone; it depends on deeper questions about the nature of intentionality and whether it can be naturalized. The argument has been enormously productive in forcing clarity about these questions, even if it has not settled them.
