# Further Reading: The Chinese Room

## Primary Sources

**John Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980)** — The original Chinese Room paper. Searle imagines himself in a room following rules for manipulating Chinese symbols without understanding Chinese, arguing that computation is insufficient for understanding (intentionality). Responses from multiple commentators appear in the same *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* issue.

**John Searle, *The Rediscovery of the Mind* (1992)** — Chapter 9 revisits the Chinese Room and responds to criticisms, developing Searle's "biological naturalism" as the positive alternative.

**Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950)** — Turing's paper motivating the view that successful linguistic performance is sufficient grounds for attributing intelligence — the target of Searle's argument. Includes Turing's anticipation of objections similar to those Searle raises.

## Secondary Literature

**Daniel Dennett, "Fast Thinking" (1994)** — In *Darwin's Dangerous Idea*, includes Dennett's response to the Chinese Room: the functional organization matters, and a system that actually passes the Turing Test sufficiently deserves to be attributed understanding.

**Paul and Patricia Churchland, "Could a Machine Think?" (1990)** — A well-known response arguing that the Chinese Room fails as an objection because the correct unit of analysis is the whole system, not Searle inside the room; the system as a whole might understand Chinese.

**Margaret Boden (ed.), *The Philosophy of Artificial Intelligence* (1990)** — Collects key texts in the philosophy of AI including Searle's paper, Turing's 1950 paper, and responses that frame the debate.

**Stevan Harnad, "Other Bodies, Other Minds" (1991)** — Develops the "symbol grounding problem" as a reformulation of Searle's challenge: symbols have meaning only when grounded in non-symbolic, sensorimotor processes.

**Roger Penrose, *The Emperor's New Mind* (1989)** — Penrose argues that human mathematical intuition transcends what any algorithmic system can do, using Gödel's incompleteness theorems, providing an independent argument against machine cognition.
