# Unit Introduction: Perception

## Central Questions

Perception is where mind meets world — where the internal cognitive system makes contact with the external environment. But the exact nature of this contact is deeply contested. Are we directly aware of the external world, or only of internal representations? When I see a red apple, what exactly am I seeing — the apple itself, or some internal sense-datum caused by the apple? If the latter, how do I know that my internal representation corresponds to the external world? These questions connect perception to the theory of knowledge (epistemology) and to metaphysics, but they also have specifically psychological and philosophical dimensions that make them important on their own terms.

The central debates in philosophy of perception turn on two axes: (1) the *mediating entities* question: is perception direct (unmediated by internal representations) or indirect (mediated by sense-data or similar internal intermediaries)? and (2) the *content* question: does perception have representational content — a propositional or quasi-propositional structure — or is it purely presentational, disclosing the world without representing it under any description? These questions bear on the relationship between perception and cognition, the nature of perceptual knowledge, and the relationship between phenomenology and intentionality.

A newer set of questions concerns *predictive perception*: if the brain is a prediction machine, then perception is not the passive receipt of inputs but an active process of hypothesis confirmation and disconfirmation. This view transforms the traditional framework: rather than asking how the brain builds up a representation of the world from incoming signals, we ask how the brain generates predictions about the world and updates them in response to prediction errors. Predictive processing challenges traditional accounts of both the nature of perceptual representation and the phenomenology of perception.

## Major Positions and Debates

### Sense-Data Theory

The sense-data theory, held in various forms by Russell, Moore, Ayer, and Price, holds that the immediate objects of perceptual awareness are sense-data — mind-dependent entities that have exactly the properties they appear to have. When I see a round coin tilted away from me, I have an elliptical sense-datum; when I look at a straight stick half-submerged in water, I have a bent sense-datum. Sense-data are incorrigible: I cannot be wrong about what sense-data I have, though I can be wrong about what external objects they represent.

The sense-data theory makes epistemological sense: it identifies an indubitable foundation for perceptual knowledge. But it faces two devastating problems. The *phenomenological problem*: it is not clear that we are aware of sense-data in ordinary perception — we seem to be aware of physical objects, not inner representations. The *veil of perception* problem: if we are only directly aware of sense-data, how do we ever know that they represent an external world? The inference from sense-data to external objects seems to require something more than anything available within the sense-data framework.

### Direct Realism and Naïve Realism

Direct realism holds that we are directly aware of external physical objects in perception — not sense-data or mental images. The objects we perceive are mind-independent objects with mind-independent properties, and we are in direct cognitive contact with them. In its most extreme form — *naïve realism* — direct realism holds that the qualitative character of perceptual experience is constituted by the objects and properties perceived, not by any internal representation of them.

Disjunctivism (McDowell, Martin) is a sophisticated form of direct realism: perception and hallucination are fundamentally different kinds of mental episode. In veridical perception, we are in direct contact with external objects; in hallucination, we have a different kind of state that merely resembles perceptual experience from the inside. The *highest common factor* picture — that perception and hallucination share a common phenomenal core — is false.

The main challenge for direct realism is explaining illusions and hallucinations: if we are directly aware of external objects, how can we misperceive them? Disjunctivism answers by denying that perception and hallucination share a common phenomenal element; other direct realists accept that perception can be non-veridical while maintaining that it is still appropriately direct.

### Representationalism

Representationalism holds that perceptual states have *representational content* — they represent the world as being a certain way — and that their phenomenal character is determined by this content. When I see a red apple, my perceptual state represents there being a red apple in such-and-such location; its phenomenal character is the character of *being appeared to redly* in a certain spatial arrangement.

Representationalism explains the connection between phenomenal character and intentional content: two experiences can be phenomenally identical only if they have the same representational content. It also provides a natural account of misperception: the Müller-Lyer illusion involves a state that represents two lines as unequal when they are equal. Strong representationalism (Tye, Dretske) holds that phenomenal character is identical to a certain kind of representational content; weak representationalism allows that phenomenal character supervenes on content without being identical to it.

### Enactivism and the Sensorimotor Account

Enactivist approaches to perception (Noë, O'Regan and Noë) hold that perception is not a matter of building an internal representation of the world but of exercising sensorimotor skills — knowing how one's sensory inputs would change as one moves, manipulates objects, and explores the environment. To perceive a red apple is to have mastery of the sensorimotor contingencies associated with apples: knowing how the appearance would change as you move around it, pick it up, and so on.

Enactivism challenges the framework shared by sense-data theorists, representationalists, and direct realists: all of these assume that perception is a relation between a subject and an object (or a representation of an object). Enactivism proposes instead that perception is a form of *skillful engagement* with the environment, not a passive reception of inputs. This connects to broader debates about embodied and extended cognition (Unit 09).

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**Bertrand Russell** developed the sense-data theory in *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912), arguing that the immediate objects of perception are private sense-data and that the inference from sense-data to physical objects requires something like scientific theorizing.

**G.E. Moore** held in "The Refutation of Idealism" (1903) and related papers that we are directly aware of objects in the external world — a proto-direct-realist position.

**J.J. Gibson** developed *ecological realism*, arguing that perception is direct pick-up of *affordances* — the possibilities for action that the environment offers — rather than construction of an internal representation from sensory inputs. Gibson's work influenced enactivism and direct realism.

**John McDowell** argues in *Mind and World* (1994) that perception is conceptually structured and provides direct rational constraint on belief. McDowell's disjunctivism is motivated by the thought that without direct perceptual contact with the world, we cannot explain how perceptual experience provides reasons for belief.

**Michael Tye** defends strong representationalism in *Ten Problems of Consciousness* (1995), arguing that phenomenal properties are identical to certain representational properties — specifically, intentional properties of non-conceptual, analog representation.

**Alva Noë** develops enactivism in *Action in Perception* (2004), arguing that perception consists in the exercise of sensorimotor knowledge rather than the formation of internal representations. The book is a model of how philosophical and empirical considerations can be integrated.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Ayer, *The Foundations of Empirical Knowledge* (1940)**
The most systematic sense-datum theory, with careful attention to the argument from illusion and the epistemological case for sense-data. A good starting point for understanding the indirect realist tradition.

**2. McDowell, *Mind and World* (1994)**
A dense but important book arguing that perception is conceptually structured and provides direct rational constraint on belief. McDowell's disjunctivism and his response to the threat of the *Myth of the Given* are important for anyone thinking seriously about the epistemology of perception.

**3. Noë, *Action in Perception* (2004)**
The most developed statement of the enactivist approach to perception. Noë argues that perception is constitutively dependent on sensorimotor skill, and that this has consequences for how we think about the relationship between perception and cognition, phenomenal character, and the nature of consciousness.

**4. Tye, *Ten Problems of Consciousness* (1995)**
Tye's PANIC theory (Poised, Abstract, Nonconceptual Intentional Content) is the most fully developed representationalist account of phenomenal experience. The book addresses not just perception but the full range of phenomenal states (pain, moods, bodily sensations), making it the best single statement of the representationalist program.

**5. Clark, *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016) — chapter on perception**
Clark's treatment of perception within the predictive processing framework provides the most philosophically sophisticated account of how PP transforms our understanding of perception. The chapter on the "darkroom problem" (why don't predictive systems simply minimize prediction error by withdrawing from the world?) is especially good.

## Open Questions

**1. Is the argument from hallucination a decisive objection to direct realism?**
The argument holds that since hallucination is phenomenally indistinguishable from veridical perception, the two must have a common phenomenal element — the sense-datum. Disjunctivists deny this: they hold that the phenomenal character of veridical perception is constituted by direct contact with the world, not by any internal state shared with hallucination. Whether this is credible remains deeply disputed.

**2. Does perception have non-conceptual content?**
Tye and others argue that perceptual content is *non-conceptual*: it does not require the perceiver to possess the concepts used to describe what is represented. McDowell denies this, arguing that perceptual content must be conceptual if it is to provide rational constraint on belief. The debate has ramifications for both epistemology and philosophy of mind.

**3. Is predictive processing compatible with direct realism?**
PP seems to support a representationalist view of perception: the brain builds internal models and updates them in response to prediction error. But some philosophers argue that PP can be interpreted in a way compatible with direct realism, if the predictive models are understood as part of the perceptual relation rather than as mediating intermediaries. Whether PP is metaphysically neutral or commits us to a representationalist view is an open question.

**4. Can enactivism account for perceptual content?**
Noë's enactivism identifies perception with sensorimotor skill rather than with the formation of internal representations. But if there are no representational states in perception, how do we account for the fact that perception has content — that perceptual states can be accurate or inaccurate? Whether enactivism can deliver an adequate account of perceptual accuracy and error is not fully worked out.

**5. What is the relationship between perception and attention?**
Attention modifies perceptual experience — attended features appear more vivid, more precisely defined. Is this a perceptual fact (attention changes the content of perception) or a post-perceptual fact (attention changes how perceptual information is processed)? The relationship between attention and perceptual phenomenology remains unclear.
