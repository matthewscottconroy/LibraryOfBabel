# Sense Data Theory

Sense data theory was the dominant theory of perception in early analytic philosophy, associated with G.E. Moore, Bertrand Russell, H.H. Price, and A.J. Ayer. It holds that what we are immediately aware of in perception are not physical objects but mind-dependent entities called "sense data" — colored patches, sounds, smells, tastes, and other qualitative items that are directly given in experience.

**The Basic Claim**

When you look at a red apple, you are not directly aware of the apple itself — a physical object that exists independently of your perception. What you are directly aware of is a red, round sense datum — a mental entity whose existence and character depend on your experience of it.

The apple is not directly perceived; it is inferred or posited as the cause of the sense datum. Direct perception is always of sense data; perception of physical objects is indirect, mediated by sense data.

**The Argument from Illusion**

The primary argument for sense data theory is the "argument from illusion." Consider cases where experience is misleading:

- A straight stick partially submerged in water appears bent.
- Walls appear reddish when you remove red-tinted glasses.
- A coin appears oval when viewed at an angle.
- Hallucinations occur where no object is present.

In each case, the object of experience — the bent-appearing stick, the reddish wall, the oval-appearing coin, the hallucinated object — has properties (bent, reddish, oval) that the physical object lacks. If what we directly experience has properties that physical objects lack, then what we directly experience is not the physical object. It must be a mind-dependent entity — a sense datum — that has those properties.

This argument proceeds from the phenomenology of illusory experience to a general claim about all perception: even in veridical cases, what we are directly aware of is sense data, not physical objects.

**The Argument from Perceptual Relativity**

A related argument appeals to perceptual relativity: the phenomenal character of perception varies with viewing conditions — angle, distance, lighting, the state of the perceiver — even when the physical object remains the same.

The table looks different shapes from different angles; colors look different under different illumination. But the table itself doesn't change. What changes is the sense datum — the visual presentation. This supports the view that what we are directly aware of is the presentation, not the object.

**Problems for Sense Data Theory**

*The ontological commitment problem*: Sense data are a strange kind of entity — neither physical nor purely conceptual. Where are they? What are they made of? How do they relate to the physical world? Price developed elaborate ontologies of sense data, but critics found them unsatisfying.

*The regress of perception*: If we perceive physical objects by perceiving sense data, how do we perceive sense data? If perception always requires an intervening mental entity, then perceiving sense data would require perceiving data-of-sense-data, and so on. Sense data theorists typically hold that sense data are known by "acquaintance" without mediation — but this distinction needs justification.

*The translation problem*: If we are only directly aware of sense data, how do we know anything about physical objects? The sense datum account of perceptual knowledge is epistemic: our knowledge of the physical world is inferred from knowledge of sense data. But the inference seems difficult to justify, and critics argued that this leads to skepticism about the external world.

*The phenomenological criticism*: Austin, in *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962), argued that the sense data theory misrepresents the phenomenology of perception. We do not experience sense data and then infer objects; we experience objects directly. The sense datum framework distorts ordinary perceptual experience by assimilating all cases (veridical, illusory, hallucinatory) to a single model that fits none of them well.

**The Legacy of Sense Data Theory**

Sense data theory has been largely abandoned in contemporary philosophy, but it contributed significantly to the philosophy of perception by forcing clarity about the relationship between phenomenal experience and physical objects, the nature of perceptual error, and the epistemology of perception. The problems it raised — how to account for perceptual error, how to understand the relationship between appearance and reality — continue to define the landscape of contemporary theories of perception.
