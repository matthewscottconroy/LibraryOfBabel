# The Adverbial Theory of Perception

The adverbial theory of perception, developed by Roderick Chisholm and C.J. Ducasse, was proposed as a way to preserve the phenomenological insights of sense data theory while avoiding its problematic ontological commitments. Instead of positing sense data as entities that are objects of perception, the adverbial theory treats apparent qualitative properties of experience as modifications of the act of sensing itself.

**The Core Move**

Sense data theory says: "I perceive a red, round sense datum." There is an object of perception — the sense datum — which is red and round.

Adverbial theory says: "I am perceiving red-ly and round-ly." The "red" and "round" are adverbs modifying the act of sensing, not adjectives describing a separate object.

The idea is that when you see a red apple, you are "sensing redly" — your act of perception has a certain qualitative character — without there needing to be any red object (a sense datum) that you are sensing. The red quality characterizes the sensing, not a sensed entity.

**The Linguistic Analysis**

The adverbial theory relies on a particular way of paraphrasing sensory reports. "I am having an experience of red" becomes "I am sensing red-ly" or "I am experiencing red-wise." The property terms ("red," "round," "loud") become adverbs modifying the mental verb rather than predicates of an object.

This is not just linguistic tidying; it is a substantive ontological claim. There are no sense data — no mental objects with properties. There are only mental acts with qualitative modifications. The phenomenal character of experience is a feature of the act, not of any object.

**Advantages**

The adverbial theory avoids the ontological proliferation of sense data. We do not need to posit a special category of mental entities with strange properties (where exactly is a sense datum? what is it made of?). We need only mental acts — events of perceiving — with qualitative characters.

It also handles cases of hallucination more naturally. When I hallucinate a pink elephant, there is no pink elephant sense datum that I perceive. There is just a hallucinatory perception-act that has the qualitative character of being pink-elephant-like.

**Problems**

*The many-property problem*: Suppose I am perceiving a large, red, round object. The adverbial theory says I am sensing large-ly, red-ly, and round-ly. But this does not capture the fact that the redness and roundness go together — that they characterize the same apparent object. "I am seeing a large red round shape" says something different from "I am seeing a large shape, a red thing, and a round thing." The adverbial theory has difficulty capturing the structural unity of experience.

This is sometimes called the "many-properties problem": if the theory treats each sensory quality as a separate modification of the sensing act, it loses the information about which qualities belong together as aspects of a single apparent object.

*The regress worry*: The adverbial theory says that experience has qualitative modifications without positing objects of experience. But what does it mean for an act to have a qualitative modification? The "red-ly" character of the sensing seems to require something like a red quality in the experience — and explaining what this quality is may require reintroducing something like sense data.

*The phenomenology*: Adverbial theory is motivated partly by its phenomenological plausibility. But many philosophers of mind find it phenomenologically implausible: experience presents itself as an awareness of objects with qualities, not as a modification of an act. The adverbial reconstruction loses the object-directedness that seems phenomenologically primary.

**The Current Status**

The adverbial theory is now less discussed than direct realism and representationalism, but it was an important step in the philosophy of perception. By challenging the sense data framework's commitment to perceptual objects, it opened space for theories that focus on the intentional, representational character of experience rather than on perceived objects.

Contemporary intentionalist theories of perception preserve the spirit of the adverbial theory's move — locating the qualitative character of experience in the experience itself rather than in a perceived object — while providing a more developed account of how perceptual experience represents the world.
