# Further Reading: Classical Theories of Perception

## Primary Sources

**G.E. Moore, "The Refutation of Idealism" (1903) and "The Status of Sense-Data" (1913–14)** — Moore helped establish the sense-datum theory: in perceptual experience we are directly acquainted with mental entities (sense-data) that have the sensible qualities things appear to have. These papers helped set the agenda for twentieth-century analytic philosophy of perception.

**H.H. Price, *Perception* (1932)** — The most systematic defense of the sense-datum theory. Price distinguishes sensing (awareness of sense-data) from perceptual judgment, and analyzes the distinctions between veridical perception, illusion, and hallucination within the sense-datum framework.

**C.J. Ducasse, "Moore's Refutation of Idealism" (1942)** — Ducasse defends the adverbial theory against the sense-datum theory: instead of saying one is aware of a red sense-datum, we should say one senses "redly" — the redness is a modification of the act of sensing, not a property of an object.

## Secondary Literature

**A.J. Ayer, *The Foundations of Empirical Knowledge* (1940)** — Ayer defends the sense-datum theory as the appropriate analysis of perceptual experience, arguing that appeals to sense-data resolve the problems of illusion and hallucination.

**Roderick Chisholm, *Perceiving: A Philosophical Study* (1957)** — A careful study of the logic of perceptual language and the analysis of knowledge through perception, including a sustained defense of the sense-datum approach.

**J.L. Austin, *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962)** — Austin's devastating critique of the argument from illusion, which he argues relies on confused uses of ordinary language. Austin shows that the sense-datum theory rests on philosophical mistakes about how words like "see," "real," and "looks" function.

**Mohan Matthen, "Seeing, Doing, and Knowing" (2005)** — A contemporary treatment that connects classical debates about perceptual theory to current issues in epistemology and cognitive science.
