# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Classical Theories of Perception

## Key Arguments

**The Argument from Illusion**
A.J. Ayer and C.D. Broad argue that since perceptual experiences can be qualitatively identical when one is veridical and one illusory (seeing a straight stick vs. seeing a straight stick that appears bent in water), and since in the illusory case we are directly aware of a sense-datum (a bent-appearing object) rather than the straight stick, it follows that in both cases we are directly aware of sense-data—not of physical objects directly. The argument from illusion is the classical motivation for indirect realism and sense-data theory.

**The Argument from Hallucination**
The argument from hallucination extends the argument from illusion: since a hallucination can be qualitatively indistinguishable from a genuine perception, and since in the hallucinatory case there is no external object to be perceived, there must be an internal object of awareness—a sense-datum or phenomenal object. By the common kind assumption (veridical and hallucinatory experiences of the same type are experiences of the same kind of thing), veridical perceptions also involve awareness of such internal objects. Indirect realism holds that we perceive physical objects only indirectly, via sense-data.

**The Adverbial Theory as a Response**
The adverbial theory (C.J. Ducasse, Roderick Chisholm) holds that perceptual reports should be analyzed adverbially: rather than saying "I perceive a red sense-datum," we should say "I am appeared to redly." Perceptual experience is characterized by the manner in which one is appeared to, not by an inner object that appears. This eliminates the ontological commitment to sense-data while preserving the phenomenal character of experience. The adverbial theory faces the "many properties problem": how do we analyze "I am appeared to redly and squarely" without reintroducing objects?

**Reid's Direct Realism Against the Way of Ideas**
Thomas Reid argued against the "way of ideas" (the empiricist tradition from Descartes through Locke to Hume) that the postulation of ideas or sense-data as intermediaries between mind and world leads to skepticism: if we only ever perceive ideas, we have no grounds for inferring an external world. Reid argued that perception is direct—we perceive external objects themselves, not internal representatives of them. The argument was neglected for centuries but anticipated contemporary direct realism and disjunctivism.

**The Causal Theory of Perception (Grice)**
H.P. Grice argued that perception requires a causal connection between the perceived object and the perceptual state: to see X is (roughly) to have a visual experience causally produced by X in the right way. This causal condition distinguishes genuine perception from veridical hallucination (a case where you seem to see X and X is present, but your experience is caused by something else). The causal theory makes perception externally grounded and is compatible with either indirect or direct realism.

## Core Concepts

**Sense-Data Theory**
Sense-data theory holds that the immediate objects of perceptual awareness are sense-data—subjective, mind-dependent entities that are directly given to consciousness. Sense-data have the qualitative properties that experience represents: the redness of a red experience, the elliptical shape of a coin seen at an angle. Physical objects are inferred to exist beyond sense-data (indirect realism) or are constructs out of them (phenomenalism). Sense-data theory was dominant in early analytic philosophy but was largely abandoned due to the epistemological problem of inference, the qualia problem, and the lack of independent evidence for sense-data.

**Indirect Realism**
Indirect realism (or representationalism in the classical sense) holds that we perceive physical objects only indirectly, by being directly aware of mental intermediaries—sense-data, ideas, or representations—that are caused by and represent physical objects. Locke is the paradigmatic indirect realist: primary qualities (extension, shape) resemble the properties of physical objects; secondary qualities (color, taste, smell) are powers in objects to cause sense-data that do not resemble those powers. Indirect realism faces the veil of perception problem: if we only perceive representations, how can we know what lies beyond them?

**Adverbialism**
Adverbialism holds that perceptual experience is not a relation between a subject and a phenomenal object (sense-datum) but a way of being appeared to: "I perceive a red after-image" is analyzed as "I am appeared to redly." Perceptual experience is characterized by the manner (adverb) of experience, not by any object. This dissolves the ontological commitment to sense-data while preserving phenomenal character. The main challenge is the "many properties problem": if I am appeared to redly and squarely, do I have one experience with two adverbial qualifications, or two experiences, and how do they combine?

**The Causal Theory of Perception**
The causal theory holds that perceiving an object requires a causal connection between the object and the perceptual experience: S perceives X if and only if X causes (in the right way) S's experience. The "right way" condition is needed to exclude deviant causal chains (the experience that X causes by a roundabout route, such as seeing X on a TV monitor that turns out to be live footage, rather than recording). The causal theory explains why hallucinations and illusions fail to be genuine perceptions (they are not caused by the object perceived) and is compatible with both indirect realism and direct realism.

**The Common Kind Assumption**
The common kind assumption holds that veridical and qualitatively identical hallucinatory experiences are experiences of the same kind—the same type of mental state. This assumption is used in the argument from hallucination: since hallucinatory and veridical experiences are qualitatively indistinguishable, and since in hallucination there is no external object, both kinds of experience must have the same kind of immediate object (a sense-datum or phenomenal state). Disjunctivism (McDowell, Hinton) rejects the common kind assumption, holding that veridical and hallucinatory experiences are fundamentally different kinds of state.

## Thought Experiments

**The Bent Stick in Water**
A straight stick half-submerged in water appears bent to vision while feeling straight to touch. Both the visual and tactile experiences cannot be equally accurate; one must be an illusion. The visual experience of a "bent stick" is not a perception of an actually bent stick but of a sense-datum or appearance. The argument from illusion uses this case to support sense-data theory: if we are aware of a bent appearance in the illusory case, we are always aware of appearances, not things themselves, even in veridical perception.

**Veridical Hallucination**
Imagine that you are having an exact hallucination of a red apple—a hallucinatory experience that is qualitatively identical to a genuine perception of a red apple. Now suppose there is actually a red apple in front of you, but it plays no causal role in your hallucination (your hallucination is caused by a brain stimulation). Do you perceive the apple? Most theories say no—this is a case of coincidental veridical experience but not genuine perception. The case motivates the causal condition: perception requires the right causal connection between object and experience.

**The Double Vision Case**
You press your finger against your eyeball and see double—two candles instead of one. Are you now seeing two sense-data? Direct realists have difficulty here: the doubling seems to be a feature of experience rather than of the physical world. Sense-data theorists take this as evidence that the immediate objects of vision are sense-data (since there is only one candle, the doubling must be in the visual data). The case is used to argue that visual experience has phenomenal properties that are not properties of physical objects, requiring some internal representation or sense-datum to be the bearer of those properties.
