# Direct Realism

Direct realism holds that in ordinary, veridical perception we are directly aware of mind-independent physical objects and their properties. We see tables and chairs, not representations of tables and chairs or sense data caused by tables and chairs. The world is present to us in perception without any mental intermediary.

This is in many ways the commonsense view — we certainly think of ourselves as seeing the world directly — but making it philosophically precise and defending it against various objections is a substantial undertaking.

**Naive vs. Critical Direct Realism**

Naive direct realism is the simplest version: in perception, we directly perceive objects exactly as they are. The table really is the shape and color it appears to be. The world and its appearance in experience are, in the best case, identical.

Naive direct realism faces the obvious objection that it cannot account for illusions and perceptual variability. The table appears different colors under different lighting conditions; the stick looks bent in water. If we directly perceive the table as it is, why does it sometimes look other than it is?

Critical direct realism (or "sophisticated" direct realism) accepts that experience can misrepresent reality while maintaining that veridical perception involves direct awareness of the physical world. In veridical cases, we directly perceive the object; in illusory cases, our perception represents the object inaccurately, but there is still a real object that we are (in some sense) in contact with.

**Disjunctivism**

The most influential contemporary development of direct realism is "disjunctivism," associated with J.M. Hinton, Paul Snowdon, and John McDowell. Disjunctivism holds that perceptual experience is not a single kind of mental state with a common phenomenological core across veridical and hallucinatory cases.

Instead, there are two fundamentally different kinds of experience:
- Veridical perception: a genuine encounter with a mind-independent object, in which the object itself is a constituent of the experience.
- Hallucination (or illusion): a mental state that resembles perception from the inside but lacks direct contact with a real object.

On this view, there is no single kind of experience — no common factor — shared by seeing a red apple and hallucinating a red apple. The experiences may be phenomenologically similar (indistinguishable from the inside), but they are metaphysically different: veridical experience constitutively involves the object; hallucinatory experience does not.

This is the "disjunction": either (a veridical perception or) (a hallucination). The sense data theorist wrongly abstracts a common element from both and calls it "sense data." Disjunctivism denies the abstraction.

**McDowell's "World-Involving" Experience**

John McDowell defends a version of direct realism in which perceptual experience is "world-involving": the world itself — mind-independent objects and properties — is part of the content of perceptual experience. The experience does not merely represent the world; the world is constitutively part of it.

This connects McDowell's epistemology: if the world is part of experience (not just represented by it), then veridical perception provides direct knowledge of the world. The epistemological access to the world is not mediated by inner representations; the world is given in experience.

McDowell's approach connects to the "openness" of perception: in seeing, the world is open to us, disclosed in experience. This phenomenological point — that perception has an "openness" or "transparency" that representationalist accounts miss — is central to his defense of direct realism.

**The Argument from Hallucination**

The principal challenge to direct realism is the argument from hallucination. A hallucination can be indistinguishable from veridical perception. If they are indistinguishable, they must have something in common — presumably some phenomenal quality or experiential state. And this common factor is not the physical object (since in the hallucination case there is no relevant physical object). So the common factor must be something mental — a sense datum or mental representation.

If this argument is valid, direct realism is false: even in veridical cases, what we are aware of is the mental common factor, not the physical object.

Disjunctivism responds by denying the "common factor" premise. Even if veridical and hallucinatory experiences are indistinguishable from the inside, this does not show they have a common nature. Many things look identical without being identical. The indistinguishability is an epistemic fact; it does not entail a metaphysical common factor.

**Evaluating Direct Realism**

Direct realism has significant phenomenological support: experience presents itself as direct openness to the world. It has epistemological virtues: it avoids the "veil of perception" that sense data theory erects between mind and world.

Its challenges are real: accounting for illusions, hallucinations, and perceptual variability without positing mental intermediaries requires philosophical care. But contemporary disjunctivist developments have shown that direct realism can be defended with considerable sophistication.
