# Representationalism About Perception

Representationalism holds that perceptual experience has intentional content — it represents the world as being a certain way. The phenomenal character of experience is determined by its representational content: seeing red is seeing things as red-colored, and the "what it's like" of seeing red is fully determined by what the experience represents.

This connects the theory of perception to the broader theory of intentionality and mental content, making perceptual phenomenology continuous with the rest of cognitive science rather than a special domain requiring different explanatory resources.

**The Core Claim**

The representationalist thesis has two parts:

*Intentionalist claim*: Perceptual experiences are intentional states — they have content that represents the world as being a certain way. Seeing a red apple is not just "having a red experience"; it is representing a red apple as present.

*Phenomenal intentionalism*: The phenomenal character of an experience (what it's like to have it) is fully determined by its intentional content. Two experiences with the same intentional content have the same phenomenal character; two experiences that differ in phenomenal character differ in intentional content.

The second claim is stronger and more controversial. It holds that there is nothing to the "what it's like" of experience beyond how the experience represents the world to be.

**Dretske's Representationalism**

Fred Dretske argued in *Naturalizing the Mind* (1995) that the phenomenal qualities of experience are the properties that experience represents objects as having. The redness you experience when seeing a red apple is not a property of your experience; it is a property of the apple that your experience represents.

This "intentionalist" or "externalist" account of qualia has the striking implication that qualia — the supposedly intrinsic, internal, qualitative properties of experience — are actually properties of external objects, represented by experiences that have a transparent character.

The metaphor of "transparency" captures this: when you try to attend to your experience of redness, you "see through" the experience to the redness in the world. You don't attend to an internal property of your experience; you attend to the redness of the apple.

**Tye's PANIC Theory**

Michael Tye developed a systematic representationalist theory in *Ten Problems of Consciousness* (1995). His "PANIC" theory holds that phenomenal states are Poised, Abstract, Non-conceptual, Intentional Contents.

*Poised*: The content is poised to directly affect belief formation and other cognitive processes. It is "front and center" in cognition.

*Abstract*: The content involves properties (redness, roundness) rather than specific objects.

*Non-conceptual*: The content can be instantiated without having the relevant concepts. You can see a specific shade of color without having a concept for that precise shade.

*Intentional*: The content represents properties as instantiated.

Tye argues that experiences with PANIC content constitute phenomenal experience. The phenomenal character is constituted by the PANIC content; there is nothing further to explain.

**The Transparency Argument**

Both Dretske and Tye rely on the "transparency" or "diaphanousness" of experience. G.E. Moore noticed that when he tries to attend to his experience of blue, he always "sees through" the experience to the blue thing in the world. The experience itself seems transparent — you look through it, not at it.

Representationalists take this transparency as evidence that the qualitative character of experience is constituted by its representational content — there is no "layer" of experience separate from what the experience represents.

Critics argue that transparency is misinterpreted. The difficulty of attending to the experience as such does not show that experience lacks intrinsic qualitative character; it shows that introspection is hard or that experience is phenomenologically structured as "world-directed." Phenomenologists like Zahavi argue that pre-reflective self-awareness is present even in experience that is ostensibly "about" the world.

**Problems for Representationalism**

*The inverted qualia objection*: If two experiences have the same content, they have the same phenomenal character. But inverted qualia — same function, different qualia — seem conceivable. If they are possible, they are a counterexample to phenomenal intentionalism. The representationalist must argue that inverted qualia are not genuinely possible.

*The "what it's like" residue*: Some philosophers argue that there is something to the "what it's like" of experience over and above its representational content. Block argues that phenomenal consciousness has an "intrinsic qualitative feel" that is not captured by any intentional description.

*Mental paint*: Some argue that we are aware of our own experience as well as its content — that there is "mental paint" (Block's term) as well as "the world seen through" the experience. Representationalism denies mental paint; its defenders argue that the intuition of mental paint reflects misunderstanding of what transparency means.
