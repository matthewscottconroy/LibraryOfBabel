# Further Reading: Direct Realism and Representationalism

## Primary Sources

**John McDowell, *Mind and World* (1994)** — McDowell defends a form of direct realism about perception: in non-hallucinatory perception, the world itself is present to the mind, and conceptual content is already present in experience. A sophisticated response to the disjunctivist tradition and a critique of the "Given."

**Michael Dummett, "Common Sense and Physics" (1979)** — Defends a form of naive realism about perception, arguing against the retreat to indirect or representative realism demanded by the argument from illusion.

**Fred Dretske, *Seeing and Knowing* (1969)** — Dretske's early analysis of perception distinguishes "epistemic" from "non-epistemic" seeing, arguing that genuine perception is not constituted by belief and that direct awareness of the world is possible.

**Michael Tye, *Ten Problems of Consciousness* (1995)** — Tye defends representationalism — the view that phenomenal properties of experience are representational properties — against qualia-based objections. His PANIC theory holds that experiences are poised, abstract, non-conceptual, intentional content.

## Secondary Literature

**Paul Snowdon, "Perception, Vision and Causation" (1980)** — An influential defense of disjunctivism: the perceptual situation in veridical perception is fundamentally different from that in hallucination, and no common factor (sense-datum or representation) is shared.

**John Campbell, *Reference and Consciousness* (2002)** — Campbell argues that conscious perception of an object is what enables singular reference to it, connecting direct realism about perception to issues in the philosophy of language.

**Alex Byrne and Heather Logue (eds.), *Disjunctivism: Contemporary Readings* (2009)** — A collection of key papers on disjunctivism as a rival to both sense-datum theory and representationalism, including papers by Hinton, Snowdon, McDowell, and their critics.
