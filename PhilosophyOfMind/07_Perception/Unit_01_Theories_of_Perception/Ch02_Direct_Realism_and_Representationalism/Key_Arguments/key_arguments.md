# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Direct Realism and Representationalism

## Key Arguments

**The Disjunctivist Argument Against the Common Kind Assumption**
McDowell, Hinton, and Snowdon argue that the argument from hallucination rests on the common kind assumption—that veridical and hallucinatory experiences are the same type of mental state. Disjunctivism denies this: a genuine perception of a red apple is fundamentally different from a mere hallucination that happens to match, even if they are subjectively indistinguishable. Genuine perception is a state in which the world itself is present to the subject; hallucination is a distinct, defective state. Denying the common kind assumption allows direct realism to stand.

**The Transparency Argument for Direct Realism**
G.E. Moore observed that when we introspect visual experience, we seem to "see through" the experience to the objects themselves—the experience is "transparent" in that introspective attention reveals not mental intermediaries but the colors and shapes of physical objects. If experience were really of internal sense-data, introspection would reveal the sense-data; since it reveals objects, experience is directly of objects. The transparency argument supports direct realism and representationalism: experience is constituted by its representational content, and that content is about the external world.

**The Intentionalist/Representationalist Argument**
Tye, Dretske, and Harman argue that the phenomenal character of experience is constituted by its representational content: what an experience is like depends entirely on what it represents. The redness of a visual experience just is the representation of a red external object (or of red light). If two experiences have the same representational content, they have the same phenomenal character; if they differ phenomenally, they must differ representationally. This view (intentionalism or representationalism) explains how phenomenal properties can be physical (representational properties are causal-functional properties) without eliminativism.

**The Argument from Perceptual Presence**
Direct realists argue that perception has a distinctive phenomenological character of "presence"—objects are not inferred or constructed but given directly. This character of presence is explained by the direct epistemic access to external objects that direct realism posits: the object is literally part of the perceptual state, not merely represented by it. Indirect realism cannot easily explain the phenomenology of perceptual presence, since on that view all that is present to the subject is the internal representation.

**McDowell's Argument for Conceptualism**
McDowell argues in Mind and World that perceptual experience must have conceptual content—content that is articulable in concepts that can figure in beliefs—in order for perception to serve as rational evidence for beliefs. If perception had non-conceptual content, it could not stand in rational (rather than merely causal) relations to belief. McDowell's conceptualism is a form of direct realism: the world is directly given in experience, but it is given as conceptually structured—as falling under concepts that belong to the subject's rational capacities.

## Core Concepts

**Direct Realism (Naive Realism)**
Direct realism holds that in successful perception, we are directly aware of external physical objects—we perceive the objects themselves, not any mental intermediaries. The objects we perceive are mind-independent: they would exist whether or not anyone perceived them. Direct realism contrasts with indirect realism (we perceive objects via inner representations) and idealism (objects depend on perception for their existence). Contemporary direct realists (McDowell, Snowdon, Martin) often develop "relationalist" versions: perception is a genuine relation between subject and object, not just a representational state.

**Disjunctivism**
Disjunctivism holds that perceptual experience is not a single type of mental state that occurs both in veridical and in hallucinatory cases; rather, the perceptual case is one kind of state (direct awareness of an external object) and the hallucinatory case is a different kind of state (a mere appearance). The two states may be phenomenologically indistinguishable to the subject, but they are metaphysically different. Disjunctivism supports direct realism by blocking the argument from hallucination: that argument requires the common kind assumption that disjunctivism rejects.

**Intentionalist Representationalism**
Intentionalist representationalism (Tye, Dretske, Harman) holds that the phenomenal character of experience is identical with its representational (intentional) content—specifically, with the accurate representational content of the experience. Seeing red just is accurately representing a red surface. On this view, phenomenal properties are not qualia in the traditional sense (intrinsic, non-representational) but are constituted by representational properties. This makes phenomenal consciousness compatible with physicalism and functionalism, since representation is naturalizable.

**The Transparency of Experience**
The transparency of experience is Moore's observation that perceptual experience is "transparent" to introspection: when we try to attend to the experience rather than the object, we find ourselves looking through the experience to the external world. The experience itself has no additional phenomenal properties beyond its representational content—there are no "inner color patches" or sense-data that we are aware of in addition to colors of objects. Transparency supports representationalism: the phenomenal character of experience just is its representational content.

**The Common Kind Assumption**
The common kind assumption holds that veridical perceptions and qualitatively identical hallucinations are states of the same mental kind—they share all intrinsic mental properties. This assumption underlies the argument from hallucination (which infers from the hallucinatory case to the veridical case). Disjunctivists deny this assumption: veridical perception involves a genuine relation to an external object, which is constitutive of its mental character; hallucination lacks this relation and is therefore a different kind of state, even if introspectively indistinguishable.

## Thought Experiments

**The Brain-in-a-Vat and Phenomenal Presence**
A brain in a vat is stimulated to have experiences qualitatively identical to normal perception. Direct realists face a challenge: the BIV's experiences are qualitatively the same as genuine perceptions, yet there are no external objects present. Disjunctivists respond that the BIV's experiences are hallucinatory—they merely seem to be experiences of external objects. Representationalists respond that the BIV represents the same properties the normal perceiver represents, but the representations are not caused by the relevant properties—hence the BIV is hallucinating, not perceiving.

**McDowell's Demon Revisited**
McDowell argues against the Cartesian demon scenario as a model of the epistemology of perception: if we were systematically deceived by a demon, our experiences would be hallucinations rather than perceptions, and our "knowledge" would be undermined. But actual perception is a different kind of state: it is a genuine relation to the world in which the world is present to the subject. The demon scenario does not show that perception and hallucination are the same kind of state; it shows only that they can be subjectively indistinguishable, which is compatible with their being metaphysically distinct.

**The Case of Perfect Hallucination**
Imagine a perfect hallucination: a hallucination of a red apple that is indistinguishable from genuine perception of a red apple, and that occurs in circumstances where a red apple is present directly in front of the subject. Does the presence of the apple make this a genuine perception, or is it still a hallucination? The causal theory says it is still a hallucination if the apple plays no causal role in the experience. Disjunctivism agrees: the right kind of causal connection—which constitutes a genuine perceptual relation—is absent. The case tests our intuitions about what makes something a genuine perception.
