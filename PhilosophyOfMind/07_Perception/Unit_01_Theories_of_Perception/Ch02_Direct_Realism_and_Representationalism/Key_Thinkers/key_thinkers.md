# Key Thinkers: Direct Realism and Representationalism

---

**John McDowell** (b. 1942)

McDowell defends a form of disjunctivism and direct realism: in veridical perception, the fact that P (e.g., that the cat is on the mat) is directly present to the mind — perception is a form of openness to the world, not mediation through mental representations. He argues against the "Given" — the idea that experience can serve as evidence independently of conceptual structure — while insisting that experience is already conceptually structured. His view opposes both classical sense-datum theory and modern representationalism.

*Key works:* *Mind and World* (1994); "Criteria, Defeasibility, and Knowledge" (1982); *Perception as a Capacity for Knowledge* (2011)

---

**Michael Tye** (b. 1950)

Tye is the leading defender of representationalism (or intentionalism) about perceptual experience. He argues that all phenomenal properties of experience are representational properties — what it is like to undergo an experience is fully determined by its representational content. His PANIC theory holds that phenomenal states are Poised, Abstract, Non-conceptual, Intentional Content states: they represent properties of the external world and their "feel" just is this representational character. This view makes phenomenal consciousness tractable for a physicalist theory.

*Key works:* *Ten Problems of Consciousness* (1995); *Consciousness, Color, and Content* (2000); *Consciousness and Persons* (2003)

---

**Fred Dretske** (1932–2013)

Dretske's representationalism holds that experience consists in the acquisition of information — all experiential properties are informational properties. In *Naturalizing the Mind* (1995), he argues that phenomenal properties are represented, not instantiated: when one sees red, one's experience represents redness as a feature of the environment. This "displaced perception" account makes phenomenal consciousness fully explicable in terms of the information-bearing relations between mind and world.

*Key works:* *Seeing and Knowing* (1969); *Knowledge and the Flow of Information* (1981); *Naturalizing the Mind* (1995)

---

**J.M. Hinton** (1939–2000)

Hinton is generally credited with introducing disjunctivism in "Visual Experiences" (1967), arguing that "having an experience as of seeing X" does not pick out a common type of mental event shared between veridical perception and hallucination. Rather, perceptual verbs express disjunctions of fundamentally different states: genuine perception (in which the object is present) or mere hallucination. His work influenced Snowdon, McDowell, and the contemporary disjunctivist tradition.

*Key works:* "Visual Experiences" (1967); *Experiences: An Inquiry into Some Ambiguities* (1973)
