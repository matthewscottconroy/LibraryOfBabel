# Non-Conceptual Content of Perception

The debate about non-conceptual content in perception concerns whether the content of perceptual experience must be expressible in the concepts the perceiver possesses, or whether experience can have content that outstrips the perceiver's conceptual capacities.

**The Motivating Observation**

Consider a case: an expert ornithologist and a novice observe the same bird. The ornithologist sees a finch of a specific species; the novice sees "a small brown bird." Their experiences differ, presumably, in what they represent. But the novice's visual system registers exactly the same retinal pattern as the ornithologist's. Is there a sense in which both perceive the same thing, despite their different conceptual capacities?

Or consider color experience. The human visual system can discriminate millions of distinct color shades — far more than our color vocabulary can name. We can experience color differences that we lack the concepts to capture linguistically. It seems that perceptual experience has richer content than our concepts can express.

These observations motivate the idea of non-conceptual content: perceptual content that does not require the perceiver to possess the relevant concepts.

**The Conceptualist Position**

John McDowell argues in *Mind and World* (1994) that all perceptual experience has conceptual content — the content of experience is always expressible using concepts the perceiver possesses (perhaps under appropriate enrichment).

McDowell's motivation is epistemological: he wants to account for how perceptual experience can rationally justify beliefs. For experience to justify a belief, the content of the experience must be the same kind of thing as the content of the belief — it must be conceptual. Non-conceptual content, being sub-conceptual, could not stand in rational justificatory relations to beliefs.

This requires a liberal account of concept possession: concepts are not limited to explicitly articulated categories. The concept one applies to a specific shade of red that one can recognize but cannot name is still a concept — a capacity to apply that concept across multiple applications.

**The Anti-Conceptualist Position**

Gareth Evans argued, against McDowell, that perceptual content is "informational" rather than conceptual. The information system that underlies perception operates below the level of conceptual thought. Perceptual states have analog content — content that is not divided into discrete conceptual categories but that varies continuously with the properties of the environment.

This non-conceptual content is still content in the sense that it represents the world: it has accuracy conditions. But it does not require the possessor to have any concepts.

Christopher Peacocke developed a systematic theory of non-conceptual content in terms of "scenarios" — specifications of how things are arranged in the perceived environment in terms of distances, directions, and spatial relations. Scenario content is more primitive than propositional content and does not require concept possession.

**The Richness of Perceptual Experience**

Ned Block's argument for phenomenal overflow is relevant here. Block argues that the visual experience of briefly presented arrays — as demonstrated in Sperling's (1960) iconic memory experiments — is phenomenally richer than can be accessed by attention and reported. The experience contains more information than the conceptual report can capture.

If phenomenal overflow occurs, then perceptual experience has phenomenal content that exceeds what can be conceptualized and reported — strong evidence for non-conceptual content.

**The Infant and Animal Consideration**

Non-conceptualism about perception has the virtue of making perception available to non-conceptual beings. Infants and non-human animals perceive the world — they respond differentially to colors, shapes, and faces — but they do not possess many of the concepts that characterize adult human perception.

If all perceptual content were conceptual, infants and animals would have no perceptual content at all — which seems wrong. Non-conceptual content allows for perception without concept possession.

McDowell's response: infants and animals are not in the business of rational justification; they have sensitivity to the environment (sub-personal processing), but this is not the same as genuine perceptual experience with intentional content. Genuine perceptual experience requires a mind that is conceptually structured.

**The Significance for Philosophy of Mind**

The conceptual/non-conceptual debate matters for:

*Epistemology*: If perceptual content is non-conceptual, how does it rationally constrain belief? The Sellarsian point — that only what is "in the space of reasons" can justify beliefs — seems to require conceptual content.

*Cognitive development*: The debate bears on how conceptual thought develops from more primitive forms of cognition.

*Animal cognition*: Whether animals have perceptual states with genuine intentional content depends on the requirements for content.

*The hard problem*: If phenomenal experience can have non-conceptual content — content that outstrips the subject's concepts — this is evidence that phenomenal character is not simply constituted by conceptual representation.
