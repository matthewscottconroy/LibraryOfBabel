# Further Reading: Perceptual Content

## Primary Sources

**Gareth Evans, *The Varieties of Reference* (1982)** — Evans argues that perceptual content involves nonconceptual, scenario-based representation of egocentric space. His analysis of demonstrative thought grounded in perception established the framework for subsequent debates about nonconceptual content.

**Christopher Peacocke, *A Study of Concepts* (1992), Chapter 3** — Peacocke develops the notion of "scenario content" as the nonconceptual component of perceptual experience: a content that specifies how surfaces and objects are arranged relative to the perceiver, without requiring the subject to possess any concepts.

**John McDowell, *Mind and World* (1994)** — McDowell argues against the notion of nonconceptual perceptual content, insisting that the content of experience is always already conceptually structured ("spontaneity permeates sensibility"). Experience can serve as a reason for belief only if it has conceptual content.

## Secondary Literature

**Tim Crane, "The Nonconceptual Content of Experience" (1992)** — A clear defense of nonconceptual perceptual content, arguing that there are aspects of perceptual experience (fine-grained color, spatial relations) that outrun our conceptual capacities.

**Susan Hurley, *Consciousness in Action* (1998)** — Hurley's complex treatment of the relationship between perception, action, and content, arguing against a simple input-output model in favor of a "dynamic singularity" of perception and action.

**Alex Byrne, "Perception and Conceptual Content" (2005)** — A careful survey of the conceptual/nonconceptual content debate, examining the different versions of the nonconceptualist position and the main objections to each.

**Fred Dretske, "Conscious Experience" (1993)** — Argues that the phenomenal character of experience consists in its representational content, supporting the view that perceptual content is at least partly nonconceptual in the relevant sense.
