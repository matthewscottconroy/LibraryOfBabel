# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Perceptual Content

## Key Arguments

**McDowell's Argument for Conceptualist Perception**
McDowell argues in Mind and World that perceptual experience must have conceptual content—content that is articulable in concepts that can figure as premises in inferential reasoning. If experience had non-conceptual content (content that does not involve concepts), it could not stand in rational, justificatory relations to beliefs; it could only stand in causal relations. Since we treat perceptual experience as a reason for believing (not merely a cause of believing), experience must be conceptually structured. This is McDowell's "no outside-the-space-of-reasons" constraint.

**Evans's Argument for Non-Conceptual Content**
Gareth Evans argued that perceptual states have a distinctive, non-conceptual "information state" that is finer-grained than any concept the subject possesses. The precise shade of red I perceive is richer than any concept I have for shades of red—I can discriminate more shades than I have concepts for. Evans's "fineness of grain" argument supports non-conceptual content: perception carries information that exceeds the subject's conceptual resources. This argument is used to distinguish perceptual content from belief content and to resist McDowell's collapsing of perception into conceptual judgment.

**Crane's Argument for Non-Conceptual Perception**
Tim Crane argues that perception does not require the perceiver to possess concepts for the properties perceived: a child who has no concept of "ellipse" can still perceive an object as elliptical. Perception tracks properties that exist independently of whether the perceiver has the concept—it is concept-independent in a way that belief is not. This argument from the possibility of concept-independent perception supports non-conceptualism and challenges McDowell's view that only conceptually structured experience can serve as evidence for belief.

**The Argument from Perception-Belief Fixation**
Philosophers in the Sellarsian tradition (McDowell, Brandom) argue that the relation between perception and belief is normative and rational: you see something red, and this gives you a reason to believe there is something red here. For this rational relation to hold, the perceptual state must have content that is already "in the space of reasons"—conceptually structured. A non-conceptual perceptual state could at most cause a belief; it could not serve as a rational reason for one. This argument motivates conceptualism by appeal to the epistemic role of perception in knowledge-acquisition.

**Peacocke's Scenario Content**
Christopher Peacocke distinguished two kinds of perceptual content: "scenario content" (the way the scene is represented as laid out in egocentric space—distances, directions, and surfaces represented relative to the subject's body) and "protopropositional content" (the content that attributes properties to objects at locations). Scenario content is pre-conceptual and spatially fine-grained; protopropositional content is closer to conceptual content. This distinction supports non-conceptualism by showing that the most basic perceptual content is not propositional and does not require concept possession.

## Core Concepts

**Conceptualism**
Conceptualism holds that the content of perceptual experience is fully conceptual: all the properties that experience represents are represented via concepts that the perceiver possesses and can deploy in thought and inference. McDowell is the primary defender. Conceptualism supports the view that experience can stand in rational, justificatory relations to beliefs (not merely causal ones), and it preserves the unity of the mind by making experience and belief aspects of a single, coherent rational agency.

**Non-Conceptualism**
Non-conceptualism holds that perceptual experience has a kind of representational content that does not require the perceiver to possess concepts for the properties represented. Evans's fineness of grain, Crane's concept-independence, and Peacocke's scenario content are paradigmatic arguments. Non-conceptualism makes room for a significant epistemic and phenomenological difference between perception (which has non-conceptual content) and belief (which is conceptual), and it allows for rich, detailed perceptual experience in creatures that lack the conceptual repertoire to articulate what they perceive.

**The Space of Reasons**
McDowell's "space of reasons" is the domain of rational justification—the domain of claims and counterclaims that can be evaluated for truth, consistency, and inferential correctness. Beliefs and actions are in the space of reasons; they can be supported or undermined by reasons. McDowell argues that if perceptual experience is to serve as a reason for belief (not merely a cause), it must itself be in the space of reasons—it must have conceptual content that can serve as a premise in inference. This motivates his conceptualism and his rejection of the "Given" (myth of the given).

**Fineness of Grain**
Fineness of grain refers to the level of detail at which perceptual content specifies the properties of perceived objects. Visual experience represents colors at a fineness of grain that exceeds the discriminative power of the subject's color concepts: I can perceive more distinct shades of red than I have names or concepts for. Evans argues that this shows perceptual content is non-conceptual—it is finer-grained than any concepts the subject possesses. McDowell responds that subjects have "demonstrative concepts" (that shade) that are formed in the moment of experience and are as fine-grained as needed.

**Scenario Content**
Peacocke's scenario content is the most basic level of perceptual representation: a spatial representation of the scene around the perceiver in egocentric coordinates, specifying how surfaces, edges, and objects are arranged in the scene. Scenario content is not propositional—it does not predicate properties of objects using concepts—but it is representational: it can be accurate or inaccurate with respect to the actual spatial arrangement of the scene. Scenario content is offered as the non-conceptual core of perceptual experience, underlying and supporting the more conceptual layers of perceptual judgment.

## Thought Experiments

**The Unnameable Shade**
You are presented with a particular shade of red that is unique and that you have no prior concept for. You can clearly see it and discriminate it from neighboring shades. Do you perceive it? And if you do, what is the content of your experience? Non-conceptualists say you perceive that shade as having its particular quality, even without possessing a concept of it. Conceptualists say you form a demonstrative concept ("that shade") in the moment, which is as fine-grained as the experience. The case is a direct test of whether concept possession is required for perceptual content.

**Dretske's Seeing and Not Recognizing**
Dretske describes cases of seeing objects without recognizing what they are: you see a thimble across the room without knowing it is a thimble. You perceive it but do not conceptualize it under the description "thimble"—yet there is a genuine visual experience of it. This supports non-conceptualism: perceptual content represents the object's shape, size, and color independently of any conceptual identification. McDowell responds that you can have a demonstrative concept ("that thing over there") that captures just the perceptual content without using the descriptive concept "thimble."

**The Staircase Illusion (Schröder)**
The Schröder staircase is an ambiguous figure that can be seen as ascending from left to right or from right to left. When the interpretation flips, the phenomenology changes even though the retinal stimulus is identical. If the conceptualist is right that experience has conceptual content, this change should correspond to a change in the concepts applied; if the non-conceptualist is right, the change might occur at the level of scenario content, without any change in the concepts deployed. The case is used to probe how much of the phenomenological change is attributable to conceptual re-interpretation and how much to pre-conceptual restructuring.
