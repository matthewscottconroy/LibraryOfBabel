# Key Thinkers: Perceptual Content

---

**Gareth Evans** (1946–1980)

Evans's posthumous *The Varieties of Reference* introduced the influential concept of "nonconceptual content" in perception. He argued that perceptual states have a "scenario content" — a representation of how things are in the perceiver's immediate environment that does not require the subject to possess the concepts needed to describe that content. Evans connected this to his account of singular reference: demonstrative thoughts ("that red thing") derive their content from perceptual states that lock onto objects in the environment.

*Key works:* *The Varieties of Reference* (1982); "Semantic Theory and Tacit Knowledge" (1981); "Understanding Demonstratives" (1981)

---

**Christopher Peacocke** (b. 1950)

Peacocke develops the most detailed account of perceptual content, distinguishing "scenario content" (nonconceptual, specifying a way of filling out the perceiver's environment) from "protoproposition content" (specifying the property and object involved in perception) and from "conceptual content" (requiring concept possession). Scenario content captures the richness and fineness of grain of visual experience, explaining why our capacity to discriminate colors exceeds our capacity to categorize them.

*Key works:* *A Study of Concepts* (1992); *Being Known* (1999); "Scenarios, Concepts, and Perception" (1992)

---

**John McDowell** (b. 1942)

McDowell is the most prominent critic of nonconceptual perceptual content. In *Mind and World*, he argues that positing a "Given" of nonconceptual perceptual content generates an "epistemic friction" problem: nonconceptual content cannot stand in rational relations to judgment and belief, so it cannot serve as evidence for perceptual claims. Experience can play its epistemological role only if its content is already conceptual. McDowell appeals to a broadly Kantian idea that concepts and intuitions are both required for knowledge.

*Key works:* *Mind and World* (1994); *Perception as a Capacity for Knowledge* (2011); "Sellars on Perceptual Experience" (1998)

---

**Michael Tye** (b. 1950)

Tye defends the view that the phenomenal content of perceptual experience is both representational and nonconceptual. His PANIC theory holds that phenomenal states represent properties of external objects, and that this representation is nonconceptual — the subject need not possess the concepts corresponding to the represented properties. This allows Tye to explain the richness of experience (we can visually represent more colors than we can name) while maintaining a fully representationalist account.

*Key works:* *Ten Problems of Consciousness* (1995); *Consciousness, Color, and Content* (2000); "Nonconceptual Content, Richness, and Fineness of Grain" (2006)
