# Further Reading: Predictive Perception

## Primary Sources

**Helmholtz, *Treatise on Physiological Optics*, Vol. 3 (1867, trans. 1925)** — Helmholtz argued that perception involves "unconscious inference" — the visual system automatically draws conclusions about the external world from ambiguous retinal input. A foundational text for the view that perception is a form of hypothesis-testing.

**Karl Friston, "A Theory of Cortical Responses" (2005)** — The key paper introducing the hierarchical predictive coding model to neuroscience: the brain predicts its sensory inputs at every level of cortical hierarchy, and only prediction errors are passed upward for further processing.

**Anil Seth, *Being You: A New Science of Consciousness* (2021)** — Seth develops the "controlled hallucination" view that all perceptual experience is the brain's best guess about its hidden causes, with prediction error signals updating the model. Accessible and philosophically rich.

## Secondary Literature

**Jakob Hohwy, *The Predictive Mind* (2013)** — A systematic philosophical treatment of predictive processing and its implications for the nature of perception, consciousness, and the self. Addresses concerns about representationalism and the relationship to enactivism.

**Hermann von Helmholtz, "The Recent Progress of the Theory of Vision" (1868)** — A more accessible version of Helmholtz's views on unconscious inference and the theory of vision, written for a general audience.

**Clark Glymour, "What Went Wrong? Reflections on Science by Observation and the Bell Curve" (1998)** — Provides context for Bayesian approaches to inference that underlie predictive processing, connecting the framework to the broader history of probabilistic reasoning.

**Samuel Gershman, "What Does the Free Energy Principle Tell Us about the Brain?" (2019)** — A neuroscientist's accessible introduction to the free energy principle and its relationship to predictive coding, clarifying the empirical content of Friston's framework.

**Andy Clark, "Busting Out: Predictive Brains, Embodied Minds, and the Puzzle of the Evidentiary Veil" (2017)** — Examines the tension between the predictive brain's "veil of inference" and direct realist or enactivist accounts of perception, arguing they are more compatible than critics suggest.
