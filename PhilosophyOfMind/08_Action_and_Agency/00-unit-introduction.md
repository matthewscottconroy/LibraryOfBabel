# Unit Introduction: Action and Agency

## Central Questions

What distinguishes an action from a mere bodily movement? When my leg kicks in response to a reflex hammer, something happens to my body; when I kick a ball, *I* do something. This distinction — between things that happen to an agent and things the agent does — is the starting point for the philosophy of action. The central questions are: What are the mental states (intentions, desires, beliefs) that make a movement into an action? How do reasons cause actions? When an action is caused by the right states in the right way, is it genuinely free? And what is the relationship between the reasons for which an agent acts and the causal story about why the action occurred?

The *reasons-causes* debate, launched by G.E.M. Anscombe and crystallized by Donald Davidson, is the organizing dispute. Anscombe argued that actions are intelligible under descriptions — they make sense as expressions of reasons — in a way that mere events are not. To understand an action, you need to identify the intention with which it was done; this intentional description is not reducible to a causal-mechanical description. Davidson agreed that actions are intelligible under intentional descriptions but argued that this does not prevent the reasons for an action from being its causes: reasons are causes, and actions are events with both intentional and causal descriptions.

A second set of questions concerns *agency* in a richer sense: what does it mean to be the author of one's actions? Harry Frankfurt's account of free will in terms of higher-order desires — the capacity to identify with one's first-order desires and to endorse or reject them — opened a tradition of compatibilist accounts of freedom that does not require the falsity of determinism.

## Major Positions and Debates

### The Reasons-Causes Debate

Anscombe's *Intention* (1957) argued that intentional action cannot be understood as the product of mental states that cause behavior. The point is not merely that reasons are not causes but that the *framework* of intentional explanation is different from causal explanation: to explain an action is to show that it makes sense given the agent's reasons, beliefs, and desires, and this kind of intelligibility is not the same as causal necessity.

Davidson's "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963) challenged this: the reason for which an agent acts must cause the action, otherwise the agent would not be acting for that reason but merely acting coincidentally with having that reason. If I want to turn on the light and I believe that flipping the switch will do so, and I flip the switch, what makes it the case that I acted for those reasons is that they caused my flipping. Davidson argued that this is compatible with the irreducibility of intentional descriptions.

The debate continues in discussions of *causal deviance*: even if reasons must cause actions, they must cause them in the *right way*. Consider an agent who wants to kill his uncle and believes he can do so by driving recklessly. He drives recklessly, becomes so nervous about killing his uncle that he swerves involuntarily, and kills him. The mental states (desire, belief) caused the bodily movement that caused the death, but not through the right kind of process to count as intentional action. Specifying the "right kind of process" without circularity is the deviant causal chain problem.

### Frankfurt Cases and the Structure of Agency

Harry Frankfurt's "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" (1969) introduced thought experiments designed to show that moral responsibility does not require alternative possibilities. The standard compatibilist view is that an agent is morally responsible if and only if she could have done otherwise. Frankfurt imagined cases in which a neuroscientist is poised to intervene if the agent shows signs of deciding otherwise, but the agent decides on her own and the neuroscientist never needs to intervene. Intuitively, the agent is responsible — she acted on her own reasons — but there was no alternative possibility.

Frankfurt cases have generated an enormous literature. Critics argue that there are always some alternative possibilities (different ways of choosing, different timings) that the cases fail to eliminate. Defenders argue that the cases establish what is really at stake in responsibility: not the existence of alternatives but the *source* of action in the agent's own reasons and evaluations.

Frankfurt's "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971) introduced the hierarchical account of free will: what distinguishes persons from mere animals is the capacity to have higher-order desires — desires about which desires to act on. A free will is a will one can identify with; freedom is acting in accordance with a second-order desire that one's first-order desire be effective. This source compatibilism has been enormously influential.

### Mental Causation and the Exclusion Problem

If actions are caused by mental states (beliefs, desires, intentions), then the causal efficacy of the mental is a presupposition of the philosophy of action. But as noted in Unit 02, the *exclusion problem* threatens to show that mental properties as such are causally inert. If every physical action is fully caused by prior physical events, and mental events are just physical events under mental descriptions, then mental *properties* (their being reasons, their having intentional content) play no causal role.

The standard response for action theory is to hold that mental properties are causally relevant in virtue of being identical to physical properties (reductive physicalism) or in virtue of being *constituted by* physical properties (non-reductive physicalism with a more robust notion of causal relevance). Neither response is fully satisfying.

### Skill, Habit, and the Limits of Intentional Action

Much action theory focuses on paradigm cases of deliberate, intentional action. But a large class of actions — skilled performance, habitual behavior, expert action — seems not to involve explicit intention formation. The expert tennis player does not form an intention to place each shot; the experienced typist does not think through each keystroke. Whether these are properly described as intentional actions, and what account of agency applies to them, is a significant question.

Hubert Dreyfus argued that skilled performance does not involve any mental representation at all — expert behavior is a form of absorbed coping that is beyond the reach of intentional explanation. This connects to debates about the relationship between conscious deliberation and skilled behavior and to phenomenological accounts of agency.

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**G.E.M. Anscombe** (*Intention*, 1957) argued that intentional action is characterized by a distinctive kind of knowledge — the agent's knowledge of what she is doing — that is not derived from observation. This *practical knowledge* is the key to understanding intentional action.

**Donald Davidson** argued in "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963) that reasons are causes: what makes a reason the reason *for* an action is that it causes it. Davidson's event ontology — mental events are physical events under mental descriptions — grounds this causal claim.

**Harry Frankfurt** developed the hierarchical account of free will and the Frankfurt cases in two seminal papers (1969, 1971). Frankfurt's work shifted the focus of free will discussion from the existence of alternatives to the source of action in the agent's own reasons.

**John Bishop** (*Natural Agency*, 1989) developed the most careful treatment of the deviant causal chain problem, arguing for an *agency theory* of action that cannot be reduced to standard causal theory.

**Timothy O'Connor** defends agent causation — the view that agents as substances can cause their actions, rather than merely their mental states causing them — as the only account that preserves genuine freedom against the exclusion problem.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Anscombe, *Intention* (1957)**
Difficult but rewarding; Anscombe's account of intentional action as characterized by practical knowledge, reasons, and a distinctive form of explanation remains the starting point for action theory. The book is notoriously compressed; students typically benefit from secondary literature as a guide.

**2. Davidson, "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963)**
The paper that established the reasons-are-causes thesis and opened the debate about the relationship between intentional and causal explanation. Crisp and precise; available in *Essays on Actions and Events* (1980).

**3. Frankfurt, "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971)**
The paper that introduced the hierarchical account of free will and defined the source compatibilist tradition. Beautifully written and philosophically important; available in *The Importance of What We Care About* (1988).

**4. Frankfurt, "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" (1969)**
The paper that introduced Frankfurt cases and challenged the Principle of Alternate Possibilities. Essential for understanding contemporary compatibilism.

**5. Kim, "The Nonreductivist's Troubles with Mental Causation" (1989)**
The most precise statement of the exclusion argument as it applies to intentional action. Available in *Supervenience and Mind* (1993). Essential for understanding why the mental causation problem is not just a metaphysical curiosity but a serious threat to the intelligibility of action theory.

## Connections to Other Units

Action and agency are connected to the mind-body problem through the mental causation problem: if mental states cause actions, they must be causally efficacious, which requires an account of how mental properties can be causally relevant. *Functionalism* (Unit 06) provides the natural framework for understanding mental causation: mental states cause actions in virtue of their functional properties. *Consciousness* (Unit 03) bears on agency through the question of whether conscious intentions cause actions or merely accompany them — the Libet experiments (discussed in Unit 16) suggest that neural processes initiating action precede conscious awareness of intention by several hundred milliseconds.

*Personal Identity* (Unit 11) connects to agency through questions about diachronic responsibility: what makes me responsible for what a past self did depends on what kind of continuity obtains between past and present. *Ethics and Philosophy of Mind* (Unit 19) examines the moral dimensions of agency: what conditions are required for moral responsibility, and how do psychiatric conditions affect attributions of agency.

## Open Questions

**1. Does Libet's work show that free will is an illusion?**
Benjamin Libet's experiments found that neural activity associated with voluntary action begins approximately 550 milliseconds before subjects report becoming aware of their intention to move. Some interpret this as showing that conscious intentions do not initiate action; others argue that the experiments are compatible with free will (the conscious intention may still play a causal role, or the timing of conscious awareness may be mismeasured). Whether neuroscience can settle questions about free will is contested.

**2. Can the deviant causal chain problem be solved without circularity?**
The problem of specifying what it means for reasons to cause actions in the right way has resisted analysis. Every proposed criterion seems to either miss some cases of genuine action or include some cases of deviant causation. Whether this problem can be solved by a non-circular account of the right causal process remains open.

**3. What is the relationship between skilled action and explicit intention?**
Expert performers seem to act without explicit intention: asking a tennis player to describe what she intends before each shot may actually worsen performance. Does this mean that skilled action is not genuinely intentional? Or should we develop a more nuanced account that distinguishes explicit from implicit intentionality?

**4. Do Frankfurt cases succeed?**
Critics have developed increasingly sophisticated versions of the objection that there are always residual alternatives in Frankfurt cases. The debate over whether Frankfurt cases succeed in showing that PAP is false — and therefore that responsibility does not require alternative possibilities — remains vigorous.

**5. Is agent causation coherent?**
Libertarian accounts of free will typically require that agents (as substances) cause their actions, in addition to (or instead of) their mental states causing them. But it is unclear what agent causation amounts to over and above the causation of the action by the agent's mental states. Whether agent causation is a coherent notion, and whether it helps with the problem it is designed to solve, is genuinely contested.
