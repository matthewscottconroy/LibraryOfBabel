# Basic Actions

What distinguishes an action from a mere bodily movement? When you deliberately raise your arm, that is an action. When your arm jerks in a reflex, that is a bodily movement but not an action. Both involve the same muscles and the same physical movement — what makes one an action?

**Davidson's Account of Actions**

Donald Davidson's seminal paper "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963) proposed that actions are bodily movements caused by beliefs and desires (or more precisely, by "reasons" — belief-desire pairs that rationalize the action). An action is a movement caused in the right way by the agent's own mental states.

What distinguishes an action from a non-action is the presence of appropriate mental causation: the movement is caused by the agent's intention (or belief-desire complex), and this causation has the right kind of structure.

**Basic vs. Non-Basic Actions**

Arthur Danto introduced the concept of a "basic action" to address the regress problem for action. If every action is done by doing something else (I raise my arm by contracting my bicep; I contract my bicep by sending neural signals...), there threatens to be an infinite regress. Basic actions are those not done by doing anything else — they are the ultimate bottom of the action hierarchy.

On Danto's account, basic actions are direct bodily movements — moving a limb, contracting a muscle (in some sense). Non-basic actions are achieved by means of basic actions: I write by moving my hand, I signal a turn by raising my arm.

This raises the question of how basic basic actions are. Some argue that the most basic level is the firing of motor neurons; others that it is the willing or intending to perform a movement; still others that there are no genuinely basic actions at all — every action involves a causal chain of components.

**The Problem of the Deviant Causal Chain**

Davidson acknowledged that not every case where beliefs and desires cause movement is a case of action. Suppose I want to scare a mountain lion and my belief and desire are so intense that they make my hands shake, which accidentally knocks a rock, which scares the lion. My belief and desire caused the scaring — but it was not an intentional action.

This is the "deviant causal chain" problem: causes of the right type (beliefs, desires) can produce effects (movements) via causal routes that do not constitute action. The challenge is specifying what kind of causal connection between mental states and movements constitutes action.

Various proposals have been made: the causal connection must go through intention; it must involve the right kind of feedback from the movement to the agent's mental states; it must be of the kind that is produced by a functioning motor control system.

**Intentionalism and Volitionalism**

Intentionalist accounts of action (Davidson, Bratman) hold that actions are movements caused by intentions. The intention is a distinctive mental state — different from desire and belief — that directs and controls the action.

Volitional accounts (classical: Locke, Reid; contemporary: McCann) hold that actions involve volitions — mental acts of willing that directly cause bodily movements. The will is a capacity for a distinctive kind of mental causation.

Both views face the deviant causal chain problem: intending or willing to move does not guarantee that the resulting movement is an action, if the causal chain from intention/volition to movement is deviant.

**Bodily Awareness and the Phenomenology of Action**

A growing literature on the phenomenology of action has emphasized the role of bodily awareness and the "sense of agency" — the felt sense of being the author of one's actions. This phenomenological dimension of action connects philosophy of action to empirical research on motor control and to the study of pathologies like anosognosia (where patients fail to recognize that their movements are caused by their own intentions).

The sense of agency connects action to the broader question of self: what makes an action mine is partly that I experience myself as its author — that there is a distinctive first-person phenomenology associated with voluntary action that is absent in passive movement.
