# Reasons and Causes

Donald Davidson's 1963 paper "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" made one of the most influential arguments in twentieth-century philosophy of mind: that reasons for action are causes of action. This claim sounds obvious, but it was deeply controversial at the time and remains contested. Understanding the dispute requires distinguishing two questions that can easily run together: whether reasons explain actions, and whether they do so by causing them.

**The Background: Anti-Causalism**

The dominant view in analytic philosophy of action in the 1950s, influenced by Wittgenstein and Ryle, held that reason-explanation is categorically different from causal explanation. When we explain why someone raised their arm by saying they intended to signal a turn, we are not giving a cause — we are giving a reason, specifying what the action meant or what purpose it served. The logical connection between a reason and the action it rationalizes seems different in kind from the contingent connection between cause and effect.

Elizabeth Anscombe's *Intention* (1957) developed a sophisticated version of this view. Intentional actions, Anscombe argued, are those to which a certain kind of "why?" question applies — the question asks for the agent's reason. The answer to such a question specifies the action's intentional description, not a prior mental event that caused the movement. The logical grammar of intentional action explanation is irreducibly teleological: we explain actions by their reasons, not by prior causes.

**Davidson's Counterargument**

Davidson's challenge to anti-causalism was to show that reason-explanation must be causal to be genuine explanation at all. His argument has two parts.

The first part concerns the connection between reasons and actions. A reason rationalizes an action by showing it in a favorable light — by presenting some feature of the action that makes it intelligible as something the agent had reason to do. This is the normative dimension of reason-explanation. But rationalization alone is insufficient for explanation. Suppose I have a reason to turn right (there is a gas station that way) and a reason to turn left (that is the scenic route). I turn left. The right-turn reason existed but doesn't explain my action. What distinguishes the reason that explains from the one that doesn't? Davidson's answer: the operative reason must have caused the action. The reason that explains is the one that actually produced the behavior, not merely one the agent happened to have.

The second part of Davidson's argument addresses the worry that mental states cannot cause physical events (the mind-body worry). Davidson's solution is anomalous monism: mental events are token-identical to physical events. When a belief-desire pair causes a movement, the mental event that constitutes the cause is also a physical event — the causation is physical causation. But the event is correctly described at both levels. This allows reasons to be causally efficacious without requiring that mental properties are reducible to physical ones.

**The Structure of Reason-Explanation**

Davidson's account requires that reasons have two components: a pro-attitude (desire, want, or broadly positive motivation toward some type of action) and a belief (that the action to be explained falls under the type favored by the pro-attitude). The combination of a pro-attitude toward actions of kind K and the belief that action A is of kind K constitutes a "primary reason" for A.

This belief-desire structure is now standard in philosophy of action. But its implications are substantial. It suggests that all intentional action is in some sense directed by the agent's beliefs and desires — that practical reasoning is a matter of taking means to desired ends, given beliefs about the world. This instrumentalist picture of rational agency has been criticized as too narrow (it leaves no room for deontological or intrinsically motivated action), but it remains the dominant framework.

**The Causal Theory and Its Challenges**

The causal theory of action explanation faces several well-known problems.

The deviant causal chain problem (discussed in the section on basic actions) is one: beliefs and desires can produce action via causal routes that don't constitute intentional action. This shows that the causal theory needs refinement — not that reasons aren't causes, but that the causal connection must be of a specific kind.

A deeper challenge comes from the question of whether mental state types — being in a desire-state, being in a belief-state — can be genuine causal kinds. Davidson's anomalous monism holds that mental properties are not reducible to physical properties, which seems to undermine the idea that it is qua mental states that beliefs and desires cause anything. The worry: if the causal work is done by the physical realizer, not by the mental property, then mental causation is epiphenomenal at the type level even if efficacious at the token level. This is the causal exclusion problem, taken up more fully in Unit 3.

**Non-Causal Alternatives**

Not everyone accepts that reason-explanation is causal explanation. Contemporary non-causalists come in several varieties.

*Constitutivists* (Anscombe, Thompson, Alvarez) argue that the connection between reasons and intentional actions is partly constitutive: to act for a reason is not merely to have one's action caused by a mental state, but to have one's action be (in part) constituted by that reason. The reason is internal to the action description, not a prior event that produces it.

*Normativists* (Scanlon, Broome) argue that reasons are considerations that speak in favor of actions — facts in the world, not mental states. The explanation of why someone acted for reasons is not causal but normative: we understand the action by seeing that it was a reasonable response to the relevant considerations.

These alternatives remain lively research programs, but the broadly Davidsonian causal picture — that reasons explain by causing, and that intentional action is action caused by the agent's mental states in the right way — dominates contemporary philosophy of action and cognitive science.
