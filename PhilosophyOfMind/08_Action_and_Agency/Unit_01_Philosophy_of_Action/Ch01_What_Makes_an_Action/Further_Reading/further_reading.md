# Further Reading: What Makes an Action?

## Primary Sources

**Donald Davidson, "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963)** — Davidson's landmark paper arguing that reasons (belief-desire pairs) are causes of actions, not merely rationalizing descriptions of them. This restored causal explanation to the philosophy of action against Wittgensteinian anti-causalist views.

**G.E.M. Anscombe, *Intention* (1957)** — Anscombe's foundational analysis of intentional action, arguing that the question "Why are you doing that?" marks the scope of intentional action. Actions are intentional under some descriptions but not others; practical reasoning connects action to ends without being a form of theoretical inference.

**Arthur Danto, "Basic Actions" (1965)** — Danto introduces the concept of "basic actions" — actions performed without doing anything else in order to do them (e.g., moving one's arm). The concept structures the analysis of the hierarchy of actions and the relationship between mental and bodily events.

## Secondary Literature

**Jennifer Hornsby, *Actions* (1980)** — Hornsby defends a view on which actions are inner mental events (tryings) that cause bodily movements. The bodily movement and its causing trying are distinct, which has implications for the individuation of actions and the problem of deviant causal chains.

**Harry Frankfurt, "The Problem of Action" (1978)** — Frankfurt argues that what distinguishes genuine actions from mere bodily movements is not the presence of reasons or intentions but the agent's guidance of their behavior — the active involvement of the agent's will.

**Randolph Clarke, "Toward a Credible Agent-Causal Account of Free Action" (1993)** — Presents an agent-causal account of action, arguing that genuine agency requires that the agent — not just events in the agent — causes the action. Relevant for debates about mental causation and free will.

**Myles Brand, *Intending and Acting* (1984)** — A systematic analysis of the structure of intention and its relationship to action, distinguishing prospective intentions ("I will do X") from the proximal intentions that immediately cause action.
