# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: What Makes an Action?

## Key Arguments

**Davidson's Causal Theory of Action**
Donald Davidson argues that actions are events caused by the agent's reasons—specifically, by belief-desire pairs that rationalize the action. An action is intentional if and only if it is caused (in the right way) by a mental state that constitutes a reason for it. The causal theory distinguishes actions from mere happenings: my arm going up is an action when it is caused by my intention to raise my arm, but not when it rises from a reflex. This makes the causation by mental states both necessary and sufficient for intentional action, and it is the dominant account in analytic philosophy of action.

**Anscombe on Intentional Action Under a Description**
G.E.M. Anscombe argues that an action is intentional under a certain description if the question "Why are you doing that?" has an answer—if the agent can give reasons for it. The same bodily movement can be both intentional under one description ("I am pumping water") and unintentional under another ("I am poisoning the village water supply")—if the agent does not know the water is poisoned. Intentionality is not a property of events but of events under descriptions, and it is connected to the agent's practical knowledge of what they are doing.

**The Deviant Causal Chain Problem**
Davidson's causal theory faces the problem of deviant causal chains: a belief-desire pair can cause an action in a way that is not the right kind. A climber who wants to lighten his load and believes letting go of his companion would do so may be so unnerved by this belief that his hand trembles and he lets go—which is caused by the belief-desire pair but is not an intentional letting-go. The deviant causal chain problem shows that "caused by reasons" is insufficient for intentional action; a further condition (on the causal process being "the right kind") is needed.

**The Argument for Basic Actions**
Arthur Danto argues that some actions are "basic"—they are not done by doing anything else. Raising my arm is basic: I do not first raise my arm by contracting muscles (that is not something I do but something that happens in me). Higher-level actions (signing a contract, starting a revolution) are done by doing something more basic. The notion of basic action identifies the level at which agency directly operates and raises questions about the relationship between intentional action and the physical processes that implement it.

**Mele's Argument Against Over-Intellectualization of Action**
Alfred Mele argues that many mental-state-based accounts of action over-intellectualize everyday action: most human behavior is not preceded by explicit deliberation, intention-formation, or practical reasoning. Habitual, skilled, and automatic actions are genuine actions but involve minimal conscious deliberation. Any account of agency that requires full-blown propositional intentions for every action misrepresents the structure of most human activity. This challenges Bratman's planning theory when extended to all action.

## Core Concepts

**Intentional Action**
An intentional action is an event done for a reason—an event that is not merely a reflex, a spasm, or a compulsion, but that the agent performs because they take it to satisfy some desire given some belief. Davidson's causal theory makes reasons (belief-desire pairs) constitutive causes of intentional actions. Anscombe's approach makes intentionality a feature of events under specific descriptions, connected to the agent's practical knowledge and their ability to answer "why" questions about what they are doing.

**Reasons-Causation**
Reasons-causation is Davidson's thesis that the reason why an agent acts (their belief-desire pair that rationalizes the action) is also the cause of their acting. This collapses the distinction between causal and rational explanation: to explain an action by giving reasons is to give the causes of the action. The thesis is controversial because it seems to assimilate reason explanation to mechanical causation. Davidson's response is that reasons, under their physical descriptions, are among the causes of the action, even if they are not causes under their mental descriptions.

**Basic Action**
A basic action (Danto) is an action that is not performed by performing any other action—it is the action at the base of any action hierarchy. Moving my finger is more basic than pressing a key, which is more basic than typing a word. What counts as basic may vary by context (a pianist's basic actions in performance may be more complex than a novice's) and there is dispute about whether bodily movements are basic or whether there is a more primitive level (an intention to move, a neural event). The notion of basic action is important for understanding the relationship between intentional agency and physical causation.

**The Deviant Causal Chain**
A deviant causal chain is a causal chain from a mental state to a behavior that is not the "right" kind of causal chain for the behavior to count as an intentional action—even if the behavior was caused by the relevant mental states. The problem is identifying what makes a causal chain "right." Davidson and others have proposed that the action must be caused in the way that is characteristic of intentional actions (involving the operation of practical reasoning, motor control, etc.), but specifying this without circularity is the central technical challenge for the causal theory.

**Practical Knowledge (Anscombe)**
Practical knowledge, as Anscombe develops it, is the agent's knowledge of what they are doing in the process of doing it—knowledge that is constituted by, not derived from, the action. I know I am raising my arm not by observing it but by raising it. Practical knowledge is non-observational: it does not wait on perceptual feedback but is the mode of awareness that consists in intentional agency itself. This concept captures something causal theories often miss: the distinctive first-person character of intentional action, where knowing and doing are not separable.

## Thought Experiments

**Davidson's Climber**
Davidson asks us to imagine a mountain climber who wants to loosen the rope to lighten his load and believes that letting go of his companion would do this. The thought—that he could let go—so unnerves him that his hand trembles and he lets go. This is caused by the belief-desire pair and results in the intended outcome, but it is not an intentional letting-go: the deviant causal chain (via trembling) means the action was not performed intentionally. The thought experiment shows that causal theory needs to specify the "right kind" of causal chain.

**Oedipus's Action Under a Description**
Oedipus intentionally kills the stranger at the crossroads (the action is intentional under the description "killing the stranger"), but he does not intentionally kill his father (the action is not intentional under the description "killing my father"), even though the stranger is his father. Anscombe uses this type of case to show that intentionality is description-relative: the same physical event is intentional under some descriptions and not others, depending on what the agent knows and intends. The case challenges any account that treats intentionality as a simple property of events.

**The Wayward Causal Chain in Perception**
To show that the deviant causal chain problem is not peculiar to action theory, consider a parallel case in perception: a student wants to remember a theorem for tomorrow's exam, and anxiety about the theorem causes them to dream about it, and they wake up remembering it. The dream state was caused by the desire to remember, and the outcome (remembering) is what was desired. But this is not the normal case of successfully remembering: the causal chain is "wayward." The analogy shows that the problem of specifying the "right kind" of causal chain is a general problem for causal theories of intentional states.
