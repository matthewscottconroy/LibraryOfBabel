# Prior Intention and Immediate Intention

Intentions occupy the center of philosophical accounts of action, but "intention" is not a single uniform thing. We speak of intending to do something in the future — I intend to call my friend tomorrow — and of doing something intentionally right now, in the very act of doing it. These seem to be different psychological phenomena that stand in a complex relationship to each other. Unpacking this relationship has been one of the central tasks of the philosophy of intention.

**Davidson's Analysis**

On Davidson's original account, all intentional action reduces to action done for a reason. There is no need to posit intention as a distinct mental state beyond the beliefs and desires that rationalize the action. "Doing something intentionally" just means "doing it in a way caused by the appropriate beliefs and desires."

This analysis has the advantage of parsimony: it avoids positing a sui generis mental state of intention. But it faces serious problems. I can intend to do something without having a specific desire to do it right now — my intention to call tomorrow is not a current desire to call, since calling now is not on the table. More pressing: intentions seem to have a normative and coordinative function that bare beliefs and desires lack. My intention to meet you at noon generates commitments, constrains further planning, and persists over time in a way that a momentary desire does not.

**Bratman's Planning Theory**

Michael Bratman's work in the 1980s and 1990s made the most influential case for treating intention as a genuinely distinct mental state with its own characteristic functional role. Bratman distinguishes between two related concepts: *prior intentions* (intentions formed in advance of acting) and *immediate intentions* (the intention that guides action in its very execution).

Prior intentions are planning states. When I intend to go to the store tomorrow, I am not merely predicting that I will go or desiring to go — I am committing myself to a course of action that will shape my other plans and deliberations. Bratman identifies three features that distinguish intentions from mere desires:

1. *Resistance to reconsideration*: Forming an intention involves settling a question about what to do. Unlike desires, which may fluctuate, intentions are supposed to persist unless there is good reason to revise them. This resistance to reconsideration makes planning efficient — I do not need to re-deliberate every morning about whether to go to work.

2. *Coordination*: Prior intentions coordinate present and future action, both within the individual (my intention to write a paper shapes how I plan my week) and between individuals (your intention to meet me at noon meshes with mine to create a joint plan).

3. *Consistency requirements*: A set of intentions should be mutually consistent and should be consistent with what the agent believes is possible. If I intend to be in two places at once, something has gone wrong in my practical thinking.

**Immediate Intention**

The "immediate intention" in action is the mental state that guides action as it is happening. This is closely related to what other theorists call "motor intention" or "intention-in-action" (Searle). It is the intention that is somehow embodied in the movement itself, giving it its directed, controlled character.

John Searle distinguishes between "prior intentions" (what we might form before acting) and "intentions-in-action" (which are present in the act itself and give it its intentional character). On Searle's view, an action like reaching for a cup involves an intention-in-action that is causally self-referential: it is an intention that the very experience of reaching will be caused by the state of the arm, which is caused by the intention-in-action itself. This self-referential structure is what makes it an intention rather than a mere desire.

The relationship between prior and immediate intention is complex. Often they are connected: I form a prior intention to make a cup of tea, and when I act, the immediate intention in my action flows from the prior intention. But they can come apart: I may start doing something for reasons I cannot articulate, guided by an immediate intention that was never preceded by a prior intention at all. Much spontaneous, skilled, habitual behavior may involve immediate intention without prior intention.

**Bratman on Rational Integration**

Bratman's deeper contribution is to locate intention in the context of what he calls "planning agency." Human beings are distinctive among animals in their capacity to plan ahead — to form intentions now that govern future behavior. This planning capacity requires that intentions have the normative properties he identifies: stability, consistency, and coordination.

This framework has implications beyond philosophy of action. It suggests that rational agency is not simply a matter of responding to current desires and beliefs, but of maintaining an integrated set of commitments over time. The capacity for this kind of integrated planning agency is, arguably, part of what makes us the kind of minded creatures we are — creatures with a past and a future, not just a present.

It also connects to questions about the unity of the self. My intentions over time form a network that constitutes something like a practical identity — a coherent self that persists through change in part because it maintains consistent commitments. Narrative theories of personal identity (discussed elsewhere) can be seen as extending Bratman's account of planning agency to the level of life-plans.

**Weakness of Will**

One important challenge for any account of intention is the phenomenon of *akrasia* — weakness of will, acting against one's better judgment. I intend to diet, judge that I should not eat the cake, yet eat it anyway. How is this possible?

Davidson famously argued that akrasia involves a kind of logical inconsistency in the agent's practical reasoning: the agent has an all-things-considered judgment that she should not act, yet acts. On his account, this is possible because the motivational force of a desire can exceed what the all-things-considered judgment endorses — the judgment that one ought not eat the cake coexists with the overwhelming desire that wins out.

Bratman's planning theory handles akrasia differently: weak-willed action involves a failure to maintain appropriate resistance to reconsideration. The prior intention is abandoned too easily in the face of temptation. This connects akrasia to broader questions about rational self-governance — the capacity to act as the integrated planning agent one aspires to be.
