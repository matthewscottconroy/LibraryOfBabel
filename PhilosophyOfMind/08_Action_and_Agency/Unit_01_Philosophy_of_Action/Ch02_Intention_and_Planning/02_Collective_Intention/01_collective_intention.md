# Collective Intention and Shared Agency

Intentional action is often collective. Two people carry a table together; a jazz ensemble improvises; a corporation pursues a strategy; a government enacts a policy. In all these cases, something like intention seems to be operating at a group level. But what is a group intention, and how does it relate to the individual intentions of group members? The philosophy of collective intentionality addresses these questions.

**The Problem of Shared Agency**

Simple summativism — the view that group intentions are nothing more than the aggregate of individual members' intentions — fails to capture what is distinctive about genuine collective action. Consider: you and I both intend to paint our respective halves of a fence. These are parallel individual intentions with the same target. This differs from our intending to paint the fence together. In the latter case, something is shared — a joint project, a mutual commitment, a willingness to coordinate and adjust.

Michael Bratman's account of shared cooperative activity identifies several requirements for genuine shared action:
1. Each participant must intend that the group carry out the joint activity.
2. Each must intend that this happen through the appropriate meshing of individual sub-intentions.
3. These intentions must be common knowledge among participants.

The crucial point is that collective intention involves intending with reference to the group and its activity, not merely having parallel intentions with a common target. I intend that *we* lift this table, not merely that I lift my end while you lift yours.

**John Searle's Account**

Searle argues that collective intentionality is a primitive, irreducible form of mental state. "We-intentions" are not reducible to "I-intentions" plus beliefs about others. When a group of people cooperate, each person has a genuine we-intention — a first-person-plural form of intentionality — that cannot be analyzed into a personal intention plus mutual beliefs.

Searle's view has the advantage of taking the phenomenology of collective action seriously: participating in a joint activity often feels different from acting alone, and this difference seems to be reflected in the agent's mental states, not merely in the social arrangements around them.

The challenge for Searle is to explain how we-intentions can exist "inside individuals' heads" while being genuinely collective. His answer — that we-intentions are simply a different irreducible form of intentionality — is philosophically modest (it takes the phenomenon at face value) but perhaps too quick: it stops explanation at the point where explanation is most needed.

**Margaret Gilbert's Plural Subject Theory**

Margaret Gilbert argues that genuine joint commitment is required for collective action, and that joint commitment creates obligations that individual intentions cannot. On her view, a group's sharing an intention is not a matter of each member having certain mental states; it is a matter of the group members forming a "plural subject" — pooling their wills together in a way that binds them to a joint undertaking.

Gilbert illustrates with a simple case: walking together. You and I are walking together if and only if we have jointly committed to walk as a body. This joint commitment is not just a matter of each of us intending to walk alongside the other; it involves a mutual obligation. If I suddenly veer off without explanation, I owe you an apology — I have violated a commitment that was genuinely shared.

This normative dimension of collective intention — the obligations it generates — is central to Gilbert's account and marks her view off from Bratman's more purely psychological approach.

**Collective Intentionality and Social Ontology**

The debate about collective intention connects to deeper questions in social ontology: what kinds of things are institutions, corporations, and social practices, and how do they exist in relation to individual human minds?

If collective intentions are real and not reducible to individual intentions, this seems to require that certain facts about the social world cannot be fully explained in individualistic, agent-by-agent terms. Social holists (Durkheim, some sociologists) hold that social facts are genuinely irreducible; social individualists hold that they must ultimately be explicable in terms of individual mental states and behavior.

The philosophy of collective intentionality mostly stakes out a middle ground: collective intentions are not in some supernatural "group mind" distinct from individual minds, but they do require that individual intentions be constituted in a particular way — that individuals take on we-intentions, joint commitments, or membership in plural subjects. This allows social facts to "supervene" on facts about individuals without being reducible to mere aggregates of individual mental states.

**Collective Beliefs and Other Attitudes**

The discussion of collective intention extends naturally to other collective mental attitudes. Can groups believe things? Several philosophers (Gilbert again, Raimo Tuomela) argue that groups can have beliefs in a sense that is not merely the sum of members' beliefs. A committee might reach a position through voting that no individual committee member actually endorses; a scientific community might "believe" a theory in the sense of being committed to operating on its basis, even though individual scientists vary in their credence.

These collective epistemic states raise questions for epistemology (what justifies collective belief?), ethics (who is responsible for collective actions?), and philosophy of mind (is collective mental life a genuine extension of individual mentality, or a merely useful fiction?). The answers remain contested, but the questions have become central to how philosophers think about the boundaries of minds and agents.
