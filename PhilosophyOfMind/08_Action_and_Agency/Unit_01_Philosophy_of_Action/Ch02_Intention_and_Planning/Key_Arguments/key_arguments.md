# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Intention and Planning

## Key Arguments

**Bratman's Argument for Plans as Irreducible**
Michael Bratman argues that future-directed intentions (plans) cannot be reduced to sets of belief-desire pairs: plans are not merely predictions about what one will want plus strong desires to act. Plans have distinctive normative properties—they create requirements of consistency, means-end coherence, and follow-through—that belief-desire pairs do not generate. To plan to do A is to be committed to doing A in a way that influences future deliberation (foreclosing reconsideration) and practical reasoning (generating intentions for means to A). Plans are irreducible mental states of a distinctive kind.

**The Argument That Prior Intentions Differ from Desires**
Prior intentions (intentions formed before the moment of action) differ from desires in their functional role: intentions are in some sense "settled" commitments that resist revision on the basis of passing desires. A plan to exercise tomorrow is not extinguished by a passing desire to stay in bed, while a desire to stay in bed is extinguished when the exercise plan is formed. This stability and functional difference provides an argument for the irreducibility of intention to desire, and supports Bratman's planning theory.

**Anscombe's Argument That Intention Is Not a Mental Cause**
Anscombe argues against the standard belief-desire (Davidsonian) model by denying that intentions are mental states that precede and cause actions. Intentional action is not (or not primarily) caused by a prior mental event of intending; rather, to act intentionally is to act with practical knowledge of what one is doing. The "intention-as-prior-mental-event" model generates regress problems (what about the intention to intend?) and misrepresents the grammar of intentional action. For Anscombe, the connection between intention and action is logical-practical, not causal.

**The Desire-Belief Model of Practical Reason**
Humeanism about practical reason holds that all motivation traces to desire: reason is "the slave of the passions" (Hume). The desire-belief model holds that all actions are explained by beliefs (about how to satisfy desires) and desires (the motivational states that provide the end). Bratman and others argue this model is incomplete: it cannot account for the rationality constraints on planning (consistency, coherence over time), for intentions as distinct from strong desires, or for the normativity of commitment.

**The Argument for Weak-Willed Action as Challenging the Humean Model**
Akrasia (weakness of will) is the phenomenon of acting against one's own better judgment—knowingly doing what one judges all-things-considered to be worse. Davidson argued that akrasia requires that the agent's intention is governed by a sub-maximal belief-desire pair (a conditional rather than unconditional judgment) while the all-things-considered judgment is not directly action-motivating. This is a problem for the Humean model if the all-things-considered judgment is itself a desire, and it raises questions about the relationship between judgment and motivation.

## Core Concepts

**Prior Intention**
A prior intention is an intention formed before the moment of action—typically as part of a plan. Prior intentions are future-directed: they commit the agent to acting in a certain way at a later time. Bratman argues that prior intentions have distinctive functional properties: they settle the question of what to do (within the plan), they coordinate present and future deliberation, and they support coordination with others. Prior intentions are distinct from desires (which do not have this settling, coordinating function) and from in-the-moment decisions.

**Immediate Intention**
An immediate intention (or "intentional in action") is the intention present in the action itself—what one is doing intentionally while doing it. Anscombe calls this "practical knowledge" of one's current action. The immediate intention is constituted by the ongoing action and the agent's knowledge of it; it is not a separate mental event that precedes and causes the action. The distinction between prior intention (formed before) and immediate intention (present in action) is important for understanding the structure of agency across time.

**Bratman's Planning Theory**
Bratman's planning theory holds that human agency is essentially future-directed: we are planning creatures who form intentions now for future actions, coordinate our activities across time, and coordinate with others through shared plans. Plans are partial (they don't specify every detail), hierarchical (means-ends structures), and normatively constrained (consistency, coherence, stability). The planning theory extends beyond individual intentions to shared intentional activities (you and I planning to meet) and provides a framework for understanding both rational agency and practical rationality.

**Weakness of Will (Akrasia)**
Weakness of will is acting contrary to one's all-things-considered better judgment: I judge that exercising would be better but watch television anyway. Davidson analyzed akrasia as possible because the intentional action is caused by a pro-attitude that governs a "conditional" or "prima facie" judgment, not the unconditional all-things-considered judgment. The problem of akrasia is important because it tests the relationship between practical judgment and motivation: if to judge that A is best is to be motivated to do A, akrasia is impossible; but it clearly occurs.

**The Humean Theory of Motivation**
The Humean theory holds that only desires can motivate action; beliefs alone cannot move us to act (they can only guide the satisfaction of pre-existing desires). Practical reason is instrumental: reason identifies means to given ends (desires), but cannot itself generate ends. This view implies that the desire-belief model exhausts the motivational structure of action. Critics argue it cannot account for cases where moral judgment directly motivates without any further desire, or for the way rational commitments (plans) resist revision on the basis of passing desires.

## Thought Experiments

**Bratman's Two-Option Deliberation Case**
You deliberate between options A and B, judge A to be better, and form an intention to do A. Later, passing considerations in favor of B arise. Bratman argues that rational agents do not re-open deliberation just because competing considerations arise: the prior intention to do A rationally settles the question within a range of normal circumstances. The thought experiment illustrates the "stability" feature of intentions and shows that intentions cannot be reduced to whatever belief-desire pair is currently strongest—they have a life of their own that resists erosion by passing whims.

**Davidson's Toxin Puzzle (modified)**
Kavka's toxin puzzle: you are offered a million dollars if, at midnight, you sincerely intend to drink a mildly unpleasant toxin tomorrow afternoon. You need not actually drink it—just intend to. Once you have the money (given at midnight), there is no reason to drink the toxin, so you will not. But knowing you will not drink it, how can you sincerely intend to? The puzzle probes the relationship between intention and action, and specifically whether it is possible to form an intention knowing it will not be executed. Bratman uses it to examine the normativity of commitments.

**The Case of Akrasia**
You have resolved to exercise every morning. In the morning, a strong urge to stay in bed arises. You know that exercising is better all-things-considered, yet you stay in bed. Davidson's analysis: you act on a "prima facie" judgment in favor of staying in bed, not on your all-things-considered judgment (which favors exercising). But why doesn't the all-things-considered judgment motivate? The case probes the gap between rational judgment and motivation and tests whether practical reason is sufficient for action or whether desire must always be the driving force.
