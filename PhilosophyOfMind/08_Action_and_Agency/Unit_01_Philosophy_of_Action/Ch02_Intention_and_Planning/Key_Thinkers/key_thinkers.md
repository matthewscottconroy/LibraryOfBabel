# Key Thinkers: Intention and Planning

---

**Michael Bratman** (b. 1945)

Bratman's "planning theory of intention" is the dominant account in contemporary philosophy of action. He argues that intentions are distinct from mere belief-desire complexes: they settle practical questions, initiate plans that become part of an interconnected planning network, and create rational pressures of consistency and means-end coherence. Intentions function to coordinate action over time and with other agents. His theory also provides the framework for understanding collective intentions and shared agency.

*Key works:* *Intention, Plans, and Practical Reason* (1987); *Faces of Intention* (1999); *Structures of Agency* (2007); *Shared Agency* (2014)

---

**Donald Davidson** (1917–2003)

Davidson's account of intention in "Intending" (1978) distinguishes three cases: intentions-in-acting (present in the unfolding of an action), future-directed intentions, and the intention with which one acts. He argues that all are analyzable in terms of beliefs and desires combined with all-things-considered evaluative judgments — no sui generis "intention" state is needed. This contrasts with Bratman's view and has generated sustained debate about the adequacy of purely belief-desire accounts.

*Key works:* "Intending" (1978); "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963); *Essays on Actions and Events* (1980)

---

**John Searle** (b. 1932)

Searle distinguishes "prior intentions" — intentions formed before an action begins — from "intentions-in-action" — the intentional component present in the action itself. Both are intentional states with conditions of satisfaction, but intentions-in-action are causally self-referential: they represent the action and the causation of the action by that very intention. Searle's account connects intention to his broader analysis of intentionality and biological naturalism.

*Key works:* *Intentionality* (1983); *Rationality in Action* (2001); *The Construction of Social Reality* (1995)

---

**Alfred Mele** (b. 1951)

Mele has contributed detailed empirical and conceptual work on intention, motivation, and self-control. His analysis of akrasia (acting against one's better judgment) and his distinction between "distal" (future-directed) and "proximal" (action-initiating) intentions have refined the planning theory. Mele is also known for his work on the neuroscience of free will, engaging closely with Libet experiments and their implications for intentional action.

*Key works:* *Springs of Action* (1992); *Autonomous Agents* (1995); *Free: Why Science Hasn't Disproved Free Will* (2014)
