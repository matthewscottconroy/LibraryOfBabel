# Libertarianism About Free Will

The problem of free will is one of philosophy's most persistent puzzles, and its persistence reflects the fact that the considerations on multiple sides are genuinely compelling. Libertarianism about free will — not to be confused with the political position — holds that free will is real, that it is incompatible with determinism, and that determinism is false. This combination allows the libertarian to maintain that genuine freedom exists: we are not merely complex causal machines playing out a script written into the initial conditions of the universe.

**What Free Will Requires**

Libertarianism begins from an analysis of what free will involves. The core intuition is that for an action to be genuinely free, the agent must have been able to do otherwise — not merely in some counterfactual sense, but in a robust sense that implies the actual causal history of the universe does not necessitate the action. This is the "leeway" requirement on free will.

If the universe is deterministic — if the laws of nature plus the complete state of the universe at any earlier time entail the complete state at any later time — then there is a clear sense in which no one could ever have done otherwise than they did. Your action was the inevitable result of causes stretching back to before you were born. The libertarian takes this implication seriously: if determinism is true, free will (in the relevant sense) does not exist.

**The Challenge: Luck and Control**

The libertarian's commitment to indeterminism creates its own problem, sometimes called the "luck" or "consequence argument in reverse." If your action was not determined — if the causal history of the universe did not necessitate it — then something in the causal chain leading to your action was genuinely random. But randomness seems just as inimical to responsible agency as determinism. If an indeterministic event in your brain caused your action, it is hard to see how that improves your control over it. You are not responsible for quantum-level randomness any more than you are for the necessitated consequences of prior causes.

This is the libertarian's central challenge: to explain how indeterminism can be freedom-conferring rather than merely luck-introducing. The most sophisticated libertarian theories are attempts to solve this problem.

**Agent Causation**

The oldest and most robust libertarian tradition invokes agent causation: the idea that agents (persons, selves) are a distinctive kind of cause that cannot be reduced to event causation. When you freely choose, you — as an agent — initiate a causal chain. This initiation is not determined by prior events, nor is it random: it is caused by you as a substance or self.

Thomas Reid and Roderick Chisholm developed this tradition in the early modern and analytic periods respectively. Chisholm argued that we must distinguish "transeunt" causation (one event causing another) from "immanent" causation (an agent causing an event). Free action involves immanent causation: you, as an agent, are the unmoved mover of your own actions.

The challenge for agent causation is metaphysical: what is an agent that it can be a cause? How does agent causation connect to the physical world? If agent causation is irreducible to event causation, it seems to require that agents stand outside the physical causal order — a view that is difficult to reconcile with what we know about the physical basis of mind.

**Non-Occurrentist Libertarianism**

An alternative strategy is to locate freedom not in a special kind of agent causation, but in the structure of practical deliberation. Carl Ginet and others have argued that the phenomenology of deliberation — the felt openness of the future, the genuine weighing of alternatives — gives us direct evidence that the future is not determined. On this view, the first-person perspective on deliberation is irreducibly indeterministic in character.

**Reasons-Responsive Libertarianism**

Robert Kane's influential account attempts to naturalize libertarianism by locating the relevant indeterminism at the level of neural processes during moments of genuine practical conflict. In situations of "self-forming actions" — cases where an agent faces a genuine choice between competing values — the neural underpinnings of practical deliberation may be genuinely indeterministic. What makes the resulting action free is not the randomness as such, but the fact that the agent's own reasons and values are the locus of the indeterminism: the quantum-level events are amplified in a way that reflects the genuine weighing of competing reasons.

Kane's strategy is to deny that randomness undermines agency when the random process is itself an expression of the agent's practical deliberation. The problem of luck is not fully dissolved, but the intuitive force of the objection is reduced: if I must choose between two paths I genuinely value, and the choice is indeterministic because my reasons pull genuinely in both directions, the outcome is not simply random noise — it is an expression of who I am.

**Libertarianism and the Self**

Libertarian accounts of free will often connect to broader questions about the nature of the self. The demand for libertarian freedom seems to presuppose a self that stands in a special relationship to its actions — not merely as the source of their causes, but as their author in a deeper sense. This connects libertarianism to discussions of personal identity, narrative selfhood, and the phenomenology of agency. The question of whether there is any metaphysical position that can do justice to both the scientific picture of the causal order and the first-person sense of genuine openness remains one of the most challenging in philosophy of mind.
