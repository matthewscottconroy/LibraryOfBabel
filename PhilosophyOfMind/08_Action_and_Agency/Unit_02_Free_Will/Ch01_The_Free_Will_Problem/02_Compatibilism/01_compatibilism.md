# Compatibilism

Compatibilism holds that free will and determinism are compatible — that the truth of determinism would not undermine the kind of freedom worth wanting. This is the most widely held position among professional philosophers, and it has a long pedigree stretching from Hobbes and Hume through Mill, Moore, and Ayer to contemporary defenders like Harry Frankfurt, Susan Wolf, John Martin Fischer, and Daniel Dennett.

**The Compatibilist Strategy**

The compatibilist does not deny the force of the determinism-versus-freedom intuition. Instead, the compatibilist argues that the intuition rests on a confused conception of freedom. What we actually care about — what grounds moral responsibility, what matters in practical deliberation, what distinguishes the autonomous agent from the coerced one — does not require indeterminism.

The classic compatibilist move, made by Hume and echoed by many successors, is to analyze freedom as acting from one's own desires and motivations without external compulsion. A person is free when nothing prevents her from doing what she wants to do. Constraint, coercion, and compulsion are the relevant threats to freedom — not the causal determination of one's own desires by prior events in the world.

On this analysis, the relevant contrast is not between "causally determined" and "causally undetermined," but between "acting from internal causes (one's own character, values, and motivations)" and "acting from external causes (force, manipulation, addiction, or compulsion)." The person who acts at gunpoint lacks freedom not because her action is determined, but because the determining cause is external coercion. The person who acts from her settled values and deliberative commitments acts freely even if her action is determined.

**Frankfurt's Hierarchical Account**

Harry Frankfurt's enormously influential paper "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971) shifted the debate by introducing hierarchical desires. Frankfurt argues that what matters for free will is not whether an action was determined, but whether the agent acts from the kind of will she endorses at a higher level.

Frankfurt distinguishes first-order desires (desires to do things) from second-order desires (desires about which first-order desires one wants to have or to move one to action). A person has free will when her effective will — the desire that actually moves her to action — is one she endorses at the second-order level. The heroin addict who wishes she did not want heroin has a first-order desire for heroin but a second-order desire not to be moved by that desire; she acts unfreely. The person who wholeheartedly embraces her values and acts from them acts freely.

Frankfurt's account elegantly handles addicts, phobics, and compulsives: what is wrong with their agency is not that they are determined, but that they act from motivational states they themselves disavow. Freedom is a matter of the hierarchical structure of one's motivational system, not of its causal history.

**Susan Wolf's Reason-Responsiveness**

Susan Wolf's compatibilism emphasizes the ability to act in accordance with reason. A free agent is one who is able to recognize and respond to reasons — particularly moral reasons — and to act on that recognition. The relevant contrast is not between determined and undetermined, but between reason-responsive agents (who can adjust their behavior in light of reasons) and those whose agency is impaired by delusion, manipulation, or neurological dysfunction.

Wolf's account has a normative asymmetry built in: freedom requires the ability to act well (to respond to good reasons), but not the ability to act badly. This asymmetry is controversial but captures something intuitive — we do not think the capacity to do terrible things is a prerequisite for moral responsibility.

**Fischer's Guidance Control**

John Martin Fischer distinguishes two senses of "could have done otherwise." The kind required for moral responsibility, he argues, is not alternative possibilities (the libertarian's leeway requirement) but "guidance control" — acting from a reasons-responsive mechanism that is the agent's own. Fischer argues that even if we cannot do otherwise in the libertarian sense, we can still be responsible when our action flows from our own reasons-responsive deliberation.

Fischer's account handles Frankfurt cases — cases designed to show that alternative possibilities are not required for responsibility. In Frankfurt cases, a neuroscientist or demon stands ready to intervene if you are about to make a different choice, but does not need to intervene because you make the target choice on your own. You could not have done otherwise (the intervention was always standing by), yet you seem fully responsible. Fischer takes these cases to confirm that alternative possibilities are not required for responsibility.

**The Manipulation Objection**

The compatibilist faces a serious challenge from manipulation scenarios. Suppose a neuroscientist covertly programs all of your values, beliefs, and reasoning capacities from birth, and you then act "freely" from these programmed states. On the compatibilist account, you satisfy the conditions for free will — you are acting from your own values, in a reasons-responsive way, with hierarchical endorsement. But intuitively, the programming seems to undermine freedom.

Compatibilists respond by arguing that what matters is the history of how character-formation occurs: normal socialization and development are compatible with freedom, while covert manipulation is not. Derk Pereboom and others have used manipulation cases to argue that no compatibilist account can draw a principled line between normal causal determination and manipulation. This remains the sharpest edge of the debate.

**Why Compatibilism Matters**

The practical stakes of the free will debate are high: moral responsibility, punishment, praise, and blame all presuppose that agents are free in some relevant sense. Compatibilism is attractive partly because it preserves the framework of moral responsibility even if the universe turns out to be deterministic. The compatibilist can say: whatever physics tells us about the causal order, the kind of freedom that matters for holding people accountable is not threatened.
