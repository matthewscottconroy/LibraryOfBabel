# Hard Determinism and Free Will Skepticism

Hard determinism holds that determinism is true and that free will is incompatible with determinism, and therefore free will does not exist. Free will skepticism is the broader view that, whatever the ultimate truth about determinism, the kind of free will required for robust moral responsibility does not exist. These positions are unpopular partly because their practical implications are revisionary — they threaten to undermine the entire framework of blame, punishment, and desert that structures ordinary moral life.

**The Classical Argument**

The hard determinist argument is simple and powerful. The past is fixed; the laws of nature are not up to you; the past plus the laws entail everything about the future, including your actions. Therefore your actions were determined before you were born. Therefore you could not have done otherwise. Therefore you are not free in any robust sense.

Baron d'Holbach made this argument in the eighteenth century with characteristic bluntness: a man who thinks he is free is like a stone that thinks it chose to fall. Everything we call "will" is the product of prior causes — brain states, environmental influences, the entire causal history of the universe. There is no homunculus within who stands apart from the causal order and "really" chooses.

**Contemporary Hard Incompatibilism**

Derk Pereboom's "hard incompatibilism" is the most carefully developed contemporary version. Pereboom argues that neither compatibilist nor libertarian accounts succeed in securing the kind of free will required for basic desert — the kind of moral responsibility on which retributive punishment is grounded. He accepts that determinism would rule out this kind of responsibility, and he also argues that the kind of indeterminism the libertarian invokes (quantum-level randomness, agent causation) fails to deliver the control required for basic desert.

Pereboom is not a nihilist about agency. He distinguishes basic desert moral responsibility (deserving reward or punishment purely on account of what one has done, as a matter of justice) from weaker notions of responsibility that can survive his skepticism. We can still hold attitudes of gratitude, forward-looking concern, and relationship-appropriate responses toward agents even if they do not deserve punishment or reward in the basic desert sense. The goal is a revised moral psychology that preserves what matters in our reactive practices while abandoning what is indefensible.

**P.F. Strawson and the Reactive Attitudes**

P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962) is the most influential intervention in the free will debate of the twentieth century, and it mounted a challenge to both hard determinism and to certain versions of compatibilism that locate freedom in "objective" conditions.

Strawson argued that our practices of moral responsibility are grounded in what he called "reactive attitudes" — resentment, gratitude, indignation, moral praise and blame — that are not mere cognitive assessments of causal history but expressions of the interpersonal attitudes we hold toward each other as fellow members of a moral community. These reactive attitudes are not optional add-ons to human life; they are constitutive of our form of life as social, engaged beings.

The determinist might urge us to adopt what Strawson calls the "objective attitude" — viewing others as objects to be managed, predicted, and treated rather than as agents to be engaged with. But Strawson argues that the wholesale adoption of the objective attitude is not a real possibility for human beings. We are constitutively participants in the practices of holding one another responsible, and it would take something far more devastating than a philosophical argument about causal determination to dislodge us from them.

This is not quite compatibilism: Strawson does not argue that freedom and determinism are compatible in any straightforward metaphysical sense. His point is more pragmatic — that the question of whether determinism is true does not touch the grounds of our responsibility practices, which are grounded in our reactive attitudes rather than in metaphysical facts about the causal order.

**Free Will Skepticism in Practice**

If the free will skeptic is right, the implications for moral and legal practice are substantial. Retributive punishment — the idea that criminals deserve to suffer as a matter of justice — loses its foundation. The appropriate response to wrongdoing becomes forward-looking: deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, and victim protection rather than desert-based suffering.

Pereboom argues that this is not as revisionary as it sounds. We already accept forward-looking justifications for many of our institutions, and the emotional attitudes of resentment and indignation can be partially reconstructed on forward-looking grounds (expressing disapproval and communicating norms) even without the backstory of basic desert.

Hard determinists often note that their view does not undermine all reactive attitudes. Gratitude, forgiveness, and even certain forms of moral anger can survive: we can still care deeply about how people treat us, prefer and reward good behavior, and express values through our responses, without assuming that the wrongdoer "really deserved" punishment in the deepest metaphysical sense.

**The Luck Argument**

A more recent strand of skepticism focuses not on determinism but on luck. Even granting libertarian indeterminism, agents do not deserve credit or blame for factors that were matters of luck — their genes, upbringing, social position, initial temperament. Since so much of what makes us who we are was a matter of luck, and since our actions flow from who we are, it is hard to see how we can deserve credit or blame for them in the ultimate sense required by retributive justice.

This "luck argument" (associated with Thomas Nagel and later Pereboom) is distinct from the argument from determinism. It targets the presuppositions of desert-based responsibility rather than the metaphysics of causation, and it has bite even for libertarians who think they have solved the determinism problem.
