# Further Reading: The Free Will Problem

## Primary Sources

**Harry Frankfurt, "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" (1969)** — Frankfurt argues that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise (the Principle of Alternate Possibilities), using "Frankfurt cases" in which an agent acts of their own accord despite being unable to do otherwise. Transformed the free will debate.

**P.F. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment" (1962)** — Strawson argues that the free will problem cannot be resolved by metaphysical argument, because our practices of holding responsible — our "reactive attitudes" of resentment, gratitude, and indignation — are constitutive of human relationships and not in question regardless of causal determinism.

**Peter van Inwagen, *An Essay on Free Will* (1983)** — Van Inwagen's "consequence argument" provides the most rigorous statement of the incompatibilist position: if determinism is true, our actions are consequences of the laws of nature and events in the remote past, and we have no control over these, so we have no control over our actions.

## Secondary Literature

**Harry Frankfurt, "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971)** — Frankfurt's hierarchical theory of free will: free will requires that one's first-order desires be endorsed by second-order volitions (desires about what desires one wants to act from). An influential compatibilist account.

**David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section VIII (1748)** — Hume's classic compatibilist account: freedom consists not in the absence of causal determination but in acting from one's own motives and character without external compulsion.

**Susan Wolf, *Freedom Within Reason* (1990)** — Wolf argues that free will requires the ability to act in accordance with Reason — not mere coherence of desires but genuine responsiveness to normative reasons. A sophisticated alternative to both libertarianism and standard compatibilism.

**Gary Watson, "Free Agency" (1975)** — Watson argues for a reasons-responsiveness account of free will, distinguishing between what an agent wants and what an agent values (their "valuational system"), with free action stemming from the valuational system.

**Robert Kane, *The Significance of Free Will* (1996)** — The most developed contemporary defense of libertarian free will, arguing that genuine indeterminism at the neural level (quantum indeterminacy) provides the space needed for self-forming actions.
