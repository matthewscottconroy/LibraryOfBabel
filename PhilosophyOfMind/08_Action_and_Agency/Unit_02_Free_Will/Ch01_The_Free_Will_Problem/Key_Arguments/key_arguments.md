# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: The Free Will Problem

## Key Arguments

**Van Inwagen's Consequence Argument**
Peter van Inwagen argues that if determinism is true, then all our actions are the consequences of the laws of nature and events in the remote past. We have no power over the laws of nature, and we have no power over events in the remote past. Therefore, we have no power over the consequences of these things—including all our actions. The argument establishes incompatibilism: free will (the ability to have done otherwise) is incompatible with determinism, not a matter of our attitudes or how we talk about freedom.

**Frankfurt's Challenge to the Principle of Alternate Possibilities (PAP)**
Harry Frankfurt argues that the principle of alternate possibilities (PAP: you are morally responsible for your action only if you could have done otherwise) is false, by constructing cases in which an agent seems fully responsible but could not have done otherwise. In a Frankfurt case, a neuroscientist has arranged to cause you to choose A if you show signs of choosing otherwise—but you choose A entirely on your own. Since the intervention was unnecessary, you seem responsible, even though you could not have done other than choose A. Frankfurt cases are designed to separate moral responsibility from the ability to do otherwise.

**Fischer's Semi-Compatibilism**
John Martin Fischer argues that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise (contra PAP, following Frankfurt) but does require "reasons-responsiveness": the agent's action must be produced by a mechanism that would respond to reasons in normal circumstances. The agent need not have been able to do otherwise on this specific occasion, but they must be the kind of agent whose motivation-producing mechanism is responsive to reasons. Fischer's semi-compatibilism preserves moral responsibility within a broadly deterministic world without requiring alternative possibilities.

**Strawson's Argument from Reactive Attitudes**
P.F. Strawson argues in "Freedom and Resentment" that the free will debate is often conducted at too abstract a level. What is fundamental is our participation in the "reactive attitudes"—the practical attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, love, moral approval) that constitute personal relationships. These reactive attitudes are not justified by metaphysical theses about determinism or indeterminism; they are fundamental to human life. A world without these attitudes would be incomprehensible as a human life. Moral responsibility is grounded in the practice of holding and expressing reactive attitudes, not in any metaphysical freedom.

**Wolf's Reason View of Freedom**
Susan Wolf argues that free will requires the ability to act in accordance with reason—the "Reason View." An agent acts freely when they act on the basis of their best judgment about what is good, and when that judgment is itself formed by properly functioning rational faculties. Free will is thus not mere freedom from external constraint or even freedom from one's own desires, but the ability to align one's will with what is actually good. This asymmetric account holds that you act freely when you do what is right (no further condition of "could have done otherwise" is needed) but not when you do what is wrong.

## Core Concepts

**Compatibilism**
Compatibilism holds that free will—the kind of free will needed for moral responsibility—is compatible with determinism. Compatibilists typically argue that what matters for freedom is not the ability to have done otherwise in an absolute metaphysical sense but something like: acting without external compulsion, acting from one's own desires and values, being reasons-responsive. Hume, Hobbes, and contemporary philosophers (Fischer, Dennett, Wolf) are compatibilists. The central challenge is to show that the "freedom" compatibilists preserve is genuinely valuable and not merely a pale substitute for the libertarian's robust freedom.

**Incompatibilism**
Incompatibilism holds that free will (in the robust sense required for moral responsibility) is incompatible with determinism. Hard incompatibilists (hard determinists) accept determinism and conclude that no one is ever morally responsible. Libertarians (Kane, van Inwagen) accept that free will requires genuine alternative possibilities and argue for the incompatibilist claim while maintaining that free will exists (and hence that determinism is false at the relevant level). Incompatibilism is motivated by the intuition that if all actions are determined by prior causes, agents are never the ultimate source of their actions.

**The Principle of Alternate Possibilities (PAP)**
PAP holds that a person is morally responsible for their action only if they could have done otherwise. This principle connects moral responsibility to the incompatibilist conception of freedom: if determinism eliminates alternative possibilities, and PAP is correct, then determinism eliminates moral responsibility. Frankfurt's cases are designed to show PAP is false: moral responsibility is possible even when the agent could not have done otherwise. Most contemporary compatibilists have abandoned PAP in favor of reasons-responsiveness or historical accounts.

**Reactive Attitudes**
Strawson's reactive attitudes are the emotional and practical responses we have to agents that reflect our assessment of their will—resentment (toward those who wrong us), gratitude (toward those who benefit us), indignation (toward those who wrong others), moral approval and disapproval. These attitudes are not justifiable or unjustifiable by appeal to metaphysical facts about determinism; they are constitutive of interpersonal relations. Strawson argues that the philosophical question about free will should be recast in terms of when it is appropriate to adopt the "objective stance" (suspending reactive attitudes, as with the mentally ill) rather than the personal stance.

**Libertarianism About Free Will**
Libertarianism (about free will, not politics) holds that free will exists and is incompatible with determinism. Libertarians argue that genuine alternative possibilities exist, enabled by either agent causation (Kane), indeterministic quantum events in neural processes (Penrose), or causal indeterminism at the level of practical reasoning. The challenge for libertarianism is to show how indeterminism in neural processes contributes to—rather than undermining—genuine agency: random indeterminism does not seem to make action more "free" in any morally relevant sense.

## Thought Experiments

**Frankfurt's Neuroscientist Case**
Jones is about to decide whether to vote for candidate A. A neuroscientist who wants Jones to vote for A has implanted a device that will cause Jones to choose A if he shows signs of choosing otherwise. Jones chooses A entirely on his own, with no intervention from the device. Is Jones responsible for voting for A? Frankfurt argues yes—Jones's choice was entirely his own, not the product of the device. Yet Jones could not have done otherwise (the device would have intervened). Therefore PAP is false: responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise.

**Van Inwagen's Consequence Argument Illustration**
Imagine a detailed physical description of the universe one million years before your birth, plus all the laws of nature. These together entail exactly what you will do this morning—whether to eat toast or cereal. You had no power over the initial conditions; you have no power over the laws. Therefore you have no power over what was entailed by them. Van Inwagen invites us to feel the force of this: in a deterministic world, your actions are "rolled out" by the universe's initial conditions in a way that leaves no room for you to have done otherwise.

**Strawson's Visit to the Rehabilitation Center**
Strawson asks us to imagine visiting a rehabilitation center for people with a particularly virulent illness that consistently causes them to harm others. We would adopt the "objective" stance toward them—treating them as objects of therapy, not as participants in the practice of holding and expressing reactive attitudes. Now imagine being told that, by a strange medical discovery, all human wrongdoing is caused by a virus of this kind. Do we abandon all reactive attitudes toward everyone? Strawson argues our resistance to this conclusion shows that the reactive attitudes are fundamental and not straightforwardly overridable by abstract metaphysical considerations.
