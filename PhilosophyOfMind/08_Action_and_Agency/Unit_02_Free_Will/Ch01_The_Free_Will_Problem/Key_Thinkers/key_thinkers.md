# Key Thinkers: The Free Will Problem

---

**Peter van Inwagen** (b. 1942)

Van Inwagen is the leading contemporary defender of incompatibilism. His "consequence argument" states that if determinism is true, then our actions are consequences of laws of nature and events before our birth, neither of which is in our power; therefore, neither are the consequences of these — our actions — in our power. This argument has been extremely influential in establishing the terms of the free will debate. Van Inwagen himself does not know how to reconcile his incompatibilism with a positive account of libertarian free will.

*Key works:* *An Essay on Free Will* (1983); "The Incompatibility of Free Will and Determinism" (1975); *Metaphysics* (1993)

---

**P.F. Strawson** (1919–2006)

Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962) transformed the free will debate by locating the issue in the sphere of human relationships rather than metaphysics. He argued that practices of moral responsibility — holding people accountable through reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation) — are not in question in the face of determinism, because these attitudes express our participation in human relationships, not theoretical claims about the causal structure of the world. This "participant stance" account continues to generate debate.

*Key works:* "Freedom and Resentment" (1962); *Individuals* (1959); *Skepticism and Naturalism* (1985)

---

**Harry Frankfurt** (b. 1929)

Frankfurt transformed compatibilism with two pivotal contributions: his Frankfurt cases (arguing that moral responsibility does not require alternate possibilities) and his hierarchical theory of free will (arguing that free will consists in acting from first-order desires endorsed by second-order volitions). The Frankfurt case literature has generated a vast secondary literature, and his hierarchical account influenced reason-responsive compatibilism. Frankfurt later developed a theory of love and caring as central to what matters to persons.

*Key works:* "Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility" (1969); "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971); *The Importance of What We Care About* (1988)

---

**Susan Wolf** (b. 1952)

Wolf argues that free will requires both freedom from constraint (the ability to act on one's desires) and responsiveness to Reason (the ability to recognize and be guided by normative reasons for action). Her "Reason View" holds that a person is fully responsible only if they could have done otherwise by recognizing the reasons that were actually present. Wolf is also known for her argument that moral sainthood — perfect responsiveness to morality — would be incompatible with a fully human, flourishing life.

*Key works:* *Freedom Within Reason* (1990); "Sanity and the Metaphysics of Responsibility" (1987); *Meaning in Life and Why It Matters* (2010)

---

**Robert Kane** (b. 1938)

Kane is the most prominent contemporary defender of libertarian free will. He argues that some actions — "self-forming actions" — are genuinely undetermined, grounded in quantum indeterminacy in neural processes at moments of genuine internal conflict. These actions can be free in a robust, non-compatibilist sense because the agent both rationally endorses whatever outcome occurs and remains fully responsible for it. Kane's account is the most scientifically informed attempt to ground libertarian free will.

*Key works:* *The Significance of Free Will* (1996); *Through the Moral Maze* (1994); *A Contemporary Introduction to Free Will* (2005)
