# The Libet Experiments

Benjamin Libet's experiments in the 1980s became perhaps the most cited empirical findings in the free will debate, spawning decades of follow-up research, philosophical commentary, and popular discussion. Libet's results seem to show that the neural preparation for voluntary action begins before subjects are aware of deciding to act — a finding with obvious implications for how we think about conscious will and its role in initiating behavior.

**The Experimental Setup**

Libet's paradigm was elegantly simple. Subjects were asked to perform a spontaneous wrist-flick at a time of their own choosing, while watching a clock with a rapidly rotating hand. They were asked to note the position of the clock hand at the moment they first became aware of the intention or urge to move — the moment of "W" (will). Meanwhile, EEG measured the electrical activity of the motor cortex, and EMG measured muscle activity.

The key finding was the relationship between three events: the readiness potential (RP, also called Bereitschaftspotential), the conscious awareness of intending to move (W), and the actual muscle activation (M).

The readiness potential — a slow build-up of electrical activity in the motor cortex associated with voluntary movement preparation — typically began about 550 milliseconds before the muscle movement. Subjects reported the moment of conscious intention at about 200 milliseconds before movement. This means that unconscious neural preparation was underway roughly 350 milliseconds before subjects were consciously aware of deciding to act.

**The Initial Interpretation**

The straightforward interpretation seemed devastating for the role of conscious will: the brain has already committed to the action before the agent consciously decides to perform it. Conscious awareness of intention appears to be an after-the-fact report on a process already underway, not the initiating cause of the action.

Daniel Wegner drew the broadest conclusion from such findings in his *The Illusion of Conscious Will* (2002): conscious will is systematically an epiphenomenon. We experience ourselves as consciously causing our actions, but this experience is a construction — the brain produces both the action and the feeling of willing, and the feeling of willing does not cause the action. On this interpretation, the Libet experiments provide empirical support for a kind of epiphenomenalism about conscious experience.

**Libet's Own Interpretation**

Importantly, Libet himself did not draw this conclusion. He noted that subjects retained the ability to "veto" — to inhibit the action after the readiness potential had begun. In trials where subjects consciously stopped themselves from moving, they reported being aware of the urge to move (which was "pre-loaded" in the readiness potential) and then consciously choosing not to act. Libet proposed that conscious will may not initiate action, but it can control or veto actions initiated unconsciously. This is a more modest but still significant role for consciousness in the governance of behavior.

**Methodological Critiques**

The Libet experiments have faced extensive methodological criticism.

*The introspection problem*: Subjects are asked to report the precise moment of conscious awareness by correlating their phenomenal experience with the position of a clock hand. This is a difficult introspective task, and there is evidence that retrospective clock reports are unreliable. The reported W time may itself be confabulated or influenced by the movement itself.

*The RP interpretation*: More recent research has questioned whether the readiness potential reflects a "decision" to move at all. It may reflect a general state of motor preparation or noise in neural activity rather than a committed decision. Some researchers argue the RP is present even on trials where subjects ultimately do not move, which suggests it is not a neural correlate of the decision itself.

*The generalizability problem*: The Libet paradigm uses highly artificial, spontaneous, low-stakes movements that have no apparent meaning or reason. It is unclear whether findings about such thin behavioral acts generalize to the complex, reason-responsive, reflectively endorsed decisions that are the proper subject of the free will debate.

**Subsequent Research**

More recent experiments using fMRI have extended Libet's findings, finding that brain activity predictive of left or right button presses can be detected as much as 10 seconds before subjects report the decision. John-Dylan Haynes and colleagues used the predictive content of pre-decision brain activity to correctly predict subjects' choices at above-chance levels before subjects were aware of having made a choice.

These findings deepen the challenge to conscious will, but face similar methodological objections — especially about the reliability of self-reported decision times and the nature of what the measured brain activity represents.

**Philosophical Assessment**

The philosophical consensus is that the Libet experiments, while genuinely interesting, do not straightforwardly establish that free will is an illusion or that conscious will is epiphenomenal.

The main reasons: First, the experiments address "thin" voluntary action, not the complex, reason-responsive deliberation that matters for free will. Second, the identification of RP onset with "the decision" is questionable. Third, even granting that conscious intention follows rather than precedes neural preparation, this is compatible with consciousness playing an important governance role (as Libet himself suggested) and with the will being efficacious through patterns of neural activity that flow through conscious states at a different timescale.

The experiments are best understood as illuminating the relationship between conscious experience and underlying neural processes in voluntary action — a genuinely important empirical question — rather than as settling the philosophical debate about free will.
