# Further Reading: Neuroscience and Free Will

## Primary Sources

**Benjamin Libet, Curtis Gleason, Elwood Wright, and Dennis Pearl, "Time of Conscious Intention to Act in Relation to Onset of Cerebral Activity (Readiness-Potential)" (1983)** — The landmark Libet experiment: the readiness potential (neural preparation for movement) precedes the subject's reported awareness of the intention to move by approximately 350 milliseconds, apparently suggesting that conscious will comes after the brain "decides."

**Benjamin Libet, "Unconscious Cerebral Initiative and the Role of Conscious Will in Voluntary Action" (1985)** — Libet's own interpretation of his results: conscious will has a "veto" power that can override the process initiated unconsciously, preserving a role for conscious agency.

**Patrick Haggard, "Human Volition: Towards a Neuroscience of Will" (2008)** — A comprehensive review of neuroscientific research on voluntary action, readiness potentials, and the neural correlates of the sense of agency. Excellent survey article.

## Secondary Literature

**Alfred Mele, *Free: Why Science Hasn't Disproved Free Will* (2014)** — A philosopher's careful response to neuroscientific arguments against free will, arguing that Libet-style experiments do not establish determinism or the non-existence of conscious causation of action.

**Daniel Dennett, "Correcting a Surprisingly Widespread Error" in *Freedom Evolves* (2003)** — Dennett argues that the Libet experiment has been widely misinterpreted; conscious decisions need not precede all neural preparation for movement, and the "veto" function is important.

**Adina Roskies, "Neuroscientific Challenges to Free Will and Responsibility" (2006)** — A careful philosophical analysis of what neuroscientific results actually show about freedom and responsibility, arguing that Libet-style findings are less threatening than often assumed.

**John Dylan Haynes et al., "Reading Hidden Intentions in the Human Brain" (2007)** — An fMRI study suggesting that the outcome of decisions can be decoded from brain activity up to 10 seconds before the subject reports making a conscious decision, raising the stakes of the neuroscientific challenge to free will.

**Eddy Nahmias, "Is Free Will an Illusion? Confronting Challenges from the Modern Mind Sciences" (2011)** — An accessible philosophical treatment evaluating the most influential neuroscientific arguments against free will, arguing that they rest on misconceptions about what free will requires.
