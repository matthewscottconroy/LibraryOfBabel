# Key Thinkers: Neuroscience and Free Will

---

**Benjamin Libet** (1916–2007)

Libet is the neuroscientist whose experiments on the timing of voluntary action have had the greatest philosophical impact. His 1983 experiment showed that the readiness potential (RP) — the neural correlate of preparing to move — precedes subjects' reported awareness of intending to move by around 350ms. This was widely interpreted as evidence that conscious will is an after-thought rather than a cause of action. Libet himself argued that the conscious veto of action was still intact, preserving a role for free will.

*Key works:* "Time of Conscious Intention to Act" (1983); "Unconscious Cerebral Initiative and the Role of Conscious Will" (1985); *Mind Time: The Temporal Factor in Consciousness* (2004)

---

**Daniel Wegner** (1948–2013)

Wegner's research on the "illusion of conscious will" is the most influential psychological argument against conscious causation of action. He argued that the experience of willing an action is a post-hoc inference — we experience ourselves as causing our actions not because we actually do, but because our conscious thoughts about acting are predictably associated with subsequent movements. His work drew on experiments on automatism, hypnosis, and Ouija boards to support a deflationary account of agency.

*Key works:* *The Illusion of Conscious Will* (2002); "The Mind's Best Trick: How We Experience Conscious Will" (2003); "Précis of The Illusion of Conscious Will" (2004)

---

**Alfred Mele** (b. 1951)

Mele is the philosopher who has most systematically examined neuroscientific challenges to free will. In *Free* (2014), he argues that Libet-style experiments are typically over-interpreted: they do not show that decisions are made unconsciously (the RP may not be a decision marker), and they are compatible with subsequent conscious influence on action. Mele distinguishes "free will" as required for moral responsibility from the more demanding libertarian notion that neuroscience is supposed to threaten.

*Key works:* *Free: Why Science Hasn't Disproved Free Will* (2014); *Effective Intentions* (2009); *Autonomous Agents* (1995)

---

**Patrick Haggard** (b. 1965)

Haggard is a neuroscientist at University College London whose research has extended and refined Libet's paradigm, examining the neural correlates of voluntary movement, the sense of agency, and the "binding" of actions to their effects. His work has made the neuroscience of voluntary action more nuanced, distinguishing different components of agency (preparation, initiation, awareness, ownership) and their neural substrates.

*Key works:* "Human Volition: Towards a Neuroscience of Will" (2008); "Conscious Intention and Motor Cognition" (2005); "Sensory Attenuation" (various)
