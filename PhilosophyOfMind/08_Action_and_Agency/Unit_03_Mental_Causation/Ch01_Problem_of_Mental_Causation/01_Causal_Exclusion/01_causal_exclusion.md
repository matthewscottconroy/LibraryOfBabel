# The Causal Exclusion Problem

Mental causation — the capacity of mental states to cause things to happen — seems obviously real. Your belief that there is coffee in the kitchen causes you to walk there. Your pain causes you to pull away from the hot stove. If mental states did not cause anything, they would be mere passengers in the causal flow of the universe, and the common-sense picture of human agency would be radically wrong.

Yet there is a powerful argument that mental causation is philosophically problematic — indeed, that it is strictly impossible if certain background assumptions are accepted. This is the causal exclusion problem, developed most systematically by Jaegwon Kim.

**The Structure of the Problem**

The exclusion problem arises from a tension between three apparently reasonable commitments:

1. *Mental-physical supervenience*: Mental properties supervene on physical properties. There cannot be a difference in mental properties without a difference in physical properties. (Most non-dualists accept this.)

2. *Physical causal closure*: Every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. The physical world is causally closed — you do not need to appeal to non-physical causes to explain physical effects. (Widely accepted by anyone who takes physics seriously.)

3. *Mental causation*: Some physical events (including behaviors) have mental states as among their causes.

The tension: if every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, and mental states are distinct from physical states (as non-reductive physicalists hold), then there seems to be no causal work left for mental states to do. The physical causes already "use up" all the causal power needed to produce the physical effects. Positing additional mental causes would be causal overdetermination — the physical effect has two sufficient causes, mental and physical.

Overdetermination is not logically impossible, but it is typically taken to be unusual and suspicious. We do not think that every physical effect has two sets of sufficient causes. If the physical cause is sufficient, the mental cause is redundant — and causally redundant entities raise the question of whether they are doing anything at all.

**Kim's Formal Argument**

Kim's version of the exclusion argument runs as follows. Suppose mental event M causes physical event P*. M supervenes on some physical event P. P also causes P* (by physical causal closure). Now: either M is identical to P (type or token identity), or M is not. If M is identical to P, we have reductive physicalism, and mental causation is secured — but at the cost of reduction. If M is not identical to P, then P* has two sufficient causes: P (the physical supervenience base of M) and M itself. This is problematic overdetermination.

Kim's conclusion is that non-reductive physicalism — the view that mental properties are real but not reducible to physical properties — cannot accommodate genuine mental causation. The mental either gets "excluded" by the physical (epiphenomenalism) or it must be reduced to the physical (reductionism). The non-reductive physicalist is caught between these unwelcome alternatives.

**Why This Matters**

The stakes are high. Non-reductive physicalism has been the dominant position in philosophy of mind for several decades — it promises to accommodate the reality of mental properties while honoring the findings of physical science. Kim's argument suggests that this position is unstable: if mental properties are neither reducible to physical properties nor identical to them, they cannot be genuine causes.

The implications spread outward. If mental properties are not causally efficacious, the entire picture of rational agency — acting for reasons, being responsive to evidence, deliberating and choosing — is undermined. We would be sophisticated machines whose "mental" properties are decorative rather than functional.

**The Exclusion Problem and the Hard Problem**

The causal exclusion problem connects to the hard problem of consciousness. Phenomenal properties — the qualitative "what it's like" of experience — are precisely the properties that seem hardest to reduce to physical properties. If Kim's argument is right, these properties are causally inert: they have no causal powers that their physical realizers do not already account for.

This connection gives the exclusion problem extra bite. Not only does it threaten the causal role of intentional properties (beliefs, desires, intentions), it threatens the causal role of qualitative experience — the redness of red, the painfulness of pain. A physicalism that must concede that qualia are causally inert seems to have lost something central to the reality of the mental.
