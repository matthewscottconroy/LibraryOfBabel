# Further Reading: The Problem of Mental Causation

## Primary Sources

**Jaegwon Kim, "Epiphenomenal and Supervenient Causation" (1984)** — Kim argues that if mental properties are non-physical but supervene on physical properties, then physical properties exclude mental properties from doing independent causal work. This is an early statement of the "causal exclusion" problem.

**Jaegwon Kim, *Mind in a Physical World* (1998)** — Kim's systematic treatment of the exclusion problem, arguing that non-reductive physicalism is unstable because it cannot account for mental causation without collapsing into either reduction or epiphenomenalism.

**Donald Davidson, "Mental Events" (1970)** — Davidson's anomalous monism: mental events are identical to physical events and thus causally efficacious, but mental descriptions cannot be reduced to or systematically connected to physical descriptions. The mental is "anomalous" — there are no strict psychophysical laws.

## Secondary Literature

**Stephen Yablo, "Mental Causation" (1992)** — Yablo argues that the exclusion problem can be dissolved by careful analysis: mental properties can be causally sufficient without "excluding" the physical if mental and physical properties stand in the right determinable-determinate relationship.

**Brian McLaughlin, "Type Epiphenomenalism, Type Dualism, and the Causal Priority of the Physical" (1989)** — Examines whether non-reductive physicalism can avoid the conclusion that mental property types are epiphenomenal, surveying the main responses to the exclusion problem.

**Karen Bennett, "Why the Exclusion Problem Seems Intractable, and How, Just Maybe, to Tract It" (2003)** — Argues that the exclusion problem rests on conflicting intuitions about causation, and proposes conditions under which overdetermination by mental and physical properties is acceptable.

**Ned Block, "Do Causal Powers Drain Away?" (2003)** — Block defends the causal efficacy of mental properties against exclusion arguments, arguing that the structure of causal explanation in science supports the autonomy of higher-level properties.
