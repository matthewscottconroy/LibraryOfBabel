# Key Thinkers: The Problem of Mental Causation

---

**Jaegwon Kim** (1934–2019)

Kim is the philosopher most responsible for placing the causal exclusion problem at the center of philosophy of mind. He argues that if mental properties are distinct from physical properties but supervene on them, then when a mental property M causes a physical effect E, the physical property P on which M supervenes is also a sufficient cause of E — but causal overdetermination is implausible, so either M reduces to P (reducing the mental to the physical) or M is epiphenomenal (causally inert). Kim used this to challenge non-reductive physicalism.

*Key works:* *Supervenience and Mind* (1993); *Mind in a Physical World* (1998); *Physicalism, Or Something Near Enough* (2005)

---

**Donald Davidson** (1917–2003)

Davidson's anomalous monism provides one of the most influential responses to the problem of mental causation. Mental events are identical to physical events, making them causally efficacious. But there are no strict psychophysical laws connecting mental and physical descriptions — the mental is "anomalous." This means mental causation is real (mental events cause physical events) but the causal description applies by virtue of the physical identity, not the mental description. Critics argue this "solution" makes mental properties themselves epiphenomenal.

*Key works:* "Mental Events" (1970); "The Material Mind" (1973); *Essays on Actions and Events* (1980)

---

**Stephen Yablo** (b. 1957)

Yablo's response to the exclusion problem appeals to the determinable-determinate relationship between mental and physical properties. A mental property (scarlet) is to a physical property (red) as a determinable is to a determinate — the determinate is not a different cause from the determinable but a more specific instance of it. Yablo argues that mental and physical causes can both be genuine without generating problematic overdetermination, since mental properties are not at the same level as the physical properties on which they supervene.

*Key works:* "Mental Causation" (1992); "Cause and Essence" (1992); "Blank Slate" (2001)

---

**David Papineau** (b. 1947)

Papineau argues that the causal completeness of physics — the principle that every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause — is the key premise in arguments for epiphenomenalism about mental causation. He examines whether this principle can be combined with the reality of mental causation, arguing for a form of type identity theory as the most stable resolution: mental properties just are physical properties, and mental causation is physical causation.

*Key works:* *Philosophical Naturalism* (1993); *Thinking about Consciousness* (2002); *Knowing the Score* (2017)
