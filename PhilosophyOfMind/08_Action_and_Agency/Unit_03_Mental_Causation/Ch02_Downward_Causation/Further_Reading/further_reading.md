# Further Reading: Downward Causation

## Primary Sources

**Donald Campbell, "'Downward Causation' in Hierarchically Organised Biological Systems" (1974)** — The paper that introduced the term "downward causation," arguing that higher-level systemic organization in biology constrains and partly determines what happens at lower levels (e.g., how natural selection shapes genetic change). Foundational for subsequent debates.

**Jaegwon Kim, "The Nonreductivist's Troubles with Mental Causation" (1993)** — Kim argues that downward causation requires mental/higher-level properties to have genuine causal powers distinct from those of the physical micro-level, which conflicts with the completeness of physics. A central challenge to downward causation.

**Roger Sperry, "Mind, Brain, and Humanist Values" (1965)** — Sperry argues that conscious mental properties exercise downward causal influence on neural processes, using split-brain research as evidence for the genuine efficacy of psychological states.

## Secondary Literature

**George Ellis, "Top-Down Causation and Emergence: Some Comments on Mechanisms" (2012)** — Ellis provides a systems-theoretic account of downward causation in biology and physics, arguing that top-down constraints from higher-level organizations are real causal factors. Engages the Kim-style objections.

**Alicia Juarrero, *Dynamics in Action: Intentional Behavior as a Complex System* (1999)** — Argues that intentional agency involves genuine downward causation of a dynamical-systems kind: context-sensitive constraints at the systems level shape lower-level processes in ways irreducible to bottom-up summation.

**Murphy and Brown, *Did My Neurons Make Me Do It?* (2007)** — An interdisciplinary treatment of top-down causation, emergence, and moral responsibility, arguing that higher-level causal factors are real and not merely re-descriptions of lower-level events.

**Christian List and Peter Menzies, "Non-Reductive Physicalism and the Limits of the Exclusion Principle" (2009)** — Argues that the exclusion principle cannot be applied to relate higher-level and lower-level causal explanations without question-begging, defending the autonomy of higher-level causal explanations.
