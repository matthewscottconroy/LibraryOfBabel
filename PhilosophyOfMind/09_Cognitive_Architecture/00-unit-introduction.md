# Unit Introduction: Cognitive Architecture

## Central Questions

Cognitive architecture asks: what is the fundamental organizational structure of the mind? If the mind is, in some sense, a computational or information-processing system, what kind of system is it? Does cognition consist in the serial manipulation of discrete symbolic representations (classical computationalism)? Or in the parallel activation of patterns across distributed networks of simple units (connectionism)? Is the mind a central system that processes all information uniformly, or is it organized into specialized *modules* that process different domains independently and automatically? And how much of cognition is contained within the skull — or is cognitive processing partly constituted by the body, the environment, and social practices?

These questions are not purely empirical, though empirical findings are crucial for answering them. They are partly conceptual: what would it mean for cognition to be *classical* as opposed to *connectionist*? What is the difference between a system that merely uses the environment as an external memory and a system in which environmental elements are *constitutive* of cognitive processes? Getting clear on these conceptual distinctions is necessary before we can say what the evidence shows.

The 4E framework — *Embodied*, *Embedded*, *Extended*, and *Enacted* cognition — represents a broad challenge to the traditional picture of cognition as brain-based, symbolic, and world-independent. On the 4E view, cognition is not a process that happens inside the skull and then outputs behavior; it is a process in which body, environment, and agent are continuously coupled, and in which the traditional boundary between the cognitive system and its environment is not theoretically fundamental.

## Major Positions and Debates

### Classical Computationalism and the Turing Legacy

The classical computational theory of mind (CTM), inspired by the Turing machine and the digital computer, holds that cognition consists in the computation of functions over symbolic representations. Mental states are computational states; mental processes are symbol manipulations governed by rules that operate on the formal (syntactic) properties of representations. The *functionalist thesis* (from Unit 06) and the LOT hypothesis (from Unit 05) are expressions of classical CTM.

Fodor's *The Modularity of Mind* (1983) proposed a specific architectural claim within the classical framework: cognition is organized into *input systems* (perceptual modules, each dealing with a specific domain and processing information in a fast, automatic, domain-specific, informationally encapsulated way) and a *central processor* (responsible for belief fixation, reasoning, and decision-making). The input systems are the clearest examples of classical computational systems; the central processor is more mysterious and may not be classical.

Fodor's modularity thesis is weaker than it appears: the modules are peripheral, not central; the central processor remains poorly understood. Jerry Fodor's *The Mind Doesn't Work That Way* (2000) is a sustained complaint that cognitive science, despite its successes with input processing, has made no progress on the central system. This is the most honest assessment available from within the classical tradition.

### Connectionism: A Continuing Challenge

As discussed in Unit 05, connectionist systems represent information as distributed activation patterns rather than discrete symbolic structures. The success of deep learning since 2010 — and especially the development of large language models and multimodal systems — has given connectionism a dramatic empirical vindication. Systems with billions of parameters, trained on enormous corpora of human-generated text and images, exhibit cognitive performances that classical systems could not approach.

The philosophical question is how to interpret this success. Does it show that cognition is essentially sub-symbolic, or that classical symbolic structures emerge from sub-symbolic processing at the right scale? The *emergent structure* view holds that something like symbolic processing emerges at higher levels of description even if the underlying implementation is connectionist. The *sub-symbolic* view holds that the classical framework is simply the wrong description of cognition.

### Massive Modularity

Evolutionary psychology (Cosmides, Tooby) proposed *massive modularity*: not just peripheral input systems but the entire cognitive system is organized into specialized, domain-specific modules, each shaped by evolutionary pressures to solve specific adaptive problems. On this view, there is no domain-general central processor; there are only more and less specific modules.

Massive modularity challenges the classical picture by denying a domain-general reasoning system. It also challenges the 4E picture by emphasizing the *internal* and *innate* organization of cognition. Fodor attacked massive modularity on the grounds that genuinely central cognitive processes (planning, reasoning, belief revision) are not modular in any interesting sense.

### The Extended Mind

Andy Clark and David Chalmers' "The Extended Mind" (1998) proposed that cognitive processes can extend beyond the skull. Otto, a memory-impaired subject, keeps a notebook in which he records information. When Otto wants to know the address of MOMA, he looks it up in his notebook. Clark and Chalmers argue that for Otto, the notebook is playing exactly the role that biological memory plays for Inga (who simply recalls the address). If the notebook is playing the role of memory, then Otto's cognitive processes *extend* into the notebook.

The extended mind thesis is controversial on several dimensions. Critics (Rupert, Adams, Aizawa) argue that the parity principle — if it would count as cognition if it were in the head, it counts as cognition outside the head — cannot bear the weight put on it: what is in the head is implemented in a different medium with different properties, and this matters. Proponents argue that the medium should not be what matters; what matters is the functional role.

### Embodied Cognition

Embodied cognition holds that the body is not merely the output system for cognitive processes but a constitutive part of cognition. Evidence for embodied cognition includes: the influence of posture and gesture on reasoning (spreading out arms improves performance on tasks requiring mental expansion); the role of motor simulation in language comprehension (reading about kicking activates motor cortex for leg actions); the dependence of mathematical cognition on spatial representations that are grounded in bodily experience.

Merleau-Ponty's phenomenological account of the body-subject (Unit 15) is the philosophical foundation for embodied cognition: the body is not an object that the mind controls but the medium through which the world is disclosed. Contemporary embodied cognition research attempts to operationalize this phenomenological insight into testable hypotheses.

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**Jerry Fodor** (*The Modularity of Mind*, 1983; *The Language of Thought*, 1975) is the central figure in classical computationalism. His modularity thesis, his critique of connectionism, and his late work on the difficulty of explaining central cognition are essential.

**Andy Clark** (*Being There*, 1997; *Supersizing the Mind*, 2008; *Surfing Uncertainty*, 2016) is the most prolific advocate of extended, embodied, and predictive approaches to cognition. Clark's ability to integrate philosophical argument with empirical findings makes his work essential across multiple debates.

**David Chalmers** co-authored the extended mind paper with Clark; his role has been to press the metaphysical and philosophical implications of the extended mind thesis, and to raise the question of whether mental states genuinely extend.

**Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch** (*The Embodied Mind*, 1991) introduced the enactivist framework to English-language philosophy, combining phenomenology (Merleau-Ponty), cognitive science, and Buddhist philosophy of mind.

**Edwin Hutchins** (*Cognition in the Wild*, 1995) developed the distributed cognition framework, showing through detailed ethnographic study of ship navigation that cognitive processes are distributed across persons, instruments, and social practices.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Fodor, *The Modularity of Mind* (1983)**
The most influential architectural hypothesis in cognitive science of the 1980s. Fodor's distinction between modular input systems and a non-modular central processor remains important, if only as a foil. The book is accessible and crisply argued.

**2. Clark and Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (1998)**
The founding document of the extended mind thesis. An argument that fits on a few pages but has generated a decade of philosophical and empirical work. Essential reading.

**3. Varela, Thompson, and Rosch, *The Embodied Mind* (1991)**
The book that brought enactivism to a philosophical audience. Despite its age, the core arguments remain important, and the integration of cognitive science with phenomenology and Buddhist philosophy is unusual and illuminating.

**4. Rumelhart and McClelland (eds.), *Parallel Distributed Processing*, Vol. 1 (1986)**
See Unit 05; also essential here as the foundational document of connectionism's challenge to classical cognitive architecture.

**5. Clark, *Surfing Uncertainty: Prediction, Action, and the Embodied Mind* (2016)**
The most philosophically sophisticated treatment of the predictive processing framework and its implications for cognitive architecture, embodiment, and consciousness. Clark integrates Friston's mathematical framework with the 4E tradition.

## Open Questions

**1. Has the success of deep learning vindicated connectionism?**
Modern neural networks achieve cognitive-level performances across a remarkable range of domains. But whether this means that cognition is fundamentally connectionist — or whether neural networks happen to approximate something more like classical symbolic processing at scale — is genuinely contested. The question has both empirical dimensions (what are these systems actually doing?) and philosophical ones (what would it mean for cognition to be classical vs. connectionist?).

**2. Is the extended mind thesis consistent with functionalism?**
The extended mind thesis holds that cognitive processes can extend beyond the skull. But functionalism holds that mental states are individuated by their functional roles, which could in principle be realized anywhere. Does this mean that functionalism endorses the extended mind, or does it mean that extending functional organization beyond the skull is a bizarre special case? The relationship between functionalism and the extended mind thesis is less obvious than it appears.

**3. What is the right unit of cognitive analysis?**
Classical computationalism takes the individual brain as the fundamental unit of cognitive analysis. Distributed cognition takes larger systems (persons + artifacts + social practices) as the unit. Which is the right level of analysis, and does this depend on the phenomenon being explained? The question of the right grain of analysis for cognitive science is not settled.

**4. Can modularity be empirically tested?**
Fodor's criteria for modularity (domain-specificity, informational encapsulation, fast processing, mandatory processing, etc.) are in principle testable. But the specific thresholds for each criterion are unclear, and there are few cases where the criteria give a clear verdict. Whether the modularity hypothesis is genuinely empirically constrained or is too flexible to be tested is a methodological concern.

**5. How does predictive processing relate to the classical/connectionist debate?**
Predictive processing (PP) is neutral between classical and connectionist implementations — the generative models in PP could be implemented by either kind of system. But PP does seem to favor a certain kind of architecture (hierarchical, bidirectional, with top-down and bottom-up flows). Whether PP resolves the classical/connectionist debate, transcends it, or is silent about it is an open question.
