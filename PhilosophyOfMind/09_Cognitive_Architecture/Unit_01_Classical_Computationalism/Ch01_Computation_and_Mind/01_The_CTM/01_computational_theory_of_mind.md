# The Computational Theory of Mind

The computational theory of mind (CTM) holds that mental processes are computational processes — that thinking, reasoning, perceiving, remembering, and deciding are forms of information processing that can be described and explained in computational terms. This hypothesis has been extraordinarily productive in cognitive science, providing the conceptual foundation for cognitive psychology, artificial intelligence, and computational neuroscience. It has also been philosophically controversial, raising deep questions about whether computation can capture what is distinctive about mentality.

**The Core Hypothesis**

The CTM can be stated in different ways at different levels of specificity. At its most general, it holds that the mind is a computational system: it takes in information, processes it according to rules or algorithms, and produces outputs (behavioral responses, other mental states). At a more specific level, the classical version of the CTM — associated with Turing, Newell and Simon, and Fodor — holds that mental processes are formal operations on mental representations. The mind manipulates symbols according to rules that are sensitive to the symbolic form of representations, in a way that systematically tracks (and is explained by) their content.

Alan Turing's work established the theoretical framework. A Turing machine is a formal, mechanical device that processes symbols according to explicit rules, and Turing showed that any computation can in principle be performed by such a machine. The functionalist insight — that the mind might be to the brain as a program is to a hardware — connects Turing's formal framework to the mind-body problem. Mental properties are the program-level properties of the brain, not the physical hardware properties.

**Why Computation Is Attractive**

The appeal of the CTM is partly its promise to explain how mental states can have both causal properties and semantic (representational) properties. Mental states seem to be about things (they are intentional) and they also cause behavior (they are causally efficacious). Computation offers a model of how these two features can be unified: computational processes are sensitive to the formal (syntactic) properties of representations, and if formal properties systematically correspond to semantic properties (if the syntax mirrors the semantics), then formal processes will systematically track and preserve semantic relationships.

Fodor called this the "syntactic engine that mirrors a semantic engine" — the brain's formal operations can be causally explained at the formal level while being semantically interpretable at the content level. This is one version of the broader functionalist claim that mental properties are multiply realizable functional properties of systems.

**Turing and the Imitation Game**

Turing's famous "imitation game" (the Turing test) proposed that intelligence could be operationally defined as the capacity to produce linguistic behavior indistinguishable from that of an intelligent human. While the Turing test has been widely misunderstood as a claim about what intelligence is, it reflects a broader computational presumption: if a system produces the outputs characteristic of intelligence, the mechanisms underlying it are (in some sense) cognitively relevant.

The Turing test also marks the beginning of the philosophical debate about whether computation is sufficient for genuine mentality — a debate that has continued through Searle's Chinese Room, debates about strong AI, and more recent discussions of large language models and artificial general intelligence.

**The Classical View: GOFAI**

What is sometimes called "Good Old-Fashioned AI" (GOFAI) represents the classical expression of the CTM in artificial intelligence. GOFAI systems represent knowledge explicitly in symbolic form — rules, logical predicates, frames, semantic networks — and process that knowledge using formal reasoning algorithms. Chess programs, theorem provers, expert systems, and natural language parsing systems of the 1960s-1980s are paradigmatic examples.

The GOFAI approach embodies the classical cognitive science assumption: that cognition is essentially symbolic manipulation, and that the key to intelligence is the right system of representations and rules for operating on them. Newell and Simon's "physical symbol system hypothesis" stated this explicitly: any system capable of genuine intelligent behavior must be a physical symbol system — a system that manipulates symbolic structures in ways that preserve their semantic relationships.

**Challenges to the CTM**

The computational theory faces challenges from multiple directions, several of which are examined in subsequent sections.

The symbol grounding problem (Harnad) asks how formal symbols get their meaning. In GOFAI systems, symbols have meaning only derivatively — meaning attributed by designers and users. Original intentionality seems to require something more than formal symbol manipulation.

The frame problem (McCarthy, Hayes) asks how a computational system can efficiently represent and update knowledge about which aspects of the world remain unchanged when an action is performed — a problem that proves remarkably difficult for formal systems.

Connectionists argue that the brain does not work by explicit symbol manipulation at all; its architecture is better captured by neural network models. Embodied and situated cognitivists argue that the CTM abstracted away from the body and environment in a way that distorts our understanding of cognition.

Despite these challenges, the CTM remains foundational to cognitive science. The question is not whether it captures something real — computation is clearly central to cognition — but whether it captures everything that matters about the mind, and whether the classical, symbolic version is the right form of the computational hypothesis.
