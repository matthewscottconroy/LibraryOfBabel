# Further Reading: Computation and Mind

## Primary Sources

**Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950)** — Turing proposes the imitation game (Turing Test) as an operational criterion for machine intelligence and discusses nine objections to machine thought. The founding text for computational approaches to mind.

**Jerry Fodor, *The Language of Thought* (1975)** — Fodor argues that thought requires a representational system with combinatorial syntax and semantics — "Mentalese." Computation over mental representations provides the model for all cognition.

**Allen Newell and Herbert Simon, "Computer Science as Empirical Inquiry: Symbols and Search" (1976)** — The "Physical Symbol System Hypothesis": a physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action. Establishes symbolic AI as the framework for cognitive science.

## Secondary Literature

**Jerry Fodor, *The Modularity of Mind* (1983)** — Fodor argues that peripheral cognitive systems (perception, language) are modular — informationally encapsulated, domain-specific, fast, and automatic — while central cognition is not. This architecture grounds the computational theory of mind in specific claims about cognitive organization.

**Zenon Pylyshyn, *Computation and Cognition* (1984)** — Defends classical symbolic computation as the right level of description for cognitive science, distinguishing the computational level from the algorithmic and implementational levels.

**David Marr, *Vision* (1982)** — Marr's multi-level framework for the computational study of vision exemplifies the classical approach: analyzing cognitive tasks at the computational, algorithmic, and implementational levels.

**Margaret Boden, *The Creative Mind: Myths and Mechanisms* (1990)** — Boden examines the scope of computational models of creativity, asking how much of human intelligence can be captured within classical symbolic frameworks.

**Norbert Wiener, *Cybernetics: Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine* (1948)** — Wiener's foundational text connecting information, control, and the theory of the mind. Essential background for understanding the computational paradigm's origins.
