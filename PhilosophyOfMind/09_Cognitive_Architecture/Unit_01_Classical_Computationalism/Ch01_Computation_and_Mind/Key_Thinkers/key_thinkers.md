# Key Thinkers: Computation and Mind

---

**Alan Turing** (1912–1954)

Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" proposed the imitation game as a test for machine thought and argued that there is no principled obstacle to machines thinking. His earlier work on computability (1936) defined what it means for a function to be effectively computable, establishing the theoretical foundations for the computational theory of mind. Turing anticipated many objections to machine intelligence and provided the framework within which AI and cognitive science would develop.

*Key works:* "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" (1936); "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950)

---

**Allen Newell** (1927–1992) **and Herbert Simon** (1916–2001)

Newell and Simon are the pioneers of symbolic AI and the computational theory of mind. Their Physical Symbol System Hypothesis holds that a system manipulating symbol structures is both necessary and sufficient for general intelligent action. Their "General Problem Solver" was an early implementation of this vision. Simon's broader work extended the computational paradigm to decision-making, economics, and the study of bounded rationality.

*Key works:* "Computer Science as Empirical Inquiry" (1976); Newell: *Unified Theories of Cognition* (1990); Simon: *The Sciences of the Artificial* (1969); *Models of My Life* (1991, memoir)

---

**Jerry Fodor** (1935–2017)

Fodor's language of thought hypothesis is the central theoretical contribution to classical computationalism about the mind. He argued that all genuine cognitive processes — reasoning, inference, language understanding — are computational processes over discrete, compositional mental representations (Mentalese). His modularity thesis further specified how the computational architecture is organized, distinguishing peripheral modules from central cognition. Fodor was simultaneously the most influential advocate and one of the most penetrating internal critics of the classical program.

*Key works:* *The Language of Thought* (1975); *The Modularity of Mind* (1983); *The Mind Doesn't Work That Way* (2000)

---

**David Marr** (1945–1980)

Marr's multi-level framework for vision — distinguishing computational, algorithmic, and implementational levels of analysis — became the standard methodology for computational cognitive science. His actual theory of early vision (computation of intensity changes, edges, and surface layouts) exemplified classical representational analysis and established what a successful computational theory of a cognitive capacity looks like.

*Key works:* *Vision* (1982); "Representing Visual Information" (1978, with Nishihara); "Understanding Computation" (1977)
