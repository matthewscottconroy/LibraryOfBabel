# Further Reading: Problems for Computationalism

## Primary Sources

**John McCarthy and Patrick Hayes, "Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence" (1969)** — Introduces the "frame problem": how can a classical AI system efficiently track which facts change and which remain constant after an action? A fundamental problem for classical symbolic AI.

**Hubert Dreyfus, *What Computers Can't Do* (1972; rev. *What Computers Still Can't Do*, 1992)** — Dreyfus argues that classical AI fails because human cognition is not rule-governed symbolic processing but embodied, context-sensitive coping. A sustained phenomenological critique.

**Stevan Harnad, "The Symbol Grounding Problem" (1990)** — Harnad argues that symbols in a computational system are meaningless because they are only defined by their relations to other symbols; genuine meaning requires grounding in non-symbolic sensorimotor processes.

## Secondary Literature

**Jerry Fodor, *The Mind Doesn't Work That Way* (2000)** — Fodor argues that classical computationalism fails at the level of central cognition (abductive reasoning, belief fixation) because of the "frame problem" and holism: central cognitive processes are not modular and are informationally unencapsulated, making them unamenable to computational modeling.

**Brian Smith, "The Owl and the Electric Encyclopedia" (1991)** — A critical examination of the frame problem and its implications for AI and cognitive science, arguing that it reveals deep assumptions about the nature of representation and understanding.

**Lucy Suchman, *Plans and Situated Actions* (1987)** — Suchman argues from ethnomethodology that human action is not executed from pre-formed plans but is situationally improvised. This challenges the classical AI view that action is execution of symbolic plans.

**William Ramsey, *Representation Reconsidered* (2007)** — Argues that the notion of "mental representation" is used more loosely than classical computationalism requires, and that this threatens the coherence of the computational theory of mind.
