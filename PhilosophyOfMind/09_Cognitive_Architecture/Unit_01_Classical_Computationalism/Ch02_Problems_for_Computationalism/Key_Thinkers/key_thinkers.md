# Key Thinkers: Problems for Computationalism

---

**Hubert Dreyfus** (1929–2017)

Dreyfus mounted the most sustained philosophical critique of classical AI and computationalism. Drawing on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, he argued that human intelligence rests on embodied, non-representational skills — forms of coping with the world — that cannot be captured by discrete symbolic rules. His critique anticipated the practical failures of "good old-fashioned AI" (GOFAI) in areas like natural language understanding and scene recognition. His later work engaged more positively with connectionism while maintaining that embodiment is essential.

*Key works:* *What Computers Can't Do* (1972/1992); *Being-in-the-World* (1991); "Why Heideggerian AI Failed" (2007)

---

**Stevan Harnad** (b. 1945)

Harnad's symbol grounding problem poses a fundamental challenge to classical computationalism. He argues that a system that only manipulates formal symbols — defining each symbol in terms of other symbols — produces a "Chinese Room" in Searle's sense: symbols have no meaning because they are never grounded in anything outside the symbol system. For symbols to have genuine semantic content, at least some must be grounded in perceptual and sensorimotor interaction with the environment — which classical symbolic systems lack.

*Key works:* "The Symbol Grounding Problem" (1990); "Other Bodies, Other Minds" (1991); "Grounding Symbols in the Analog World" (1993)

---

**Jerry Fodor** (1935–2017)

Fodor, though the greatest champion of classical computationalism, became its most incisive internal critic. In *The Mind Doesn't Work That Way* (2000), he argued that central cognition — inference, belief fixation, abductive reasoning — exhibits Quinean holism: the relevance of any piece of information to any question is potentially unlimited, making central processes impossible to model computationally. The frame problem is, for Fodor, the fundamental unsolved challenge for classical AI.

*Key works:* *The Mind Doesn't Work That Way* (2000); "Is Science Biologically Possible?" (2000); *In Critical Condition* (1998)

---

**Hilary Putnam** (1926–2016)

Putnam, who had been one of the originators of functionalism, became a critic of computationalism in *Representation and Reality* (1988). He argued that computation is always relative to an interpretation — there is no fact of the matter about what computation a physical system is performing without a human observer's specification. If this is correct, the claim that "the brain computes" cannot be a substantive physical claim but only an observer-relative description.

*Key works:* *Representation and Reality* (1988); *The Threefold Cord: Mind, Body, and World* (1999)
