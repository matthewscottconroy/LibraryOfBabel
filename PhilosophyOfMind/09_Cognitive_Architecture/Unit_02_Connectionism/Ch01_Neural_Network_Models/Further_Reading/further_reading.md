# Further Reading: Neural Network Models and Connectionism

## Primary Sources

**Rumelhart, McClelland, and PDP Research Group, *Parallel Distributed Processing*, Vols. 1–2 (1986)** — The defining text of the connectionist revolution in cognitive science. Vol. 1 lays out the architecture and learning rules (especially backpropagation) of parallel distributed processing (PDP) networks; Vol. 2 applies them to specific cognitive domains. Essential reading.

**Paul Smolensky, "On the Proper Treatment of Connectionism" (1988)** — Argues that connectionist models implement a distinct "subsymbolic" level of cognitive description between the neural and the classical symbolic, and that this level is explanatorily privileged for cognition.

**David Rumelhart and James McClelland, "On Learning the Past Tenses of English Verbs" (1986)** — A celebrated example of connectionist modeling applied to language acquisition, arguing that a simple associative network can learn the past tense without explicit rules, challenging nativist linguistics.

## Secondary Literature

**Jerry Fodor and Zenon Pylyshyn, "Connectionism and Cognitive Architecture: A Critical Analysis" (1988)** — The classic critique: connectionist networks lack the systematicity and productivity of thought because they do not represent constituent structure. If one can think "John loves Mary," one can think "Mary loves John" — but connectionist networks do not respect this constraint.

**Andy Clark, *Microcognition: Philosophy, Cognitive Science, and Parallel Distributed Processing* (1989)** — A philosophical examination of connectionist models, exploring what kinds of cognitive processes they best model and how to reconcile connectionism with classical cognitive science.

**Paul Smolensky, "Tensor Product Variable Binding and the Representation of Symbolic Structures in Connectionist Systems" (1990)** — Attempts to show how connectionist networks can, in principle, represent structured symbolic information through distributed patterns, responding to Fodor-Pylyshyn systematicity objections.

**Gary Marcus, *The Algebraic Mind: Integrating Connectionism and Cognitive Science* (2001)** — Argues that connectionist models cannot account for the algebraic, rule-governed aspects of cognition and proposes a hybrid architecture.
