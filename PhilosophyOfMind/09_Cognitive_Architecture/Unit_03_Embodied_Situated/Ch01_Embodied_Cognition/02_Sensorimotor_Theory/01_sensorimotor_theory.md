# Sensorimotor Theory of Perception

The sensorimotor theory of perception, developed primarily by Alva Noë and Kevin O'Regan, proposes that perceptual experience is not a matter of having a brain state that represents how the world is, but a matter of being practically engaged with the world in a way governed by sensorimotor knowledge. To perceive is not to receive a picture of the world in the mind; it is to possess and exercise a kind of practical understanding — to know how one's sensory experience would change in response to movement.

**The Core Proposal**

The traditional view of perception treats it as a process that takes sensory input and produces a rich internal representation of the world — a "picture in the head" that the cognitive system then uses to guide behavior. The quality of perceptual experience, on this view, is a function of the quality of the internal representation.

Noë and O'Regan's "sensorimotor contingency" approach proposes instead that perceptual experience is constituted by the perceiver's knowledge of and engagement with sensorimotor contingencies — the lawful relationships between movements and changes in sensory stimulation. To see a circular plate from an angle as circular is to know, implicitly, that if you moved your head such-and-such a way, your visual experience would change in such-and-such a way — the elliptical projection would transform in a way appropriate to viewing a circle from an angle.

This knowledge is practical, not propositional. It is manifested in how the perceiver moves and responds, not in any explicit representation of the sensorimotor regularities. Perception, on this account, is more like a skill than like reception.

**Evidence from Change Blindness**

Noë and O'Regan used the phenomenon of change blindness to argue against the picture-in-the-head model. In classic change blindness experiments, observers fail to notice large changes in a visual scene when those changes occur during a saccade, a blink, or a brief flicker. If perceivers had a rich internal representation of the scene, they should notice that it has changed. The failure to notice changes suggests that we do not maintain a rich internal picture of the visual world.

On the sensorimotor account, this is no surprise: perception is not a matter of maintaining an internal picture but a matter of skillful engagement with the world. We do not need a complete internal model of the scene because the world itself serves as its own representation — we can access any part of it by attending and moving. Change blindness reveals that perception is more sparse and action-oriented than the picture-in-the-head model assumes.

**The Enactive Approach**

Sensorimotor theory is closely related to the broader enactive approach to cognition. The core enactive claim is that cognition is a form of action — not a process that takes input from the world, computes on it, and then acts, but a form of engagement in which perception and action are inseparable. Perceiving is a way of exploring the world; acting is a way of affecting what is available for perception.

This resonates with J.J. Gibson's ecological approach to perception, which emphasized that animals perceive in terms of affordances — possibilities for action that objects and environments offer — rather than in terms of neutral sensory properties. The meaning of a perceptual scene is primarily practical, not representational.

**Challenges to Sensorimotor Theory**

Critics have raised several objections.

*The vividness of experience*: If perception is just skillful interaction with the world, why does experience feel so rich and present? The phenomenology of perception seems to involve more than merely having the right know-how; it seems to involve something like having the world present to one, not just being disposed to respond to it in appropriate ways.

*Hallucination and phenomenal character*: In hallucination, there is no actual world to be engaged with, yet hallucinatory experience has phenomenal character. If experience were constituted entirely by actual sensorimotor engagement, it is unclear how hallucination would be possible. Noë's response is that hallucinations are disrupted forms of the normal experience-world relationship, but critics find this unsatisfying.

*The neural story*: Normal brain activity produces conscious experience even when all sensorimotor engagement is blocked — as in paralyzed patients or subjects under anesthesia who show signs of consciousness. This suggests that sensorimotor engagement cannot be constitutively necessary for consciousness.

**Sensorimotor Theory and the Hard Problem**

Like many anti-representationalist approaches, sensorimotor theory is better at explaining the functional and behavioral aspects of perception than at explaining phenomenal consciousness. Even if perceptual experience is constituted by sensorimotor knowledge in the functional sense, the question of why there is something it is like to have that experience remains unaddressed. The hard problem is not dissolved by replacing internal representations with sensorimotor engagement; it re-emerges as the question of why sensorimotor engagement is accompanied by phenomenal character.
