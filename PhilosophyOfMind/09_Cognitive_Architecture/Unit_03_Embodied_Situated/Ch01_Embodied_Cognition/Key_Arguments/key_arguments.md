# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Embodied Cognition

## Key Arguments

**Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's Argument for Enactive Cognition**
In The Embodied Mind, Varela, Thompson, and Rosch argued that cognition is not the recovery of a pre-given world but the enaction of a world: cognitive systems bring forth a domain of significance through their sensorimotor coupling with the environment. Cognition is autonomous in the sense that the organism's own organization determines what counts as relevant input; it is not merely responding to pre-given features. This enactive approach draws on phenomenology (Merleau-Ponty), biology (Maturana's autopoiesis), and cognitive science to challenge classical representationalism.

**The Sensorimotor Contingency Theory (O'Regan and Noë)**
Kevin O'Regan and Alva Noë argue that perceiving is not having a neural representation of the world but knowing the sensorimotor contingencies—the systematic ways in which sensory stimulation changes with movement. To see is to master the sensorimotor laws of visual exploration: knowing that moving your head produces a specific visual flow, that moving toward an object makes it loom. This mastery need not be represented explicitly; it is a practical skill. The theory challenges both sense-data theory and neural representationalism by locating visual experience in the skilled body-world relationship.

**The Body-Schema Argument for Constitutive Role**
Merleau-Ponty and Gallagher argue that the body is not merely enabling for cognition (a necessary causal condition) but constitutive: the specific structure and capacities of the body partly determine the content and form of cognitive processes. The concepts we have of space, movement, grasping, and facing are structured by the body that has these capacities. Without a body of this kind, these concepts would not have the content they have. This argument challenges any view that sees cognition as substrate-independent computation that merely happens to be implemented in a biological body.

**The Argument Against Amodal Symbols**
Barsalou and others argue against "amodal symbol systems"—symbol systems in which concepts are abstract, arbitrary, and disconnected from perceptual and motor systems. Instead, they argue for "perceptual symbol systems": mental representations that are modal (grounded in sensory and motor systems), multimodal, and simulative (activating the perceptual and motor systems used in actual perception and action). This approach grounds cognitive science in the embodied structure of the organism and predicts that concept use should activate perceptual and motor cortex—a prediction with empirical support.

**The Ecological Validity Argument**
Classical cognitive science studied cognition in artificial laboratory conditions that abstracted away from the body and environment. Gibson, Hutchins, and others argued that studying cognition in its natural, ecologically embedded setting reveals features that laboratory studies miss: the role of affordances, the distribution of cognitive labor across body and environment, and the context-sensitivity of intelligent behavior. Ecological validity is not just a methodological point but an argument about the subject matter: cognition in the world is not the same kind of thing as cognition isolated in a brain.

## Core Concepts

**Embodied Cognition**
Embodied cognition holds that cognitive processes are not confined to the brain but are constitutively shaped by the body's structure, capacities, and sensorimotor engagement with the world. Body shape determines which affordances are available; motor capacities determine which action concepts have content; perceptual apparatus determines which features of the world are salient. Embodied cognition challenges the computationalist view that cognition is substrate-independent symbol manipulation and the neuroscientific view that cognition is nothing but brain processes.

**Sensorimotor Contingencies**
Sensorimotor contingencies are the systematic, predictable ways in which sensory stimulation changes as a result of movement: moving the eyes produces a specific retinal flow; closing the eyelids interrupts vision; moving toward an object makes it loom. O'Regan and Noë argue that mastery of sensorimotor contingencies constitutes perceptual experience: to see is not to have a neural picture of the world but to know how visual stimulation changes with action. This is a thoroughly anti-representationalist account of perception, grounding experience in bodily skill.

**Autopoiesis**
Autopoiesis (Maturana and Varela) is the property of living systems of self-production: an autopoietic system continuously produces and maintains its own components through a network of processes. The boundary of the system is itself produced and maintained by the processes it bounds. Autopoiesis is used to ground the concept of sense-making: an autopoietic system has a perspective on its environment in terms of what promotes or threatens its continued self-production. This biological notion of self-maintained organization is extended by Varela, Thompson, and Rosch to cognition as a whole.

**Sense-Making**
Sense-making (Varela, Thompson; developed further by Di Paolo, Thompson, Noë) is the activity by which an organism constitutes its environment as meaningful—as containing threats, affordances, food, mates—in terms of its own organization. Sense-making is the primitive form of intentionality: the organism's world is constituted by and for the organism, not given independently. Sense-making is grounded in autopoiesis and is extended in human cognition by language and culture, which provide new dimensions of meaning. Sense-making contrasts with representation: the world is not a pre-given collection of facts but an enacted domain of relevance.

**Amodal vs. Modal Representations**
Classical cognitive science posited amodal symbolic representations: arbitrary symbols whose content is independent of the sensory-motor systems used to learn or apply the concept. Embodied cognitive science (Barsalou, Glenberg, Gallese) argues for modal representations: representations that are grounded in perceptual and motor systems, re-activating them when deployed in thought and language comprehension. Evidence for modal representations includes activation of motor cortex during tool use concepts, activation of visual cortex during visual imagery, and embodied simulation in language comprehension.

## Thought Experiments

**The Ship's Navigator (Hutchins)**
Hutchins studied the navigation of a ship: the cognitive process of determining the ship's position is not carried out by any individual navigator but is distributed across instruments, charts, multiple crew members, and their practices of coordination. The "cognitive system" that accomplishes navigation includes the human crew and the tools they use. This distributed cognition thought experiment challenges the view that cognition is confined to individual brains and motivates the "extended mind" and embedded cognition theses.

**O'Regan's Change Blindness Demonstration**
O'Regan uses change blindness (the failure to notice large changes in a visual scene when they occur during a saccade or other visual disruption) to argue against the "picture in the head" model of visual experience. If visual experience were a rich, detailed internal representation, we would notice even small changes. Since we don't notice large changes, there is no detailed internal representation; visual experience is not a picture but a skill of access—the world serves as its own external memory, accessible via sensorimotor exploration. The argument supports the sensorimotor contingency account.

**The Robot That Plays with Blocks**
Consider a robot designed to pick up and move blocks. A classical design gives the robot an explicit internal model of the environment (a map of block positions) that it updates at each step. Brooks's behavior-based robots (the "subsumption architecture") use no explicit internal model: instead, they have multiple layers of behavior-specific controllers that respond directly to sensor inputs. The behavior-based robot is simpler, more robust, and faster—"the world is its own best model." The thought experiment illustrates how rich embodied interaction can replace explicit internal representation, supporting the embodied cognition critique of classical AI.
