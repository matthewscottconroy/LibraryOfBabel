# Key Thinkers: Embodied Cognition

---

**Maurice Merleau-Ponty** (1908–1961)

Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology established that the body is the subject of cognition, not merely its instrument or vehicle. His analyses of motor skills, phantom limb, and perceptual reorganization show that cognitive capacities are acquired, maintained, and structured through bodily practice. His concept of the "body schema" — the lived body's pre-reflective orientation to the world — anticipates contemporary research on motor cognition, affordances, and embodied action.

*Key works:* *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945); *The Structure of Behavior* (1942); *The Visible and the Invisible* (1968)

---

**Francisco Varela** (1946–2001)

Varela, a neuroscientist and Buddhist practitioner, co-developed enactivism and applied the concept of autopoiesis to cognition. He argued that living systems create their own cognitive domains through their autonomous, self-producing organization, and that mind is not a property of isolated brains but of the dynamic coupling between organism and environment. His integration of neuroscience, phenomenology, and Buddhist philosophy was distinctive and influential.

*Key works:* *The Embodied Mind* (1991, with Thompson and Rosch); *Principles of Biological Autonomy* (1979); *Ethical Know-How* (1999)

---

**Andy Clark** (b. 1957)

Clark is the philosopher most responsible for systematizing and promoting embodied cognition within analytic philosophy of mind. He argues that the brain, body, and world form a "cognitive system" and that the boundary between cognition and environment is more porous than classical cognitive science assumes. His concept of "scaffolded cognition" — using environmental structures to support and extend cognitive performance — anticipates the extended mind thesis.

*Key works:* *Being There* (1997); *Natural-Born Cyborgs* (2003); *Supersizing the Mind* (2008); *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016)

---

**Evan Thompson** (b. 1962)

Thompson developed the enactivist framework articulated in *The Embodied Mind* into a comprehensive philosophy of mind in *Mind in Life* (2007). He argues that all living things are "minded" in a minimal sense, and that the roots of consciousness lie in the self-organizing processes of life. Thompson engages extensively with phenomenology (especially Husserl and Merleau-Ponty) and with Buddhist philosophy, developing what he calls "neurophenomenology."

*Key works:* *The Embodied Mind* (1991, with Varela and Rosch); *Mind in Life* (2007); *Waking, Dreaming, Being* (2015)
