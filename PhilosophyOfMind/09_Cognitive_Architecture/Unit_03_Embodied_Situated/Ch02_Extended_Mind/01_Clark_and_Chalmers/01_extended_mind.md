# The Extended Mind

Andy Clark and David Chalmers's paper "The Extended Mind" (1998) is one of the most cited and debated papers in recent philosophy of mind. It argues that the boundary between mind and world — between cognitive processes and the external environment — is not fixed at the skull or even the skin. In some circumstances, external objects can become genuine components of cognitive processes, extending the mind into the world.

**The Parity Principle**

Clark and Chalmers's core argument rests on what they call the parity principle: if a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process.

The argument is that we should not privilege the location of a cognitive process over its functional role. If the criterion for something's being cognitive is functional — if it is what a process does, not where it happens, that determines whether it is cognitive — then there is no principled reason to draw the cognitive boundary at the skin.

**Otto and Inga**

Clark and Chalmers illustrate with a thought experiment about Otto, who has Alzheimer's disease, and Inga, who does not. Both want to go to MoMA. Inga consults her biological memory, recalls the address, and goes. Otto consults his notebook, finds the address written there, and goes.

Both consult an information-bearing resource before acting; both retrieve the relevant information; both proceed on the basis of that information. The process is functionally equivalent, except that Inga's resource is in her head and Otto's is in his notebook.

Clark and Chalmers argue that Otto's notebook satisfies the conditions for being part of his memory. Specifically, they propose that a mental state M is a belief if it is available to the agent, consistently reliable, and automatically endorsed. Otto's notebook entries satisfy these conditions: they are accessible when needed, reliable, and Otto typically accepts and acts on the information in them without scrutiny. So Otto's notebook is, in the relevant sense, part of Otto's mind.

**Cognitive Extension and Technology**

Clark extends this analysis in *Being There* and *Natural-Born Cyborgs* to argue that human beings are constitutively tool-using creatures — "cognitive hybrids" who naturally offload cognitive processing onto the environment. Writing, calculation, maps, and smartphones are not merely aids to thought; they become, when integrated appropriately, genuine components of the cognitive processes of their users.

This is not merely a metaphorical claim. Clark argues that the integration of external resources into cognitive processes is a genuine form of cognitive extension: when a person habitually uses a smartphone for navigation, their wayfinding cognitive system literally extends into the device. This is not exotic or unusual — it is the normal condition of cognitively sophisticated creatures.

**Conditions for Cognitive Extension**

Clark and Chalmers propose that for an external resource to count as part of the mind, it must meet several conditions:

1. The resource must be reliably available and typically deployed.
2. The agent must be able to access and use the information without difficulty.
3. The agent must endorse the information automatically (without demanding independent verification).
4. The information must be available for use by the agent in reasoning and action.

These conditions are meant to capture what is required for a resource to be sufficiently integrated with the agent's cognitive processes to count as part of them, rather than merely as an external tool that the agent consults.

**Extended Mind and Personal Identity**

The extended mind thesis has implications for personal identity and the concept of the self. If cognitive processes extend into the environment, then interfering with an agent's external cognitive resources is not merely impeding their cognition but tampering with their mind. Clark draws the striking implication that Otto's notebook, in the relevant sense, is part of Otto himself.

This has ethical implications: there may be reasons to protect people's access to their cognitive extensions — their notebooks, devices, and practices — as part of what it means to respect their cognitive autonomy.

**Responses to the Extended Mind**

The extended mind thesis has attracted extensive criticism, examined in the following section. The main objections concern whether functional equivalence is sufficient for cognitive equivalence, whether the conditions Clark and Chalmers propose are too permissive, and whether there is a principled distinction between cognitive extensions and cognitive tools.
