# Further Reading: The Extended Mind

## Primary Sources

**Andy Clark and David Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (1998)** — The landmark paper arguing that mental states can extend beyond the boundaries of brain and body into the environment. "Otto's notebook" case: a man with Alzheimer's uses a notebook as a cognitive prosthetic; his beliefs are partly constituted by the notebook's contents.

**Andy Clark, *Supersizing the Mind: Embodiment, Action, and Cognitive Extension* (2008)** — Clark's book-length defense and development of the extended mind thesis, responding to critics and expanding the framework to include language, tools, and social structures as components of extended cognitive systems.

**Hilary Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975)** — Putnam's twin earth argument can be read as establishing an "extended" view of meaning: the content of mental states depends on the environment, not just on what is "in the head."

## Secondary Literature

**Jerry Fodor, "Where Is My Mind?" (2009)** — Fodor's critical response to the extended mind thesis, arguing that the cognitive coupling condition (the "parity principle") is not sufficient for mental state attribution: the notebook is a tool, not a part of the mind.

**Robert Rupert, "Challenges to the Hypothesis of Extended Cognition" (2004)** — Argues that the extended mind thesis conflates cognition with cognitive support: external tools assist cognition but are not themselves part of the cognitive system.

**Mark Rowlands, *The New Science of the Mind: From Extended Mind to Embodied Phenomenology* (2010)** — A comprehensive defense and refinement of extended mind theory, distinguishing the thesis from weaker "embedded" cognition views and engaging with phenomenological alternatives.

**John Sutton, "Exograms and Interdisciplinarity: History, the Extended Mind, and the Civilizing Process" (2010)** — Applies the extended mind framework to historical memory and cultural practices, arguing that exograms (external memory stores) partially constitute extended cognition.

**Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa, *The Bounds of Cognition* (2008)** — The most sustained critique of extended mind theory, arguing that genuine cognition requires intrinsic representational content and causal-computational processes inside the brain.
