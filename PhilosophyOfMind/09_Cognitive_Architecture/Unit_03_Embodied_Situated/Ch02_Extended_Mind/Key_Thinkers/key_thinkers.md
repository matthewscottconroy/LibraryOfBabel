# Key Thinkers: The Extended Mind

---

**Andy Clark** (b. 1957)

Clark is the most prominent defender of the extended mind hypothesis. In his seminal 1998 paper with Chalmers and in *Supersizing the Mind* (2008), he argues for the "parity principle": if a process in the world plays the same cognitive role as an internal process would, it deserves to be counted as part of the cognitive system. Clark argues that humans are "natural-born cyborgs" — our cognitive systems naturally incorporate external artifacts, language, and social structures as genuine cognitive components.

*Key works:* "The Extended Mind" (1998, with Chalmers); *Supersizing the Mind* (2008); *Natural-Born Cyborgs* (2003)

---

**David Chalmers** (b. 1966)

Chalmers co-authored the original extended mind paper and has been the philosophical commentator on its implications. He has developed the "extended mind" into a broader thesis about "cognitive systems" and has argued that extended mind is compatible with his views on phenomenal consciousness — even if the physical substrate of a belief is partly external, the phenomenal character of experience remains in the head. His work on virtual reality consciousness is a recent extension of these ideas.

*Key works:* "The Extended Mind" (1998, with Clark); *The Singularity: A Philosophical Analysis* (2010); "The Virtual and the Real" (2017)

---

**Fred Adams** (b. 1948) **and Ken Aizawa** (b. 1960)

Adams and Aizawa are the most persistent and systematic critics of the extended mind thesis. They argue that the "coupling-constitution fallacy" — inferring that because two systems are tightly coupled, they constitute a single cognitive system — is a logical error. Genuine cognition requires intrinsic representational content (content that is not derived from observer interpretation) and cognitive processes that are genuinely computational, neither of which, they argue, is present in external tools like notebooks or calculators.

*Key works:* *The Bounds of Cognition* (2008); "The Bounds of Cognition" (2001, article); "Defending the Bounds of Cognition" (2010)

---

**Robert Rupert** (b. 1964)

Rupert argues that the extended mind thesis conflicts with the best practice of cognitive science, which individuates cognitive systems by their internal causal structure rather than by their functional role in achieving cognitive tasks. He defends "embedded cognition" — the view that cognition is deeply shaped by and dependent on the environment — without the further, more radical thesis that the environment constitutes part of the cognitive system.

*Key works:* "Challenges to the Hypothesis of Extended Cognition" (2004); *Cognitive Systems and the Extended Mind* (2009)
