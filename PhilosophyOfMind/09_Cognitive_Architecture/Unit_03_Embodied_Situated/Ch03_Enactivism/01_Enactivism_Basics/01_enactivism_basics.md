# Enactivism: The Basics

Enactivism is the view that cognition is not a matter of representing an independent world in the mind, but a matter of enacting — bringing forth — a world of significance through the activity of a living being. The enactive approach, developed by Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch in their book *The Embodied Mind* (1991), synthesizes phenomenology, biological theory, and cognitive science into a distinctive account of mind as lived, embodied activity.

**The Central Claim**

The word "enaction" captures two interrelated ideas. First, cognition is enacted: it is achieved through the active engagement of a living being with its environment, not through the passive reception of inputs by a computational system. Second, what counts as a world — the meaningful domain in which an organism lives — is brought forth through the organism's activity: it is not pre-given but created in the process of living.

This is a radical departure from both classical cognitive science (which pictures the mind as computing over representations of a pre-given world) and from naive realism (which pictures the mind as directly accessing an independently existing reality). For enactivism, the relationship between mind and world is one of mutual specification: the organism specifies what counts as an environment, and the environment shapes the organism's cognitive capacities.

**Autopoiesis and Cognition**

Varela, Thompson, and Rosch drew on the biological theory of autopoiesis (self-production), developed by Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela. An autopoietic system is one that continuously produces and maintains itself — that regenerates its own components and the processes that produce them. Living cells are paradigm autopoietic systems: they continuously synthesize their own components, maintain their boundaries, and regulate their interactions with the environment.

The enactive insight is that this self-productive character of living systems is the basis for cognition. A living being is not a passive input-output system; it is a self-producing system that constitutes its own identity and determines for itself what counts as relevant. For a bacterium, what matters is the chemical gradient that indicates food or danger; for a human being, what matters is determined by a complex history of development, culture, and meaning-making.

On this view, cognition is not something that happens in the nervous system. It is a property of the living system as a whole — of the organism in its environment. The nervous system is important, but it is embedded in a body and that body is embedded in an environment, and cognition is the activity of this whole system.

**The Rejection of Internal Representations**

A distinctive feature of radical enactivism (developed further by Hutto and Myin) is the rejection of content-bearing mental representations. On this view, basic cognition — the cognition of simple organisms, and the unreflective, habitual engagement of more complex ones — does not involve representing the world. The organism acts, not on the basis of internal models of the world, but on the basis of sensorimotor habits and resonances with environmental features.

This is the most controversial aspect of enactivism. Classical cognitive science, predictive processing, and most forms of functionalism take representation to be central to cognition. Enactivists argue that this is a theoretical prejudice: we reach for representational explanations because they seem intuitive, not because they are the most accurate description of what is happening.

**Sense-Making**

A central concept in enactivism is sense-making — the organism's active constitution of a domain of significance. Sense-making is not just information processing; it is the organism's taking a stand on its environment, treating certain features as relevant, others as irrelevant, from the perspective of its own ongoing life.

For Varela, this sense-making is the basis of value and meaning in the biological world. An organism does not just detect facts; it evaluates them in terms of their significance for its own maintenance and development. This evaluative, perspective-taking character of living organisms is what makes their relationship to the world genuinely meaningful rather than merely mechanical.

**Enactivism and Phenomenology**

The enactive approach draws heavily on phenomenology, particularly Husserl's analysis of intentionality, Merleau-Ponty's account of the lived body, and Heidegger's concept of being-in-the-world. These phenomenologists emphasized that consciousness is always embodied and situated — that we are never detached observers of a world but always already engaged with it through our bodily practices and projects.

Thompson's later work in *Mind in Life* deepens this connection, arguing that phenomenology and biology converge on a common conception of the living mind as embodied, enacted, and emergent. The phenomenological account of lived experience and the biological account of autopoiesis illuminate the same phenomenon from different directions.
