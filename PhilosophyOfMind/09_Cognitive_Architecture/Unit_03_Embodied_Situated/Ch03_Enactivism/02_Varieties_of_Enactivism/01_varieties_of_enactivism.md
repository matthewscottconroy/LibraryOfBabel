# Varieties of Enactivism

"Enactivism" is not a single position but a family of related views that share the core commitment to cognition as embodied, active engagement with the world, while disagreeing about how radical this commitment should be and how it relates to representation, consciousness, and the sciences of mind.

**Autopoietic Enactivism**

The original form developed by Varela, Thompson, and Rosch, autopoietic enactivism grounds cognition in the biology of self-producing living systems. Cognition, on this view, is coextensive with life: any living system that maintains itself and orients to its environment is cognitive in the minimal sense. The bacterium swimming toward glucose is engaging in primitive cognition — not because it has a brain, but because it is a self-maintaining system that constitutes a domain of significance.

Autopoietic enactivism accepts a form of representationalism at higher cognitive levels: human beings do use representations in symbolic thought and language. The key claim is that this representational capacity is grounded in and emerges from the more basic capacity for embodied, non-representational engagement.

**Radical Enactivism**

Hutto and Myin's "Radical Enactive Cognition" (REC) takes a more thoroughgoing anti-representationalist stance. On their view, basic cognition — perception, action, affect — does not involve content-bearing representations at all. There are no mental states that represent the world or have semantic content; there are only contentless sensorimotor patterns and environmental couplings.

Content — the kind of intentionality that involves representing a world — enters the picture only with the kind of socially scaffolded, language-involving practices that are specific to humans (or human-like creatures). Basic organisms relate to their environments through direct sensorimotor engagement, not through internal models with content.

This is the most controversial form of enactivism. It requires a sharp distinction between basic cognition (contentless) and higher cognition (content-involving), and it faces the challenge of explaining how content-involving cognition emerges from contentless sensorimotor engagement without invoking the representational resources it has forsworn.

**Sensorimotor Enactivism**

O'Regan and Noë's sensorimotor theory (discussed in the previous chapter) represents a less radical form of enactivism focused specifically on perception. This view accepts that perception involves a kind of knowledge — knowledge of sensorimotor contingencies — but denies that this knowledge is constituted by internal representations. The knowledge is practical, not propositional; it is manifested in sensorimotor competencies rather than stored in internal models.

Sensorimotor enactivism occupies a middle position: it is not representationalist in the classical sense, but it accepts that perception involves a form of knowledge that goes beyond mere sensorimotor coupling.

**4E Cognition and the Enactivist Family**

Enactivism is one of four "E's" in the framework of "4E cognition" — embodied, embedded, extended, and enactive. While these four perspectives are related and often go together, they are conceptually distinct:

- *Embodied* cognition: the body shapes and partially constitutes cognitive processes.
- *Embedded* cognition: cognition is designed for and essentially depends on environmental scaffolding.
- *Extended* cognition: cognitive processes can extend beyond the body into the environment (Clark-Chalmers).
- *Enactive* cognition: cognition is constituted by activity and engagement, not representation.

The relationships among these views are complex. Enactivism supports embodiment (the body is essential to enactive engagement) and often supports embeddedness (the environment is the domain of engagement). But enactivism is more hostile to the extended mind thesis than might be expected: radical enactivists typically want to dissolve the mind-world boundary rather than simply extend it, whereas Clark's extended mind thesis moves the boundary outward while retaining the mind-body framework.

**Enactivism and Phenomenal Consciousness**

A central challenge for all forms of enactivism is to explain phenomenal consciousness — the "what it's like" of experience. Enactivists often emphasize the connection between cognition and life, or cognition and sensorimotor engagement, but these connections do not obviously explain why there is phenomenal experience.

Thompson's "Mind in Life" proposes that consciousness is a feature of living systems with the right kind of organization — that there is a continuum from basic living processes to full-blown phenomenal consciousness. This is an ambitious and controversial proposal that takes phenomenology seriously as a guide to the structure of consciousness while grounding it in biological theory.

Zahavi has argued that the enactive account provides resources for understanding the minimal or pre-reflective self — the implicit sense of being an experiencing subject that underlies all experience. The auto-affection involved in autopoietic self-maintenance may provide the biological basis for the minimal phenomenal self-model.

The debate about whether enactivism can genuinely address phenomenal consciousness, or whether it merely redescribes the functional aspects of experience in different terms, remains one of the most important unresolved issues in the field.
