# Further Reading: Enactivism

## Primary Sources

**Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch, *The Embodied Mind* (1991)** — The founding text of enactivism, arguing that cognition is not information processing in a disembodied brain but the enacted bringing-forth of a world through the autonomous, sensorimotor activity of an organism. Integrates cognitive science, phenomenology, and Buddhist philosophy.

**Alva Noë, *Action in Perception* (2004)** — Noë argues that perceiving is a form of skillful activity — it is constituted by the exercise of sensorimotor knowledge (knowledge of how stimulation changes with movement). Perception is not something that happens in the brain alone but involves the whole embodied agent in interaction with the world.

**Evan Thompson, *Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind* (2007)** — A comprehensive development of enactivism that argues for the continuity of life and mind: cognitive processes are rooted in the self-organizing, sense-making activity of living organisms.

## Secondary Literature

**Shaun Gallagher, *Enactivist Interventions: Rethinking the Mind* (2017)** — Gallagher examines enactivism across a range of topics including social cognition, mental illness, and the extended mind, arguing for an "enactivist" approach that avoids both classical representationalism and certain forms of panpsychism.

**Max de Lemos and Colin Klein (eds.), "Varieties of Enactivism" special issue, *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* (2015)** — A useful collection distinguishing different versions of enactivism (autopoietic, sensorimotor, radical, liberal) and their relationships to each other and to mainstream cognitive science.

**Jakob Hohwy and Anil Seth, "Predictive Processing as a Systematic Basis for Identifying the Neural Correlates of Consciousness" (2020)** — Examines the relationship between predictive processing and enactivism, exploring whether the two frameworks are compatible or in tension.

**Hanne De Jaegher and Ezequiel Di Paolo, "Participatory Sense-Making: An Enactive Approach to Social Cognition" (2007)** — Extends enactivism to social cognition, arguing that meaning-making in social interaction is a joint, participatory process that cannot be reduced to the activities of individual cognitive agents.
