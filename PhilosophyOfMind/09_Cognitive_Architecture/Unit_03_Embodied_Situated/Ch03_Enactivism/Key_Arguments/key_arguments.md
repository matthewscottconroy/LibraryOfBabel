# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Enactivism

## Key Arguments

**The Autopoietic Account of Cognition**
Maturana and Varela argued that living systems are autopoietic: they continuously produce and maintain themselves through a circular network of processes. Cognition, on this view, is not information processing but self-maintaining structural coupling with the environment: an organism "knows" its environment in the sense that it responds to perturbations in ways that maintain its own organization. This grounds cognition in biology rather than computation and replaces the representationalist framework with an organizational one.

**Radical Enactivism's Challenge to Basic Content**
Hutto and Myin's "Radical Enactive Cognition" (REC) argues that basic (non-linguistically scaffolded) cognition involves no contentful mental representations at all—not even minimal "vehicle representations." Basic organisms respond to their environment in ways that exhibit intentional properties (they are selective, directed) without those processes being genuinely about anything in the full-blown semantic sense. Genuine representational content enters only with language and symbolic cultural practices, which provide the scaffolding for normative, propositional content.

**The Argument Against the Need for Representations in Basic Cognition**
Radical enactivists argue that basic forms of perception and action do not require representing the world's features to navigate it successfully. The bacterium that swims up a sugar gradient is not representing the sugar concentration gradient; it is participating in a dynamic coupling with the chemical environment. The same holds for more complex organisms: perception is a skilled engagement with the world, not a representational modeling of it. This challenges the minimal representationalism of enactive (Varela, Thompson, Rosch) and predictive processing approaches.

**Thompson's Life-Mind Continuity Thesis**
Evan Thompson argues that there is a deep continuity between life and mind: the organizational properties that make something alive (autopoiesis, sense-making, metabolic self-maintenance) are continuous with the organizational properties that make something a mind. Minds are not brains; they are the activity of living bodies in their environments. This continuity thesis grounds phenomenology in biology: consciousness is the highest development of the same organizational capacities present in all living things, making the study of mind inseparable from the study of life.

**The Debate Between Enactivism and Representationalism**
The central dispute between enactivism and representationalism concerns whether any form of cognition requires internal representations. Representationalists (including predictive processing theorists) argue that even basic cognition requires predictive models of the environment; without internal representations, adaptive behavior cannot be explained. Radical enactivists argue that the appearance of representation in cognition reflects the observer's vocabulary, not the organism's actual processing. The debate is partly empirical (what is actually going on in neural systems?) and partly conceptual (what counts as "representation"?).

## Core Concepts

**Autopoiesis**
Autopoiesis (Maturana and Varela, from Greek: self-production) is the property of living systems of producing and maintaining their own components and boundaries through a network of processes. An autopoietic system is organizationally closed: it produces the components that constitute it through internal processes that it itself governs. Autopoiesis defines the boundary between living and non-living systems and grounds the enactivist account of cognition: a system has a cognitive domain in virtue of the same organizational closure that makes it autopoietic—it enacts a perspective by maintaining itself.

**Sense-Making**
Sense-making is the process by which an organism constitutes its environment as meaningful—as having value, significance, and relevance relative to the organism's own organization and needs. Sense-making requires that the organism has an identity to maintain (autopoiesis) and can distinguish what promotes from what threatens that identity. The simplest organisms (bacteria) engage in sense-making when they distinguish up-the-gradient (toward sugar, good) from down-the-gradient (away from sugar, bad). Human sense-making is culturally and linguistically scaffolded but is continuous with this basic biological form.

**Structural Coupling**
Structural coupling is the ongoing, history-dependent co-evolution of an organism and its environment: each perturbs the other, and each responds by reorganizing its own structure. The organism selects which perturbations are relevant (those that threaten or support its organization) and responds to them in ways determined by its own structure—not by any "representation" of the environment. The history of structural coupling shapes both the organism and the environment in an ongoing dance that cannot be reduced to information transfer from environment to organism.

**Radical Enactive Cognition (REC)**
REC (Hutto and Myin) is the view that basic cognition—the kind that does not involve language or cultural scaffolding—is entirely non-representational and non-contentful. Basic organisms are "content-free" in the sense that their neural and bodily processes do not represent anything or carry semantic content. Contentful mental representations (beliefs, propositional attitudes) arise only within linguistic and social practices that provide the normative scaffolding for semantic correctness. REC is distinguished from other forms of enactivism by its rejection of minimal representationalism.

**Enaction**
Enaction is the process by which cognizers bring forth or constitute their world through action and perception—not by representing a pre-given world but by enacting a domain of relevance. The term is Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's: "we propose as a name the term enactive to emphasize the growing conviction that cognition is not the representation of a pregiven world by a pregiven mind but is rather the enactment of a world and a mind on the basis of a history of the variety of actions that a being in the world performs." Enaction emphasizes the active, organism-relative character of any cognitive engagement with the world.

## Thought Experiments

**The Paramecium Navigation**
A paramecium navigates toward food and away from toxins through a combination of random swimming and chemotactic adjustment. Does the paramecium represent the food gradient? Radical enactivists say no: the paramecium participates in a dynamic coupling with the chemical environment. Its behavior is food-directed without involving any internal symbol that stands for the food. The thought experiment tests intuitions about the minimum conditions for intentionality and representation, and it supports REC's claim that basic purposive behavior requires no genuine representation.

**The Frog's Tongue Revisited**
Varela and Thompson reinterpret the frog's fly-detection system: the frog does not represent flies—it participates in a sensorimotor coupling with small dark moving objects that is constituted by the frog's history of structural coupling with its environment. The system is not a "fly detector" in the representational sense but a participant in a dance of mutual perturbation between frog and environment. The teleological account (Millikan) says it represents flies because it was selected to track flies; the enactivist says there is no representation, only ongoing coupling. The contrast between these accounts is the core disagreement between enactivism and teleosemantic representationalism.

**The Ecological Niche as Mind**
Gibson's ecological psychology and enactivism together suggest a thought experiment: imagine a mind without a brain—a mind constituted entirely by the relationship between an organism's action capacities and its environment's affordance structure. An organism's "cognitive space" is defined by which affordances it can detect and use; changing the environment changes the organism's effective cognitive domain. The thought experiment is designed to show that mind is not a property of the brain but of the organism-environment system, and that studying the brain in isolation is studying only half of the relevant system.
