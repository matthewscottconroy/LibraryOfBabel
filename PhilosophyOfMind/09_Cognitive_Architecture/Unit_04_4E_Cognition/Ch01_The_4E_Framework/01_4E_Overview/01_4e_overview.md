# The 4E Framework

The phrase "4E cognition" refers to a cluster of approaches that challenge the standard cognitive science picture of the mind as an internal, brain-based computational system. The four E's stand for embodied, embedded, extended, and enactive cognition. Together, they represent a family of views that seek to reconnect the mind to the body, the environment, and the activity of living, rather than treating it as a disembodied information processor.

**Why 4E?**

The 4E label is useful because it captures a family resemblance among approaches that share deep motivations while differing in specific commitments. All four approaches react against what Mark Rowlands calls "the Cartesian legacy" in cognitive science: the picture of the mind as an inner theater — a separate, immaterial (or at least biologically isolated) substance that receives inputs from the body and world, processes them internally, and then issues commands to the body.

The Cartesian picture, even in its contemporary computational guise, treats the body as a peripheral device and the environment as a domain of inputs and outputs. The 4E approaches, each in their own way, argue that this picture systematically distorts our understanding of cognition by abstracting away from the features that make cognition what it is.

**Embodied Cognition**

Embodied cognition holds that the particular physical character of the body — its shape, its sensorimotor capacities, its developmental history — is not merely incidental to cognition but constitutive of it. The concepts we form, the categories we employ, and the reasoning we do are shaped by how we are bodily situated. Lakoff and Johnson's work on conceptual metaphor argues that our most abstract concepts are structured by embodied metaphors grounded in physical experience — we understand argument as combat, time as spatial movement, abstract quantities as physical magnitudes, because our bodies relate to the world in these ways.

Embodied cognition does not require that all cognition is bodily or that the brain plays no special role. It requires that the kind of cognition we have — its specific structure and content — is partly determined by the kind of body we have.

**Embedded Cognition**

Embedded cognition emphasizes the dependence of cognitive processes on environmental scaffolding. Cognitive tasks that seem to be purely internal — remembering, calculating, planning — are routinely and systematically offloaded onto the environment: notebooks, calendars, lists, spatial organization, social practices.

This offloading is not an accident or a convenience; it reflects a deep feature of how human cognition is organized. Humans are essentially tool-using creatures who extend their cognitive processes into their environments. The environment is not just the backdrop for cognition but an integral part of the cognitive system.

Embedded cognition is more conservative than extended cognition (Clark-Chalmers): it holds that cognitive processes depend on environmental scaffolding, not that they literally extend into the environment. The embedded view is consistent with holding that the cognitive process itself occurs entirely in the brain, while acknowledging that the brain's performance depends essentially on its environmental embedding.

**Extended Cognition**

As discussed in the previous chapter, extended cognition (Clark and Chalmers) holds that cognitive processes can literally extend into the environment. When an external resource is sufficiently well integrated with an agent's cognitive processes — reliably available, easily accessible, automatically endorsed — it becomes a genuine component of those processes.

Extended cognition is the most philosophically radical of the 4Es in a certain sense: it directly challenges the spatial and material boundary of the mind, dissolving the distinction between internalist and externalist explanations of mental content.

**Enactive Cognition**

Enactive cognition, as discussed in the previous sections, holds that cognition is constituted by the activity of a living system — by sense-making, sensorimotor engagement, and the enactment of a domain of significance. On radical versions, basic cognition does not involve representation at all; it is pure activity.

**Tensions and Interactions**

The 4E approaches are not fully unified. There are tensions between them:

- Extended mind and radical enactivism: the extended mind thesis moves the boundary of the mind outward, while radical enactivism rejects the mind-world boundary entirely. These are different moves.
- Embodied cognition and classical cognitive science: some forms of embodied cognition are compatible with computational approaches (the body provides inputs that shape computation), while others are fundamentally anti-representationalist.
- Embedded and extended: embedded cognition is consistent with classical cognitive science modified to acknowledge environmental dependence; extended cognition is a more radical revision.

Despite these tensions, the 4E approaches share enough to constitute a recognizable research program. Their common target is the isolated, disembodied, world-independent computational mind; their common commitment is to reconnecting mind to body, environment, and life.
