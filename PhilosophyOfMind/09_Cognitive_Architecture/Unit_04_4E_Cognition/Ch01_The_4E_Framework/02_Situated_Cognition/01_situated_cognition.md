# Situated Cognition

Situated cognition is the view that cognitive processes are deeply influenced by — and cannot be properly understood in isolation from — the physical and social context in which they occur. This claim encompasses both weaker and stronger versions: from the relatively conservative observation that context shapes cognitive performance, to the more radical claim that cognitive processes are literally constituted by situational factors.

**The Contextual Sensitivity of Cognition**

One of the most robust findings in cognitive psychology is that performance on cognitive tasks varies dramatically with context. Memory is context-dependent: people recall items better in the same environmental context in which they were learned. Problem-solving is context-dependent: the same problem presented in a familiar context is solved more readily than in an abstract one. Reasoning is context-dependent: logical reasoning that is difficult in abstract form becomes much easier when embedded in a familiar social scenario (Cosmides and Tooby's work on the Watt selection task showed that performance improved dramatically when the task was framed as detecting cheaters rather than confirming a rule).

These context effects might be interpreted as merely showing that context influences an otherwise context-independent cognitive system. The situated cognition view goes further: the cognitive system is not context-independent at all. It is designed for and adapted to specific contexts, and its functioning cannot be understood apart from those contexts.

**Situated Action and Activity Theory**

Lucy Suchman's *Plans and Situated Actions* (1987) challenged the planning-based approach to AI and cognitive science by arguing that intelligent action is primarily improvised response to a continuously unfolding situation, not the execution of a pre-formed plan. Plans are vague, schematic resources that agents draw on as action unfolds, not programs that determine action in advance.

This observation connects to Dreyfus's critique of classical AI and to the phenomenological concept of thrownness — the idea that we are always already in a situation that we did not choose and that shapes our practical options. Intelligent action is not planning plus execution; it is situated improvisation that draws on practical understanding of the kind that cannot be fully explicated in advance.

Activity theory, developed in the Soviet tradition by Vygotsky and Leontiev and extended by Engeström, provides a systematic framework for analyzing cognition in the context of socially organized activities. The unit of analysis is not the individual mind but the activity system — a structured set of practices with tools, rules, division of labor, and community. Individual cognitive processes are embedded in and shaped by these activity systems.

**Distributed Cognition**

Edwin Hutchins developed the concept of distributed cognition to describe cognitive systems that span individual minds, external media, and social processes. In *Cognition in the Wild* (1995), Hutchins studied navigation on a US Navy ship and argued that the cognitive process of navigation was not located in any individual crew member's mind but was distributed across the crew, instruments, charts, and procedures.

On Hutchins's view, a cognitive system is any system that processes information and maintains representational states; this system can extend across brains, bodies, tools, and social practices. The unit of cognitive analysis should be the distributed system, not the individual.

Distributed cognition differs from extended cognition in its emphasis on group-level and socially organized processes rather than the individual-object coupling that Clark and Chalmers emphasize. The relevant boundary for distributed cognition is the functional system that processes information, wherever that system's components are located.

**Situated Learning**

Lave and Wenger's work on situated learning proposed that knowledge is not a thing that can be acquired independently of practice, but is always embedded in a particular community of practice. To know something is to be able to participate in the practices through which that knowledge is used and developed.

This challenges traditional education, which typically removes learners from the contexts of application and teaches abstract knowledge outside its natural habitat. Situated learning theory argues that effective learning must be situated — must occur in the context of practice, with guidance from more experienced practitioners, and in a way that connects knowledge to its uses.

**Situated Cognition and Cognitive Science**

Situated cognition research has productively challenged cognitive science to attend to the details of how people actually think in real environments — workplace settings, everyday problem-solving, social interaction — rather than the artificial conditions of laboratory experiments. This has generated rich collaborations between cognitive science, anthropology, human-computer interaction, and education.

The deeper philosophical challenge is whether situatedness is a design feature (the cognitive system is adapted for situated use) or a constitutive feature (cognitive processes are literally constituted by situational factors). This parallels the embedded-versus-extended debate: is the environment a context for cognition or a component of it?
