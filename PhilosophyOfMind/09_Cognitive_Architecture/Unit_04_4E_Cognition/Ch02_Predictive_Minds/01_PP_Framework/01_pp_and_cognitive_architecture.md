# Predictive Processing as Cognitive Architecture

Predictive processing (PP) has been proposed not just as a theory of perception or consciousness, but as a comprehensive account of cognitive architecture — a unified framework for understanding how the brain implements cognition across all its domains. Andy Clark, in particular, has argued that the PP framework provides the most compelling synthesis of classical computational cognitive science and embodied/4E approaches.

**PP as a Unified Architecture**

The central claim of PP as cognitive architecture is that the brain is fundamentally a prediction machine. At every level of the neural hierarchy, the brain constructs generative models that predict incoming sensory information. Prediction errors — mismatches between predicted and actual input — propagate upward to update the models. Action, on this account, is the process of changing the world to match predictions rather than changing predictions to match the world.

This provides a single computational principle that can, in principle, account for perception, attention, memory, motor control, learning, emotion, and social cognition. The apparent diversity of mental faculties reduces, on the PP account, to different modes of a single computational process applied at different levels of abstraction and on different timescales.

**PP and Classical Computationalism**

PP shares with classical cognitive science the commitment to the brain as a computational system that processes representations. The brain maintains probabilistic models of the world (the generative models), and these models have content — they represent states of the world.

But PP departs from classical cognitive science in several respects. The representations are probabilistic (Bayesian) rather than discrete and symbolic. They are organized hierarchically, with higher levels encoding more abstract and slower-changing properties. And the processing is primarily predictive and error-correcting rather than feedforward input-processing.

PP is thus a form of computationalism, but one that is more consistent with neural plausibility and with the hierarchical, recurrent character of actual neural processing than classical symbolic systems were.

**PP and Embodied/4E Approaches**

Clark argues that PP and 4E cognition are natural allies rather than competitors. The PP account of action — in which actions are performed to bring the world into alignment with predictions, a process called active inference — implies that action and perception are deeply interconnected. The organism doesn't passively receive sensory information and then act; it acts in order to generate the sensory information its models predict, constantly probing and testing its predictions against the world.

This active, exploratory character of cognition resonates with enactivist and embodied approaches: cognition is not passive reception but active world-engagement. The organism's generative models are, in part, models of its own sensorimotor possibilities — they represent not just what the world is like, but what actions are available and what sensory consequences those actions will have.

**The Predictive Processing Account of Higher Cognition**

PP accounts of perception have received the most attention, but the framework has been extended to cover many aspects of higher cognition.

Attention, as discussed elsewhere, is modeled as precision weighting — adjusting the influence of different sensory inputs on the system's estimates. Learning is the process of adjusting the generative model to reduce long-term prediction error. Memory, on some accounts, is prediction in the time domain: the brain uses past patterns to predict future inputs, and what we call "memory" is the reconstruction of past events from stored generative models.

Emotion has been interpreted in PP terms as the representation of the body's own states — interoceptive predictions and prediction errors. The experience of emotion is the experience of the body in a certain state, modeled and interpreted in terms of the organism's broader situation and goals.

**Challenges to PP as Unified Architecture**

The ambition of PP to provide a unified cognitive architecture faces several challenges.

First, the move from predictive processing of sensory input to all of cognition requires substantial extension. It is not obvious that memory, imagination, and social cognition are simply instances of the same prediction-error minimization process that governs perception.

Second, the explanatory claims of PP can be difficult to test. The framework is general enough to accommodate a wide range of findings, which makes it powerful but also potentially unfalsifiable for specific predictions.

Third, the relationship between PP and consciousness remains complex. PP accounts offer a rich treatment of the functional architecture of cognition; whether they address the phenomenal character of experience — why predictions and error signals are accompanied by phenomenal experience — is less clear.

Despite these challenges, PP has become one of the most productive research programs in cognitive science and philosophy of mind, generating new predictions and new integrations across formerly disparate domains.
