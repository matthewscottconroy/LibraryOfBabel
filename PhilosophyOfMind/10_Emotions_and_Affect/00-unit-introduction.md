# Unit Introduction: Emotions and Affect

## Central Questions

Emotions pose a distinctive challenge for philosophy of mind: they seem to be simultaneously felt (they have phenomenal character), directed (they are about things), and evaluative (they involve assessments of the world). Fear is a feeling, but it is also fear *of* something, and it registers that thing as dangerous. Whether the feeling, the directedness, and the evaluation are all aspects of a single state or are separable components — and which, if any, is the essence of emotion — is the central theoretical question.

The debate has practical as well as theoretical stakes. Emotions influence reasoning and decision-making in ways that challenge classical models of rationality. They ground moral responses (empathy, indignation, guilt) and raise questions about the relationship between emotion and moral judgment. The discovery of patients with ventromedial prefrontal damage (Damasio's work) who suffer both impaired emotional responses and severely impaired decision-making suggests that emotions are not merely subjective coloring added to rational deliberation but constitutively involved in rationality itself.

Three major theoretical families organize the field: *feeling theories* hold that emotions are essentially felt bodily states; *cognitive-evaluative theories* hold that emotions are essentially appraisals of the world as good or bad in some relevant respect; *hybrid theories* hold that both elements are essential and that neither is reducible to the other. Each family comes in many versions, and each faces characteristic problems: feeling theories struggle to account for the intentionality of emotion; cognitive theories struggle to account for their phenomenology; hybrid theories must explain how the two elements are unified into a single state.

## Major Positions and Debates

### The James-Lange Theory

William James's famous question — "Do we run because we are afraid, or are we afraid because we run?" — encapsulates the feeling theory of emotion. The James-Lange theory holds that emotions are the perception of bodily changes: when we encounter a bear, our body responds (heart racing, muscles tensing, legs running), and the emotion just *is* the felt perception of these bodily changes. Without the bodily changes, there would be no emotion; the feeling *is* the emotion.

The theory is counterintuitive but has several virtues: it connects emotions to the physical world, explains their motivational force (we flee because we are already in flight), and makes emotion essentially embodied. The main objection (Cannon's critique) is physiological: bodily responses are too slow and too undifferentiated to explain the variety and swiftness of emotional experience. A more philosophical objection is that the theory cannot account for the intentional directedness of emotion: fear is not just a feeling of arousal but a feeling *of* something as dangerous.

### Cognitive Appraisal Theories

Cognitive appraisal theories (Arnold, Lazarus) hold that emotions are responses to evaluations of the world. Fear is not a feeling of bodily arousal but an appraisal of a situation as threatening combined with an associated felt state and motivation to avoid the threat. Different emotions correspond to different appraisal patterns: anger involves appraising something as a wrong done to me; shame involves appraising myself as falling short of a standard.

Appraisal theories provide a natural account of the intentionality of emotion: the appraisal is about something, and this directedness is constitutive of the emotion. They also explain the diversity of emotional experience: the same situation can generate different emotions in different people because they appraise it differently. The main objection is that appraisal theories make emotion too cognitive: phobias and many basic emotions seem to occur without any evaluation that the subject would endorse, and they can persist even when the subject knows the evaluation is mistaken.

### Nussbaum's Cognitive Theory and the Eudaimonistic Appraisal

Martha Nussbaum's *Upheavals of Thought* (2001) develops a sophisticated neo-Stoic cognitive theory of emotion. On Nussbaum's account, emotions are *eudaimonistic appraisals*: evaluative judgments that see things in the world as mattering to one's own flourishing. Grief is the judgment that something of great importance has been irreversibly lost; love is the judgment that another person is of central importance to one's flourishing. These are evaluative cognitions, not mere feelings; they can be appropriate or inappropriate, rational or irrational.

Nussbaum's theory grounds a rich account of moral emotions (compassion, indignation) and their role in moral life. Her account has been criticized for over-intellectualizing emotion: young children and many animals have emotions without anything like the evaluative structure Nussbaum describes; and emotions can be recalcitrant in the face of correct appraisal in ways that are hard to explain if emotions just *are* evaluative judgments.

### Basic Emotion Theory and Its Critics

Paul Ekman's basic emotion theory holds that there are a small number of universal, biologically grounded basic emotions (happiness, sadness, fear, anger, disgust, surprise) that are expressed in universal facial expressions, recognized across cultures, and associated with distinct physiological patterns. This theory has dominated affective science and has been influential in policy (the TSA, briefly, used Ekman-derived techniques for detecting emotion in airports).

Lisa Feldman Barrett's *theory of constructed emotion* challenges the basic emotion thesis at every point. Barrett argues that emotions are not natural kinds discovered in the brain but active constructions built from core affective states (valence and arousal) and conceptual knowledge. There are no emotion-specific neural circuits; emotional categories are culturally and conceptually variable; facial expressions do not reliably indicate emotions. This debate is not purely philosophical — it turns on contested empirical evidence — but the philosophical stakes (what kind of thing an emotion is, whether emotions are natural kinds) are clear.

### The Somatic Marker Hypothesis

Antonio Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis (*Descartes' Error*, 1994) holds that emotion is constitutively involved in rational decision-making. Patients with ventromedial prefrontal damage, like the famous Phineas Gage and Damasio's patient Elliot, lose emotional responsiveness and simultaneously lose the ability to make good decisions in real life, even while retaining intact reasoning capacities. Damasio argues that emotional signals — somatic markers — provide fast evaluative signals that guide decision-making, and that rationality without emotion is actually impaired.

The somatic marker hypothesis provides the most direct empirical argument that emotions are not epiphenomenal accompaniments of cognition but are constitutive components of rationality. It connects philosophical questions about the relationship between reason and emotion to specific neural mechanisms (the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, the amygdala, the insula).

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**William James** introduced the feeling theory and the fundamental question about the direction of causation between feeling and bodily response. His *Principles of Psychology* chapters on emotion and instinct remain essential reading.

**Martha Nussbaum** (*Upheavals of Thought*, 2001) developed the most philosophically sophisticated cognitive theory of emotion, connecting it to ancient philosophy, literary culture, and moral psychology.

**Ronald de Sousa** (*The Rationality of Emotion*, 1987) argued that emotions are best understood as providing the *relevance* that is presupposed by practical and theoretical reasoning — without emotions, there is nothing to determine which considerations matter.

**Peter Goldie** (*The Emotions: A Philosophical Exploration*, 2000) developed the notion of *feeling towards* — emotions as ways of feeling that are directed at objects — and argued that the phenomenology and intentionality of emotion are inseparable.

**Antonio Damasio** (*Descartes' Error*, 1994; *The Feeling of What Happens*, 1999) provided the neurological evidence for the somatic marker hypothesis and argued that Descartes' picture of a disembodied rational mind is empirically falsified by brain lesion studies.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. James, *The Principles of Psychology*, Chapter 25 (1890)**
The original statement of the feeling theory of emotion. James's vivid phenomenological descriptions and his counterintuitive claim that the bodily response precedes the feeling are still fresh and worth reading in the original.

**2. Nussbaum, *Upheavals of Thought: The Intelligence of Emotions* (2001)**
The most comprehensive philosophical treatment of emotions, integrating ancient philosophy, literary examples, and detailed phenomenological description. The first three chapters, which develop the cognitive theory, are essential; the later chapters apply the theory to compassion and grief.

**3. Damasio, *Descartes' Error: Emotion, Reason, and the Human Brain* (1994)**
The most important empirical argument for the constitutive role of emotion in cognition. Damasio's descriptions of his patients are vivid and his theoretical account is accessible. Essential for understanding how neuroscientific findings bear on philosophical theories of emotion.

**4. de Sousa, *The Rationality of Emotion* (1987)**
The most technically sophisticated philosophical treatment of emotion. De Sousa argues that emotions are paradigm scenarios that provide the relevance presupposed by rational action, and that this gives emotions a constitutive role in rationality. Demanding but rewarding.

**5. Barrett, *How Emotions Are Made: The Secret Life of the Brain* (2017)**
The most accessible presentation of the constructionist theory of emotion, which challenges the basic emotion paradigm at every point. Barrett argues that emotions are created by the brain as active constructions, not readouts of pre-existing biological states. The book connects philosophy, affective science, and neuroscience in an unusually accessible way.

## Open Questions

**1. Are emotions natural kinds?**
The debate between Ekman's basic emotion theory and Barrett's constructionist theory turns on whether emotional categories carve nature at its joints. The empirical evidence is contested; the philosophical question — what it would mean for emotions to be natural kinds — is also contested. The meta-question of what methodology is appropriate for settling this is itself unresolved.

**2. Can emotions be rational or irrational?**
If emotions are feelings, they seem to be outside the scope of rationality — feelings are not the kind of thing that can be true or false, appropriate or inappropriate. But if emotions are appraisals, they can be fitting or unfitting, and can be evaluated as rational or not. The question of whether emotions can be rational connects to debates about practical reason and the possibility of an affective epistemology.

**3. Is empathy constitutively moral?**
Some philosophers argue that empathy — feeling with others — is constitutively involved in moral judgment and moral motivation; others (Bloom, against empathy) argue that empathy is actually a poor guide to moral action, being subject to in-group bias and scope insensitivity. Whether empathy is necessary for moral response or is merely one (potentially distorting) input to moral judgment is a live debate.

**4. What is the relationship between mood and emotion?**
Moods (depression, anxiety, elation) are affective states without specific intentional objects: depression is not depression *about* anything in particular. How do moods relate to emotions (which are directed toward specific objects)? Do moods function as background affective contexts that shape emotional responses, or are they a distinct kind of mental state? The relationship between mood and emotion is philosophically underdeveloped.

**5. Do emotions constitute values or merely track them?**
Some philosophers hold that emotions are not just responses to values but constitutive of values: it is in virtue of fearing something that it counts as dangerous, and in virtue of cherishing something that it counts as precious. This *sentimentalist* view of value raises questions about the objectivity of value and the status of moral disagreement that connect emotions to metaethics.
