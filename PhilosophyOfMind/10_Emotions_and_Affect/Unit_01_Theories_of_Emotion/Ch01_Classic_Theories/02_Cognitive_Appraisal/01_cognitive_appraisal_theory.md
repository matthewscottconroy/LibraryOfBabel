# Cognitive Appraisal Theory

Cognitive appraisal theory holds that emotions are responses to how the subject evaluates — appraises — their situation. What determines which emotion a person feels is not the objective features of a situation, nor the raw physiological state of the body, but the person's cognitive evaluation of that situation relative to their goals, values, and beliefs. The same situation can produce different emotions in different people, or in the same person at different times, because the emotion depends on the appraisal, not the situation itself.

**Schachter and Singer: The Two-Factor Theory**

A pivotal experiment by Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer (1962) demonstrated that the same state of physiological arousal could be experienced as different emotions depending on the cognitive context. Subjects were injected with epinephrine (which produces arousal) and placed in a room with a confederate who behaved either euphorically (dancing, making paper airplanes) or angrily (complaining about questionnaires). Subjects who were not told what the drug would do experienced emotions consistent with the confederate's behavior: happiness or anger.

Schachter and Singer concluded that emotion is the product of two factors: undifferentiated physiological arousal and a cognitive label that identifies the arousal as a particular emotion. The arousal provides the intensity; the cognitive label determines the quality. This two-factor theory implies that emotions are partly constituted by cognitive appraisals of one's situation.

**Lazarus's Appraisal Theory**

Richard Lazarus developed the most detailed cognitive appraisal theory. On his account, emotions are produced by a series of appraisals along several dimensions:

- *Primary appraisal*: Is this relevant to my goals and wellbeing? Is it beneficial or harmful?
- *Secondary appraisal*: What resources do I have to deal with it? Who is responsible for it?
- *Relational meaning*: What does this situation mean for my relationship to my goals and environment?

Different combinations of appraisal dimensions yield different emotions. Fear involves appraising a threat that one is uncertain how to handle. Anger involves appraising a demeaning offense by another agent for which one can hold them responsible. Guilt involves appraising oneself as having transgressed a moral norm one endorses. Each emotion has what Lazarus calls a "core relational theme" — a distinctive appraised relationship between self and world.

**Philosophical Significance**

Appraisal theory connects to the philosophical view that emotions are intentional states — states that are "about" something and that represent their objects in evaluative terms. On this view, fear is not just an agitated feeling; it is a state that represents something as threatening. Anger is a state that represents something as a wrong that deserves response. This representational, evaluative character of emotion is precisely what appraisal theory identifies as central.

This connects emotions to reasons and rationality. If emotions involve appraisals — evaluative representations — then they can be appropriate or inappropriate, accurate or inaccurate. Fear of a genuine threat is an appropriate response; fear of a harmless stimulus is not. Emotions can be criticized not just causal-mechanically but normatively: they can be right or wrong, warranted or unwarranted.

This connection between emotion and rational appraisal was central to Aristotle's treatment of the emotions in the *Rhetoric* and *Nicomachean Ethics*. Contemporary neo-Aristotelian philosophers like Martha Nussbaum have developed this thread into a "cognitive theory" of emotion that treats emotions as forms of evaluative judgment about what matters.

**Challenges to Pure Cognitivism**

Appraisal theory has been challenged by the observation that emotions can occur without cognitive appraisal — or at least without deliberate, conscious appraisal.

Joseph LeDoux's work on the "low road" of fear processing showed that the amygdala can produce fear responses without involving the cortical areas associated with conscious evaluation. The amygdala receives direct inputs from sensory areas and can trigger fear responses before conscious appraisal has occurred. This suggests there is a subcortical pathway for emotion that bypasses the kind of cognitive evaluation Lazarus describes.

Robert Zajonc argued that "affect precedes cognition" — that emotional responses can occur faster than cognitive evaluation, and that evaluative preferences can be formed without cognitive processing. The mere exposure effect (liking familiar stimuli more, even without conscious recognition of their familiarity) is one illustration.

Appraisal theorists respond by distinguishing between automatic, unconscious appraisals and deliberate, conscious ones. Lazarus acknowledged that appraisal can be rapid and unconscious; the key claim is not that appraisal is deliberate, but that it is structured by the subject's goal-relevance assessments, however automatically those are generated. The "low road" fear response might be understood as an automatic, subcortical appraisal of threat-relevance.

**The Intentionality of Emotion**

The deepest philosophical contribution of appraisal theory is its vindication of the intentionality of emotion. Emotions are not just felt agitations; they are about things, and they represent those things in evaluative terms. This makes emotions epistemically significant — they can disclose genuine features of the world (threats, goods, wrongs, losses) — and it makes them capable of rationality assessment. The study of when emotions are appropriate and when they are not is a form of emotional epistemology that sits at the intersection of cognitive science and ethics.
