# Further Reading: Classic Theories of Emotion

## Primary Sources

**William James, "What Is an Emotion?" (1884)** — James argues that emotions are perceptions of bodily changes: we do not tremble because we are afraid; we are afraid because we perceive that we are trembling. Simultaneously proposed by Carl Lange; the view became the James-Lange theory. A foundational text.

**Walter Cannon, "The James-Lange Theory of Emotions: A Critical Examination and an Alternative Theory" (1927)** — Cannon argues against the James-Lange view, showing that visceral changes are too slow and undifferentiated to account for the speed and variety of emotional experience. Proposes that emotions are generated in the thalamus.

**Magda Arnold, *Emotion and Personality* (1960)** — Arnold introduces the concept of "appraisal" — the intuitive evaluation of the significance of a situation — as the cognitive precursor to emotion. The first systematic cognitive appraisal theory.

**Richard Lazarus, *Psychological Stress and the Coping Process* (1966)** — Lazarus develops appraisal theory into a comprehensive account of stress, coping, and emotion, arguing that how one evaluates (appraises) a situation determines the emotional response.

## Secondary Literature

**Schachter and Singer, "Cognitive, Social, and Physiological Determinants of Emotional State" (1962)** — The "two-factor theory": emotions require both physiological arousal and cognitive labeling of that arousal. Classic and influential, though subsequently contested.

**Paul Ekman, "An Argument for Basic Emotions" (1992)** — Ekman summarizes evidence for the existence of a small set of biologically basic, cross-culturally universal emotions (happiness, sadness, fear, anger, disgust, surprise), each with a distinctive facial expression and physiological profile.

**Carroll Izard, *The Psychology of Emotions* (1991)** — A systematic treatment of the "discrete emotions" theory, arguing that there are specific basic emotions with unique phenomenological, physiological, and expressive profiles.

**Randolph Nesse, "Evolutionary Explanations of Emotions" (1990)** — Argues that each negative emotion corresponds to an evolved response to a specific class of adaptive problem, connecting emotion theory to evolutionary biology.
