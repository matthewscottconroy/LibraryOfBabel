# Key Thinkers: Classic Theories of Emotion

---

**William James** (1842–1910)

James's 1884 paper "What Is an Emotion?" proposes the most counter-intuitive and influential theory of emotion in the history of psychology: emotions are not causes of bodily responses but perceptions of them. Fear is not what causes the racing heart; the racing heart (and the running, and the trembling) is what, when perceived, constitutes fear. This peripheralist, somatic view was elaborated by Carl Lange independently and became the James-Lange theory, which defined the terms of emotion debate for a century.

*Key works:* "What Is an Emotion?" (1884); *The Principles of Psychology*, Chapter 25 (1890); *Psychology: Briefer Course* (1892)

---

**Walter Cannon** (1871–1945)

Cannon's critique of the James-Lange theory — delivered in a famous 1927 paper — established several counterarguments: visceral changes are too slow to cause the fast onset of emotion; artificial visceral changes (e.g., from adrenalin) do not produce genuine emotion; the same visceral state accompanies different emotions; and animals with viscera surgically separated from the brain still show emotional behavior. Cannon proposed a central (thalamic) theory in which the thalamus generates both emotional experience and bodily response simultaneously.

*Key works:* *Bodily Changes in Pain, Hunger, Fear and Rage* (1915); "The James-Lange Theory of Emotions" (1927); *The Wisdom of the Body* (1932)

---

**Magda Arnold** (1903–2002)

Arnold introduced "appraisal" as the cognitive mediator between situation and emotion: an emotion is triggered not by a stimulus per se but by the agent's evaluative perception of the stimulus as good or bad, relevant or irrelevant, manageable or overwhelming. Her concept of appraisal became the central theoretical tool of cognitive appraisal theories and influenced all subsequent emotion researchers in the cognitive tradition.

*Key works:* *Emotion and Personality* (1960); "Perennial Problems in the Field of Emotion" (1970)

---

**Paul Ekman** (b. 1934)

Ekman is the psychologist most associated with the "basic emotions" hypothesis — the view that a small number of emotions are biologically universal, each with a distinctive facial expression recognizable across cultures. His cross-cultural research with preliterate populations (Fore people of Papua New Guinea) appeared to confirm the universality of facial expressions. While his findings have been contested in recent years (particularly by Lisa Feldman Barrett), his work dominated emotion research for decades.

*Key works:* "Universal and Cultural Differences in Facial Expressions of Emotion" (1972); "An Argument for Basic Emotions" (1992); *Emotions Revealed* (2003)
