# Emotions as Evaluative Judgments

Evaluative theories of emotion hold that emotions are a form of evaluative cognition — a way of apprehending or representing the significance of situations for the subject's values, goals, and wellbeing. On this view, emotions are not mere feelings or bodily agitations; they are cognitive states with intentional content that can be assessed as accurate or inaccurate, rational or irrational, appropriate or inappropriate.

**The Rationalist Tradition**

The idea that emotions involve evaluation has deep roots in ancient philosophy. Aristotle defined each emotion in terms of the appraisal it involves: fear is the expectation of harm, anger is the perception of undeserved slight, pity is the perception of undeserved misfortune. Each emotion is a response to a certain kind of evaluative situation, and it is appropriate or inappropriate depending on whether the situation actually has the relevant features.

The Stoics went further: emotions are false or excessive judgments. Fear is the judgment that something bad is about to happen — but for the Stoic sage, the only genuine bad is moral evil, so fear of physical harm is a mistaken judgment about what is truly bad. The prescription follows: correct your emotional life by correcting your evaluative judgments.

**Nussbaum's Neo-Aristotelian Cognitive Theory**

Martha Nussbaum's *Upheavals of Thought* (2001) develops the most sophisticated contemporary version of the cognitive theory. For Nussbaum, emotions are "eudaimonistic judgments" — evaluations of how the world is going with respect to what the subject deeply values. Grief involves the judgment that something of great worth has been lost. Fear involves the judgment that something threatens what one values. Love involves seeing the object as having extraordinary worth.

This cognitive account makes emotions epistemically significant: they can provide access to evaluative truths about the world. Our emotional responses are not noise in the signal of cognition; they are a form of perception of evaluative reality. Nussbaum draws on Henry James's novels to argue that great literature cultivates emotional perception — the capacity to respond appropriately to what matters — in ways that dry argument cannot.

Emotions are also intelligent: they track the values they concern. Appropriate grief is sensitive to whether the loss was real, who was lost, what they meant to the subject. This sensitivity to evaluative particulars is precisely what makes emotions cognitively significant rather than merely reactive.

**The Judgment Theory and Its Problems**

The strict identification of emotions with judgments faces several challenges.

*The phenomenal objection*: Emotions have a distinctive phenomenal character — fear feels like something, and that feeling is not exhausted by any judgment one makes. I can make the judgment "this dog is dangerous" without experiencing fear. Conversely, I can feel fear even when I judge that there is no danger (phobic fear). The judgment and the feeling come apart.

*The recalcitrance objection*: Emotions can persist despite explicit counter-judgments. Knowing that the Müller-Lyer lines are equal does not make them look equal; knowing that your fear of spiders is irrational does not dissolve the fear. If emotions were identical to judgments, explicitly abandoning the judgment should eliminate the emotion.

*The passivity objection*: Emotions are undergone rather than done; they happen to us rather than being things we perform. Judgments are typically active — we deliberate and conclude. But emotion seems passive in a way that makes identifying it with judgment misleading.

**Modified Evaluative Theories**

Contemporary evaluative theories have developed in response to these objections.

Nussbaum acknowledges the phenomenal dimension but argues that the "felt" quality of emotion is compatible with its being a form of judgment — the feeling is the evaluative perception, not a separate accompanying sensation. The phenomenal character of grief is the experience of loss as mattering.

Roberts's "concern-based construals" account emotions as construals — ways of seeing situations in terms of one's concerns — rather than explicit judgments. Construals have both cognitive content and phenomenal character; they are neither pure judgments nor pure feelings but something in between.

Prinz's hybrid account holds that emotions involve both a cognitive appraisal component and a feeling component (a "gut feeling"), where the feeling is a somatic response that is calibrated, through learning, to represent evaluative situations. Emotions are embodied appraisals — not judgments in a purely cognitive sense, but evaluative representations implemented in somatic responses.

**Emotion and Moral Epistemology**

The evaluative theory of emotion has important implications for moral epistemology. If emotions are (or involve) perceptions of evaluative properties — if sympathy is a perception of another's suffering as mattering, if indignation is a perception of wrongdoing — then emotional responses can serve as evidence about moral reality. The moral sentimentalist tradition (Hume, Hutcheson, Smith) drew on this idea; contemporary neo-Aristotelians (Nussbaum, McDowell) have developed it into a sophisticated account of moral perception.
