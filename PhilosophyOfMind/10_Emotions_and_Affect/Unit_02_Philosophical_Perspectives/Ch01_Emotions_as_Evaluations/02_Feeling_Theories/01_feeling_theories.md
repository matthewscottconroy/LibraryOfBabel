# Feeling Theories of Emotion

Feeling theories hold that emotions are, at their core, felt bodily states — that what makes an emotional episode an emotion is its phenomenal character, not its evaluative content, functional role, or cognitive structure. These theories take the "feel" of emotion seriously as central rather than peripheral. They connect naturally to the James-Lange tradition and to broader views about the centrality of phenomenal consciousness to mental life.

**The Phenomenological Priority Claim**

The core of feeling theories is a priority claim: what is distinctive about emotion is how it feels, not what it represents or what it causes. This is why it seems inadequate to analyze emotion purely in functional or cognitive terms. A creature that instantiates all the right functional roles and cognitive connections of fear but lacks any felt quality of apprehension has not fully captured what fear is.

Consider the difference between believing that an exam is tomorrow and feeling anxious about it. Both might involve representations of an upcoming threat to one's goals; but the anxiety is distinguished by its felt quality — the bodily tightness, the intrusive worry, the altered sense of time. That phenomenal quality seems essential to what anxiety is, not merely a symptom of it.

Dualists have always been comfortable saying that this felt quality is a non-physical, irreducibly mental property. Physicalists who want to respect the phenomenological priority claim face the standard challenges: how do physical states have this quality, and why?

**William James Revisited**

James's original theory was, in part, a feeling theory: emotions are the feelings of bodily changes. But James went further than many contemporary feeling theorists in saying that the emotion just is the feeling of bodily changes — there is no residue of emotional state beyond the felt bodily response.

This creates the problem of emotional intentionality: if emotions are just felt bodily states, what explains the fact that fear is about a threat, that grief is about a loss? A bodily state can feel a certain way without being about anything. But emotions seem to be about things in a way that mere bodily states are not.

James's own resolution was to emphasize that the bodily changes are themselves responses to something — they occur because of something perceived or imagined. The emotional feeling is the felt response to the perceived situation. But this response still needs to be characterized as a response to a certain type of situation (a threatening one, a lost one), which seems to reintroduce evaluative content.

**The Role of Phenomenal Character**

One sophisticated version of the feeling approach accepts that emotions have both phenomenal and intentional dimensions, but insists that the phenomenal dimension is primary — that the felt quality cannot be reduced to the evaluative or functional content.

Jesse Prinz's "embodied appraisal" theory holds that emotions are somatic responses that represent evaluative situations in virtue of their causal history and their normal functional role. But Prinz also insists that emotions involve distinctive phenomenal feelings that are not simply reducible to their representational roles. The fear feeling is a distinctive type of experience, not merely a functional state of "treating something as threatening."

This connects to the broader debate about phenomenal consciousness and qualia: if emotions have irreducible phenomenal character, this poses challenges parallel to those raised by color qualia and pain. The hard problem of consciousness applies to emotional experience as well.

**Mood and Undirected Feeling**

Feeling theories face a particular challenge in accommodating the difference between emotions and moods. Moods (anxiety, depression, elation) are affective states that seem to share the phenomenal character of emotions — they have distinctive felt qualities — but lack the object-directedness of emotions. Moods are not about anything in particular; they are global affective tones that color all experience.

Evaluative theories can explain emotions by their intentional content while having to give a separate account of moods. Feeling theories seem better positioned to handle moods as paradigm cases of the felt quality that is primary — but this raises the question of whether emotions and moods are the same kind of thing (felt states) or different (intentional plus felt, versus felt alone).

**Feelings and Self-Knowledge**

A distinctive aspect of feeling theories is their connection to introspection and self-knowledge. If emotions are, essentially, felt states, then the subject has a kind of privileged access to their emotions — the access given by the feeling itself. I know I am afraid partly by feeling afraid; the phenomenal character is immediately given to me in a way that its functional role is not.

This has implications for emotion recognition: detecting your own emotions should be relatively easy (you just feel them) compared to detecting others' emotions (you must infer them from behavior). But the actual psychology is more complex: people can misidentify their emotions, suppress awareness of them, or fail to recognize them altogether. This suggests that emotion has dimensions beyond its immediately felt quality — dimensions that are accessible to investigation but that require work to discern.
