# Further Reading: Emotions as Evaluations

## Primary Sources

**Robert Solomon, *The Passions* (1976)** — Solomon argues that emotions are judgments: to be angry is to judge that one has been wronged; to be afraid is to judge that something is dangerous. Emotions are intentional attitudes with propositional content, not mere feelings. A founding text of the cognitivist tradition in emotion philosophy.

**Martha Nussbaum, *Upheavals of Thought: The Intelligence of Emotions* (2001)** — Nussbaum argues that emotions are evaluative judgments that ascribe value to objects and situations. Her neo-Stoic account holds that emotions are both cognitive and normatively appropriate: grief is correct when one loses something of genuine value. A major philosophical treatment.

**Ronald de Sousa, *The Rationality of Emotion* (1987)** — De Sousa argues that emotions play an essential role in practical rationality, not by being rational or irrational themselves, but by "keying in" to paradigm scenarios that provide the relevance filter for reasoning. Emotions make reasoning tractable by limiting the options we consider.

## Secondary Literature

**Patricia Greenspan, *Emotions and Reasons: An Inquiry into Emotional Justification* (1988)** — Greenspan argues that emotions involve both affective and evaluative components, and that they can be "appropriate" or "inappropriate" relative to the situation — introducing a normative dimension that neither pure feeling nor pure judgment theories accommodate.

**Aaron Ben-Ze'ev, *The Subtlety of Emotions* (2000)** — A comprehensive philosophical treatment of the evaluative, intentional, and motivational dimensions of emotion, with analyses of specific emotions including envy, jealousy, love, and compassion.

**Jesse Prinz, "Are Emotions Feelings?" (2005)** — Examines whether emotions are best understood as feelings (somatic states) or as evaluative judgments or representations, arguing for an embodied representationalist account that avoids the dichotomy.

**Ronnie de Sousa, "The Rationality of Emotion" (1990)** — An influential article surveying the debate about whether emotions can be appropriate or inappropriate, rational or irrational, and what standards of evaluation apply.
