# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Emotions as Evaluations

## Key Arguments

**Solomon's Argument That Emotions Are Judgments**
Robert Solomon argued that emotions are not feelings or bodily states but cognitive judgments—evaluative assessments of the world. Fear is the judgment that something is dangerous; anger is the judgment that one has been wronged. Emotions involve responsibility and rationality because they are forms of judgment: we can ask whether the judgment is accurate, proportionate, and well-founded. This account makes emotions appropriate subjects of normative evaluation and places them firmly within the domain of cognition. It challenges feeling theories (which treat emotions as passive states) by making emotions active assessments.

**Nussbaum's Argument That Emotions Are Intelligent Responses to Value**
Martha Nussbaum argues in Upheavals of Thought that emotions are "intelligent responses to the world"—they involve recognition of important objects, persons, or events and our vulnerability to them. Grief involves recognition that someone important has been lost; compassion involves recognition that another is suffering through no fault of their own. Emotions are evaluations of flourishing-relevant situations and are epistemically valuable: they provide access to evaluative aspects of the world that neutral description misses. Suppressing emotions for the sake of "pure" rationality impoverishes moral cognition.

**The Recalcitrant Emotion Problem**
A recalcitrant emotion is an emotion that persists despite the agent's contrary judgment: I know the airplane is safe, yet I remain afraid. This poses a challenge for cognitive-evaluative theories (Solomon, Nussbaum) that identify emotions with evaluative judgments: if my emotion is the judgment that the plane is dangerous, and I judge it to be safe, how can I both fear and judge it safe? Solutions include: (1) the emotion reflects a lower-level, automatic appraisal that conflicts with the explicit judgment; (2) the emotion is not identical to the explicit judgment but to a different evaluative attitude (de se beliefs, imaginings).

**Brady's Argument That Recalcitrant Emotions Are Rational**
Michael Brady argues that recalcitrant emotions are not simply irrational conflicts: the emotional evaluation can be epistemically appropriate as an alert—drawing attention to evidence that challenges the explicit judgment. Fear in a "safe" airplane may be tracking something that justifies attention even if not panic. The recalcitrant emotion is a kind of evidence-sensitive attention mechanism; it reflects a more fine-grained evidential sensitivity than the conscious, deliberate judgment. This argument rehabilitates recalcitrant emotions as potentially rational and informative.

**The Argument That Emotions Are Perceptions of Value**
Döring, Tappolet, and de Sousa argue that emotions are not judgments (propositional attitudes that can be true or false) but perceptions: specifically, perceptions of evaluative properties (the fearful, the admirable, the shameful). Like perceptual states, emotions have a distinctive phenomenology; they present their objects as having certain evaluative features. Like perceptions, they can be accurate or inaccurate (fitting or unfitting) rather than true or false. This account avoids the recalcitrant emotion problem: perceptions and judgments can conflict without incoherence.

## Core Concepts

**Cognitive-Evaluative Theory**
Cognitive-evaluative theories hold that emotions are constitutively cognitive: they involve the evaluation or appraisal of situations as having certain evaluative properties. On the strongest version (Solomon), emotions just are evaluative judgments; on softer versions (Lazarus, Frijda), emotions involve appraisals that cause and partly constitute the emotion. Cognitive-evaluative theories explain why emotions are appropriate subjects of rational assessment and why they can be educated or revised through changes in belief and value.

**Recalcitrant Emotion**
A recalcitrant emotion is an emotional response that conflicts with and persists alongside the agent's sincere, all-things-considered evaluative judgment about the emotion's object. Classic examples: fear of flying despite knowing the statistics (flying is safer than driving); envy of a friend despite judging envy to be unwarranted; guilt about a past act that one judges, on reflection, to be entirely justified. Recalcitrant emotions are philosophically significant because they test the relationship between emotion and judgment and are used to argue for or against the identification of emotions with evaluative judgments.

**Fitting vs. True**
Philosophers distinguish between emotions that are fitting (appropriately calibrated to their objects—fear of genuine dangers, admiration of genuine achievements) and emotions that are merely felt or experienced. The fitting/unfitting distinction for emotions is analogous to the truth/falsity distinction for beliefs: an emotion can be more or less fitting regardless of its phenomenology. This dimension of emotional evaluation is emphasized by theories that treat emotions as perceptions of value—perceptions can be accurate or inaccurate—and challenges both feeling theories (which have no fitting/unfitting distinction) and pure judgment theories (which conflate fitting with true).

**Emotional Rationality**
Emotional rationality concerns the conditions under which emotional responses are rational, proportionate, and evidence-sensitive. An emotion is rational if it is based on accurate appraisal of the situation, proportionate to the evaluative significance of its object, and responsive to evidence and argument. Nussbaum argues that emotions can be educated—that through reflection, therapy, and moral development, one can make one's emotions more accurate and proportionate. This rehabilitates emotions as rational, cognitive states, not irrational disturbances that reason must overcome.

**Emotions and Moral Perception**
Nussbaum, Iris Murdoch, and others argue that emotional responses are central to moral perception: the capacity to perceive morally significant features of situations—to see that this person is suffering, that this action is cruel, that this outcome is unjust. Purely cognitive moral reasoning that bypasses emotional response misses morally relevant features of situations. Emotions are not obstacles to moral reason but are themselves forms of moral attention and understanding. This view has implications for moral education: developing good emotional responses is essential to moral development.

## Thought Experiments

**The Fearful Flyer**
You are flying on a commercial aircraft. You have read all the statistics: your risk of dying in a plane crash is negligible. You explicitly judge the flight to be safe. Yet you grip the armrest, your heart races, and you are afraid. This recalcitrant fear conflicts with your explicit judgment. Which account handles this best? Solomon's judgment theory struggles (you both judge it safe and fear it as dangerous); the perception theory handles it better (your emotion presents it as dangerous even while your judgment overrides this presentation); de Sousa's emotion theory may invoke different "emotional paradigm scenarios" that the flight activates.

**The Pathetic Fallacy**
The "pathetic fallacy" (Ruskin) is the attribution of emotional properties to inanimate objects: seeing the waves as "angry," the sky as "weeping," the forest as "menacing." Nussbaum argues that this is not a fallacy but a form of imaginative evaluation: seeing the waves as angry involves perceiving them as having a significance for human concerns that they genuinely have (they are powerful and threatening). The thought experiment tests the boundary between emotional projection (attributing emotional properties that aren't there) and emotional perception (accurately tracking evaluative properties that are there).

**The Stoic's Challenge**
The Stoics argued that all emotions (in the passionate sense) are false judgments about value: fear is the judgment that future harm is bad, when in fact only virtue is good; grief is the judgment that loss is bad, when in fact only one's own badness is genuinely bad. On the Stoic view, philosophical progress consists in replacing false emotional evaluations with true ones. The thought experiment asks: if you genuinely came to believe, reflectively and deeply, that your possessions and social relationships were not genuinely valuable—could you feel grief or fear about losing them? The answer tests whether emotions follow beliefs (as Solomon holds) or have some independence from explicit evaluative judgment.
