# Key Thinkers: Emotions as Evaluations

---

**Robert Solomon** (1942–2007)

Solomon is the philosopher most strongly associated with the judgment theory of emotion. In *The Passions* (1976), he argued that emotions are judgments or evaluations — forms of taking a stand on the world — not passive feelings or mere sensations. To be ashamed is to judge that one has done something dishonorable; to love someone is to judge that they are extraordinary. Solomon later moderated this position to accommodate the felt, non-cognitive dimensions of emotion, but his identification of emotions with evaluative intentionality remains foundational.

*Key works:* *The Passions* (1976); *About Love* (1988); *Not Passion's Slave* (2003); *True to Our Feelings* (2007)

---

**Martha Nussbaum** (b. 1947)

Nussbaum develops a neo-Stoic cognitivist account of emotion as evaluative judgment. Emotions are, for her, forms of intelligent perception of the importance of things and people. They involve recognition of the vulnerability of what we value — of being dependent on what we cannot fully control. She applies this account to ethics, arguing that emotions are rational, normatively appropriate responses that should inform rather than distort moral reasoning. Her account has been especially influential in political philosophy and the ethics of care.

*Key works:* *The Fragility of Goodness* (1986); *Upheavals of Thought* (2001); *Hiding from Humanity: Disgust, Shame, and the Law* (2004)

---

**Ronald de Sousa** (b. 1940)

De Sousa's *The Rationality of Emotion* argues that emotions are "intentional in an object-directed way" and that their rationality is not intrinsic but relational: emotions are keyed to "paradigm scenarios" — situations encountered in childhood that set the template for emotional responses. The rationality of an emotion is assessed by whether its paradigm scenario is appropriate to the current situation. De Sousa bridges philosophy of mind, cognitive science, and ethics through his analysis of emotional rationality.

*Key works:* *The Rationality of Emotion* (1987); *Emotional Truth* (2011)

---

**Patricia Greenspan** (b. 1944)

Greenspan argues that a pure judgment theory of emotion is inadequate because it misses the felt, motivational dimension. She develops an "evaluative feeling" account: emotions involve both affective components (feelings with positive or negative valence) and propositional attitudes (evaluative assessments of situations). This account allows emotions to have propositional content and normative appropriateness while preserving their distinctive phenomenology — something pure cognitivist theories sacrifice.

*Key works:* *Emotions and Reasons* (1988); "Emotions, Rationality, and Mind/Body" (2004)
