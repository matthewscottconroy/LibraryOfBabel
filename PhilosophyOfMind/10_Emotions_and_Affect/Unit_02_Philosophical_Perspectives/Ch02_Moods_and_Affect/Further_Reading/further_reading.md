# Further Reading: Moods and Affect

## Primary Sources

**Martin Heidegger, *Being and Time*, Division I, Chapter 5 (1927, trans. 1962)** — Heidegger's analysis of "moods" (Stimmungen) as the fundamental attunements through which Dasein finds itself in the world. Moods are not private inner states but ways in which the world is disclosed: anxiety reveals the groundlessness of existence; boredom reveals the emptiness of engagement. A foundational phenomenological treatment.

**William James, *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, Lectures VI–VII (1902)** — James discusses the "sick soul" and the divided self, with rich phenomenological descriptions of melancholy, anhedonia, and the relationship between affective state and world-perception.

**Edmund Rolls, *Emotion and Decision-Making Explained* (2014)** — Rolls provides a neuroscientific and computational account of how emotions and mood (long-lasting states) influence decision-making through their effects on the representation of expected value.

## Secondary Literature

**Peter Goldie, *The Emotions: A Philosophical Exploration* (2000)** — Goldie argues that emotions involve "feelings towards" — thick, intentional, phenomenologically rich states that cannot be decomposed into pure feeling plus evaluative judgment. Moods are analyzed as diffuse, objectless forms of emotional attunement.

**Aaron Ridley, "Moods and Their Intentionality" (1993)** — Examines whether moods can be genuinely intentional — directed at objects — or whether they are better characterized as non-intentional colorings of experience. Argues for a "phenomenological intentionality" for moods.

**Adam Pautz, "What are the Contents of Experiences?" (2010)** — Examines how the intentional content of experience relates to affective states, raising questions about whether moods have genuine representational content or only phenomenal character.

**Joseph Mikels et al., "Following Your Heart or Your Head: Focusing on Emotions versus Information" (2005)** — Empirical work on how affective states (moods) influence decision-making strategies, showing that positive mood promotes global processing and negative mood promotes local processing.
