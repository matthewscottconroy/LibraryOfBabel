# Key Thinkers: Moods and Affect

---

**Martin Heidegger** (1889–1976)

Heidegger's analysis of moods (Stimmungen) and attunement (Befindlichkeit) in *Being and Time* is the most philosophically sophisticated treatment of mood in the Western tradition. He argues that moods are not subjective inner states imposed on a neutral world but ontological disclosures of the world itself. In anxiety (Angst), for example, everything becomes uncanny — stripped of familiar significance — revealing the groundlessness of everyday existence. Heidegger's account makes moods epistemically fundamental rather than cognitively posterior.

*Key works:* *Being and Time* (1927), Division I; *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (1929/30); *What is Metaphysics?* (1929)

---

**Peter Goldie** (1946–2011)

Goldie's *The Emotions* (2000) is the most influential recent analytic philosophical treatment of emotions and moods. He argues that emotions involve "feelings towards" — intentionally directed phenomenal states — and that neither pure cognitivist nor pure feeling theories capture their richness. His analysis of moods treats them as pervasive colorings of experience — diffuse, objectless, yet evaluatively structured — that influence how one relates to everything rather than to specific objects. Goldie also emphasizes the narrative structure of emotional experience over time.

*Key works:* *The Emotions: A Philosophical Exploration* (2000); *On Personality* (2004); *The Mess Inside: Narrative, Emotion, and the Mind* (2012)

---

**William James** (1842–1910)

James's phenomenology of the emotional and religious life, particularly in *The Varieties of Religious Experience* and his accounts of melancholy and depression, provides rich phenomenological material for analyzing moods. He distinguished "healthy-minded" from "sick-souled" temperaments and described how pervasive negative affect — the "anhedonia" of depression — colors all of experience rather than being directed at specific objects. This phenomenological sensitivity anticipated later work on mood as a global attunement.

*Key works:* *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902); "What Is an Emotion?" (1884); *The Principles of Psychology*, Chapter 25 (1890)

---

**Lisa Feldman Barrett** (b. 1966)

Barrett's "core affect" concept — a basic two-dimensional space of valence (pleasant/unpleasant) and arousal (activated/deactivated) — provides a scientific framework for understanding mood. She distinguishes core affect from the more specific, conceptually constructed emotions, and argues that moods represent prolonged states of core affect not yet attributed to a specific cause or object. Her work connects phenomenology to neuroscience and has implications for clinical psychology and emotion regulation.

*Key works:* *How Emotions Are Made* (2017); "The Ongoing Problem with Basic Emotions" (2006); "Core Affect and the Psychological Construction of Emotion" (2003)
