# Key Thinkers: Empathy and Sympathy

---

**Adam Smith** (1723–1790)

Smith's moral psychology in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* is built on "sympathy" — the imaginative projection of oneself into another's situation to share their feelings and assess the appropriateness of their responses. Smith distinguished sympathy from merely feeling what another feels (emotional contagion), emphasizing the role of imagination. His "impartial spectator" — an imagined idealized observer — serves as the standard for moral judgment. Smith's account anticipates modern debates about empathy, perspective-taking, and the relationship between affective and cognitive empathy.

*Key works:* *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759); *The Wealth of Nations* (1776)

---

**Edith Stein** (1891–1942)

Stein's phenomenological analysis of empathy (Einfühlung) in her 1917 doctoral dissertation remains the most rigorous treatment. She distinguishes empathy from projection, emotional contagion, and mere inference, arguing that in empathy we genuinely "co-experience" the other's experience — not as our own, but as theirs. We have a first-person access to their third-person experiential states. Stein situates empathy within Husserl's broader framework of intersubjectivity.

*Key works:* *On the Problem of Empathy* (1917/1964); *Finite and Eternal Being* (1936/2002)

---

**Simon Baron-Cohen** (b. 1958)

Baron-Cohen is a psychologist who has studied empathy as a cognitive and affective capacity, particularly its impairment in autism spectrum conditions and its weaponization in psychopathy. His "empathy spectrum" model distinguishes cognitive empathy (understanding others' mental states) from affective empathy (feeling what others feel). His work on "systemizing" and "empathizing" cognitive styles has been influential, though contested, in psychology and philosophy.

*Key works:* *Mindblindness* (1995); *The Essential Difference* (2003); *Zero Degrees of Empathy* (2011)

---

**Frans de Waal** (b. 1948)

De Waal is a primatologist who has argued extensively that empathy has deep evolutionary roots visible in non-human primates. His "Russian doll" model of empathy presents it as having concentric layers: emotional contagion (the oldest layer), sympathetic concern, and perspective-taking (the more recently evolved outer layer). De Waal uses comparative evidence to argue that human moral emotions are continuous with, not discontinuous from, primate social emotions.

*Key works:* *Good Natured: The Origins of Right and Wrong in Humans and Other Animals* (1996); *Primates and Philosophers* (2006); *The Age of Empathy* (2009)

---

**Jesse Prinz** (b. 1971)

Prinz is a prominent critic of empathy as a foundation for morality. He argues that empathy is systematically biased — we empathize more readily with attractive, familiar, proximate individuals — making it an unreliable guide to impartial moral concern. He argues that sympathy (concern for others' welfare without emotional resonance) is a better basis for moral motivation, and that emotional contagion and empathy should be distinguished both psychologically and normatively.

*Key works:* *Against Empathy* (2011, article); *The Emotional Construction of Morals* (2007); "Is Empathy Necessary for Morality?" (2011)
