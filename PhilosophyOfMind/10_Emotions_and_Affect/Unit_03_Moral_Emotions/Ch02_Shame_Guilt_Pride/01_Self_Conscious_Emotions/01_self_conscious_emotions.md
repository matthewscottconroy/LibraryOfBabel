# Self-Conscious Emotions

Shame, guilt, pride, embarrassment, and related emotions are sometimes grouped as "self-conscious emotions" because they essentially involve self-evaluation. These emotions require a kind of reflective self-awareness — the capacity to represent oneself as an object of evaluation — that is absent in more basic emotions like fear and disgust. They are also deeply social: they typically arise in the context of actual or imagined evaluation by others, and they play a central role in the regulation of social behavior and moral life.

**The Structure of Self-Conscious Emotions**

Self-conscious emotions share a common structure: they involve the self being evaluated, or representing itself as evaluated, against a standard (one's own or others'). But they differ in the aspects of self that are evaluated and in how the evaluation is registered.

*Shame* involves a global, negative evaluation of the self — feeling that one is fundamentally defective, inferior, or unworthy. Shame tends to produce the desire to hide or disappear, to escape from social gaze. The body posture associated with shame is characteristic: hunched, downcast, shrunken. Shame is painful because it concerns the self as a whole, not just a specific action.

*Guilt* involves a negative evaluation of a specific action or behavior, not of the self as a whole. Feeling guilty does not imply feeling that one is a bad person; it implies feeling that one has done something bad. Guilt is associated with reparative motivation — the desire to make amends, apologize, or correct the wrong. This forward-looking, reparative character distinguishes guilt from shame.

*Pride* involves a positive evaluation of the self, either globally (authentic pride: feeling that one is capable and worthy) or in relation to a specific achievement (hubristic pride: feeling superior to others on the basis of an outcome). Positive psychologists distinguish the healthy, growth-oriented form of pride from the self-aggrandizing, comparative form.

**Development and the Self**

Self-conscious emotions emerge developmentally later than basic emotions, and their emergence tracks the development of self-concept. Before children develop a stable self-concept (typically around 18 months), they cannot experience shame or guilt — there is no "self" to be ashamed of or to feel guilty for an action. The emergence of self-conscious emotions coincides with the emergence of mirror self-recognition and other indicators of self-awareness.

June Price Tangney's research has systematically studied the development and consequences of shame and guilt across the lifespan. Her findings consistently support the distinction between shame and guilt: shame-prone individuals tend to externalize blame, react with anger to perceived criticisms, and show poorer psychological adjustment. Guilt-prone individuals tend to take responsibility, engage in reparative behavior, and show better psychological outcomes.

**Social Functions**

Self-conscious emotions serve critical social and moral functions. Shame and guilt enforce social norms by making norm violations emotionally costly; pride reinforces norm compliance and achievement. This functional role connects self-conscious emotions to moral learning and internalization.

Cultures vary in their relative emphasis on shame-based versus guilt-based moral regulation. "Honor cultures" (historically associated with the Mediterranean, Middle East, and parts of East Asia) rely more heavily on shame — public evaluation and the maintenance of face — as a social regulator. "Guilt cultures" (more common in Protestant Northern Europe and North America) rely more on internalized guilt. These cultural differences affect not just moral behavior but the phenomenology of moral emotion: what it feels like to violate a norm differs across cultural contexts.

**Shame and Trauma**

The relationship between shame and psychological trauma is particularly important. Survivors of sexual assault, abuse, and other forms of interpersonal violation often experience intense shame — not guilt (for something they did) but shame (for something done to them). This shame is paradoxical and deeply harmful: it blames the victim rather than the perpetrator.

Contemporary clinical psychology recognizes that shame is a central feature of many forms of psychopathology: depression, social anxiety, borderline personality, post-traumatic stress. Therapeutic approaches that directly address shame — distinguishing it from appropriate guilt, challenging shame-inducing self-representations — have become increasingly important.

The philosophical significance of this clinical picture: shame and guilt, while both involving self-evaluation, have dramatically different moral and psychological structures. Conflating them — or treating all negative self-evaluation as functionally equivalent — misses what is distinctive and destructive about the specific character of shame.
