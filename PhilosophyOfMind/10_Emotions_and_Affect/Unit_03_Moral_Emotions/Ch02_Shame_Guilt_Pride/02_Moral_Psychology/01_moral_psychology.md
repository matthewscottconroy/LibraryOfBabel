# Moral Psychology and the Emotions

Moral psychology studies how people actually form moral judgments, develop moral character, and regulate moral behavior — as opposed to how they should, which is the domain of normative ethics. Emotion is central to moral psychology, and the relationship between emotional and rational processes in moral life is one of the discipline's central questions.

**The Rationalist and Sentimentalist Traditions**

The history of moral psychology divides between rationalist and sentimentalist traditions. For rationalists (Kant, the Stoics, much of analytic ethics), moral judgment is fundamentally a product of reason — the recognition of moral principles or obligations through rational faculties. Emotions may accompany moral life but are not its cognitive core.

For sentimentalists (Hume, Smith, Hutcheson), moral judgment is fundamentally a product of emotional response — the sentiment of approval or disapproval, the feeling of benevolence or resentment. Reason, on Hume's view, is the "slave of the passions" — it tells us how to achieve our goals, but the goals themselves, including moral ones, are set by feeling.

Contemporary moral psychology has provided empirical resources for this debate. The evidence suggests that both traditions have captured something important: moral judgment involves both emotional and cognitive processing, and the relative contribution of each depends on the type of moral situation.

**Haidt's Social Intuitionist Model**

Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionist model has been enormously influential in contemporary moral psychology. Haidt argues that moral judgments are primarily products of rapid, intuitive, emotional processes — moral intuitions — and that moral reasoning is largely post-hoc rationalization of those intuitions.

Haidt's evidence: when asked to justify their moral judgments on "dumbfounding" scenarios (harmless but disgusting actions that people judge wrong without being able to articulate why), people stick to their judgments even when their stated reasons are refuted. People also judge quickly even when reasoning is incapacitated. And the pattern of moral judgments across scenarios correlates better with emotional sensitivity than with explicit moral principles.

The philosophical implication: if Haidt is right, most moral reasoning is rationalization rather than genuine cognition. We decide emotionally, then reason to justify the decision. This poses serious challenges to the view that moral judgments are tracked by moral reasoning.

**Critiques of Haidt**

Haidt's model has been extensively criticized.

Peter Railton and others have argued that moral intuitions are themselves a form of quick, reliable moral knowledge — analogous to expert perception in other domains. Expert chess players don't laboriously reason through each position; they perceive patterns and respond rapidly. Moral intuitions may similarly be the product of accumulated moral learning, not mere emotional noise.

This "expertise" account of moral intuition redeems the epistemic status of fast, emotionally-tinged moral responses without reducing them to irrational bias. The emotional coloring of moral intuitions may be partly what makes them responsive to morally relevant features — features that matter because of their significance for people's wellbeing and dignity.

Joshua Greene's work distinguishes two types of moral dilemmas and argues that different types involve different cognitive systems. "Personal" moral dilemmas (involving direct harm to an identified individual) are processed primarily by emotional systems (medial prefrontal cortex, amygdala); "impersonal" dilemmas (involving abstract trade-offs) engage more deliberative reasoning. Greene controversially argues that the deontological responses characteristic of personal dilemmas reflect emotional noise rather than genuine moral insight — that utilitarian calculation, when deliberative processing takes over, provides better moral guidance.

This neural dual-process account has been extensively debated: is the emotional response to personal harm really noise, or is it tracking something morally important (the wrongness of treating persons as means to ends) that the utilitarian calculation misses?

**Character and Virtue**

Moral psychology is also concerned with character — the stable dispositional properties that incline a person toward certain patterns of behavior. The Aristotelian tradition holds that virtue is the central concept of moral psychology: the good person is not one who follows the right rules but one who has the right character — who perceives situations correctly, responds emotionally appropriately, and acts from settled dispositions of excellence.

This virtue-theoretic picture connects moral psychology to the broader psychology of character. There has been substantial debate about whether stable character traits exist or whether behavior is primarily situationally determined (Doris, Harman). The situationist challenge to virtue ethics has generated a rich literature on the relationship between character, situation, and moral consistency.

The emerging picture is nuanced: character traits exist but are more context-sensitive than traditional virtue ethics assumes; moral development involves both the acquisition of stable dispositions and the development of the practical wisdom to recognize when and how to apply them.
