# Further Reading: Shame, Guilt, and Pride

## Primary Sources

**Helen Rawlings Lynd, *On Shame and the Search for Identity* (1958)** — An early phenomenological and psychological analysis of shame, arguing that shame — unlike guilt — involves a global threat to self-identity rather than a local judgment about a specific action.

**Herbert Morris, "Guilt and Shame" (1976)** — Morris distinguishes guilt (a response to having violated a moral norm) from shame (a response to having failed to live up to a standard of who one is or wants to be). Foundational for the philosophical analysis of the two emotions.

**Michael Lewis, *Shame: The Exposed Self* (1992)** — A developmental psychologist's account of shame and related self-conscious emotions, tracing their development from infancy and examining the neurological and behavioral correlates of shame.

## Secondary Literature

**Bernard Williams, *Shame and Necessity* (1993)** — Williams argues that the ancient Greek ethics of shame is, in many respects, more adequate than modern guilt-based ethics. He develops an account of shame as a response to the internal critic (the "internalized other") and connects it to integrity, character, and the self.

**June Tangney and Ronda Dearing, *Shame and Guilt* (2002)** — A comprehensive empirical and theoretical treatment of shame and guilt, distinguishing their phenomenology, moral psychology, and developmental trajectories, and presenting evidence that shame — unlike guilt — is associated with destructive outcomes.

**Gabriele Taylor, *Pride, Shame, and Guilt: Emotions of Self-Assessment* (1985)** — Taylor's philosophical analysis of the three self-conscious emotions, arguing that they are all forms of "self-assessment" that involve a comparison between one's actual and ideal or standard self.

**Martha Nussbaum, *Hiding from Humanity: Disgust, Shame, and the Law* (2004)** — Nussbaum argues that shame and disgust, unlike guilt, are inappropriate bases for criminal law and moral criticism because they involve projective attitudes toward whole persons rather than specific harmful actions.
