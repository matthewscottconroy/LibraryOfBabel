# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Shame, Guilt, and Pride

## Key Arguments

**Tangney's Empirical Argument for the Shame-Guilt Distinction**
June Price Tangney argues that shame and guilt are phenomenologically and functionally distinct, despite both being negative self-conscious emotions. Shame involves a painful focus on the whole self ("I am bad"), activates desires to hide or escape, and tends to elicit denial and externalization rather than repair. Guilt involves a focus on specific behavior ("I did a bad thing"), motivates repair (apologizing, making amends), and is less likely to produce destructive defensive responses. Tangney's research shows that guilt-proneness predicts prosocial behavior and psychological health, while shame-proneness predicts aggression, psychopathology, and less empathy.

**Sartre's Argument That Shame Requires Another's Gaze**
Sartre argues in Being and Nothingness that shame is fundamentally an experience of being seen—it arises from the look of another who fixes me as an object, reducing me from free subjectivity to a thing with determined properties. "I am ashamed of myself before the other." The shame experience reveals both my freedom (I can be seen as this particular being) and my vulnerability to the other's objectifying gaze. This social-ontological account makes shame essentially intersubjective, not merely self-referential, and grounds the social function of shame as a mechanism of social regulation.

**Williams's Distinction Between Guilt and Shame in Moral Psychology**
Bernard Williams argues in Shame and Necessity that guilt is an essentially moral emotion tied to having violated rules or harmed others, while shame is a deeper ethical emotion tied to the integrity of one's whole self and identity. Modernity has focused on guilt (and the moral framework of obligation and blame) at the expense of shame—but shame is not merely a primitive, pre-moral emotion. Shame is responsive to the ideal self one aspires to be; it is an important source of moral motivation that cannot be reduced to guilt about rule violations. Williams rehabilitates shame as morally important, not merely an instrument of social control.

**Taylor's Account of Pride as Self-Assessment**
Gabriele Taylor argues that pride is an emotion of self-assessment: pride occurs when one views one's own achievement, trait, or possession positively and takes responsibility for it or identifies with it. Pride requires both that the object of pride be excellent (or believed to be so) and that the subject stand in the right relationship to it (it is one's own doing, trait, or group membership). The relationship between pride and identity is central: pride reinforces and expresses one's self-conception. Taylor distinguishes appropriate from inappropriate pride (vanity, arrogance) by whether the self-assessment is accurate.

**The Moral Psychology of Self-Conscious Emotions**
Self-conscious emotions (shame, guilt, pride, embarrassment) differ from basic emotions in requiring self-evaluative cognition: they are aroused by the evaluation of the self against standards, rules, and goals. Mascolo and Fischer argue that self-conscious emotions are fundamentally social: they arise in the context of social evaluation and regulate self-presentation and conformity to social norms. The moral significance of self-conscious emotions lies in their role in motivating prosocial behavior (guilt motivates repair; pride reinforces achievement) and deterring antisocial behavior (shame deters norm violations—but may also produce defensive aggression when triggered excessively).

## Core Concepts

**Shame**
Shame is a self-conscious emotion involving the painful awareness of the self as wholly inadequate, bad, or inferior in the eyes of others (or in one's own reflective self-assessment from an imagined external perspective). Shame focuses on the whole self, not just on specific behaviors: "I am a bad person." Phenomenologically, shame involves a desire to hide, disappear, or escape. Functionally, shame can motivate defensive responses (denial, externalization, aggression) rather than constructive repair. Sartre's phenomenological account emphasizes shame's intersubjective structure: it is fundamentally constituted by the experience of being seen by another.

**Guilt**
Guilt is a self-conscious emotion involving the painful awareness of having done something wrong—having violated a moral rule, harmed another, or acted against one's values. Guilt focuses on specific behavior, not on the whole self: "I did a bad thing." Phenomenologically, guilt involves feelings of regret, remorse, and a desire to repair the harm done. Functionally, guilt motivates prosocial behavior: apologizing, making amends, changing behavior. The distinction between shame and guilt is morally important because they have different adaptive and maladaptive consequences for moral behavior.

**Pride**
Pride is a self-conscious emotion involving a positive evaluation of the self in connection with an achievement, trait, or possession that is taken to reflect credit on the self. Pride is associated with social recognition, enhanced self-esteem, and motivation to repeat or extend the achievement. It can be "authentic" (based on genuine achievement and accurate self-assessment) or "hubristic" (based on inflated self-image and grandiosity). Taylor distinguishes appropriate pride from vanity and arrogance; Tangney's research shows that authentic pride predicts motivation and prosocial behavior, while hubristic pride predicts narcissism and aggression.

**The Shame-Guilt Distinction (Tangney)**
Tangney's empirical distinction between shame and guilt rests on: (1) the phenomenological focus (self vs. behavior); (2) the motivational consequences (hide/escape vs. repair); (3) the psychological correlates (psychopathology vs. prosocial behavior); (4) the other-orientation (shame produces less empathy and more anger; guilt produces more empathy and apology). This distinction has been supported by self-report measures (Test of Self-Conscious Affect, TOSCA) and behavioral studies. The main challenge is that shame and guilt are often co-experienced and that the distinction may not be as sharp in every case.

**Self-Conscious Emotions**
Self-conscious emotions (Tracy and Robins; Lewis) are emotions that require self-evaluative cognition: they arise from a comparison of the self (one's actions, traits, or outcomes) against standards, rules, or goals, and they involve the self as their primary object. They include shame, guilt, pride, embarrassment, envy, and jealousy. Self-conscious emotions are distinguished from basic emotions (fear, anger, sadness) in requiring a more sophisticated cognitive apparatus—a stable self-concept and the ability to assess the self against standards—and in having a fundamentally social structure.

## Thought Experiments

**The Stolen Bicycle**
Consider two people who have stolen a bicycle: one feels primarily shame (they see themselves as a thief—as a bad person—and try to avoid thinking about it), the other feels primarily guilt (they regret stealing the bicycle, want to return it, and feel bad about what they did). Tangney's research predicts that the guilt-focused response is more likely to lead to repair behavior (returning the bicycle) while the shame-focused response is more likely to lead to avoidance and rationalization. The case tests whether the theoretical distinction between shame and guilt has practical moral consequences.

**Sartre's Look in the Keyhole**
Sartre describes a man spying through a keyhole who is suddenly caught—hears footsteps, realizes he is being observed. At the moment of being seen, his subjective freedom collapses; he is fixed as "the Peeping Tom"—a thing with that property. Shame arises from being-for-others: the other's gaze constitutes me as an object with determinate, mortifying properties. The thought experiment reveals that shame is not merely a feeling about oneself but a fundamental structure of intersubjective existence: being with others always involves the risk of being fixed and defined by the other's gaze.

**Williams's Heroic Shame**
Williams imagines a Greek hero who is captured and must choose between death and surrendering vital information. The hero who chooses death over dishonor does not primarily fear violating a rule (guilt framework) but cannot live with the self he would become—the coward, the traitor. His motivation is shame-based: the intolerable image of himself that would result from the shameful act. Williams argues that this heroic shame-motivation is morally real and important, not merely a primitive precursor to guilt-based morality. The thought experiment rehabilitates shame as a sophisticated moral motivation.
