# Key Thinkers: Shame, Guilt, and Pride

---

**Bernard Williams** (1929–2003)

Williams's *Shame and Necessity* reintroduces shame as a philosophically serious emotion against the dominant modern tendency to regard guilt as the paradigmatic moral emotion. He argues that shame — which involves the internalized gaze of an "other" witnessing one's inadequacy — is in many respects a more adequate response to moral failure than guilt, because it reaches one's character, not just one's actions. Williams connects this to his broader critique of impartial, duty-based moral theory and his defense of agent-relative integrity.

*Key works:* *Shame and Necessity* (1993); *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* (1985); *Moral Luck* (1981)

---

**Gabriele Taylor** (b. 1926)

Taylor's *Pride, Shame, and Guilt* (1985) provides the most systematic philosophical analysis of the three self-conscious moral emotions. She argues that all three involve a form of "self-assessment" — comparison of one's actual self with some standard or ideal — and that they differ in what is assessed and the nature of the comparison. Pride involves favorable self-assessment; shame involves the experience of being seen as inadequate by an internal or external observer; guilt involves the recognition that one has acted wrongly.

*Key works:* *Pride, Shame, and Guilt: Emotions of Self-Assessment* (1985)

---

**Martha Nussbaum** (b. 1947)

Nussbaum has written extensively on the moral psychology and legal implications of shame, guilt, and disgust. In *Hiding from Humanity* (2004), she argues that while guilt (directed at specific harmful actions) has a legitimate role in law and morality, shame (directed at whole persons as flawed) and disgust (involving projective fantasies of contamination) do not. Her neo-Stoic account holds that appropriate emotions are truth-tracking and impartial; shame and disgust typically fail this standard.

*Key works:* *Upheavals of Thought* (2001); *Hiding from Humanity* (2004); *The Monarchy of Fear* (2018)

---

**June Price Tangney** (b. 1958)

Tangney is the empirical psychologist who has done the most systematic research on shame and guilt, developing reliable measures and examining their psychological consequences. Her research challenges the common assumption that shame is morally productive: she finds that shame — unlike guilt — is associated with avoidance, externalization, anger, and increased destructive behavior, while guilt is associated with reparative motivation and empathy. This empirical work has important implications for philosophical accounts of the moral status of these emotions.

*Key works:* *Shame and Guilt* (2002, with Dearing); "Moral Affect: The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly" (1991); *Handbook of Self and Identity* (2005, ed. with Leary)

---

**Michael Lewis** (b. 1937)

Lewis is a developmental psychologist who placed the "self-conscious emotions" — shame, guilt, pride, embarrassment, and jealousy — at the center of moral psychology. He traced their development from infancy, arguing that they require self-representation (a sense of oneself as an object of evaluation), attribution of responsibility, and internalized standards. His work shows that shame involves global, stable self-attribution ("I am bad") while guilt involves specific, unstable attribution ("I did something bad") — a distinction with profound implications for moral development.

*Key works:* *Shame: The Exposed Self* (1992); *Self-Conscious Emotions: The Psychology of Shame, Guilt, Embarrassment, and Pride* (ed., 1995)
