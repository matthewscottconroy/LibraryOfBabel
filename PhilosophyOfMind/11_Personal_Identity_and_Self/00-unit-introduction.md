# Unit Introduction: Personal Identity and the Self

## Central Questions

Personal identity asks what makes a person the same person over time. You are (presumably) the same person as the child in your earliest memory, even though almost every cell in your body has been replaced, your personality has changed dramatically, and your beliefs and desires are almost entirely different. What grounds this continuity? And if we can specify what grounds it, we can ask whether it is what *matters* — whether the persistence of personal identity across time is what we care about when we care about our future selves.

Derek Parfit's work in *Reasons and Persons* (1984) transformed the field. Parfit argued that personal identity consists in psychological continuity — overlapping chains of psychological connections (memories, beliefs, intentions, personality traits) — and that psychological continuity is what matters for our practical attitudes toward the future. But Parfit went further: he argued that psychological continuity is *not* the same as personal identity, and that this has revolutionary consequences. In fission cases — where one person's psychology is transplanted into two bodies — there is maximal psychological continuity but no personal identity (since identity requires uniqueness). If what matters is psychological continuity, and identity comes apart from continuity in fission cases, then identity is not what matters. This conclusion has striking implications for self-interest, ethics, and the nature of persons.

A second set of questions concerns the *self* rather than personal identity over time. Is the self a thing — an ego, a Cartesian subject, a core of personality — or is it a construction, a narrative, an illusion? Hume's bundle theory denied that there is any impression of a simple, persisting self over and above the bundle of perceptions. Kant's transcendental unity of apperception posited a formal self as a condition of experience without specifying a substantive self. Contemporary neuroscience and psychology have largely confirmed the Humean insight: the sense of a unified, continuous self is an active construction, not a readout of a pre-existing reality.

## Major Positions and Debates

### Psychological Continuity: Locke and Parfit

Locke introduced the psychological continuity view in the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (Book II, Chapter 27): what makes a future person me is that they can remember my experiences. Memory continuity is the criterion of personal identity. This view has the virtue of explaining why amnesia threatens the self, why we care about our future selves in proportion to our psychological connections to them, and why the survival of one's body in a permanent vegetative state is not the survival of oneself.

Parfit refined Locke's view: the relevant relation is not memory per se (this faces the circularity objection: to remember an experience is to remember it as *mine*, which presupposes personal identity) but *psychological connectedness and continuity*: overlapping chains of connections between mental states, including not just memories but beliefs, intentions, personality traits, and desires. Parfit also introduced the *no-branching* clause: psychological continuity grounds personal identity only if it holds uniquely. In cases of fission, neither branch is the original person, even though both are psychologically continuous with the original.

Parfit's most striking conclusion is that what matters in survival is not personal identity but psychological continuity. If I teleport to Mars (a process that destroys my body and creates a replica with all my memories), the replica is psychologically continuous with me but is not (strictly speaking) identical to me. Parfit argues that this should not concern me, because psychological continuity is all that matters for our practical attitudes toward the future.

### Animalism

Eric Olson's *animalism* holds that persons are essentially animals: we are fundamentally biological organisms, not bundles of psychological properties. The psychological continuity view gets the wrong answer in many cases: if my brain were transplanted into another body, I would follow my brain; but if an exact psychological duplicate of me were created in another body, the duplicate would not be me. Animalism provides a clean answer: you go where your organism goes.

Animalism faces the *thinking animal problem*: if I am an animal, and the animal sitting in my chair thinking will persist after my death (as a living but non-psychological organism), then there seem to be *two* thinking beings right now — me and my organism — sharing the same thoughts. This seems too many thinkers. Olson's response is to accept that personal identity is simply continuous with animal identity; our psychological properties are features of the animal rather than the grounds of our identity.

### Narrative Accounts

Narrative accounts (Schechtman, MacIntyre, Taylor) hold that the self is constituted by a self-narrative — the story one tells about one's life that gives it coherence and meaning. Personal identity over time consists in the narrative unity of a life: the integration of past, present, and anticipated future into a coherent story.

Narrative accounts are attractive because they capture the phenomenology of self-understanding: we understand ourselves as characters in stories. But they face questions about pathological cases (does the person with severe dementia cease to have a self as their narrative coherence deteriorates?) and about the relationship between narrative and metaphysical identity (is the narrative constitutive of identity or a way of representing a prior fact about identity?).

### No-Self Views: Buddhism and Neuroscience

The Buddhist *anattā* (no-self) doctrine holds that what we call the self is a conventional designation applied to a stream of causally connected mental events; there is no substantial, persisting self over and above the stream. This is strikingly similar to Hume's bundle theory and to contemporary neuroscientific accounts. Thomas Metzinger's *Being No One* (2003) argues that the phenomenal self-model — the brain's representation of itself as a subject — is a representation that models a self that does not exist as it is represented. There is no such entity as the *self*; there are only neural processes that model the organism as having a unified self.

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**John Locke** introduced the memory criterion of personal identity. His distinction between the identity of a *substance* (soul) and the identity of a *person* (consciousness) allowed for the possibility that personal identity does not require the persistence of a soul.

**Derek Parfit** is the most important philosopher of personal identity of the twentieth century. *Reasons and Persons* (1984) Part III is essential reading. Parfit's arguments establish that identity is not what matters, that self-interest as a fundamental motivation is undermined by his analysis, and that Buddhist-like conclusions follow from careful philosophical analysis of ordinary cases.

**Eric Olson** (*The Human Animal*, 1997) is the main defender of animalism and the most careful critic of psychological continuity views.

**Marya Schechtman** (*The Constitution of Selves*, 1996) developed the narrative account of personal identity, arguing that having a personal identity requires constructing a self-narrative that organizes one's life.

**Thomas Metzinger** (*Being No One*, 2003) argues from neuroscience and phenomenology that the self is a model — the phenomenal self-model — and that selves do not exist in the way they represent themselves as existing.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984), Part III**
The most important work in personal identity theory. Parfit's thought experiments (fission, teleportation, brain transplant) are carefully constructed, and his conclusion — that identity is not what matters — is genuinely revolutionary. Even philosophers who reject his conclusion must engage carefully with his arguments.

**2. Olson, *The Human Animal* (1997)**
The definitive statement of animalism. Olson's arguments against psychological continuity views are sharp, and his positive account of the relationship between persons and animals is carefully worked out.

**3. Schechtman, *The Constitution of Selves* (1996)**
The most developed philosophical treatment of the narrative approach. Schechtman argues that the psychological continuity view fails because it applies the wrong criterion (identity over time) to the right phenomenon (personal identity as self-constitution).

**4. Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book II, Chapter 27**
The founding document of the psychological continuity tradition. Locke's distinction between substance identity and personal identity, and his account of the role of consciousness and memory, remain fundamental.

**5. Metzinger, *Being No One* (2003) or *The Ego Tunnel* (2009, more accessible)**
Metzinger's account of the phenomenal self-model provides the most detailed neuroscientific and phenomenological argument for a no-self view. *The Ego Tunnel* is the more accessible version; *Being No One* is comprehensive.

## Open Questions

**1. Does Parfit's conclusion follow from his premises?**
Parfit argues that if psychological continuity is what grounds personal identity, and if continuity can obtain without identity (in fission cases), then identity is not what matters — continuity is. Critics argue that there are other ways to respond to fission cases that preserve the importance of identity. Whether Parfit's conclusion is genuinely established by his arguments is contested.

**2. Is animalism compatible with what we care about in survival?**
Olson's animalism seems to imply that what matters in survival is the persistence of our animal body, even in a permanent vegetative state. Most people find this counterintuitive: survival without psychological continuity seems like no survival at all. Whether animalism can be modified to accommodate this intuition, or whether the intuition should be revised, is open.

**3. Can the narrative self be naturalized?**
Narrative accounts of personal identity are attractive but philosophically underdeveloped. What exactly is a narrative? What makes a narrative good or bad? How do we account for cases in which a narrative is incoherent, false, or pathological? Whether a naturalistic account of narrative identity is possible is not clear.

**4. What do split-brain cases show about personal identity?**
Patients with severed corpus callosum display what looks like two independent streams of consciousness: the left and right hemispheres can have conflicting beliefs and desires. Do these patients have one self or two? The empirical facts are complicated, and the philosophical implications depend on contested assumptions about what personal identity requires.

**5. Is there a minimal self?**
Phenomenologists distinguish between the *narrative self* (the constructed, temporally extended self of personal history) and the *minimal self* (the immediate sense of being a subject of experience, without any narrative content). Is the minimal self a genuine phenomenon, or is it itself a construction? And if there is a minimal self, what is it — an aspect of phenomenal experience, a cognitive model, or something else?
