# Psychological Continuity Theories

Psychological continuity theories hold that personal identity over time consists in continuity of psychological states — primarily memories, personality, beliefs, and desires, connected by appropriate causal relationships. These theories have been developed from Locke's memory theory through Hume's bundle theory to Parfit's sophisticated account. They are the most widely defended theories in contemporary analytic philosophy.

**Locke's Memory Theory**

John Locke proposed in the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* that personal identity consists in the continuity of consciousness, specifically memory. I am the same person as the person who did something yesterday if I can remember doing it. Memory is the relation that ties earlier and later stages of a person together.

Locke's motivation was partly theological: he wanted to explain how personal identity could persist through the soul's separation from the body at death, so that the soul could be justly rewarded or punished for the deeds of the earthly life. The immaterial soul was not the basis of identity; consciousness — specifically memory — was.

As Reid pointed out, the memory criterion as stated faces a transitivity problem: if memory can chain past to present, the chain might fail to be transitive (I remember X, X remembered Y, but I don't remember Y) in ways that violate the logic of identity. This motivated more sophisticated formulations in terms of memory chains and causal continuity.

**Hume's Bundle Theory**

Hume argued that the self is nothing but a bundle of perceptions — there is no persistent self underlying or unifying the bundle. When he looked inward, Hume reported finding only particular perceptions, one after another, but no "self" that had them. The appearance of personal identity is a fiction that the imagination imposes on the bundle, by mistaking the close connection and smooth transitions between perceptions for strict identity.

Hume's view is strikingly deflationary: there is no metaphysical self, only a sequence of mental states that we narrativize into a unified person. This connects to Buddhist views of no-self (anattā) and to contemporary deflationalist positions like Parfit's.

The challenge for Hume is to explain how the bundle is unified — what makes a collection of perceptions the bundle of a single person rather than a disordered collection. Hume acknowledged this difficulty in an appendix to the *Treatise* and confessed that he could not solve it.

**Parfit's Account**

Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* developed the most sophisticated contemporary psychological continuity theory. Parfit distinguishes between psychological connectedness (direct overlapping connections: this memory is a memory of that experience) and psychological continuity (chains of overlapping connectedness: A is connected to B, B is connected to C, though A and C may not be directly connected).

Parfit argues that psychological continuity, realized in the right way (by normal brain processes or their functional equivalents), is the basis of personal identity. But he also argues that personal identity is not what matters: what we actually care about is psychological continuity, and this can hold to varying degrees. Personal identity is all-or-nothing; psychological continuity is a matter of degree. Therefore personal identity and what matters can come apart.

The practical upshot: in fission cases (where a person branches into two psychologically continuous successors), strict identity fails (a person cannot be identical to two distinct people), but psychological continuity holds to both successors. Parfit argues this means both successors have what matters in survival — each is as good as survival — even though strictly speaking neither is identical to the original.

**The Branching Problem**

Parfit's fission case illustrates the "branching problem" for psychological continuity theories. If personal identity consists in psychological continuity, and psychological continuity can "branch" (one person can have two or more psychologically continuous successors), then personal identity cannot be the same as psychological continuity — since identity is one-one, not one-many.

Parfit's resolution: abandon the view that personal identity is what matters. Others have tried different resolutions: requiring uniqueness as a condition on identity (you are the person at the end only if there is no branching), or arguing that branching cases always involve the original person's death and the creation of two new people.

**Psychological Continuity and Moral Responsibility**

The psychological continuity theory has important implications for moral responsibility. On this view, I am responsible for past actions to the extent that I am psychologically continuous with the person who performed them. But psychological continuity is a matter of degree — I am less psychologically continuous with my six-year-old self than with myself yesterday. Does this mean my responsibility diminishes with the degree of continuity?

This seems counterintuitive for severe past crimes — we typically hold people responsible for actions that are many decades in the past even if they have changed significantly. But it might support more nuanced thinking about rehabilitation and recidivism: a person who has genuinely and deeply changed might have reduced responsibility for past actions precisely because the psychological continuity is attenuated.
