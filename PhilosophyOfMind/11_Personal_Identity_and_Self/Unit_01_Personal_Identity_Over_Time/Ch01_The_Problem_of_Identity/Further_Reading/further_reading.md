# Further Reading: The Problem of Identity

## Primary Sources

**John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book II, Ch. 27 (1689)** — Locke's foundational account of personal identity as constituted by memory and consciousness rather than substance. His distinction between the identity of a man (biological continuity) and a person (psychological continuity) set the terms for all subsequent debate.

**David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book I, Part IV, Sec. 6 (1739)** — Hume's famous "bundle theory" denying any impression of a unified self; he argues the self is nothing but a bundle of perceptions in constant flux. This skeptical position remains a touchstone for no-self views and Buddhist-friendly interpretations.

**Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons*, Part III (1984)** — The most influential modern treatment of personal identity, arguing via fission cases and teletransportation that identity is not what matters in survival. Parfit's reductionism holds that persons are not separately existing entities over and above physical and psychological facts.

**Sydney Shoemaker and Richard Swinburne, *Personal Identity* (1984)** — A paired debate presenting psychological continuity theory (Shoemaker) and the simple view requiring a further fact (Swinburne). Essential for understanding the main fault lines in the metaphysics of personal identity.

**Eric Olson, *The Human Animal: Personal Identity Without Psychology* (1997)** — A rigorous defense of animalism, arguing that we are fundamentally biological organisms and that psychological continuity theories lead to absurd results. Olson's challenge to neo-Lockean views remains central to contemporary debates.

## Secondary Literature

**Harold Noonan, *Personal Identity* (2nd ed., 2003)** — A comprehensive and accessible survey of the major theories, covering Locke, Hume, Parfit, and their critics. Useful for students needing an overview of the full landscape of positions.

**Marya Schechtman, *The Constitution of Selves* (1996)** — Argues that narrative self-constitution provides a more adequate account of the features we care about in personal identity than does bare psychological continuity. Influential in linking personal identity theory to questions of self-understanding and ethics.

**Peter van Inwagen, "Materialism and the Psychological-Continuity Account of Personal Identity" (1997)** — A sharp critique of psychological continuity theories from a materialist standpoint, arguing they are incoherent when combined with physicalism. Raises the fission problem in a particularly pointed form.

**Mark Johnston, "Human Beings" (1987), *Journal of Philosophy*** — Defends a form of animalism while engaging carefully with Parfit's reductionism. Johnston introduces important distinctions between the grounds of identity and what matters practically.

**Raymond Martin and John Barresi (eds.), *Personal Identity* (2003)** — An anthology collecting classical and contemporary sources from Locke and Hume through Parfit and Olson. An indispensable teaching resource covering all major positions and objections.
