# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: The Problem of Identity

## Key Arguments

**Locke's Memory Theory of Personal Identity**
Locke argues that personal identity consists in the continuity of consciousness, specifically the ability to extend one's memory to past experiences. If a person can remember performing a past action, that remembered action is constitutively part of their identity; if they cannot, it is not. This theory targets the question of what distinguishes mere numerical identity (the same body) from genuine personal identity, and its influence has shaped nearly every subsequent debate in the field.

**The Psychological Continuity Criterion (Parfit)**
Derek Parfit refines the memory theory into a broader psychological continuity criterion: personal identity over time consists in overlapping chains of psychological connections — memories, beliefs, desires, intentions — that hold between earlier and later person-stages. Crucially, Parfit argues that this criterion admits of degrees, leading to his revisionary conclusion that identity itself may not be what matters in survival.

**The Bodily Continuity Criterion**
Against psychological accounts, some philosophers argue that personal identity is simply constituted by the continuity of the living human body. The argument is that psychological criteria presuppose a physical substrate, that bodies are what we most reliably track when we re-identify persons, and that severing personal identity from the biological organism introduces unnecessary metaphysical complexity.

**Four-Dimensionalism and the Persistence Debate**
Four-dimensionalists argue that persons, like all persisting objects, are extended in time as well as space: a person is a temporally extended "worm" composed of distinct temporal parts (person-stages). This view dissolves many puzzles about identity over change by rejecting the idea that numerically the same three-dimensional entity must persist; instead, distinct stages are unified by causal-psychological or spatiotemporal relations.

**Reid's Circularity Objection to Memory Theory**
Thomas Reid argues that Locke's memory criterion is viciously circular: to remember a past experience just is to remember that I had that experience, which already presupposes my identity with the past experiencer. Memory thus cannot constitute personal identity without already relying on it; the criterion explains nothing unless personal identity is imported as a primitive.

## Core Concepts

**Psychological Continuity**
Psychological continuity is the relation holding between person-stages when there is an overlapping chain of psychological connections — including memories, intentions, beliefs, character traits, and desires — linking them. Introduced systematically by Parfit, it is offered as the non-circular successor to Locke's memory criterion and forms the core of neo-Lockean accounts of personal identity.

**Numerical vs. Qualitative Identity**
Numerical identity is the relation an entity bears to itself alone (being one and the same thing), whereas qualitative identity is the relation between things that share all the same properties. Personal identity debates concern numerical identity over time: the question is not whether I resemble my past self but whether I am literally the same individual, and the two concepts come apart dramatically in cases of radical change.

**The Bundle Theory (Hume)**
Hume's bundle theory denies that there is any persisting self or substance underlying mental states; what we call the self is simply a bundle of perceptions — impressions and ideas — that succeed one another with great rapidity. Introspection, Hume argues, never reveals a self apart from some particular perception, making the self a fictional unity projected onto a flux of distinct mental events.

**Four-Dimensionalism (Perdurantism)**
Four-dimensionalism holds that persisting objects, including persons, exist by having distinct temporal parts at different times, much as they have distinct spatial parts at different locations. On this view, what we ordinarily call "the same person" is really a temporally extended entity whose stages are unified by some continuity relation, and apparent puzzles about identity through change are resolved by distinguishing different temporal parts.

**Personal Identity vs. What Matters in Survival**
Parfit's distinction separates the metaphysical question of whether I persist from the practical question of what we care about when we care about our future selves. He argues that psychological continuity and connectedness — not strict numerical identity — are what rationally matter in anticipation, prudential concern, and special obligations, with significant consequences for ethics and rationality.

**The Persistence Question**
The persistence question asks what conditions a person must satisfy to continue existing through time — what makes a future person the same person as a present person. It is distinct from the question of what makes a person the person they are at an instant (the synchronic question) and from questions about what we care about in survival (the practical question).

## Thought Experiments

**The Teleporter (Parfit)**
Parfit imagines a teletransportation device that records your exact physical and psychological state, destroys the original body, and reconstitutes a qualitatively identical person at another location. If we judge this to preserve identity, we are committed to some form of psychological continuity criterion; if we deny it, we must explain what is missing. The case tests intuitions about whether physical continuity is necessary for personal identity and reveals disagreement even among those who share the same criterion.

**The Prince and the Cobbler (Locke)**
Locke asks us to imagine the soul or consciousness of a prince waking in the body of a cobbler, retaining all the prince's memories and psychological states. Most readers judge the resulting person to be the prince, not the cobbler, which Locke takes as evidence that personal identity follows consciousness rather than bodily or substantial continuity. The case became the template for testing psychological vs. bodily criteria.

**Fission (Parfit)**
Parfit imagines a person's brain being divided and each hemisphere transplanted into a different body, producing two survivors each psychologically continuous with the original. If identity requires uniqueness, at most one can be identical to the original; yet by symmetry, neither has a stronger claim. The puzzle suggests that personal identity cannot simply be psychological continuity (since both resulting persons satisfy it equally) and motivates Parfit's conclusion that identity is not what matters.

**Gradual Ship of Theseus Applied to Persons**
If we gradually replaced every cell in your body and every memory and belief over a sufficiently long period, would the resulting person be you? The gradual replacement scenario tests the plausibility of both physical and psychological criteria by showing that incremental changes, each seemingly identity-preserving, can cumulatively produce a result that may not be clearly the original person, pressing on the transitivity of identity.
