# Key Thinkers: The Problem of Identity

---

**John Locke (1632–1704)**
English philosopher and founder of British empiricism. Locke introduced the memory theory of personal identity, arguing in the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) that a person at time T2 is the same person as a person at time T1 if and only if the later person can remember the experiences of the earlier. His distinction between the identity of a human animal (biological continuity) and the identity of a person (psychological continuity) established the framework for nearly all subsequent discussion, and his emphasis on consciousness rather than substance as the criterion of personal identity opened the door to both secular and morally serious treatments of the problem.
*Key works:* *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689)

---

**David Hume (1711–1776)**
Scottish empiricist and skeptical philosopher. Hume famously denied that introspection reveals any impression of a persisting, unified self; instead, entering most intimately into what he called himself, he found only a bundle of perceptions. His bundle theory in *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739) challenges the very coherence of the concept of personal identity, arguing that claims about a persisting self are a convenient fiction generated by the resemblance and causation among successive perceptions. Hume's position has been influential in Buddhism-friendly philosophy of mind and in contemporary deflationary accounts of the self.
*Key works:* *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748)

---

**Derek Parfit (1942–2017)**
British analytic philosopher, Fellow of All Souls College, Oxford. Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) transformed philosophy of personal identity by arguing, through fission cases and the teletransportation paradox, that identity is not what matters in survival—what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness, even without strict identity. His reductionism holds that persons are not separately existing entities: there is no further fact beyond the physical and psychological facts. Parfit drew radical ethical conclusions from this view, arguing it undermines certain forms of rational self-concern and supports an impersonal approach to ethics.
*Key works:* *Reasons and Persons* (1984), "Personal Identity" (1971)

---

**Sydney Shoemaker (1931–)**
American analytic philosopher, longtime professor at Cornell University. Shoemaker developed a sophisticated psychological continuity account of personal identity, arguing that the causal relations among psychological states are constitutive of personal identity rather than merely contingently correlated with it. His thought experiments, including the famous brain-transplant cases, helped clarify the structure of the psychological continuity view. He also explored the relationship between personal identity and self-knowledge, arguing that first-person self-ascriptions have a special immunity to error through misidentification.
*Key works:* *Self-Knowledge and Self-Identity* (1963), *Personal Identity* (with Swinburne, 1984), *Identity, Cause, and Mind* (1984)

---

**Eric Olson (1967–)**
British philosopher specializing in metaphysics and philosophy of mind, based at the University of Sheffield. Olson is the leading defender of animalism, the view that each of us is numerically identical with a biological organism. His *The Human Animal* (1997) systematically challenges psychological continuity theories, arguing that they generate absurd results in fission cases and fail to tell us what we fundamentally are. Olson's animalism grounds personal identity in biological rather than psychological facts, making it continuous with our best scientific understanding of living things.
*Key works:* *The Human Animal* (1997), *What Are We?* (2007)
