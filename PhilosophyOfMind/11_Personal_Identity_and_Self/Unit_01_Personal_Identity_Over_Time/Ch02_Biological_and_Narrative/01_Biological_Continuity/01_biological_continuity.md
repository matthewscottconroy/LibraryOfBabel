# Biological Continuity and Animalism

Animalism is the view that we — human persons — are fundamentally biological organisms. Personal identity over time consists in biological continuity: the continuous life of a human animal. We are not minds, souls, or psychological subjects; we are animals, and what we share with our past and future selves is the same biological life.

**The Core Claim**

Peter van Inwagen and Eric Olson are the principal defenders of animalism in contemporary philosophy. The animalist argument begins with a simple observation: there is an animal sitting in your chair right now. That animal thinks. If you also think, then there are two thinkers sharing exactly the same body — a conclusion that seems absurd. The simpler explanation is that you are the animal.

This "too many thinkers" argument challenges psychological continuity theories: if psychological continuity were the criterion for personal identity, and the animal that is currently you were to undergo a psychology transfer (your psychology is uploaded to a new substrate), the animal left behind would not be you — even though it is biologically continuous. But then there are two entities that currently meet all the criteria we use to identify persons, and they share exactly the same body. This seems unacceptable.

Animalism avoids this by identifying persons with the animal itself. There is no separate "psychological subject" over and above the biological organism. You are the organism, and your identity over time is the identity of the organism.

**What Biological Continuity Requires**

Biological continuity, on the animalist account, consists in the continued life of the organism — the continuous metabolic, homeostatic, and developmental processes that constitute animal life. This is different from the continuity of the body's matter (cells are continuously replaced) and different from the continuity of any psychological characteristics.

This has the consequence that biological continuity can persist through dramatic psychological change. Severe dementia, complete amnesia, or persistent vegetative state: the organism persists even when psychological continuity is minimal or absent. For the animalist, that is enough for personal identity. You would be the person in the vegetative state, even though there is no psychological continuity connecting you to them.

**Problems for Animalism**

Animalism faces several challenges.

*The fetus problem*: You were once a fetus. But the fetus you were had no psychological states, no personality, no memories. If animalism is right, you are identical to that fetus. This seems to commit us to ascribing moral status to early-stage fetuses equal to that of persons, or to denying moral status to persons — depending on which way we run the argument. Many philosophers find this conclusion uncomfortable.

*The brain transplant case*: Suppose your brain (with all its psychological contents) is transplanted into another body. Where do you go? Most people intuit that you go where the brain goes — that the psychological continuity following the brain is you. But animalism seems to imply that you remain with the animal's (original) body, even if it has no psychological function. This clashes with strong intuitions.

*The thinking animal problem for the animalist*: Olson accepts that if animalism is true and the brain transplant were to occur, you would stay with the original body (which is now a mindless organism). This is the animalist's position, and it requires denying the intuition that you go where the brain goes.

**Animalism and the Hard Problem**

Animalism connects to the hard problem in an interesting way. Biological organisms that are sufficiently organized have phenomenal consciousness — this is the view of biological naturalists (Searle) and most materialists. The animalist can hold that what explains consciousness is not some abstract functional organization but the biological organization of a living animal.

This might provide a more naturalistic foundation for consciousness than functionalism: if consciousness depends on biological organization specifically, then the multiple realizability argument (which supports functionalism) would be mistaken for consciousness, even if it holds for cognitive function.

**The Practical Dimension**

The animalist view has implications for medical ethics and personal survival decisions. If you are fundamentally a biological organism, then the criterion for your continued existence is the continued life of that organism — including in states of minimal psychological function. This supports stronger obligations toward patients in persistent vegetative states and against policies that equate biological death with psychological death.

The psychological continuity theorist, by contrast, might hold that a person in a persistent vegetative state with no psychological function has ceased to exist as a person, even while the organism lives. This difference in theoretical commitment generates real differences in how we should think about end-of-life care.
