# Narrative Identity

Narrative identity theory holds that personal identity is constituted by the stories — the narratives — through which we make sense of our lives. Rather than analyzing identity as a relation between psychological or biological stages, narrative theories locate identity in the temporal structure of a life as told and understood from the inside.

**The Core Claim**

Paul Ricoeur's distinction between idem-identity and ipse-identity is central to narrative approaches. Idem-identity is sameness in the numerical sense — what makes a thing the same thing across time. Ipse-identity is the kind of identity involved in personal commitment and promise-keeping: I am the one who promised, the one who committed. This ipse-self is constituted not by some underlying substance but by the narrative structure of a life — its plot, its characters, its beginning, middle, and end.

Ricoeur argues that human time is structured narratively: we understand our lives as having a beginning (birth, family, formative experiences), a developing plot (choices, encounters, turning points), and a projected end. This narrative structure is not imposed from outside but is constitutive of what it is to be a person in time. To have a personal identity is to have a life story.

Alasdair MacIntyre develops a related view in *After Virtue*: personal identity is intelligible only in the context of a narrative that situates the person in a community, a tradition, and a history of practices. I can understand what I am doing (and what I should do) only by understanding what story I am in — what character I am, what narrative my actions are episodes of.

**Dennett and the Center of Narrative Gravity**

Daniel Dennett proposes a deflationary version of the narrative self. The self, on his view, is a "center of narrative gravity" — a useful fiction, like the center of gravity of a physical object. Just as the center of gravity is an abstract, theoretically useful entity that doesn't correspond to any specific material part, the self is an abstract entity that our brain's narrative-making activity posits.

When we tell and hear stories about ourselves, our brains create and maintain a self-model — a narrative character that is the protagonist of our autobiographical story. This self-model is what we normally call "the self," but it is not a Cartesian res cogitans or a biological organism; it is a narrative construction.

Dennett's view is anti-essentialist about the self while being realistic about narratives: the story is real, even if the entity it posits is abstractly characterized. The therapeutic implication: mental health involves maintaining a coherent, functional self-narrative; mental disorders often involve disruption of the narrative self (the absence of a coherent story about who one is, as in depersonalization, or false stories, as in certain delusions).

**Narrative Identity and Authenticity**

Narrative theories connect naturally to questions about authenticity and self-determination. To live authentically, on a narrative view, is to live a life whose story is genuinely one's own — to be the author of one's narrative rather than a character imposed by others. Charles Taylor's account of identity as requiring a "moral framework" — a sense of what is supremely important, a horizon of significance — provides the evaluative backbone for narrative identity.

Taylor argues that having an identity requires having an orientation to the good — a sense of where one stands with respect to what matters. Narrative provides the structure through which this orientation is expressed: our life stories are stories about what we have cared about, fought for, and become.

This connects narrative identity to moral development: to have a narrative identity is not just to have a history but to have a history that is understood in terms of values, commitments, and progress or failure. Personal growth is narrative growth — the revision of one's life story toward greater coherence, authenticity, and moral depth.

**Challenges to Narrative Identity**

Several important challenges have been raised.

*The fiction problem*: Narratives can be false. People often construct self-narratives that are distorted, self-serving, or simply inaccurate. If identity is constituted by narrative, does the distorted narrative constitute a distorted identity? This creates a tension between what we are and the story we tell about ourselves.

*The discontinuity problem*: Many people's lives lack the narrative coherence that narrative theories require. Severe trauma, cognitive impairment, or extreme poverty may prevent the construction of a coherent life narrative. Does this mean these individuals lack personal identity? This seems unacceptable.

*Galen Strawson's "episodic" challenge*: Strawson argues that not all people experience themselves as narrative selves. He identifies an "episodic" style of self-experience in which life is not experienced as a connected narrative but as a series of largely disconnected episodes. The episodic person does not feel the past self as part of their current identity. If such self-experience is coherent (and Strawson contends that it is), then narrative identity cannot be a universal or necessary condition of personhood.

Ricoeur's response acknowledges that not all lives have the narrative structure of a good novel, but maintains that some minimal narrative intelligibility — the capacity to answer "who did that?" and "what is your story?" — is required for personal identity. Narrative may be a more modest condition than his positive account suggests.
