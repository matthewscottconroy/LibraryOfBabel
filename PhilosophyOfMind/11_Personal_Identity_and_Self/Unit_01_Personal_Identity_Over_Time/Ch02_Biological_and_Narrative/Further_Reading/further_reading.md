# Further Reading: Biological and Narrative Theories of Identity

## Primary Sources

**Peter van Inwagen, *Material Beings* (1990)** — Van Inwagen argues that only living organisms are genuine material objects; artifacts and arbitrary composites do not really exist. This metaphysical background motivates his animalist approach to personal identity and forces engagement with difficult questions about the nature of biological persistence.

**Eric Olson, *The Human Animal: Personal Identity Without Psychology* (1997)** — The definitive statement of contemporary animalism, arguing that we are fundamentally biological organisms whose identity conditions are given by biological rather than psychological continuity. Olson systematically rebuts neo-Lockean alternatives and the "thinking animal" argument shows that psychological continuity theories entail we are not the animals we appear to be.

**Alasdair MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981)** — MacIntyre's revival of Aristotelian virtue ethics includes an influential account of narrative identity: human beings are storytelling animals whose unity as agents is constituted by the narrative structure of their lives. The chapter "The Virtues, the Unity of a Human Life and the Concept of a Tradition" is essential for understanding narrative approaches to personal identity.

**Paul Ricoeur, *Oneself as Another* (1992)** — Ricoeur distinguishes *idem*-identity (sameness) from *ipse*-identity (selfhood as self-constancy), arguing that narrative provides the mediation between these two dimensions of personal identity. His account of narrative identity is more philosophically rigorous than MacIntyre's and engages seriously with the phenomenological tradition.

**Marya Schechtman, *The Constitution of Selves* (1996)** — Schechtman argues that to have a personal identity is to create a self-constituting narrative that organizes one's experiences and actions into a coherent whole. She develops the "narrative self-constitution view" as an alternative to both biological and bare psychological continuity theories, connecting personal identity to moral and practical concerns.

## Secondary Literature

**Lynne Rudder Baker, *Persons and Bodies: A Constitution View* (2000)** — Baker argues that persons are constituted by but not identical to human animals, that what is distinctive about persons is their first-person perspective. This view navigates between animalism and psychological continuity theories while taking embodiment seriously.

**Raymond Tallis, *I Am: A Philosophical Inquiry into First-Person Being* (2004)** — A wide-ranging examination of selfhood that engages with both biological and narrative approaches while remaining critical of reductionist accounts. Tallis draws on phenomenology and cognitive science to articulate a richer conception of personal existence.

**Daniel Dennett, "The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity" (1992)** — Dennett's influential essay argues that the self is a fictional construct—a center of narrative gravity—rather than a substantial entity. While deflationary, this piece is an important reference point for understanding both the appeal and the limits of narrative approaches to selfhood.

**Galen Strawson, "Against Narrativity" (2004), *Ratio*** — A sharp critique of narrative theories, arguing that not all persons experience or understand themselves through narrative, and that the normative claim that they should is false. Strawson defends an "episodic" conception of self against MacIntyre, Ricoeur, and their followers.

**Tim Bayne and Jordi Fernández (eds.), *Delusion and Self-Deception* (2009)** — Relevant chapters examine how pathological disruptions to narrative coherence—especially in dementia and dissociative disorders—bear on the relationship between narrative selfhood and personal identity, illuminating what narrative theories require.
