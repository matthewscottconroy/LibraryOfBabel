# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Biological and Narrative

## Key Arguments

**The Animalist Argument (Olson)**
Eric Olson argues that we are fundamentally human animals — biological organisms — and that any account of personal identity must respect this ontological baseline. Because organisms persist through purely biological processes of metabolism and homeostasis, personal identity cannot consist in psychological continuity: a person in a persistent vegetative state, or a fetus before psychological development, is still the same organism. The argument targets neo-Lockean views by showing they imply absurd results, such as that we were never embryos.

**Van Inwagen's Biological Criterion**
Peter van Inwagen defends a closely related view that the conditions for personal persistence just are the conditions for animal persistence: a complex physical organization maintaining life-sustaining processes. He argues that the question "what are we?" must be answered before the question "when do we persist?", and the answer — we are animals — entails that psychological criteria are either false or derivative. This has implications for debates about abortion, dementia, and the significance of death.

**Parfit's Reductionism**
Parfit argues that persons are reducible to brains, bodies, and streams of experience — there is no further fact about personal identity beyond physical and psychological facts. Reductionism denies the existence of a separately persisting entity (a Cartesian ego or Humean self-as-substance) and implies that identity statements can be indeterminate in fission cases. The view supports Parfit's practical conclusion that what matters in survival is not identity but psychological continuity and connectedness.

**The Narrative Necessity Argument (MacIntyre/Schechtman)**
Alasdair MacIntyre and Marya Schechtman argue that persons are constitutively narrative beings: personal identity is constructed through the ongoing act of integrating experiences, actions, and commitments into a coherent life story. Schechtman's narrative self-constitution view holds that a person's identity just is the narrative they tell about themselves, provided it meets certain coherence and engagement criteria. This view challenges both biological and moment-to-moment psychological accounts by emphasizing temporally extended self-interpretation.

**The Buddhist No-Self Argument**
Buddhist philosophy, especially in the Pali Canon and systematized by Nagarjuna, argues that introspection reveals no substantial, persisting self — only the five aggregates (skandhas: form, sensation, perception, mental formations, consciousness) in flux. The no-self (anatta) argument concludes that the ordinary belief in a persisting personal identity is not merely metaphysically confused but the root of suffering. This position converges with Hume's bundle theory while embedding it in a broader soteriological framework.

## Core Concepts

**Animalism**
Animalism is the view that human persons are identical to human animals — biological organisms of the species Homo sapiens — and that personal identity over time consists in whatever makes an organism persist. Developed principally by Eric Olson and Peter van Inwagen, it stands in opposition to neo-Lockean psychological criteria and implies that psychological features are neither necessary nor sufficient for personal identity.

**Narrative Self-Constitution**
Narrative self-constitution is the thesis, associated with Marya Schechtman, that personal identity is constituted by a person's self-narrative: the story one tells about one's life that integrates past, present, and anticipated future into a coherent whole. Unlike merely descriptive narrative theories, it holds that the narrative is not merely expressive of a pre-existing identity but partly constitutive of it, subject to conditions of coherence, first-person applicability, and ownership.

**Parfit's Reductionism**
Parfit's reductionism denies that personal identity is a further fact beyond physical and psychological continuity relations. Persons are reducible to collections of mental and physical events standing in specific relations; the persistence of a person adds no ontological surplus beyond these relations. The view implies that identity claims about persons can be empty or indeterminate, particularly in puzzle cases involving fission or gradual replacement.

**The Five Aggregates (Skandhas)**
In Buddhist metaphysics, what we ordinarily take to be a unified self is analyzed as five aggregates (skandhas): material form, sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. None of the aggregates individually constitutes a self, and their collective operation produces the illusion of a persisting, unified person. The doctrine is foundational for the no-self (anatta) argument and for Buddhist accounts of rebirth without a transmigrating soul.

**Narrative Identity (Ricoeur)**
Paul Ricoeur distinguishes idem-identity (sameness, numerical identity) from ipse-identity (selfhood, the identity of a promising, remembering, responsible agent). Narrative identity, on his view, mediates between these: through emplotment — the temporal configuration of events into a story — persons achieve an ipse-identity that is neither reducible to bare material continuity nor purely psychological. His account foregrounds the ethical stakes of self-narration in responsibility and promise-keeping.

## Thought Experiments

**The Dementia Case**
Consider a person with advanced Alzheimer's disease who has lost all memories and psychological connections to her earlier self while the organism persists. Animalists use this case to argue that the person survives (the animal continues), while psychological continuity theorists face the uncomfortable implication that the earlier person may have ceased to exist while the biological organism continues. The case presses on the practical and moral stakes of competing criteria.

**Fission and Reductionism (Parfit)**
Parfit uses fission — the transplantation of each cerebral hemisphere into a different body — to motivate reductionism: if identity cannot be preserved in both directions (since identity is one-one), yet both candidates are equally continuous with the original, there is no further fact that determines identity. Reductionism explains the indeterminacy by denying that a separately persisting self exists whose fate must be settled; the empty question reveals identity's practical insignificance.

**The Survivor's Narrative**
Imagine a concentration camp survivor who can maintain identity and moral continuity only by constructing a narrative that integrates unbearable experiences into a coherent life story. Narrative theorists point to cases like those described by Primo Levi to illustrate how narrative is not merely post-hoc description but constitutive of survival as a person — and how narrative disruption, forced forgetting, or imposed self-interpretations can constitute identity damage distinct from physical harm.
