# Key Thinkers: Biological and Narrative Theories of Identity

---

**Peter van Inwagen (1942–)**
American analytic metaphysician, longtime professor at Notre Dame and Duke University. Van Inwagen's animalism flows from his wider metaphysical view that only organisms genuinely exist as composite material objects. His argument that we are essentially biological organisms—and that our persistence conditions are those of living things rather than psychological continuity—provides a rigorous alternative to neo-Lockean views. Van Inwagen's work forces the question of what kind of thing a person is at the most fundamental metaphysical level.
*Key works:* *Material Beings* (1990), "Materialism and the Psychological-Continuity Account of Personal Identity" (1997)

---

**Eric Olson (1967–)**
British philosopher of mind and metaphysician at the University of Sheffield. Olson is the most systematic defender of animalism in contemporary philosophy, arguing that persons are identical to biological organisms and that psychological continuity is neither necessary nor sufficient for personal identity. His "thinking animal argument" poses a particularly sharp challenge: if a person shares a brain and psychology with a human animal sitting in the same chair, and the animal thinks, then either the person is the animal or there are two thinkers—an unacceptable result for most psychological theories.
*Key works:* *The Human Animal* (1997), *What Are We?* (2007)

---

**Alasdair MacIntyre (1929–)**
Scottish-American moral philosopher, known for his revival of Aristotelian ethics and his account of narrative identity. In *After Virtue* (1981) MacIntyre argues that human action is only intelligible within the context of a story, and that the unity of a human life is constituted by the narrative from birth to death within which individual actions find their meaning. His account has been enormously influential in both ethics and personal identity theory, though critics note it may idealize narrative coherence and underestimate fragmentation.
*Key works:* *After Virtue* (1981), *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988), *Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry* (1990)

---

**Paul Ricoeur (1913–2005)**
French philosopher working in the phenomenological and hermeneutic traditions. Ricoeur's *Oneself as Another* (1992) offers the most philosophically developed narrative theory of personal identity, distinguishing *idem*-identity (numerical and qualitative sameness over time) from *ipse*-identity (selfhood as self-constancy through promise and commitment). He argues that narrative mediates between the temporal dispersal of a life and its unity, giving identity its proper temporal form. His engagement with analytic philosophy alongside continental phenomenology makes his work uniquely bridging.
*Key works:* *Time and Narrative* (3 vols., 1984–1988), *Oneself as Another* (1992)

---

**Marya Schechtman (1960–)**
American philosopher at the University of Illinois at Chicago. Schechtman's narrative self-constitution view holds that a person constitutes her identity by developing a life narrative that integrates her experiences, actions, and characteristics into a coherent whole. Unlike purely metaphysical theories focused on numerical identity, she connects personal identity to the practical and ethical concerns that motivate our interest in the concept. Her later work has explored how her account handles disruptions such as dementia and radical character change.
*Key works:* *The Constitution of Selves* (1996), *Staying Alive: Personal Identity, Practical Concerns, and the Unity of a Life* (2014)
