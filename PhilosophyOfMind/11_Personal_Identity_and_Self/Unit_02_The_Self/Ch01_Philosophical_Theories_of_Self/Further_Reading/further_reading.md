# Further Reading: Philosophical Theories of Self

## Primary Sources

**René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641)** — The Second Meditation establishes the cogito—the thinking self as the one indubitable certainty—while inaugurating the modern conception of the self as a purely mental, non-extended substance. Descartes' dualism and his introspective method shaped the entire subsequent tradition, making questions about the nature of the thinking subject unavoidable.

**Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, "Transcendental Analytic" (1781)** — Kant's transcendental unity of apperception introduces the "I think" that must be able to accompany all my representations as the formal condition of experience. Unlike Descartes' substantial ego, Kant's transcendental self is a formal unity, not a knowable object—a distinction that has shaped Neo-Kantian and phenomenological accounts of self-consciousness.

**Johann Gottlieb Fichte, *The Science of Knowledge (Wissenschaftslehre)* (1794)** — Fichte radicalizes Kant by making the self-positing I the absolute first principle of all philosophy. His account of the I as pure activity—positing itself and its not-I—anticipates later existentialist and phenomenological accounts of self-constitution and freedom.

**Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Representation*, Vol. I (1818)** — Schopenhauer argues that the inner nature of the world, and of the self, is will rather than representation. His claim that the intellect is secondary to a blind, striving will anticipates Freudian and later unconscious-self theories and represents a significant departure from Kantian rationalism about the subject.

**David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book I, Part IV (1739)** — Hume's bundle theory treats the self as a bundle of perceptions rather than a unified, persisting subject. This empiricist skepticism about the substantial self resonates with Buddhist no-self (anattā) doctrine and continues to challenge substantialist accounts.

## Secondary Literature

**Béatrice Han-Pile, "The 'Death of Man': Foucault and Anti-Humanism" (2010)** — Examines how poststructuralist philosophy, following Nietzsche and Heidegger, deconstructed the unified self inherited from Descartes and Kant. Essential background for understanding the variety of contemporary anti-essentialist views about the self.

**Charles Taylor, *Sources of the Self: The Making of the Modern Identity* (1989)** — A magisterial intellectual history tracing how the modern conception of selfhood developed from Plato through Descartes, Locke, and Romanticism to the present. Taylor argues that our moral frameworks are inseparably tied to our conceptions of who we are, making the history of the self a moral history.

**Mark Siderits, *Personal Identity and Buddhist Philosophy* (2003)** — Examines Buddhist no-self doctrines in relation to contemporary analytic philosophy of personal identity, arguing that Buddhist reductionism anticipates Parfitian positions. Essential for any course that wishes to bring Western and Buddhist philosophical traditions into dialogue.

**Dorothea Frede and Burkhard Reis (eds.), *Body and Soul in Ancient Philosophy* (2009)** — A scholarly collection addressing Greek accounts of the soul and its relationship to the body, providing essential background for understanding the pre-Cartesian philosophical landscape of selfhood.

**Quassim Cassam, *Self and World* (1997)** — A neo-Kantian argument that self-consciousness requires consciousness of a world and that perceptual self-consciousness depends on awareness of oneself as a physical object. Bridges Kantian transcendental philosophy and contemporary analytic epistemology of self-knowledge.
