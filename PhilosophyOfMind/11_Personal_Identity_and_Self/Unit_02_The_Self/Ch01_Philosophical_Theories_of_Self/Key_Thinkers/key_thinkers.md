# Key Thinkers: Philosophical Theories of Self

---

**René Descartes (1596–1650)**
French mathematician and philosopher, founder of modern rationalism. Descartes' method of radical doubt led him to the cogito—*cogito ergo sum*—which establishes the thinking self as the one indubitable reality. For Descartes, the self is a *res cogitans*, a thinking substance entirely distinct from the body, a view that entrenched mind-body dualism and made the unity and persistence of the thinking subject a central philosophical problem. His introspective method—the first-person investigation of consciousness—set the agenda for phenomenology and analytic philosophy of mind alike.
*Key works:* *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641), *Discourse on the Method* (1637), *Principles of Philosophy* (1644)

---

**Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)**
German philosopher of the Enlightenment, founder of critical philosophy. Kant's transcendental unity of apperception posits the "I think" as the formal condition that must accompany all my representations, unifying them as mine. Crucially, Kant insists this transcendental I is not an object of knowledge—it is a formal, logical unity rather than a substantial soul. This move dissolves rationalist paralogisms about the soul while preserving the structural role of self-consciousness, influencing Fichte, Husserl, and all subsequent transcendental approaches to the self.
*Key works:* *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781/1787), *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788)

---

**Johann Gottlieb Fichte (1762–1814)**
German idealist philosopher, Kant's most immediate successor and critic. Fichte elevated the I from a formal condition of experience to the absolute first principle of all philosophy: the I posits itself, its not-I (the world), and the relationship between them. This radical idealism makes self-consciousness the primary metaphysical category rather than a feature of an already-given world. Fichte's account of the I as pure, self-positing activity influenced Hegel, existentialism, and contemporary neo-idealist accounts of self-consciousness.
*Key works:* *The Science of Knowledge* (1794), *The Vocation of Man* (1800)

---

**Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860)**
German philosopher, deeply influenced by Kant and Indian philosophy. Schopenhauer argued that behind the veil of representation—the world as we know it intellectually—lies the world as will: a blind, striving, irrational force of which the individual self is merely a particular manifestation. The self as intellect is subordinate to the will, an insight that anticipated both Freudian theories of the unconscious and existentialist critiques of rationalism. Schopenhauer's engagement with Hindu and Buddhist thought made him unusual among Western philosophers in taking seriously non-self views.
*Key works:* *The World as Will and Representation* (1818/1844), *Parerga and Paralipomena* (1851)

---

**Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE)**
Indian Buddhist philosopher, founder of the Madhyamaka school. Nāgārjuna systematically argued that all phenomena, including the self, are empty (*śūnya*) of inherent existence—they exist only dependently, in relation to other things and conceptual frameworks. His *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* provides the most rigorous philosophical defense of the Buddhist no-self doctrine, arguing that the self is neither identical to nor different from its psychophysical aggregates. His position anticipates and challenges Western metaphysics of the subject in ways increasingly recognized by analytic philosophers.
*Key works:* *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (c. 2nd century CE), *Vigrahavyāvartanī*

---

**David Hume (1711–1776)**
Scottish empiricist philosopher. Hume's bundle theory holds that introspection never reveals a simple, persisting self—only a bundle of perceptions in constant flux. The self, on this view, is a convenient fiction generated by the imagination's tendency to confuse closely resembling and causally related perceptions with a single identical thing. Hume's skeptical deflation of the self resonates with Buddhist anattā and has influenced deflationary and eliminativist accounts in contemporary philosophy of mind, as well as Parfit's reductionism about personal identity.
*Key works:* *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748)
