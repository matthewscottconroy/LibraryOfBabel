# Further Reading: Contemporary Theories of Self

## Primary Sources

**Thomas Metzinger, *Being No One: The Self-Model Theory of Subjectivity* (2003)** — Metzinger's ambitious theory argues that no such thing as a self exists; rather, the brain constructs a phenomenal self-model (PSM) that represents the organism to itself, creating the illusion of a self. This neuroscience-grounded account is philosophically rigorous and draws on data from out-of-body experiences, depersonalization, and rubber-hand illusions to support its deflationary conclusions.

**Thomas Metzinger, *The Ego Tunnel* (2009)** — A more accessible presentation of Metzinger's self-model theory, intended for a broader philosophical and scientific audience. Examines the implications of the PSM account for consciousness, free will, and the ethics of neurotechnology, and includes engagement with altered states of consciousness and meditative practice.

**Antonio Damasio, *The Feeling of What Happens: Body and Emotion in the Making of Consciousness* (1999)** — Damasio distinguishes the proto-self (brain maps of the body), the core self (moment-by-moment subjective experience), and the autobiographical self (narrative constructed from memory). His neuroscientific account grounds consciousness and selfhood in bodily feeling states and provides rich clinical evidence from patients with various brain lesions.

**Dan Zahavi, *Subjectivity and Selfhood: Investigating the First-Person Perspective* (2005)** — Zahavi defends a phenomenological account of the minimal self as the pre-reflective first-person givenness of experience. Against Metzinger's no-self view, he argues that experience itself has an ownness that cannot be eliminated without incoherence, drawing on Husserl, Heidegger, and Sartre.

**Shaun Gallagher, *How the Body Shapes the Mind* (2005)** — Gallagher distinguishes the body schema (a non-conscious system for action control) from the body image (a conscious representation of the body) and develops their implications for accounts of self. He argues that the minimal bodily self is prior to and more fundamental than narrative self-understanding.

## Secondary Literature

**Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch, *The Embodied Mind* (1991)** — A landmark work arguing for an enactive approach to cognition in which mind and self are not brain-bound but emerge from the structural coupling of organism and environment. Draws on phenomenology and Buddhist cognitive science to challenge Cartesian assumptions about the self.

**Chris Frith, *Making Up the Mind: How the Brain Creates Our Mental World* (2007)** — A leading neuroscientist's account of how the brain constructs the self's experience of agency, perception, and social interaction. Particularly valuable for its treatment of self-monitoring and the neural bases of the minimal sense of agency.

**Natalie Depraz, Francisco Varela, and Pierre Vermersch, *On Becoming Aware: A Pragmatics of Experiencing* (2003)** — Develops the methodology of neurophenomenology and first-person methods for investigating the structure of self-awareness. Bridges Husserlian phenomenology and cognitive science in examining the micro-structure of conscious experience.

**Antonio Damasio, *Self Comes to Mind: Constructing the Conscious Brain* (2010)** — Damasio's fuller neuroscientific account of how the brain constructs a self from the interaction of proto-self, core self, and autobiographical self. Particularly valuable for its discussion of consciousness in non-human animals and for the neural substrates of selfhood.

**Joel Krueger, "The Who and the How of Experience" (2011)** — A careful comparative analysis of Metzinger's self-model theory and phenomenological accounts (particularly Zahavi's), clarifying what is and is not at stake in debates between eliminativist and minimal-self views.
