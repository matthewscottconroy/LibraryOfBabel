# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Contemporary Self

## Key Arguments

**Metzinger's Argument for the Non-Existence of the Self**
Thomas Metzinger argues that there is no such thing as a self — no entity that corresponds to the phenomenal self-model (PSM) generated by the brain. The PSM is a transparent, prereflective model of the organism as a whole that makes the self appear as a given rather than a construct; but since the PSM is just a model, and all models are distinct from their referents, there is no self, only the appearance of one. This argument connects neuroscientific work on self-representation to a radical philosophical conclusion about personal ontology.

**Damasio's Two-Level Self**
Antonio Damasio distinguishes the core self — a transient, moment-to-moment sense of being a knowing subject, generated by brainstem and subcortical structures — from the autobiographical self, a temporally extended self built from dispositional records of personal experience. He argues that the core self is evolutionarily ancient and present in many animals, while the autobiographical self requires cortical memory systems. This empirical argument challenges purely cognitive or narrative accounts by anchoring selfhood in bodily homeostasis.

**The Minimal Self Argument (Zahavi)**
Dan Zahavi argues that before any narrative or reflective self, there is a minimal, pre-reflective self given in the first-personal character of experience itself: every experience is given as mine without requiring an additional reflective act. This minimal self is not a substance but the formal structure of first-person givenness. Zahavi targets both eliminativist views (Metzinger) and pure narrative views (Schechtman) by insisting on an irreducible phenomenal ground for selfhood.

**The No-Self Argument in Analytic Philosophy (Galen Strawson's Response)**
Galen Strawson argues for a series of short-lived, episodic selves — what he calls the pearl view — against narrative accounts that presuppose long-term psychological unity. He contends that experiential reality is better captured by a sequence of distinct "self-experiences," each with genuine phenomenal character but lacking the long narrative arcs that narrative theorists require. This argument targets both Schechtman and MacIntyre by showing that narrative unity is culturally and individually variable, not constitutive of personhood.

## Core Concepts

**The Phenomenal Self-Model (PSM)**
The phenomenal self-model is Metzinger's term for the neural representation of the organism as a whole that generates the first-person perspective. It is "phenomenal" because it has experiential character, and "model" because it is a representational construct rather than a direct disclosure of a substantial self. Its transparency — the fact that we cannot usually see it as a model — is what produces the naive sense that there is a real, substantial self at the center of experience.

**Core Self and Autobiographical Self (Damasio)**
Damasio's core self is the transient, moment-to-moment sense of a knowing subject that arises from the brainstem's ongoing regulation of the body's internal state; it is rebuilt afresh with each new pulse of consciousness. The autobiographical self is the more stable, temporally extended self formed through cortical memory systems that record past experiences and project future expectations. The distinction is supported by neurological dissociations: patients with damage to autobiographical memory systems can lose the autobiographical self while retaining the core self.

**Minimal Self**
The minimal self, championed by Dan Zahavi and Shaun Gallagher, is the most basic form of selfhood: the pre-reflective sense of mineness that attaches to all conscious experience without requiring explicit self-representation or narrative. It is not an object within experience but the formal structure of first-personal givenness. The concept is central to phenomenological accounts of consciousness and is used to argue that no purely third-person neuroscientific or narrative account can fully capture what it is to be a subject.

**Narrative Self**
The narrative self is the higher-order, temporally extended self constituted through the ongoing construction of a life story. Associated with figures such as MacIntyre, Ricoeur, and Schechtman, the narrative self presupposes the minimal self as its base but adds the specifically human capacity for self-interpretation across time. The concept is significant for understanding identity disruption in amnesia, trauma, and disorders like dissociative identity disorder, where narrative coherence breaks down.

**First-Person Givenness (Zahavi)**
First-person givenness is Zahavi's phenomenological term for the quality of experience by which it is given as mine: there is something it is like for me, and this for-me structure is not added to experience by a separate act but is intrinsic to phenomenal consciousness. Zahavi uses this concept to argue that even the most basic act of experience already involves a form of minimal self, distinguishing his view from both Humean bundle views and Metzingerian eliminativism.

**Autonoetic Consciousness**
Introduced by Endel Tulving, autonoetic consciousness is the capacity for self-knowing awareness that allows a person to mentally represent and reflect on their own existence across subjective time — past, present, and future. It is the mode of consciousness characteristic of episodic memory and future imagination, and its impairment in amnesia supports the view that a temporally extended self depends on specific neural memory systems. The concept links the phenomenology of selfhood to the cognitive neuroscience of memory.

## Thought Experiments

**The Rubber Hand Illusion**
Subjects who view a rubber hand being stroked while their own hidden hand is synchronously stroked experience the rubber hand as part of their body, demonstrating that the phenomenal self-model can be updated to incorporate external objects. Metzinger uses this illusion to argue that the sense of body ownership is a model-building process — a neural construction rather than a direct perception of one's actual body — supporting the PSM framework and the general claim that the self is a representational construct.

**Full-Body Illusion and Out-of-Body Experiences**
Blanke and colleagues induced out-of-body experience-like states by stimulating the temporoparietal junction, and similar effects arise in certain anaesthetic conditions. Metzinger interprets these as disruptions of the PSM: when the model of the bodily self fails to integrate correctly with spatial information, the phenomenal self-model becomes dislocated from the physical body. The case supports the view that the felt location and unity of the self is a model-dependent phenomenon, not a direct self-disclosure.

**Galen Strawson's Episodic Self**
Strawson invites readers to consider whether their own introspective reality matches narrative accounts: do we genuinely experience ourselves as unified narrative selves stretching across years, or is the immediate experience more like a series of distinct episodes? He draws on his own phenomenology to argue that many people — perhaps those with more episodic rather than narrative self-experience — do not live their lives as stories, challenging the claimed universality of narrative self-constitution.
