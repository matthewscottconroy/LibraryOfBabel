# Teleportation, Fission, and Personal Identity

Thought experiments about teleportation, brain bisection, teletransportation, and personal fission have been central to philosophical discussions of personal identity since Locke. They are designed to isolate the relevant factors in personal identity by constructing scenarios in which the usual criteria come apart — where psychological continuity and bodily continuity diverge, or where one person's psychological states are reproduced in two distinct bodies. Understanding what these cases reveal and whether they are philosophically probative is crucial for the debate.

**The Teleportation Case**

Suppose that a teleportation device scans your body and brain completely, destroys you, and creates an exact replica of you somewhere else — down to the last atom, with all your memories, personality, and dispositions intact. Is the replica you?

Psychological continuity theorists seem committed to saying yes: the replica has all the same memories, personality, and psychological states, connected by the appropriate causal chain (via the teleportation process). If psychological continuity is what matters, the replica is you.

But many people have the strong intuition that the replica is not you — that you were destroyed, and the replica is a new person who merely thinks they are you. This intuition is especially strong in cases where the original is not destroyed: if the teleportation fails to destroy the original, there are now two people who both have equal claim to be you. They cannot both be you (since they are distinct), so it seems neither was you all along — or one of them is you and the other is a lucky replica. But which one?

The force of this thought experiment is to suggest that what matters for survival may not be psychological continuity but something else — perhaps the continuity of the physical substrate (the brain) or simply the absence of gap in one's existence.

**Parfit's Fission Case**

Parfit's most powerful thought experiment involves fission. Suppose your brain is bisected and each hemisphere is transplanted into a different body. Both resulting people are psychologically continuous with you: each has half your brain, and half your brain contains enough psychological material for continuity. Both have good claim to be you.

But they cannot both be you — identity is a one-one relation, and they are distinct people. Therefore, strict personal identity fails. Yet both successors have exactly what the psychological continuity theorist says is required for survival. Parfit's conclusion: the closest thing to survival is present (double), but identity is not. This shows that identity is not what matters in survival.

Alternative responses to fission:

*Branch-line problem*: If you undergo fission, the "original you" ceases to exist, and two new people begin. This is consistent with psychological continuity theory if we add a "no-branching" condition: psychological continuity is identity-conferring only when it doesn't branch.

*The Parfitian response*: Abandon the importance of identity. Both successors have what matters — each is as good as survival. Our concern with "which one is me" is confused; what we should care about is psychological continuity, and that is doubly preserved.

*The biological response*: Animalism handles fission differently. You are the animal. If both hemispheres are transplanted, you go where the dominant hemisphere goes (or some other biological fact determines the outcome). Fission may simply result in the death of the original and the creation of two new organisms.

**Williams's Torture Case**

Bernard Williams offered thought experiments designed to show that psychological continuity is not sufficient for survival in any sense that matters to us. Suppose someone with all your memories is about to be tortured. Do you anticipate that torture? The scenario is designed so that the answer feels uncertain — and this uncertainty suggests that what matters is not just psychological continuity but something about which body the experience will occur in.

Williams argues that the first-person perspective on survival requires more than third-person psychological facts. Anticipation of one's own future experience is fundamentally different from anticipation of someone psychologically similar to oneself — and this difference cannot be captured in terms of psychological continuity.

**What the Thought Experiments Show**

Collectively, the puzzle cases reveal a deep tension between psychological and physical criteria for personal identity, and between what the theory says and what we feel about survival. Their philosophical significance is disputed:

Some philosophers (following Parfit) think they reveal that personal identity is not what matters and that the truth about personal identity can be indeterminate without this being practically significant.

Others think they reveal that our ordinary concept of personal identity is confused or incoherent and needs revision.

Still others think they reveal the limits of thought experiments: these are sufficiently unlike anything in our actual experience that our intuitions about them are unreliable guides to the metaphysics of personal identity.
