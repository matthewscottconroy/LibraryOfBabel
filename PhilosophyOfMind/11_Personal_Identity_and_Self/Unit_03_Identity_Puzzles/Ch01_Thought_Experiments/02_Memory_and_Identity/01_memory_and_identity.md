# Memory and Personal Identity

Memory is central to most philosophical accounts of personal identity and to our ordinary sense of who we are. The recollection of past experiences ties the present self to the past self; autobiographical memory is the material from which the narrative self is constructed. But memory is also fallible, reconstructive, and subject to distortion — and these facts raise difficult questions about the relationship between memory and identity.

**Locke's Memory Criterion**

Locke held that personal identity consists in the extension of consciousness backwards through memory: I am the same person as the person who performed a past action if I remember performing that action (or remember remembering it, in a chain). This makes memory the criterion and constitution of personal identity.

The memory criterion has intuitive appeal: we ordinarily use memory as evidence of personal identity, and losing memory — through amnesia, dementia, or other disruption — intuitively threatens the continuity of the self. The person with severe Alzheimer's who cannot recognize family members or recall their own past seems, in some real sense, to be a different person from the person they were.

But the memory criterion faces serious challenges:

*Circularity*: If memory is what constitutes personal identity, and we identify a "memory" as a genuine memory (rather than a false belief about the past) partly by checking whether the event remembered actually happened to the person who remembers it, then we seem to be going in a circle. Genuine memory of one's own past presupposes personal identity rather than constituting it.

*Transitivity failure* (Reid's objection): Genuine memory is transitive, but memory connections between stages of a single life may not be. The brave officer remembers the flogging; the old general remembers the officer's bravery; but the old general does not remember the flogging. If memory is transitive and the chain is memory, the old general should remember the flogging — but he does not.

**False Memories and Identity**

The falsifiability of memory creates further challenges. People can have false memories — confident recollections of events that did not occur or did not occur as remembered. Memory is reconstructive, not reproductive: it does not replay stored recordings but reconstructs events on the basis of partial information, schemas, and current knowledge.

Elizabeth Loftus's research demonstrated that false memories can be implanted through suggestion: people can come to sincerely believe they remember events that were entirely fabricated. This raises the question: if my memories of my past are false, does that undermine my identity as the person who (actually) had those experiences?

The memory criterion seems committed to the surprising answer: if what matters is memory rather than actual experience, then the person with false memories might be more continuous with a fiction than with actual past events. This seems backward — what we actually experienced seems to matter more than what we (falsely) remember.

This suggests that what is important is not memory per se but causal continuity with past experience. The kind of memory that matters for identity is not just any belief about the past but beliefs caused in the right way by the relevant past experiences.

**Amnesia and the Self**

Severe amnesia provides cases in which the memory criterion is tested against lived reality. Patient H.M., who had his hippocampus removed, was unable to form new long-term memories after the surgery. Each time he woke, he remembered his pre-surgery life but not anything that had happened since. In what sense was he the same person from day to day?

H.M. retained procedural memory (he could learn new motor skills) and could recognize and respond normally to people he encountered repeatedly, even without explicitly remembering previous encounters. He was clearly continuous in biological terms; the question is whether his inability to form episodic memories disrupted his personal identity in the relevant sense.

Different theories give different answers. The biological continuity theory says no: he was the same animal throughout. The psychological continuity theory might say yes: the relevant psychological connections (episodic memories binding each day to the next) were absent. The phenomenological theory would focus on whether his first-person sense of self — the minimal self — was intact, and it appears it was.

**Memory and the Narrative Self**

For narrative theories of identity, autobiographical memory is the material from which the narrative self is constructed. A coherent, integrated autobiography requires not just the storage of past episodes but their organization into a story that makes sense of who one is and how one came to be that way.

The relationship between memory and narrative is not simply that memory provides raw data for the narrative. Rather, narrative shapes which memories are salient, how they are interpreted, and what significance they are given. We remember differently in light of the narratives we tell about ourselves, and we revise the narrative in light of what we come to remember or discover about the past.

This mutual shaping of memory and narrative means that personal identity is not simply given by one's memories but is actively constructed through the ongoing work of self-interpretation — through the stories we tell and revise about who we have been and who we are becoming.
