# Further Reading: Thought Experiments in Personal Identity

## Primary Sources

**Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons*, Part III (1984)** — The indispensable source for thought experiments in personal identity, including fission cases (splitting one person into two by dividing the brain), the teletransportation paradox, and gradual replacement scenarios. Parfit uses these cases to argue that identity is not what matters in survival—psychological continuity without numerical identity can be all that matters practically.

**Robert Nozick, *Philosophical Explanations*, Ch. 1 (1981)** — Nozick develops the closest continuer theory of personal identity, holding that a later person is you if they are the closest continuer of your current psychological states, provided they are close enough. This theory handles fission cases by stipulating that if two continuers are equally close, neither is you, an implication that shows identity has a competitive structure.

**Sydney Shoemaker, *Self-Knowledge and Self-Identity* (1963)** — Contains influential thought experiments including the brain-transplant case, which Shoemaker uses to argue that what makes a body "your" body is its containing your brain—a move that favors psychological over bodily continuity. A founding text for post-Lockean psychological continuity theories.

**John Perry (ed.), *Personal Identity* (1975)** — An anthology that collects the key texts from Locke through Parfit alongside classic articles by Williams, Shoemaker, and others. Perry's editorial introductions remain among the clearest guides to the dialectic, and his own essay "A Dialogue on Personal Identity and Immortality" is a model of thought-experimental reasoning.

**Bernard Williams, "The Self and the Future" (1970), *Philosophical Review*** — Williams presents a pair of cases designed to show that both psychological and bodily continuity are independently compelling criteria for identity, generating contradictory verdicts on the same case depending on how it is described. This paper is essential for understanding the tension between different intuitions and the limitations of thought experiments as evidence.

## Secondary Literature

**Susan Schneider, *The Philosophy of "Her"* (2014)** — Uses science-fiction scenarios analogous to fission and teletransportation to make Parfitian questions vivid for contemporary readers. Shows how personal identity debates intersect with questions about artificial minds and digital uploading.

**Jeff McMahan, *The Ethics of Killing: Problems at the Margins of Life* (2002)** — Applies psychological continuity theories and Parfit-inspired accounts to ethical questions about killing, the badness of death, and moral status at the margins of life. Shows the practical stakes of metaphysical choices about personal identity.

**Harold Noonan, *Personal Identity* (2nd ed., 2003)** — The clearest survey of the main positions and their responses to canonical thought experiments, including careful analysis of the closest continuer theory and its competitors.

**Mark Johnston, "Fission and the Facts" (1989), *Philosophical Perspectives*** — A detailed analysis of what Parfit's fission argument actually establishes and whether it shows that identity is not what matters. Johnston argues for a more nuanced view about the relationship between identity and what matters, challenging Parfit's reductionism.

**David Lewis, "Survival and Identity" (1976)** — Lewis proposes a four-dimensionalist solution to fission cases: the two post-fission persons are different temporal stages of overlapping four-dimensional worms, both of which existed before the fission. This solution preserves the transitivity of identity but requires accepting a pluralism about persons that many find counterintuitive.
