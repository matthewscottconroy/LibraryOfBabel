# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Thought Experiments in Personal Identity

## Key Arguments

**Parfit's Argument That Identity Is Not What Matters**
Parfit argues that once we appreciate the structure of psychological continuity, we should conclude that personal identity itself — strict numerical identity — is not what matters in survival. What rationally matters is psychological continuity and connectedness, which can hold in degrees and can be branched (as in fission). Since identity cannot branch but psychological continuity can, and since the latter is what grounds prudential concern, special obligations, and anticipation, identity drops out as a further relevant fact.

**The Closest Continuer Theory (Nozick)**
Robert Nozick argues that personal identity over time consists in being the person most psychologically and causally continuous with a past person — the "closest continuer." The theory handles fission cases by stipulating that if there are two equally close continuers, neither is identical to the original (since identity is one-one). Critics object that identity should not depend on what happens to rivals; whether I survive should not vary with whether someone else has a similar brain transplant.

**The Transitivity Problem for Psychological Continuity**
Thomas Reid's prince-cobbler case and subsequent analyses show that if personal identity is equated with direct psychological connectivity (memory links), and if connectivity is not transitive (an old general may remember being a young officer who remembered being a boy, but the general does not remember being the boy), then identity fails to be transitive. Since identity is necessarily transitive, either psychological connectivity must be replaced by a transitive relation (psychological continuity via chains) or the criterion fails.

**The No-Branching Constraint**
Many psychological continuity theorists add a no-branching constraint: psychological continuity preserves identity only when it does not branch into two survivors. This handles fission by saying neither resulting person is the original (since both satisfy continuity equally and identity is one-one). But critics, including Parfit, point out that adding this constraint makes identity depend on extrinsic facts — the existence of a rival — which seems metaphysically arbitrary.

## Core Concepts

**Psychological Connectedness**
Psychological connectedness is the direct relation between two person-stages when one remembers the other's experiences, carries out the other's intentions, or retains the other's character traits and beliefs. It is distinguished from psychological continuity by Parfit: connectedness holds between adjacent stages and need not be transitive, while continuity is the transitive closure of connectedness chains. Strong connectedness correlates with what we typically care about in close personal relationships and prudential concern.

**Closest Continuer Theory**
Nozick's closest continuer theory holds that x at time t2 is the same person as y at t1 if and only if x is sufficiently continuous with y and no other person is equally or more continuous with y. The theory is designed to give intuitively correct verdicts in standard cases while handling problem cases like fission by denying identity where continuity branches. Its reliance on comparative facts about rivals generates the objection that identity should be an intrinsic matter.

**Fission**
In the context of personal identity, fission refers to cases where a person's psychological and/or neurological basis is divided and each part sustains a distinct living organism, each of which is psychologically continuous with the original. Fission is philosophically significant because it tests whether psychological continuity can be both necessary and sufficient for identity: if both resulting persons are continuous with the original but not with each other, standard criteria either deliver the wrong result (two people are one person) or require stipulation (neither is the original).

**What Matters in Survival**
Parfit's technical term for the relation that grounds rational anticipation, prudential concern, and special self-regarding obligations. He argues that what matters is not strict numerical identity but rather psychological continuity and connectedness (Relation R). Because Relation R can hold in degrees and can branch, recognizing it as what matters rather than identity is practically revisionary: we should be less concerned about our distant future selves and more concerned about other people than ordinary self-interest permits.

**The No-Branching Constraint**
The no-branching constraint is an addendum to psychological continuity criteria stipulating that continuity preserves identity only if it does not produce more than one successor. It is required to make the psychological continuity criterion yield the right result (no identity) in fission cases. Parfit objects that it makes identity an extrinsic matter — whether I survive a brain transplant depends on whether the other hemisphere is also transplanted — which seems deeply counterintuitive.

## Thought Experiments

**The Brain Bisection and Transplant (Parfit)**
Parfit imagines that your brain is divided into its two hemispheres, each transplanted into a distinct brainless body, and both resulting persons wake up with your memories and psychological profile. By symmetry, neither has a stronger claim to be you than the other; yet they cannot both be you (since they are not identical to each other). This puzzle drives Parfit's conclusion that strict identity must give way to what matters — Relation R — and that the original person has, in some sense, survived as both while being strictly identical to neither.

**Teletransportation (Parfit)**
A teletransportation device scans your complete physical and psychological state, transmits the information, and reconstitutes a qualitatively identical person at a destination — while destroying the original. Most find it initially unclear whether this is death followed by creation of a replica or genuine personal survival. When Parfit adds a variant where the original is not destroyed before the replica arrives, intuitions often shift toward denial of identity, revealing that what we care about in survival tracks causal-psychological continuity rather than strict identity.

**The Cobbler-Prince Exchange (Locke/Shoemaker)**
Locke's original thought experiment imagines the soul (or consciousness) of a prince waking in the body of a cobbler; Sydney Shoemaker's version has the brain (carrying all psychological states) transplanted from one body to another. Both cases are designed to isolate the psychological from the bodily and test which we follow in re-identifying persons. The near-universal intuition that the person follows the psychology rather than the body is the chief evidence for psychological continuity theories.

**The Gradually Replaced Ship of Theseus Applied to Persons (Gradual Brain Replacement)**
Imagine that your neurons are replaced one by one with functionally identical silicon chips: at what point, if any, does the original person cease to exist? If psychological continuity is preserved throughout, psychological criteria must say the person persists; if there is a threshold, where is it and why? The case tests the viability of any gradual-replacement criterion and highlights the tension between physical substrate intuitions and functional/psychological continuity intuitions.

**Nozick's Branch-Line Case**
Nozick imagines a case where teletransportation produces a replica while the original survives the process (the original's body is not destroyed). Now there are two persons, each equally continuous with the pre-transport person. The closest continuer theory delivers the verdict that neither is the original (since neither is closer than the other), while many feel the original is the one who did not undergo the process. The case generates pressure on all theories that make identity depend on comparative closeness to rivals.
