# Key Thinkers: Thought Experiments in Personal Identity

---

**Derek Parfit (1942–2017)**
British analytic philosopher at All Souls College, Oxford. Parfit's thought experiments in *Reasons and Persons* (1984) transformed the field by showing that fission cases—where one person's brain is divided and transplanted into two bodies, yielding two psychologically continuous successors—reveal that personal identity is not what matters in survival. His teletransportation paradox presses the question whether exact psychological replication with physical discontinuity preserves identity. Parfit concluded with "branch-line reductionism": the self is not a separately existing entity, and we should care about psychological connectedness rather than strict identity—a conclusion with radical implications for rationality and ethics.
*Key works:* *Reasons and Persons* (1984), "Personal Identity" (1971), "Divided Minds and the Nature of Persons" (1987)

---

**Robert Nozick (1938–2002)**
American philosopher at Harvard University, known for work in political philosophy and metaphysics. Nozick's closest continuer theory holds that personal identity consists in being the closest continuer of a prior person's psychological states, subject to a threshold of closeness. His innovation over simple psychological continuity theories is the competitive element: if two candidates are equally close continuers, neither is the original person. The theory handles fission gracefully and has been influential in showing that identity may be a matter of degree and relativity to context.
*Key works:* *Philosophical Explanations* (1981), *The Nature of Rationality* (1993)

---

**Bernard Williams (1929–2003)**
British moral philosopher at Cambridge and Berkeley. Williams' thought experiments were designed to show the limits of psychological continuity theories by eliciting conflicting intuitions. His "torture case" and "body-swap" arguments suggest that bodily continuity cannot be eliminated from our concept of personal identity as easily as neo-Lockeans suppose: when we imagine "our" future body being tortured, we care whether it is our body regardless of psychological continuity. Williams also provided important critiques of Parfit's reductionism, defending the importance of personal identity for ethics.
*Key works:* "The Self and the Future" (1970), *Problems of the Self* (1973), *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* (1985)

---

**David Lewis (1941–2001)**
American analytic philosopher at Princeton, known for his defense of modal realism and four-dimensionalism. Lewis's solution to Parfit's fission puzzle was to accept that persons are four-dimensional entities (worms) with temporal parts: in fission, two person-worms exist before the split, temporarily sharing their earlier temporal parts. This view preserves the transitivity and determinateness of identity while acknowledging that a single momentary stage can belong to more than one person-worm. Lewis's solution is metaphysically costly but logically elegant, and it forced sharper articulation of what exactly is at issue in puzzles about fission.
*Key works:* "Survival and Identity" (1976), *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), *Parts of Classes* (1991)

---

**Sydney Shoemaker (1931–)**
American analytic philosopher at Cornell University. Shoemaker's brain-transplant thought experiments helped establish that psychological continuity, not bodily continuity, is what matters for personal identity: if your brain were transplanted into another body, the resulting person would be you, not the other body's original occupant. He also developed the self-blindness argument—the claim that genuine self-knowledge requires immunity to error through misidentification—and explored how psychological continuity is causally grounded. His careful methodology showed how thought experiments can be used as controlled tests of conceptual claims.
*Key works:* *Self-Knowledge and Self-Identity* (1963), *Personal Identity* (with Swinburne, 1984)
