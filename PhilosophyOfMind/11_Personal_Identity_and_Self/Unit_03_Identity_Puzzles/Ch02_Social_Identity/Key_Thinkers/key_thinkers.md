# Key Thinkers: Social Identity

---

**Kwame Anthony Appiah (1954–)**
Ghanaian-British-American philosopher at New York University, working in ethics, political philosophy, and philosophy of race. Appiah's *The Ethics of Identity* (2005) examines how social identities—racial, national, religious, gender—make claims on individuals and how individuals can navigate these claims while maintaining authentic selfhood. He defends a liberal view that social identities matter but should not be allowed to fully determine individuals, arguing for the revision rather than rejection of identity scripts. His earlier work on racial identity (*In My Father's House*, 1992) was among the first rigorous philosophical treatments of race as a social category.
*Key works:* *In My Father's House* (1992), *The Ethics of Identity* (2005), *Cosmopolitanism* (2006)

---

**Judith Butler (1956–)**
American philosopher at UC Berkeley, one of the most influential theorists of gender and performativity. Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) argued that gender identity is not an expression of an inner essence but a performance—constituted through the repeated citation of gender norms. This view denaturalizes gender categories while explaining why they feel natural and necessary. Her later work on precarity and vulnerability has extended these insights to questions about race, national identity, and the conditions of livable life.
*Key works:* *Gender Trouble* (1990), *Bodies That Matter* (1993), *Undoing Gender* (2004)

---

**Charles Taylor (1931–)**
Canadian philosopher at McGill University, known for his work in political philosophy, philosophy of social science, and moral philosophy. Taylor's account of identity is inherently dialogical: who we are is always partly constituted through our relations with others and our standing in communities. His concept of "the politics of recognition" argues that misrecognition—being seen through distorting, demeaning social lenses—causes genuine harm to identity and that liberal societies must engage with demands for recognition from minority cultures. His broader historical and moral philosophy frames identity within the moral frameworks that give our lives meaning.
*Key works:* *Sources of the Self* (1989), "The Politics of Recognition" (1994), *A Secular Age* (2007)

---

**Kimberlé Crenshaw (1959–)**
American legal scholar and critical race theorist at UCLA and Columbia Law Schools. Crenshaw coined the term "intersectionality" to describe the way that multiple axes of social identity—race, gender, class—interact and compound in producing distinctive forms of oppression and privilege. Her analysis of how Black women's experiences fall through the cracks of both anti-racist and feminist frameworks showed that identity categories cannot be analyzed in isolation. Intersectionality has become one of the most widely used analytical frameworks in social theory, law, and feminist philosophy.
*Key works:* "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" (1989), "Mapping the Margins" (1991)

---

**Sally Haslanger (1955–)**
American philosopher at MIT specializing in social ontology, feminist philosophy, and philosophy of race. Haslanger defends a critical social constructionism about race and gender: these categories are real, but their reality is a social and political achievement that can and should be revised. Her "ameliorative analysis" approach asks not what gender or race "really" are but what concepts we need to advance justice. She has also developed accounts of social structures and how they constrain and enable social identities beyond individual intentions.
*Key works:* *Resisting Reality* (2012), "Gender and Race: (What) Are They? (What) Do We Want Them to Be?" (2000)
