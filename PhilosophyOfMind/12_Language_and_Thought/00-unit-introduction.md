# Unit Introduction: Language and Thought

## Central Questions

What is the relationship between language and thought? This question has at least three distinct aspects. First, is natural language the *medium* of thought — do we think *in* English or French or Mandarin — or is thought a language-independent representational system that natural language expresses? Second, does linguistic structure *shape* or *constrain* thought — does speaking a language with certain grammatical features make certain thoughts easier or harder to think? And third, what is the relationship between the *concepts* expressed by language and thought itself — how do we acquire concepts, and can concepts be possessed by non-linguistic creatures?

Jerry Fodor's Language of Thought hypothesis holds that thought requires a system of mental representations with language-like compositional structure — a *Mentalese* distinct from any natural language. This view makes natural language secondary: it is a medium for expressing thoughts already formed in Mentalese. The contrasting view — sometimes called *linguistic relativity* or the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis — holds that natural language shapes thought, so that speakers of different languages think differently about the same domain. Contemporary empirical work has found modest but genuine cross-linguistic effects on thought, a position sometimes called *weak linguistic relativity*.

A third set of questions concerns *inner speech*: much of our phenomenal experience includes a stream of verbalization — we talk to ourselves, run through arguments in our heads, rehearse what we will say. What is the cognitive function of inner speech? Is it constitutive of thought, or merely one mode of expression? And does the phenomenology of inner speech tell us anything about the relationship between language and consciousness?

## Major Positions and Debates

### Fodor's Language of Thought

Fodor's LOT hypothesis was discussed in Unit 05 as a theory of mental representation. Its implications for the relationship between language and thought are specific: natural language (English, Mandarin) is a *public* language used for communication; the medium of thought is a *private* language, Mentalese, which is not learned but innate and which has its own compositional structure. Thinking in English is not thinking in Mentalese but expressing Mentalese thoughts in English.

A consequence of LOT is that concept acquisition is not possible through language learning: one cannot learn a new primitive concept (one not already in Mentalese) from linguistic exposure. Fodor takes this implication seriously in his late work *Concepts: Where Cognitive Science Went Wrong* (1998), where he argues that all lexical concepts are innate. This *radical concept nativism* is widely regarded as implausible, but the argument that drives it — that there is no satisfactory non-circular account of concept learning — has not been decisively answered.

### Wittgenstein on Private Language

Wittgenstein's private language argument (*Philosophical Investigations*, §§243-315) is one of the most contested passages in twentieth-century philosophy. Wittgenstein argues that a genuinely private language — one that refers to objects directly available only to the speaker, such as private sensations — is impossible. The impossibility argument turns on the conditions for following a rule: to apply a term correctly, there must be a criterion of correctness, but a genuinely private criterion cannot be distinguished from seeming correct.

The private language argument has been interpreted in at least three ways: (1) as an argument against phenomenal consciousness (Kripke's skeptical reading); (2) as an argument against Cartesian inner objects as the referents of sensation words (Malcolm's reading); (3) as an argument about the social character of meaning — language is essentially public and its meaning depends on social practices (the community reading). Each interpretation has different implications for philosophy of mind: the first directly threatens qualia; the third challenges LOT (if the language of thought is genuinely private, how can it have determinate meaning?).

### Linguistic Relativity

The strong Sapir-Whorf hypothesis — that language determines thought, so that speakers of different languages have different thoughts and cannot think thoughts inexpressible in their language — is generally regarded as false. The weak version — that language *influences* (but does not determine) thought — has received modest empirical support. Languages vary in their color categories, and speakers of languages with more basic color terms show faster discrimination at linguistically marked color boundaries. Languages vary in their spatial encoding (absolute vs. relative frames of reference), and this correlates with performance on non-linguistic spatial tasks.

Whether these effects show that language shapes conceptual categories or merely perceptual/attentional strategies is contested. The philosophical significance of linguistic relativity depends partly on what the effects show: if they show that the very concepts speakers have differ across languages, they challenge the idea of a universal conceptual structure; if they show only that attention and memory strategies differ, they are compatible with a universal conceptual core.

### Concept Empiricism vs. Rationalism

Concept empiricism holds that all concepts are ultimately derived from experience — whether sensory experience (classical empiricism) or more broadly understood perceptual experience (Barsalou's grounded cognition). The classic rationalist alternative (Fodor's radical concept nativism) holds that at least some concepts are innate. Contemporary *concept empiricism* draws on simulation theories of cognition: understanding a concept involves simulating the experiences associated with it, using sensorimotor systems. Understanding the concept RED involves activating perceptual representations of redness; understanding the concept GRASP involves activating motor representations of grasping.

This debate has significant empirical dimension: neuroimaging studies show that understanding action verbs activates motor cortex areas, and understanding color words activates visual cortex areas. But these findings underdetermine the theoretical question: is activation of sensorimotor systems constitutive of concept possession, or merely a correlate of it?

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**Jerry Fodor** is central through his LOT hypothesis and his work on concepts. *The Language of Thought* (1975) and *Concepts: Where Cognitive Science Went Wrong* (1998) are the essential texts.

**Ludwig Wittgenstein** (*Philosophical Investigations*, 1953) provides the most important philosophical discussion of the relationship between language and thought. The private language argument and the discussions of rule-following are essential for understanding the limits of the LOT approach.

**Noam Chomsky** provided the linguistic infrastructure for LOT through his arguments for a universal grammar: if all natural languages share a common deep structure, this supports the idea of a universal Mentalese. The poverty of the stimulus argument — children acquire complex grammatical knowledge on the basis of limited linguistic input — supports nativism about language and (by extension) about cognition.

**Saul Kripke** (*Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*, 1982) gave the most influential interpretation of Wittgenstein's private language argument as a skeptical challenge to rule-following, with implications for the possibility of determinate mental content.

**Lawrence Barsalou** developed grounded (or embodied) concept theory, arguing that concepts are not arbitrary symbols but are grounded in perceptual and sensorimotor systems. His simulation hypothesis is the most influential contemporary version of concept empiricism.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Fodor, *The Language of Thought* (1975)**
See Unit 05. Essential here for its implications about the independence of thought from natural language and its commitment to radical concept nativism.

**2. Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* (1953)**
Sections on rule-following (§§138-242) and the private language argument (§§243-315) are essential. The *Investigations* cannot be summarized; they must be engaged with directly. A reliable secondary guide (Glock, Baker and Hacker) is advisable for first-time readers.

**3. Kripke, *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982)**
A contentious but enormously influential reading of Wittgenstein. Even if Kripke's interpretation is wrong, his presentation of the rule-following skeptical paradox is philosophically important in its own right.

**4. Whorf, "Science and Linguistics" (1940)**
The foundational statement of linguistic relativity. Whorf's claims about Hopi time have been largely debunked, but the hypothesis has been refined by contemporary researchers (Boroditsky, Regier, Kay) into testable and supported versions. Essential for understanding the history and current state of the debate.

**5. Barsalou, "Perceptual Symbol Systems" (1999)**
The most cited contemporary paper in grounded cognition. Barsalou argues that mental representations are not amodal abstract symbols but *perceptual symbols* — partial records of perceptual states stored in sensorimotor areas. This paper is the foundation for an extensive empirical research program.

*Cognitive Architecture* (Unit 09) is connected: if thoughts have language-like combinatorial structure (LOT), this constrains the cognitive architecture required to process them. *Phenomenology* (Unit 15) bears on inner speech through the phenomenological description of internal time-consciousness: inner speech, like all consciousness, is temporally extended in a distinctive way that Husserl's account illuminates.

## Open Questions

**1. Is there a language of thought?**
Fodor's productivity and systematicity arguments for LOT are the best positive case available. But the success of neural networks — which achieve cognitive performances without explicit symbolic structures — weakens the inference from cognitive productivity to LOT. Whether cognitive science needs a language-of-thought hypothesis, or whether the relevant cognitive performances can be explained without it, is genuinely open.

**2. Does language shape thought or merely reflect it?**
The empirical findings on linguistic relativity are real but modest. Do they show that language shapes conceptual categories, or only that it affects attentional strategies and memory encoding? The difference matters: if language shapes concepts, speakers of different languages think differently; if it only affects attention, the conceptual core is universal and the effects are peripheral.

**3. What is the relationship between inner speech and consciousness?**
Much phenomenal experience involves a stream of inner verbalization. Is this constitutive of self-consciousness (as Vygotsky's work on the social origins of inner speech suggests) or merely one of many formats in which thoughts can occur? Whether inner speech is necessary for self-consciousness and reflective thought is both an empirical and a philosophical question.

**4. Can there be thought without language?**
The evidence that pre-linguistic infants and non-human animals have representations of number, object permanence, and social relations suggests that some conceptual thought is possible without language. But there may be forms of thought — highly abstract, counterfactual, or self-reflective — that require language. Where exactly the boundary lies is unclear.

**5. What is the philosophical significance of aphasia?**
Patients with Broca's aphasia have severely impaired speech production while retaining apparently intact semantic understanding; patients with Wernicke's aphasia can speak fluently but with semantic errors while understanding is impaired. What do these dissociations tell us about the relationship between language and thought? The clinical and neuroscientific evidence bears on the philosophical question but does not determine a unique answer.
