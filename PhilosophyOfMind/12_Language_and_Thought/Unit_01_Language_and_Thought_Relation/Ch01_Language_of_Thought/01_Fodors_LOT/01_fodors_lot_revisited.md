# Fodor's Language of Thought: The Deeper Case

The Language of Thought hypothesis (LOT), associated primarily with Jerry Fodor, proposes that thinking is a form of computation over a mental language — "Mentalese" — with a compositional, symbol-manipulating structure analogous to natural language. The hypothesis has been discussed in the context of mental representation; here we focus on its role in the philosophy of language and thought, particularly its claims about the relationship between natural language and the underlying medium of cognition.

**The Primacy of Thought Over Language**

Fodor's LOT implies that natural language is not the vehicle of thought but its expression. Thought occurs in Mentalese; natural language serves to communicate those thoughts to others. This reverses the view (associated with Wittgenstein and Sapir-Whorf) that language shapes or constitutes thought.

The argument for this reversal: learning a natural language requires that the learner form hypotheses about the meanings of words — that they have the concept of a dog before they learn the word "dog," so that they can test hypotheses about whether "dog" refers to that concept. If the learner's concepts were given by natural language rather than prior to it, there would be no conceptual content available to attach to linguistic forms. Learning language presupposes a prior medium of thought.

This argument has been contested. Critics (particularly connectionists and Wittgensteinians) hold that conceptual mastery is constitutively tied to linguistic practice — that having the concept of a dog is, in large part, knowing how to use the word "dog" in the relevant linguistic community. The Fodorian picture of a pre-linguistic Mentalese may be a theoretical artifact rather than a psychological reality.

**Mentalese and Logical Form**

One of the most important motivations for LOT is the need to explain the logical structure of thought. We can think thoughts with the logical form of first-order predicate calculus: for any x, if x is a raven and x has been observed, then x is black. We can make inferences that respect this logical structure. This logical sensitivity suggests that the medium of thought has the relevant syntactic structure — there is something like quantifiers, predicates, and connectives in Mentalese.

Natural language is a plausible candidate for this structural role, but Fodor argues that natural language is systematically ambiguous and context-sensitive in ways that the medium of thought must not be. Logical form in thought must be unambiguous and explicit; natural language relies on context and pragmatics to fix meaning in ways that the underlying thought does not. So Mentalese is more like logical form than like surface natural language.

**What LOT Does Not Imply**

Fodor's LOT does not imply that all thought is in natural language or that we verbally rehearse all our thoughts. Much of thought is non-conscious and non-verbal: the visual system's computations, the motor system's planning, the emotional responses that shape behavior. These are subpersonal computational processes that fit the LOT framework (they compute over representations) but are not accessible as verbal thoughts.

Even at the personal level, Fodor allows that many concepts and inferences are not consciously entertained as sentences. The LOT is a claim about the underlying computational structure, not about the phenomenal character of thought. Whether thoughts are phenomenally experienced as verbal or imagistic or neither is a separate question from whether their underlying computational structure is sentential.

**Critiques from Language and Thought Relations**

Wittgenstein raised a deep challenge to any picture that makes language merely expressive of pre-linguistic thought: understanding a word is not having a mental image or an inner state that the word "stands for." Understanding is a form of knowing how to use the word in practice, embedded in a form of life. The attempt to identify the meaning of a word with a mental representation (whether Mentalese or an image) generates the "private language" problem: what fixes the identity of the mental representation across uses?

This Wittgensteinian challenge is not just about natural language but about the LOT itself: what makes a Mentalese symbol mean what it does rather than something else? Fodor's own asymmetric dependence theory (discussed in the section on mental content) is his attempt to answer this.

**Language as a Cognitive Tool**

A middle position between strong LOT (natural language is mere expression of pre-linguistic Mentalese) and strong Sapir-Whorf (language constitutes thought) holds that natural language is a cognitive tool that extends and enriches thinking without being necessary for it. Linguistic labels help organize concepts, facilitate inference, and enable communication in ways that extend and stabilize cognition beyond what would be possible without language.

This view is compatible with some version of LOT (for basic concepts and reasoning) while acknowledging that natural language shapes higher cognition in substantive ways. The concepts available in one's language affect the ease and precision with which certain distinctions can be drawn, without necessarily constituting thought from the ground up.
