# Critiques of the Language of Thought Hypothesis

The Language of Thought (LOT) hypothesis has attracted sustained criticism from multiple philosophical and empirical directions. Understanding these critiques clarifies both what the hypothesis claims and what would be required for an adequate account of the relationship between language and thought.

**The Systematicity Debate Revisited**

Fodor and Pylyshyn's systematicity argument was an argument for LOT: the systematicity of thought (anyone who can think that aRb can think that bRa) is best explained by compositional, language-like mental representations. But critics argue that systematicity is an empirical fact about creatures with the right kind of training, not an architectural necessity.

Connectionists have argued that systematicity can emerge from associative learning without requiring compositional, language-like structure. A neural network trained on the right corpus of examples can exhibit systematic behavior — generating appropriate novel combinations — through learned statistical associations rather than through the application of compositional rules.

Whether this is the right kind of systematicity for thought — whether it captures the relevant cognitive reality — remains contested. Fodor insisted that genuine systematicity is an architectural property (you have it or you don't) rather than a learned regularity that might break down at the edges. Demonstrating that connectionist systems fail at the margins of systematicity (at unusual or highly novel combinations) would support the LOT position.

**The Wittgensteinian Challenge**

Wittgenstein's later philosophy raises challenges that are not always framed as critiques of LOT but apply with force. The picture theory of meaning — the idea that a mental symbol gets its meaning by "picturing" or "representing" an external state of affairs — assumes that meaning can be fixed by some inner mental state. But Wittgenstein argued that this assumption generates the "private language" problem: there is no such thing as a private rule, because the correctness of rule-following requires the possibility of correction, which requires a community.

Applied to LOT: what fixes the meaning of a Mentalese symbol? If it is some further inner state, we face a regress. If it is the causal connection to the world (as Fodor's asymmetric dependence theory proposes), then the meaning of Mentalese symbols is not intrinsic but relational — which is fine, but it means that the story about how symbols get meaning is doing all the work, not the syntactic structure of Mentalese.

**The Concept Acquisition Problem**

One of the deepest challenges to LOT is the problem of concept acquisition. Fodor's own analysis of the structure of concepts (as being largely simple and unanalyzable) led him to the disturbing conclusion that most concepts are innate: if complex concepts can be learned by breaking them down into simpler components, and if those simpler components cannot be learned by breaking them down further, then the simpler concepts must be innate.

Fodor bit this bullet in his "extreme nativism" — proposing that most human concepts (including highly specific concepts like CARBURETOR and MICROWAVE) are innate, triggered by experience rather than learned. This conclusion is almost universally regarded as absurd, and it puts pressure on the LOT framework that generates it.

The challenge: any adequate theory of concepts needs to explain how concepts are acquired through experience without falling back on the implausible nativism that LOT's analysis of concept structure seems to require.

**Embodied and Distributed Critiques**

Embodied and situated cognitive scientists argue that LOT reflects a deeply mistaken picture of cognitive architecture. On the embodied view, concepts are not syntactic symbols stored in a central cognitive system; they are distributed patterns of activation across sensorimotor systems, grounded in the body's interaction with the world.

Barsalou's perceptual symbols theory directly challenges LOT: representations are not amodal, abstract, language-like symbols but modal, grounded, perceptual states. The concept DOG is not a Mentalese formula but a pattern of reactivation of visual, auditory, and motor cortex associated with dogs. On this view, thought is not sentential computation over abstract symbols; it is simulation and re-enactment of experience.

This anti-LOT view has predictive content: if concepts are modal and grounded, then conceptual processing should involve motor and sensory brain areas. There is substantial evidence for this prediction, though interpreting it in terms of the constitutive role versus merely facilitative role of sensorimotor systems in cognition remains contested.

**The Productivity Challenge**

LOT's appeal to the productivity of thought — the fact that we can entertain indefinitely many novel thoughts — is meant to require compositional mental syntax. But productivity does not require a language-like medium of thought; it can be achieved by systems that generate novel outputs through recombination and learning in various ways.

The deeper challenge is whether productivity is a genuine cognitive constraint or an idealization. Real human thought is not infinitely productive; it is constrained by working memory capacity, domain specificity, and the limits of training and experience. The idealized productivity that LOT invokes may not accurately describe actual cognitive capacities.
