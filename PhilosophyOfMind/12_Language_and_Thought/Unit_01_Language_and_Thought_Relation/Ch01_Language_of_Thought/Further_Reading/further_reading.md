# Further Reading: Language of Thought

## Primary Sources

**Jerry Fodor, *The Language of Thought* (1975)** — The foundational text for the Language of Thought Hypothesis (LOTH), arguing that mental processes are computations over syntactically structured mental representations with semantic content. Fodor contends that thinking requires an inner symbolic medium—"mentalese"—with compositional semantics and that this language of thought explains productivity and systematicity of thought.

**Jerry Fodor, *The Elm and the Expert* (1994)** — A later defense of LOTH against semantic challenges, particularly the problem of intentional content. Fodor argues that mental representations have narrow and wide content, and that causal-computational processes operate on narrow content while semantic evaluation involves wide content—a position with implications for how we understand thought's relationship to language.

**Jerry Fodor and Brian McLaughlin, "Connectionism and the Problem of Systematicity" (1990), *Cognition*** — A sharp critique of connectionist alternatives to LOTH, arguing that connectionist networks cannot account for the systematicity and productivity of thought without implicitly implementing a language of thought. This paper generated a major debate about whether neural network models can replace classical symbolic architectures.

**Andy Clark and Peter Millikan, various** — See Clark, *Associative Engines* (1993), which defends connectionist approaches against the Fodor-McLaughlin critique, arguing that systematicity is an empirical generalization rather than a cognitive necessity and that connectionist models need not implement classical symbolic structures.

**Paul Smolensky, "On the Proper Treatment of Connectionism" (1988), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*** — A major connectionist response to LOTH, arguing that connectionist representations can achieve compositionality through vector operations rather than discrete symbol concatenation. Smolensky's integrated connectionist/symbolic architecture later influenced debates about how far LOTH must be modified.

## Secondary Literature

**Tim Crane, *The Mechanical Mind* (3rd ed., 2015)** — An accessible philosophical introduction to the computational theory of mind and LOTH, critically assessing the arguments for and against the view that thought requires a language-like medium. Suitable for undergraduates and useful as an overview of the field.

**Georges Rey, "The Intentional Inexistence of Belief" (1995)** — An influential defense of LOTH against the view that propositional attitude attributions are merely predictive rather than describing real inner states. Rey argues that the best explanation of behavior requires genuinely language-like mental representations.

**Robert Stalnaker, *Our Knowledge of the Internal World* (2008)** — Develops a sophisticated analysis of mental content that engages with LOTH's commitments about compositionality and systematicity while defending an externalist interpretation of mental representation.

**Steven Pinker, *The Language Instinct* (1994)** — Pinker's influential defense of LOTH and Chomskyan linguistics for a general audience, arguing that language is an instinct built on an underlying language of thought and that mentalese underlies all human cognition regardless of which natural language one speaks.

**Zoltán Gendler Szabó (ed.), *Semantics versus Pragmatics* (2005)** — Contains important essays on the relationship between linguistic meaning and mental content, relevant to understanding what LOTH commits us to about the semantics of mental representations and their compositionality.
