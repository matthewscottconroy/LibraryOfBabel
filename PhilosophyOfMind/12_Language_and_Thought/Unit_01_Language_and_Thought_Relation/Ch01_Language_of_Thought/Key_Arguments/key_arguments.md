# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Language of Thought

## Key Arguments

**The Compositionality Argument for LOTH**
Fodor argues that mental states exhibit compositionality: the content and causal properties of a complex thought are systematically determined by the contents and causal properties of its constituent parts. If thinking "John loves Mary" has different content and causes than "Mary loves John," the difference must be explained by internal structure. The best explanation posits a language of thought — a system of mentally realized symbols with combinatorial syntax — whose compositional structure mirrors the compositionality of thought.

**The Systematicity Argument**
Fodor and Pylyshyn argue that thought exhibits systematicity: any creature that can think "John loves Mary" can also think "Mary loves John." This is not a contingent fact but a necessary structural feature of thought, analogous to the linguistic fact that any speaker who can produce one sentence can produce its reverse. Connectionist networks, they argue, cannot explain systematicity unless they implement a classical symbol system, because distributed activation patterns do not inherently support the required systematic combinatorial structure.

**The Productivity Argument**
Fodor argues that thought is productive: humans can in principle entertain an indefinitely large number of distinct thoughts, including thoughts never before entertained. This productivity requires a generative system — a finite set of primitive concepts and combinatorial rules — that can produce an unbounded number of structured representations. Productivity cannot be explained by a finite inventory of unstructured mental states, so thought must have the architecture of a language.

**The Inferential Coherence Argument**
Fodor argues that mental states exhibit inferential coherence: thoughts that are inferentially related share constituent concepts. If someone who believes "P and Q" thereby also believes "P," the inference is explained by the shared representation "P" appearing in both. Without syntactic structure in mental representations, there is no mechanism to explain why these inferences are reliable and systematic. The language of thought provides the syntactic vehicle for content-preserving transformations.

**Smolensky's Connectionist Challenge**
Paul Smolensky argues that connectionist tensor-product representations can exhibit compositionality and systematicity without implementing classical symbol systems: constituent representations are superimposed in the activation space of a network in a way that allows combinatorial processing. Fodor and Pylyshyn respond that this is at best a weak form of compositionality that does not preserve the full systematicity and productivity of classical language of thought. The debate turns on what counts as genuinely implementing compositional structure.

## Core Concepts

**Language of Thought Hypothesis (LOTH)**
The Language of Thought Hypothesis, developed by Jerry Fodor, is the claim that human cognition involves mental representations with a language-like combinatorial structure: there are primitive mental symbols (concepts) that can be combined by syntactic rules to produce complex representations (thoughts). Computations over these representations are defined over their syntactic form, which explains how mental processes can be both semantically coherent (tracking meaning) and causally efficacious (actually causing behavior).

**Compositionality**
Compositionality is the principle that the content of a complex representation is fully determined by the contents of its parts and the way they are syntactically combined. It is a property of natural languages (the meaning of "black cat" depends on the meanings of "black" and "cat" and their grammatical combination) and, according to LOTH proponents, must also be a property of the language of thought to explain systematic relationships between thoughts.

**Systematicity**
Systematicity, in Fodor and Pylyshyn's sense, is the property of mental capacities whereby the ability to have one thought is necessarily connected with the ability to have systematically related thoughts. If you can think that aRb, you can think that bRa. Fodor argues that any adequate cognitive architecture must explain systematicity, and that only classical symbol-manipulating systems can do so, because in such systems the same representations appear in different structural configurations.

**Mentalese**
Mentalese is the informal name for the hypothetical language of thought posited by LOTH: a system of mental symbols with their own vocabulary and combinatorial syntax, realized in the brain, in which propositional attitudes (beliefs, desires, intentions) are encoded. Mentalese is not a natural language like English; it is claimed to be the universal medium of thought underlying all natural languages and to be in part innate.

**Connectionism**
Connectionism is a family of cognitive architectures modeled on neural networks, in which cognition is implemented as the parallel distributed processing of activation patterns across interconnected units. Connectionist systems do not represent information in discrete symbolic structures but in distributed patterns of activation, making them prima facie incompatible with the language of thought. The debate between classicists (LOTH) and connectionists concerns whether distributed representations can exhibit the systematicity and compositionality that LOTH explains by positing syntactically structured symbols.

**Productivity of Thought**
Productivity is the property of thought systems whereby they can in principle generate and represent an unbounded number of distinct thoughts from finite resources. Analogous to linguistic productivity (a finite grammar generates infinitely many sentences), productivity of thought is taken by Fodor as evidence that the language of thought has a generative combinatorial syntax rather than a finite inventory of primitive, unstructured mental states.

## Thought Experiments

**The Fodorian Productivity Pump**
Fodor asks us to consider that a human being can, in principle, entertain thoughts about any arbitrarily large number — "I wonder whether 17,539 is prime" — demonstrating that there is no finite bound on the complexity of thoughts we can form. This productivity cannot be explained by a finite list of unstructured mental states; it requires a generative system. The thought experiment functions as a reductio of any non-compositional theory of thought.

**The Systematicity Test Case**
Imagine a creature that can think "the dog bit the mailman" but cannot think "the mailman bit the dog." Fodor and Pylyshyn argue that no such creature exists: the cognitive capacity for one is necessarily the cognitive capacity for the other, because both require the same structural resources. Any connectionist architecture that trained on only one of these sentences and therefore could not represent the other would be missing precisely what makes human thought systematic.

**The Alien Cognitive Architecture**
Fodor imagines a cognitive system that is semantically coherent — its states have appropriate content relationships — but lacks syntactic structure. Such a system could not perform systematic inference (since inference requires operating on shared constituents), could not exhibit productivity (since it has no generative rules), and would lose inferential coherence (since there are no shared structural components to preserve across inferences). The case argues that content alone, without syntactic structure, is insufficient for cognitive rationality.
