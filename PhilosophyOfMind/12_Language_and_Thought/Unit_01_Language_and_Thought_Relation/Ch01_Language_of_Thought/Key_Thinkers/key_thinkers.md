# Key Thinkers: Language of Thought

---

**Jerry Fodor (1935–2017)**
American philosopher and cognitive scientist at MIT and the CUNY Graduate Center. Fodor is the principal architect of the Language of Thought Hypothesis, arguing that thought consists in computations over syntactically structured, semantically evaluable mental representations—a "mentalese" that underlies natural language rather than being constituted by it. His related arguments for the modularity of mind and the irreducibility of intentionality to purely functional or causal terms make him the most systematic defender of a broadly rationalist, representational approach to the mind in the twentieth century. His critiques of connectionism and his "asymmetric dependence" theory of mental content remain central reference points.
*Key works:* *The Language of Thought* (1975), *The Modularity of Mind* (1983), *Psychosemantics* (1987), *The Elm and the Expert* (1994)

---

**Noam Chomsky (1928–)**
American linguist and philosopher at MIT. While Chomsky's primary contribution is to linguistics rather than LOTH directly, his arguments for innate, species-specific linguistic competence provided the context in which LOTH became plausible: if the language faculty is a module with innate structure, a deeper system of thought might similarly be innate and structured. His "poverty of the stimulus" argument—that children acquire grammar from insufficient input—supports the idea that knowledge of language (and perhaps thought) is not derived from experience alone.
*Key works:* *Syntactic Structures* (1957), *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965), *The Minimalist Program* (1995)

---

**Paul Smolensky (1955–)**
American cognitive scientist and linguist at Johns Hopkins University. Smolensky developed the "integrated connectionist/symbolic" (ICS) framework as a response to Fodor's critique, arguing that connectionist networks can implement compositional structure through tensor-product representations without requiring classical symbolic manipulation. His debate with Fodor and McLaughlin over whether systematicity requires a language of thought remains one of the central exchanges in philosophy of cognitive science.
*Key works:* "On the Proper Treatment of Connectionism" (1988), *The Harmonic Mind* (with Prince, 2006)

---

**Susan Schneider (1966–)**
American philosopher of mind at the University of Florida and NASA. Schneider has defended and extended LOTH while also exploring its implications for questions about consciousness and artificial intelligence. Her work on the computational nature of the mind addresses how LOTH can be combined with physicalism and what constraints the hypothesis places on the nature of mental content. Her engagement with AI and machine consciousness makes her work uniquely relevant to contemporary applications of LOTH.
*Key works:* *The Language of Thought: A New Philosophical Direction* (2011), *Artificial You* (2019)

---

**Andy Clark (1957–)**
Scottish philosopher and cognitive scientist at the University of Edinburgh. Clark is one of the most influential critics of strong versions of LOTH from a connectionist and embodied-cognition perspective. His work argues that cognition is not merely classical symbolic computation but involves continuous interaction with body and environment, and that connectionist models can account for the features LOTH was introduced to explain. His extended mind thesis further challenges the view that thought must be grounded in internal symbol systems.
*Key works:* *Associative Engines* (1993), *Being There* (1997), *Supersizing the Mind* (2008)
