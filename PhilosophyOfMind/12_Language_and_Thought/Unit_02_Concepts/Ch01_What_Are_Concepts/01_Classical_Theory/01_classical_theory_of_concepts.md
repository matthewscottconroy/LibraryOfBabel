# The Classical Theory of Concepts

The classical theory of concepts holds that concepts have necessary and sufficient conditions for their application — a definition that specifies what every instance of the concept must have and what any instance lacking those features must lack. A concept is a mental representation of a category, and the classical theory says this representation is structured by defining features.

**The Classical Picture**

On the classical view, to possess the concept BACHELOR is to know (implicitly) that bachelors are unmarried adult males. Any unmarried adult male is a bachelor; nothing that is not an unmarried adult male is a bachelor. The concept TRIANGLE is the concept of a three-sided, closed, plane figure. Any three-sided closed plane figure is a triangle; anything lacking any of these features is not. The defining features are necessary and sufficient.

This picture is intuitive and fits certain cases well. Mathematical and logical concepts seem to have clear definitions: EVEN NUMBER (divisible by 2 without remainder), PRIME (divisible only by itself and 1), SQUARE (equilateral rectangle). Legal and technical concepts often have explicit definitions: MURDER, CONTRACT, MOLECULE.

The classical theory fits naturally with the LOT framework: concepts are symbols in Mentalese with definitional structure, and inference consists in applying these definitions to the inputs of perception and memory.

**Wittgenstein's Family Resemblance**

Wittgenstein's later philosophy (particularly the *Philosophical Investigations*) mounted an influential challenge to the classical theory. Consider the concept GAME. What do all games have in common? Some games are competitive; others (Ring Around the Rosie) are not. Some games involve winning; others do not. Some games require skill; others are pure chance. Some games are played solo; others require groups. No single feature or set of features is shared by all games.

What we find instead, Wittgenstein argued, is a pattern of overlapping similarities — "family resemblances" — like the overlapping similarities among members of a family (similar nose, similar gait, similar build, without any single feature shared by all). There is no essence to game-hood; there is only this complex pattern of overlapping similarity.

This challenges the classical theory at its heart: if some ordinary, everyday concepts do not have definitions, the classical theory cannot be a general account of what concepts are.

**Eleanor Rosch and Prototype Theory**

Rosch's empirical research in the 1970s confirmed and extended Wittgenstein's observations. Her studies showed that not all members of a category are equally representative. Robins and sparrows are more typical birds than penguins and ostriches. Chairs are more typical furniture than lamps. Red and orange are more typical colors than chartreuse and magenta.

If concepts had definitions, all instances would be equally members — nothing would be "more or less" a bird. But the typicality gradient is robust and affects cognitive processing: people are faster to verify that robins are birds than that penguins are birds; more typical category members are listed first when people generate instances.

Rosch proposed that categories are structured around prototypes — idealized or most-typical instances — rather than definitions. A bird is anything sufficiently similar to the prototype of birds. Membership is a matter of degree: some things are "good" birds (robins) and some are "marginal" birds (penguins).

**The Classical Theory's Defense**

Despite these challenges, the classical theory has defenders.

First, the challenges may show only that some everyday concepts have prototype structure, not that all do. Mathematical and logical concepts still seem to have definitions; many technical concepts in science and law have explicit necessary and sufficient conditions.

Second, Fodor argued that the prototype effects can be explained without abandoning the classical theory. Typicality effects might reflect beliefs about prototypes (what most birds are like) rather than the structure of the concept BIRD itself. The concept might be defined by features that robins typically but penguins atypically satisfy, without prototype structure being built into the concept.

Third, many concepts seem to require necessary conditions even if not sufficient ones: anything that is not a physical object is not a chair; anything that is not a liquid is not a drink. The absence of a complete definition does not mean there are no constraints.

The debate between classical, prototype, and other theories of concepts has been one of the most productive in cognitive science, and the outcome is a more nuanced picture: different concepts have different structures, and no single theory is adequate for all.
