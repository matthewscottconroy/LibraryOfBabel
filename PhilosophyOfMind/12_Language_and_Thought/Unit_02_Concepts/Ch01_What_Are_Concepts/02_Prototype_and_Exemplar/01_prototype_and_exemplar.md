# Prototype and Exemplar Theories of Concepts

Prototype and exemplar theories represent the two main alternatives to the classical theory. Both emerged from empirical research on categorization and both take typicality effects seriously as evidence about the structure of mental categories. They differ in what they propose as the psychological reality underlying category membership.

**Prototype Theory**

Prototype theory, developed by Rosch and extended by Hampton, holds that concepts are mentally represented by prototypes — idealized, maximally typical instances that capture the "central tendency" of the category. Category membership is determined by similarity to the prototype: an object is a BIRD if it is sufficiently similar to the prototypical bird (feathers, flies, sings, two legs, etc.).

The prototype is not an actually encountered instance but an abstract representation of the typical or average features of category members. It can be described as a weighted list of features, where features that most category members have receive higher weight. The robin has most of the high-weight features of the bird prototype; the penguin lacks many of them.

Prototype theory explains typicality effects straightforwardly: more typical members are more similar to the prototype, so they are verified faster as category members, listed first when generating instances, and learned earlier in category learning tasks.

Prototype theory also explains the graded nature of category membership: since similarity is continuous, membership is a matter of degree. Robins are "clearly" birds; penguins are "sort of" birds; bats might be classified as birds by some people and not by others, depending on how similar they judge them to the prototype.

**The Conjunction Problem**

One of the most serious challenges to prototype theory is the conjunction problem. If PET is represented by one prototype and FISH by another, then PET FISH should be represented by some combination of these prototypes. But the prototype of PET FISH is something like a guppy or a goldfish — which is not particularly typical of either pets or fish.

Hampton found systematic cases where conjunction prototypes were not predictable from constituent prototypes: people rated properties as more typical of the conjunction than of either constituent. Guppies were rated as more typical PET FISH than as either typical pets or typical fish. Prototype combination is not a simple matter of combining prototype features.

This "non-compositionality" of prototypes is a serious problem if we want concepts to compose in the way that thought requires. LOT-style cognitive semantics requires that the meaning of a complex concept be derivable from the meanings of its components. If prototype theory gives the wrong predictions for concept conjunction, it is not a compositional theory in the required sense.

**Exemplar Theory**

Exemplar theory (Medin and Schaffer, Brooks) proposes a different psychological mechanism. Rather than abstracting a prototype from encountered instances, the cognitive system stores representations of actual encountered instances — exemplars — and classifies new instances by computing their similarity to stored exemplars.

When you see a new bird and classify it as a bird, you retrieve stored exemplars of birds and assess how similar the new object is to them. Classification is driven by the whole distribution of exemplars rather than by a single prototype.

Exemplar theory handles some cases that prototype theory handles badly. Since all actual instances are stored, exemplar theory can capture the variability within categories: a category with a bimodal distribution of features (some items are of type A, others of type B, with few intermediates) will have bimodal similarity responses in exemplar models but a single prototype in prototype models. Empirically, many categories show this variability, and exemplar models handle it well.

**Theory Theory**

A third alternative — "theory theory" — holds that concepts are embedded in theories: structured bodies of beliefs about the causal and essential properties of category members. What makes something a bird is not its similarity to a prototype but its having the right causal history (being the offspring of birds) and having the right deep structure (having bird biology, even if it lacks feathers due to a mutation).

Gopnik and Meltzoff, Carey, and others argued that children learn categories by developing naive theories about the causal structure of the world. The child's concept LIVING THING is embedded in a naive theory of biology that connects living things to growth, reproduction, and inheritance.

Theory theory explains why we resist classifying biological mutants as non-members of their natural kinds: even a hairless dog is still a dog, because having dog DNA and being the offspring of dogs is what makes something a dog, not its superficial similarity to a prototype.

**The Current Picture**

Contemporary concept research suggests that there is no single representational format for all concepts. Different types of concepts may have different structures: basic-level natural kind concepts may have prototype structure for quick categorization, with theory structure for deeper reasoning; abstract concepts may rely heavily on metaphor and embodied simulation; mathematical concepts have definitional structure. The debates among classical, prototype, exemplar, and theory-theoretic accounts have produced a richer picture of conceptual diversity than any single framework offers.
