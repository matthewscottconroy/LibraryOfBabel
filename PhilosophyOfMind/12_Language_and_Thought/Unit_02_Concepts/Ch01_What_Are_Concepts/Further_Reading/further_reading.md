# Further Reading: What Are Concepts?

## Primary Sources

**Jerry Fodor, *Concepts: Where Cognitive Science Went Wrong* (1998)** — Fodor argues against prototype theory and theory-theory, defending conceptual atomism: most concepts are primitive, unstructured mental symbols whose content is determined by causal/informational relations to the world rather than by constitutive relations to other concepts. The book is both a sustained critique of competing views and a positive argument for an intentional realist account of concepts.

**Eleanor Rosch, "Natural Categories" (1973), *Cognitive Psychology*** — Rosch's foundational paper introducing the empirical case for prototype theory: people judge membership in natural categories by comparison to prototypes (best examples) rather than by checking necessary and sufficient conditions. Her work with Mervis on family resemblance structures transformed how psychologists and philosophers think about categorization.

**Gregory Murphy, *The Big Book of Concepts* (2002)** — A comprehensive and authoritative survey of cognitive science research on concepts, covering classical theory, prototype theory, exemplar theory, and theory-theory. Murphy's own assessments and his account of the "knowledge approach" make this the best single overview of the empirical landscape.

**Lawrence Barsalou, "Perceptual Symbol Systems" (1999), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*** — Barsalou argues against amodal symbolic theories of concepts, proposing instead that concepts are grounded in perceptual simulations—reenactments of sensory, motor, and introspective states. His perceptual symbols theory has been highly influential in embodied and situated cognition approaches.

**Frank Keil, *Concepts, Kinds, and Cognitive Development* (1989)** — Keil's developmental research showed that children shift from characteristic-to-defining feature use in ways suggesting that our concepts are organized by implicit theories about the essential natures of kinds rather than by surface features. This work provides key empirical support for theory-theory of concepts.

## Secondary Literature

**Eric Margolis and Stephen Laurence (eds.), *Concepts: Core Readings* (1999)** — An invaluable anthology collecting the foundational papers across all major theories of concepts, from Frege and Russell through prototype theory, conceptual atomism, and theory-theory. The editors' introductory essay provides an excellent conceptual map.

**Edouard Machery, *Doing Without Concepts* (2009)** — Machery argues that "concepts" as a unified category should be abandoned in cognitive science, since the phenomena covered by the term are too heterogeneous. His eliminativist proposal has been controversial but forced important clarifications about what concept theories are supposed to explain.

**Susan Carey, *The Origin of Concepts* (2009)** — Carey's developmental account argues that some concepts are innate core systems while others emerge through a process of conceptual change she calls "Quinian bootstrapping." Her work synthesizes Piagetian developmental theory with nativist commitments in a sophisticated way.

**Zenon Pylyshyn, *Seeing and Visualizing: It's Not What You Think* (2003)** — Pylyshyn argues against embodied/perceptual theories of concepts, defending classical symbolic accounts of cognition. While focused on vision, the arguments have broad implications for the perceptual grounding of concepts.

**Nick Chater and George Loewenstein, "The Under-Appreciated Drive for Sense-Making" (2016), *Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization*** — Argues that humans have a powerful drive to understand causal structure in the world that drives concept formation beyond mere categorization, supporting theory-theory accounts of conceptual organization.
