# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: What Are Concepts?

## Key Arguments

**The Classical View and Its Failure (the Gettier-Wittgenstein Problem for Concepts)**
The classical view holds that every concept has a definition: a set of necessary and sufficient conditions for something to fall under it. The concept BIRD, for example, is the concept of a creature that has feathers, lays eggs, has wings, etc. Wittgenstein's attack on this view (via "family resemblance") and the psychological work of Eleanor Rosch showing graded typicality effects — robins are "better" birds than penguins — together constitute a devastating empirical and philosophical challenge: no one has successfully defined even the most common natural-kind or artifact concepts.

**Rosch's Prototype Theory**
Eleanor Rosch and colleagues argued that concepts are mentally represented not as sets of defining features but as prototypes — idealized representations of the most typical instance. Categorization works by comparing a candidate to the prototype: the closer the match, the more quickly and confidently the item is categorized. This explains graded typicality (robins are judged "more birdy" than penguins), the existence of borderline cases, and reaction time asymmetries in categorization tasks. The view challenges the classical view's commitment to all-or-nothing category membership.

**The Theory-Theory of Concepts**
The theory-theory holds that concepts are not prototypes or definitions but components of intuitive theories: folk psychological, folk biological, and folk physical mini-theories that explain why items belong together and what inferences are appropriate. Concepts are individuated by their inferential roles within these theories rather than by sensory similarity. The theory-theory explains why "creatures with the same DNA as tigers" may belong to the tiger category even if they look radically different, and why our concept of WATER is tied to the hidden kind H₂O rather than surface appearance.

**Fodor's Conceptual Atomism**
Fodor argues that most concepts are atomic — they have no internal structure that constitutes their content — and that content is fixed by a nomological relation between a concept token and the property it is the concept of, not by any internal description or prototype. The argument is that all other accounts (classical, prototype, theory-theory) run into the problem of concept innatism: if having BACHELOR requires having UNMARRIED and ADULT and MALE as parts, then what fixes the content of those parts? Atomism breaks the regress by making content externally grounded.

## Core Concepts

**Classical View of Concepts**
The classical view holds that a concept is associated with a definition: a list of features that are individually necessary and jointly sufficient for concept application. Membership in the category is all-or-nothing. This view was dominant in philosophy and psychology through the mid-20th century and underlies much of formal logic's treatment of predicates. Its failure — evidenced by the difficulty of providing non-circular definitions for ordinary concepts and by the typicality data — drove the development of prototype and theory-based alternatives.

**Prototype Theory**
In prototype theory, a concept is represented by a mental prototype — a summary representation of the most typical or central instance of the category — and categorization proceeds by similarity to the prototype. Prototypes are often represented as lists of weighted features rather than strict definitions. The theory, developed from Rosch's experimental work in the 1970s, explains why category membership is graded (some instances are more typical than others) and why categories have fuzzy boundaries.

**Graded Typicality Effect**
The graded typicality effect is the empirical finding that subjects consistently rate some instances of a category as more typical than others (robins as more typical birds than ostriches) and that typicality ratings predict reaction times in categorization tasks (more typical instances are categorized faster). This finding is inexplicable on the classical view, which treats all instances meeting the conditions as equally good members, and constitutes the primary empirical motivation for prototype theory.

**Theory-Theory**
The theory-theory (associated with Gopnik, Carey, Murphy and Medin, and others) holds that concepts are organized not by perceptual similarity to prototypes but by their roles in intuitive theories — structured bodies of knowledge about categories that include causal and explanatory principles. The theory explains why people use hidden properties (DNA, internal structure) to determine category membership even when surface features diverge, and why concepts can be revised when background theories change.

**Conceptual Atomism**
Conceptual atomism, defended by Fodor, holds that primitive concepts have no internal conceptual structure — they are not built from other concepts — and their content is determined entirely by their relations to external properties, not by inferential or descriptive roles. On this view, most ordinary concepts (DOG, RED, CHAIR) are primitive symbols whose meaning is fixed by causal-nomic relations between concept-tokens and properties in the world. The view handles the regress problem for definitional and prototype theories but requires accepting a large innate conceptual endowment.

**The Concept Innatism Problem**
Fodor's dilemma: if concepts are defined in terms of other concepts, what fixes the content of those simpler concepts? Any account that individuates concepts by their constitutive relations to other concepts faces either a vicious regress or the need to terminate at primitive concepts whose content is unexplained. Fodor concludes that most concepts, including CARBURETOR and DOORKNOB, must be innate in the sense of being directly triggered by environmental input rather than learned by assembling simpler parts.

## Thought Experiments

**Wittgenstein's Game Example**
Wittgenstein asks the reader to try to find a single feature or set of features common to everything we call a "game." Card games, board games, ball games, Olympic games, and children's ring-around-the-rosy share no single feature but are related by overlapping and crisscrossing similarities — what he calls "family resemblances." The thought experiment is designed to show that the classical view's search for definitions is misguided for many ordinary concepts, and that concepts can be grounded in patterns of use rather than defining conditions.

**The Jade Case (Putnam/Schwartz)**
Philosophers of language note that "jade" was used to refer to two different minerals — jadeite and nephrite — before the chemical distinction was known. This poses a puzzle for both prototype and classical theories: the prototype for jade fits both minerals; yet once the distinction is made, do we have one concept or two? The case illustrates that the identity conditions for natural-kind concepts may be tied to real kinds in the world rather than to any mental representation, supporting externalist theories of concepts.

**Conceptual Change in Science**
Consider the concept WHALE: before the acceptance of evolutionary taxonomy, whales were classified with fish; today they are classified with mammals. Did scientists change their concept, or did they learn new facts about the extension of the same concept? The theory-theory handles this naturally: scientists revised their folk-biological theory and thus which properties determine membership. The classical view struggles: if having-mammary-glands was not a necessary condition of the old WHALE concept, how did it become one without changing the concept entirely?
