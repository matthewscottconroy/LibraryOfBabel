# Key Thinkers: What Are Concepts?

---

**Eleanor Rosch (1938–)**
American cognitive psychologist at UC Berkeley. Rosch's empirical research in the 1970s overturned the classical theory of concepts (which requires necessary and sufficient conditions for category membership) by showing that natural categories have graded membership and are organized around prototypes—idealized best examples. Her work with Mervis on family resemblance structures, and her findings on "basic-level" categories (the most cognitively efficient level of abstraction), established prototype theory as the dominant empirical paradigm in concept research for decades.
*Key works:* "Natural Categories" (1973), "Family Resemblances: Studies in the Internal Structure of Categories" (1975, with Mervis), "Principles of Categorization" (1978)

---

**Jerry Fodor (1935–2017)**
American philosopher and cognitive scientist at MIT and CUNY. Fodor's conceptual atomism holds that lexical concepts (like TREE, RED, CAUSE) are primitive mental symbols with no internal definitional structure—their content is fixed by causal-informational relations to the world rather than by connections to other concepts. This view makes concepts inert to rational revision and denies that concept possession requires any particular inferential dispositions, positioning Fodor sharply against prototype theory and theory-theory. His arguments that neither definitions nor prototypes can capture the compositionality of concept application remain important challenges.
*Key works:* *The Language of Thought* (1975), *Concepts: Where Cognitive Science Went Wrong* (1998), *Heuristics and Biases* engagement

---

**Frank Keil (1952–)**
American developmental and cognitive psychologist at Yale University. Keil's theory-theory of concepts holds that our conceptual knowledge is organized by implicit causal-explanatory theories about natural kinds rather than by surface statistical features. His developmental research showed that children shift from characteristic to essential features in categorization in ways that track their acquisition of naive theories, and his work on the "essence placeholder" hypothesis (that we attribute real essences to natural kinds even without knowing them) has been highly influential.
*Key works:* *Concepts, Kinds, and Cognitive Development* (1989), "The Growth of Causal Understandings of Natural Kinds" (1995)

---

**Lawrence Barsalou (1952–)**
American cognitive scientist at the University of Chicago. Barsalou developed the "perceptual symbols systems" account of concepts, arguing that conceptual representations are grounded in sensorimotor experience rather than being amodal symbols. On his view, activating a concept involves partially reenacting the perceptual and motor states associated with its instances. His account connects concept research to embodied cognition and has influenced the "grounded cognition" research program.
*Key works:* "Perceptual Symbol Systems" (1999), "Grounded Cognition" (2008)

---

**Ned Block (1942–)**
American philosopher of mind at NYU. Block's distinction between the "functional" role of concepts (their inferential connections to other concepts) and their "referential" content (what they pick out in the world) has been important for understanding the tension between conceptual role semantics and informational/causal theories. His two-dimensional framework for content, and his arguments about the nature of mental representation, have helped clarify the theoretical options in debates about concepts.
*Key works:* "Advertisement for a Semantics for Psychology" (1986), *Readings in Philosophy of Psychology* (ed., 1980)
