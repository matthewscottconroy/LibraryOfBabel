# Concept Empiricism

Concept empiricism is the view that all concepts are ultimately derived from experience. We have no innate concepts (beyond perhaps the most general and formal cognitive capacities); all our specific concepts — our concepts of red, chair, democracy, justice — are acquired through perception, learning, and interaction with the world. Concept empiricism has a long philosophical history and has been developed in sophisticated contemporary forms.

**The Classical Empiricist Tradition**

Locke, Hume, and Berkeley argued that the mind at birth is a "blank slate" (tabula rasa) with no innate ideas. All our ideas — our concepts — are derived from experience, either directly as copies of sensory impressions (simple ideas) or by combining and abstracting from simple ideas (complex ideas).

Hume's version was particularly radical: every genuine idea (concept) must ultimately derive from some impression (sensory or introspective experience). If you cannot identify the impression that a concept derives from, you should be suspicious that the concept is empty or confused. This was a tool of philosophical critique: many traditional metaphysical concepts (substance, necessary connection, the self) failed Hume's impression test.

The classical empiricist account faces the problem of abstract ideas: how do we form concepts of types (TRIANGLE in general, not any particular triangle) if all our impressions are of particulars? Berkeley and Hume gave different answers (Berkeley: abstract ideas are particular ideas used as representatives of their kind; Hume: abstract ideas are particular ideas with a general term attached), but neither is fully satisfying.

**Contemporary Concept Empiricism**

Contemporary concept empiricism — developed by Barsalou, Glenberg, Prinz, and others — has been transformed by cognitive neuroscience. The contemporary empiricist view holds that concepts are grounded in perceptual and motor simulations: reactivations of the sensory and motor states that were active during encounters with instances of the concept.

Barsalou's "perceptual symbols theory" is the most developed contemporary version. Concepts are not amodal symbols stored in a central cognitive system; they are modal representations in sensorimotor areas of the brain that capture features of category members as they are experienced. The concept APPLE involves reactivations of visual cortex (the appearance of apples), motor cortex (the actions involved in handling apples), and gustatory cortex (the taste of apples). Thinking about apples involves simulating encounters with apples.

This view makes concrete predictions: conceptual processing should activate sensorimotor brain areas. There is substantial evidence for this prediction: reading action words activates motor cortex, reading color words activates color-processing areas in visual cortex, reading emotional words activates areas associated with emotional processing.

**Grounding and Meaning**

Contemporary concept empiricism directly addresses the symbol grounding problem: concepts are grounded in sensorimotor experience, so they are not free-floating symbols. The concept APPLE refers to apples because it is causally connected to encounters with apples through the sensorimotor traces those encounters leave.

This provides a naturalistic account of concept acquisition: concepts are acquired through sensorimotor interaction with the world, and they carry the traces of that interaction as their content. No mysterious process of "hooking onto" external entities is needed; grounding is built into the causal history of concept acquisition.

**Challenges to Concept Empiricism**

Concept empiricism faces several challenges.

*Abstract concepts*: How are concepts of things that have no sensorimotor properties — JUSTICE, DEMOCRACY, INFINITY, PRIME NUMBER — grounded? Barsalou proposes that abstract concepts are grounded in simulations of situations in which they apply: JUSTICE is grounded in simulations of just and unjust situations, which have sensorimotor components. But this seems to push the question back: what makes a simulated situation one of justice rather than something else?

*Systematicity and compositionality*: If concepts are modal, embodied simulations, how do they compose into complex conceptual structures? Sensorimotor simulations do not obviously support the kind of systematic composition that thought requires. Critics argue that grounded concepts are too analog and too tied to particular modalities to support the abstract, compositional character of thought.

*Mathematical and logical concepts*: The most obvious counterexamples to concept empiricism are mathematical and logical concepts. The concept PRIME NUMBER does not seem to have any sensorimotor basis; it is purely structural. Concept empiricists must either provide an account of how such concepts are grounded (through experience with counting, grouping) or acknowledge that some concepts are not grounded.
