# Concept Nativism

Concept nativism holds that some or many of our concepts are innate — not acquired through experience but triggered or activated by it, given the architecture of the cognitive system we are born with. Nativism is associated with Chomsky's linguistics, Fodor's philosophy of mind, and certain strands of developmental psychology (Spelke, Carey). It stands in sharp opposition to empiricism about concepts.

**Chomsky's Nativism and Its Extension**

Chomsky's revolution in linguistics was grounded in nativist claims about language. Children acquire their native language rapidly, with limited and imperfect input, producing correctly structured sentences they have never heard, and avoiding systematic errors that learning-by-association would predict. This "poverty of stimulus" argument supports the view that children are born with a "language acquisition device" — a set of innate constraints (Universal Grammar) that makes language learning possible.

This nativist argument about language extends naturally to concepts: if the capacity for linguistic structure is innate, some of the conceptual underpinnings of language (concepts of agents, actions, objects, events, quantification) may also be innate.

**Fodor's Extreme Nativism**

Fodor pushed concept nativism further than most would follow. His argument: the acquisition of any complex concept requires having simpler concepts from which it is assembled. But the simpler concepts themselves must be acquired or innate. If they are acquired, there must be even simpler concepts from which they are assembled — leading to an infinite regress, unless we eventually reach genuinely simple concepts.

But, Fodor argued, this regress hits bottom at a very low level: most ordinary concepts (DOG, CHAIR, APPLE) are simple in the relevant sense — they cannot be defined in terms of simpler conceptual primitives. If they cannot be learned by decomposition into primitives, they must be innate.

This "extreme nativism" — the view that most ordinary concepts are innate and are triggered by experience rather than learned from it — is almost universally rejected. The idea that the concept CARBURETOR is innate and merely triggered by experience with carburetors seems absurd.

Fodor himself acknowledged this was deeply uncomfortable but claimed the argument was sound and the alternatives were worse. He eventually proposed a "mind-world" metaphysics in which concepts are constitutively related to properties in the world rather than being internal representations with contingently acquired connections to the world — but this proposal has not been widely accepted.

**Innate Concept Cores**

A more moderate nativist position holds that some core conceptual capacities — foundational concepts that are prerequisites for more specific concept acquisition — are innate. Elizabeth Spelke's core knowledge systems are the most developed account.

Spelke proposes that infants are born with core systems for representing objects, agents, numbers, and space. These systems are not acquired from experience; they represent information in a way that is constant across human cultures and that is present from very early infancy, before extensive learning has occurred.

*Core object knowledge*: Infants expect objects to be spatially continuous, to persist when occluded, and to move on continuous paths. These expectations are violated by magic tricks and impossible events, as shown by the fact that infants look longer at impossible events (violated expectations) than at ordinary ones.

*Core number*: Infants can discriminate between small numbers of objects and can track changes in number that result from addition and subtraction. This suggests some form of innate quantity representation.

*Core agent knowledge*: Infants expect agents to be self-propelled, goal-directed, and socially responsive in ways that objects are not. They are sensitive to agents pursuing goals in efficient ways.

**The Bootstrapping Problem**

Carey's "bootstrapping" account addresses the acquisition of concepts more complex than the core systems provide. Bootstrapping refers to the process by which children construct new conceptual systems that go beyond their innate endowment, using the resources of language, cultural scaffolding, and Quinian bootstrapping — using placeholder terms to collect data that eventually leads to genuine concept revision.

This process is neither purely empiricist (pure learning from experience) nor purely nativist (triggering of innate concepts). It is a constructive process in which conceptual reorganization occurs through the combination of innate systems, linguistic categories, and cultural practices.

The bootstrapping framework suggests that the empiricism-nativism debate may be a false dichotomy: cognition involves both innate constraints that structure learning and genuine learning that goes beyond those constraints.
