# Further Reading: Empiricism vs. Nativism

## Primary Sources

**Noam Chomsky, *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965)** — Chomsky's transformational-generative grammar introduced the hypothesis that humans possess an innate Universal Grammar—an abstract set of principles and parameters that constrain all possible human languages. His poverty-of-the-stimulus argument holds that children acquire grammatical rules that cannot be learned from the input alone, requiring an innate linguistic endowment.

**Noam Chomsky, "A Review of B.F. Skinner's Verbal Behavior" (1959), *Language*** — Chomsky's devastating critique of behaviorist accounts of language acquisition established the nativist paradigm. By showing that stimulus-response conditioning cannot account for the productivity and rule-governed creativity of language, Chomsky effectively ended behaviorism's influence in linguistics and opened cognitive science.

**Michael Tomasello, *Constructing a Language: A Usage-Based Theory of Language Acquisition* (2003)** — The most sophisticated empiricist response to Chomsky, arguing that children acquire language through general cognitive skills—joint attention, intention-reading, and pattern-finding—rather than through an innate language module. Tomasello's usage-based approach treats grammatical constructions as learned generalizations over experienced utterances.

**Jerry Fodor, *The Language of Thought* (1975)** — Fodor's extreme nativism about concepts, arguing that all lexical concepts must be innate because the process of hypothesis formation and confirmation that would be needed to learn them already presupposes the concepts in question. While provocative and widely criticized, this argument sharpens what any empiricist theory of concept acquisition must explain.

**Elizabeth Spelke and Kathleen Kinzler, "Core Knowledge" (2007), *Developmental Science*** — Spelke's influential account of core knowledge systems—innate, modular systems for representing objects, agents, number, and space—provides a nativist framework that is more empirically grounded than Chomskyan linguistics while challenging empiricist developmental psychology.

## Secondary Literature

**Steven Pinker, *The Language Instinct* (1994)** — A defense of nativism about language accessible to non-specialists, arguing that the language faculty is a biological adaptation with its own innate computational architecture. Pinker's polemic against the view that thought depends on natural language is as important as his positive nativist thesis.

**Michael Tomasello, *The Cultural Origins of Human Cognition* (1999)** — Argues that uniquely human cognition—including language—emerges from cultural learning mediated by shared intentionality rather than from domain-specific innate modules. A foundational statement of the social-cultural alternative to Chomskyan nativism.

**Susan Carey, *The Origin of Concepts* (2009)** — Carey argues for a sophisticated nativism in which some conceptual knowledge is innate (core systems) while genuinely new concepts emerge through a process of "Quinian bootstrapping" that is neither simple induction nor mere triggering of innate content. Essential for moving beyond the crude nativist/empiricist dichotomy.

**Gary Marcus, *The Birth of the Mind: How a Small Number of Genes Creates the Complexities of Human Thought* (2004)** — A nativist account of how genes could encode the information needed for the language faculty and other cognitive modules, responding to the standard objection that the genome is too small to specify innate mental content.

**Annette Karmiloff-Smith, *Beyond Modularity: A Developmental Perspective on Cognitive Science* (1992)** — Karmiloff-Smith's "representational redescription" model argues that innate systems exist but that cognition develops through a process of progressive representational elaboration, providing a developmentally sophisticated middle ground between extreme nativism and empiricism.
