# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Empiricism vs. Nativism

## Key Arguments

**The Poverty of the Stimulus Argument (Chomsky)**
Chomsky argues that children acquire knowledge of complex grammatical rules — including rules governing long-distance dependencies, structure-dependent operations, and the interpretation of pronouns — on the basis of input that is insufficient to determine these rules inductively. Because the relevant constructions rarely appear in child-directed speech, and because children make systematic errors that respect syntactic structure rather than surface statistical patterns, the only explanation is that the grammatical principles are part of an innate language faculty rather than learned from experience.

**Tomasello's Usage-Based Alternative**
Michael Tomasello argues that children acquire language through domain-general social-cognitive abilities — particularly joint attention, intention-reading, and pattern-finding — applied to the specific social practice of language use. Children do not need innate grammar: they build up constructions piecemeal from concrete exemplars, using general learning mechanisms to extract patterns. This usage-based approach explains cross-linguistic variation in acquisition trajectories better than universal grammar and denies the need for a dedicated language acquisition device.

**Fodor's Concept Nativism**
Fodor argues that not only grammatical knowledge but most concepts must be innate, triggering an outcry from empiricists. His argument: concepts cannot be learned because all learning involves hypothesis testing over a hypothesis space, which already presupposes the concepts in question; concepts cannot be defined out of simpler concepts without infinite regress; therefore, most concepts — including apparently learned concepts like CARBURETOR — must be directly triggered by experience rather than assembled from components. This conclusion is counterintuitive but follows, Fodor argues, from the logic of learning.

**The Domain-Specificity Argument (Carey, Spelke)**
Susan Carey and Elizabeth Spelke argue that human cognition is organized into domain-specific systems — core knowledge systems for objects, number, agents, and geometry — that are innate and operational from infancy. These core systems provide primitive representations that cannot be explained by general-purpose learning from sensory input. The argument is empirical: infants show expectations about object permanence, numerical relations, and intentional agency that are present before they could have been learned from experience.

**The Language Acquisition Device Argument**
The LAD argument, central to Chomsky's program, holds that the speed and uniformity of language acquisition, combined with the underdetermination of grammar by input, requires positing a dedicated biological mechanism for language learning. The LAD is equipped with Universal Grammar — a set of innate parameters and principles that constrain the hypothesis space for grammatical rules. Children acquiring different languages set the parameters of UG differently based on available input, explaining both universality and cross-linguistic variation.

## Core Concepts

**Poverty of the Stimulus**
The poverty of the stimulus (POS) argument holds that the evidence available to language-learning children is insufficient to determine the grammatical rules they end up knowing — rules about structure-dependent operations, binding, and wh-movement, for example — unless innate knowledge constrains the learning process. The argument is the primary motivation for the language acquisition device and Universal Grammar. Critics argue that statistical learning over large corpora can acquire the relevant distinctions without innate grammatical knowledge.

**Universal Grammar (UG)**
Universal Grammar, in Chomsky's sense, is the innate component of the language faculty: a set of principles and parameters common to all human languages that constitutes the initial state of the language learner. UG explains why all languages share certain structural features (no language is random), why acquisition is uniform and rapid despite impoverished input, and why certain logically possible grammars are never found in natural languages. The Minimalist Program represents Chomsky's most recent attempt to characterize UG in terms of a single recursive operation (Merge).

**Language Acquisition Device (LAD)**
The language acquisition device is Chomsky's term for the dedicated biological mechanism that enables children to acquire language on the basis of primary linguistic data. It encodes Universal Grammar and operates by setting parameter values (e.g., head-initial vs. head-final phrase structure) on the basis of available input. The LAD is invoked to explain the poverty of the stimulus: without an innate initial state, the available input is insufficient to determine the rules children come to know.

**Usage-Based Construction Grammar**
In Tomasello's usage-based framework, language knowledge consists of a structured inventory of form-meaning mappings (constructions) at varying levels of abstraction, built up from concrete exemplars through general cognitive processes. Children initially acquire item-specific constructions (slots around particular verbs) and gradually abstract more general patterns. This approach explains the gradual, piecemeal character of acquisition, cross-linguistic variability, and the role of frequency and input quality in shaping knowledge.

**Domain-Specific vs. Domain-General Learning**
Domain-specific learning systems are mechanisms dedicated to acquiring knowledge in a particular domain (grammar, number, faces, geometry), often with innate initial representations and species-specific developmental trajectories. Domain-general learning systems apply the same associative, statistical, or analogy-based mechanisms across all domains. The debate between nativists (who favor domain-specific systems) and empiricists (who favor domain-general learning) is central to cognitive science and shapes theories of concept acquisition, language acquisition, and social cognition.

**Core Knowledge Systems (Spelke)**
Core knowledge systems, as theorized by Elizabeth Spelke, are a small set of innate, domain-specific cognitive systems that represent and reason about objects, agents, number, and geometry. Each system is characterized by a particular set of primitives, principles, and representational format, and each is present in infancy before relevant experience could explain it. Core knowledge systems are contrasted with culturally acquired knowledge (e.g., mathematics, reading) and are proposed as the universal foundation on which cultural learning builds.

## Thought Experiments

**Chomsky's Structure-Dependence Test**
Chomsky asks how a child knows that to form a yes/no question from "The man who is tall is happy," the auxiliary to be moved is the one in the main clause ("Is the man who is tall happy?") rather than the first auxiliary encountered linearly ("*Is the man who tall is happy?"). The child never makes the second error, even though it has probably never heard or been corrected on such a sentence. The thought experiment functions as a poverty-of-the-stimulus demonstration: structure-dependence is not recoverable from surface statistics without innate grammatical knowledge.

**The Invented Language (Nicaraguan Sign Language)**
When deaf Nicaraguan children were brought together in a school in the 1970s, they spontaneously created a new sign language — now known as Nicaraguan Sign Language — with grammatical complexity that went well beyond what their teachers modeled. Younger children who arrived later acquired a more grammatically complex form than the founders. This natural experiment is used by nativists to argue that grammatical capacity is innate and matures with age, and by interactionists to argue that language is a social creation that grows through use and community.

**Fodor's Carburetor Thought Experiment**
Fodor asks: how could a child learn the concept CARBURETOR if all concept learning requires that the relevant concept already be in the learner's hypothesis space? If learning CARBURETOR requires forming a hypothesis of the form "carburetors are things that are F," the learner must already have a representation of F. The regress suggests that complex concepts like CARBURETOR cannot be assembled from simpler innate parts but must themselves be innately available and triggered by experience — a deeply counterintuitive conclusion that motivates alternative accounts of concept acquisition.
