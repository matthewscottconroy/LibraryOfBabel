# Key Thinkers: Empiricism vs. Nativism

---

**Noam Chomsky (1928–)**
American linguist and philosopher at MIT, widely considered the most influential linguist of the twentieth century. Chomsky's poverty-of-the-stimulus argument maintains that children acquire grammatical rules far more complex than the linguistic input they receive could justify, implying an innate Universal Grammar. His critique of behaviorism launched cognitive science and established the nativist paradigm in the study of language and mind. Later work in the Minimalist Program has tried to explain the properties of Universal Grammar in terms of efficient computational principles, showing Chomsky's continued engagement with the deepest questions about linguistic structure.
*Key works:* *Syntactic Structures* (1957), *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965), *The Minimalist Program* (1995), *Language and Problems of Knowledge* (1988)

---

**Michael Tomasello (1950–)**
American developmental psychologist and primatologist at Duke University, leading figure in cultural and comparative cognition. Tomasello's usage-based theory of language acquisition argues that children learn language through general cognitive capacities for intention-reading and pattern extraction rather than through a language-specific module. His comparative research showing that chimpanzees lack the joint-attentional and cultural learning capacities that underlie human language acquisition supports his view that language is a specifically cultural rather than biologically innate phenomenon.
*Key works:* *The Cultural Origins of Human Cognition* (1999), *Constructing a Language* (2003), *A Natural History of Human Morality* (2016)

---

**Elizabeth Spelke (1949–)**
American developmental psychologist at Harvard University. Spelke's core knowledge hypothesis proposes that infants are born with several domain-specific systems for representing objects, agents, number, and geometry—representations that are innate but abstract rather than experience-derived. Her violation-of-expectation paradigm has been used to show that infants as young as a few months represent objects as persisting, bounded entities, providing some of the strongest evidence for nativist accounts of early conceptual knowledge.
*Key works:* "Core Knowledge" (2007), "Core Systems of Geometry in Animal Minds" (2006), numerous developmental papers

---

**Jerry Fodor (1935–2017)**
American philosopher and cognitive scientist at MIT and CUNY. Fodor's argument for concept nativism is both famous and controversial: since concept learning must involve forming and confirming hypotheses, and hypothesis formation already requires the concepts to be tested, all concepts that can be learned must already be somehow present before learning begins. On this view, even highly complex and culturally specific concepts like DOORKNOB or BUREAUCRACY must be innate. Most find this conclusion absurd, but Fodor argued the alternative is incoherence.
*Key works:* *The Language of Thought* (1975), *Concepts: Where Cognitive Science Went Wrong* (1998)

---

**Susan Carey (1942–)**
American developmental psychologist at Harvard University. Carey's account of conceptual development argues that children possess core innate knowledge systems (following Spelke) and that genuinely new concepts emerge through "Quinian bootstrapping"—a process in which placeholder symbols are given content through their role in a developing framework theory, with meaning shifting as the theory changes. This view allows for radical conceptual change (as in the history of science) while preserving a role for innate foundations, navigating between extreme empiricism and strong nativism.
*Key works:* *Conceptual Change in Childhood* (1985), *The Origin of Concepts* (2009)
