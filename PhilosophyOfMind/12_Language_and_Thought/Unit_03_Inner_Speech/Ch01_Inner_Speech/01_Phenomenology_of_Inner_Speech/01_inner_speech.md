# The Phenomenology of Inner Speech

Inner speech — the experience of "hearing" or "using" words in one's mind, without any audible vocalization — is a pervasive feature of conscious experience for most people. It is the inner voice that narrates our thoughts, rehearses conversations, reads text aloud in the head, and plans what to say before saying it. Despite its ubiquity, inner speech is philosophically and scientifically poorly understood.

**The Phenomenological Character**

Inner speech has a distinctive phenomenal character: it feels like silent talking, with a quasi-auditory quality — a sense of words being produced, of sounds (even if not heard), of a voice (even if not localized in space). Different people describe their inner speech differently: for some, it has a definite vocal quality; for others, it is more abstract, verbal without being quasi-auditory.

Recent research on "aphantasia" (the absence of mental imagery) and "anendophasia" (the apparent absence of inner speech) has revealed that there is more variability in inner experience than previously assumed. Some people report very little or no inner speech; their thinking, while apparently verbal and linguistic, is not experienced as "hearing" words. This challenges the assumption that inner speech is universal.

The phenomenology of inner speech is connected to broader questions about the phenomenal character of thought. Do thoughts have a distinctive phenomenal character, or are they functionally effective processes without any "feel"? Inner speech suggests that some thoughts have a quasi-sensory character — they are experienced as words, which is a form of phenomenal property.

**Vygotsky's Account**

Lev Vygotsky proposed an influential developmental account of inner speech. In his view, inner speech develops from external (social) speech: the child first learns to speak aloud to others, then speaks aloud to herself (egocentric speech, which accompanies and guides action), and finally internalizes this self-directed speech as inner speech.

On Vygotsky's account, inner speech is not just silent external speech; it undergoes transformation during internalization. Inner speech is abbreviated, predicative (omitting the subject when it is obvious), and semantically condensed. The thought "I will go to the store to buy apples" becomes, in inner speech, something like "store — apples" — stripped of the grammatical scaffolding that external communication requires.

This account connects inner speech to self-regulation: inner speech serves to guide, plan, and control behavior, especially in novel or challenging situations. The child who talks herself through a problem is using proto-inner speech for executive function — a capacity that will eventually be internalized as the silent planning and self-talk of adult cognition.

**Inner Speech and Thought**

Is inner speech the medium of thought, or its expression? This question parallels the LOT debate: inner speech could be an expression of thought that occurs in a different (non-linguistic) medium, or it could be (at least partially) constitutive of thought.

William James distinguished between thoughts as sequences of "substantive" states (definite concepts, images) and "transitive" states (the relations and movements between concepts). Inner speech captures the substantive states but misses much of the flow of thought. The experience of thinking is richer and more fluid than inner speech — inner speech is a kind of snapshot that thought moves through, not the full content of thought.

This suggests that inner speech is not the complete medium of thought but one important channel through which thought is expressed and controlled, especially for verbal and propositional content.

**Auditory Verbal Hallucinations**

The study of inner speech connects importantly to the clinical phenomenon of auditory verbal hallucinations — "hearing voices" — which occurs in schizophrenia and other conditions. The influential "misattribution" hypothesis proposes that hallucinations are misattributed inner speech: the patient's own inner speech is experienced as coming from outside, as an alien voice.

Evidence supporting this hypothesis includes the finding that subvocal motor activity (muscle movements associated with speech) occurs during hallucination, that interventions that occupy the inner speech channel (subvocal counting) reduce hallucinations, and that hallucinated voices often say things consistent with the patient's own thoughts and concerns.

The misattribution hypothesis connects inner speech to questions about the sense of agency and the sense of self: what normally marks inner speech as mine is that it is generated by my own speech production system and felt as mine. When this self-monitoring fails, one's own inner speech becomes alienated — experienced as coming from outside, as a foreign voice.
