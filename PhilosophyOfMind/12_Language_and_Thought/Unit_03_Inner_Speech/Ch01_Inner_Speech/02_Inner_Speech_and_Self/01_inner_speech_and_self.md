# Inner Speech and the Self

Inner speech is not only a cognitive tool for planning and rehearsal; it is intimately connected to our sense of self and to the maintenance of personal identity over time. The relationship between inner speech, self-narration, and the construction of a coherent self-model is one of the most intriguing topics at the intersection of phenomenology, cognitive science, and clinical psychology.

**The Self-Narrative Function**

Human beings are story-telling creatures, and inner speech is the medium in which much of this storytelling takes place. The continuous commentary that accompanies waking life — evaluating situations, planning responses, remembering and anticipating, formulating and revising intentions — is largely conducted in inner speech.

Dennett's narrative self-model account, discussed earlier, proposes that the self is constituted by a continuous narrative construction. Much of this narrative is produced in inner speech: we narrate our lives to ourselves in words, and this narration is part of how we construct and maintain our self-models.

This connects inner speech to autobiographical memory: the verbal encoding of experiences in inner speech contributes to their memorability and to their integration into the self-narrative. Events that we narrate to ourselves (or others) are better remembered and more readily incorporated into the narrative self.

**Inner Speech and Dialogue**

A distinctive feature of inner speech noted by Mikhail Bakhtin and developed by Hermans in "dialogical self theory" is its dialogical character: inner speech is not a monologue but a dialogue. We have internal conversations, represent multiple voices and perspectives, argue with imagined interlocutors, and experience our inner speech as including voices other than our own.

Hermans proposes that the self is a "society of mind" composed of multiple "internal characters" or "positions" that hold dialogical relations with each other and with imagined external characters. The self is not a unified narrator but a complex of voices and perspectives in ongoing negotiation.

This dialogical account of inner speech challenges the picture of the self as a unified inner narrator and connects to clinical observations about dissociative disorders: in dissociation, the dialogical complexity of inner life can be experienced as involving genuinely separate "parts" or "alters" with distinct voices, perspectives, and self-narratives.

**Inner Speech and Agency**

Inner speech serves executive functions: planning actions, rehearsing social scenarios, formulating goals, and monitoring progress. The developmental connection (from Vygotsky's account) is that inner speech internalizes the self-regulatory function of external social speech. The adult who silently rehearses a difficult conversation before having it is using the descendant of the child who talked herself through a difficult task.

This connection between inner speech and agency connects to questions about voluntary action. The deliberate, self-directed use of inner speech — choosing to verbally plan, mentally rehearse, or talk through a problem — is a paradigm case of rational self-governance. The agent who uses inner speech to control her behavior is, in a real sense, exercising her rational agency through the medium of language.

This has implications for the relationship between language and rationality: to the extent that inner speech is the medium of deliberate self-regulation, the development of inner speech (itself a product of language acquisition) is partly constitutive of the development of rational agency.

**Silence and Non-Verbal Selfhood**

Not everyone has extensive inner speech, and the growing recognition of anendophasia — the condition of having little or no inner speech — raises interesting questions. People who report minimal inner speech do not thereby seem to lack a sense of self, autonomy, or rational agency. This suggests that inner speech is not constitutively necessary for the self-narrative or agency functions it typically supports.

The phenomenological diversity in inner experience — some people think primarily in words, others in images, others in neither — suggests that what matters for the self is not the particular medium of inner experience but the functional organization of self-representation, self-monitoring, and self-regulation. Inner speech is one medium among others through which these functions can be implemented.
