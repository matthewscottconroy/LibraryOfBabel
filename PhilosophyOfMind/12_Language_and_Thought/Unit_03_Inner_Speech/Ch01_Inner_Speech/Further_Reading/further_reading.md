# Further Reading: Inner Speech

## Primary Sources

**Lev Vygotsky, *Thought and Language* (1934/1986)** — Vygotsky's foundational developmental account argues that inner speech emerges from the internalization of external, social speech: children first speak aloud to regulate their own behavior, then move to "egocentric speech," and finally to fully internalized inner speech. Inner speech on this account is functionally abbreviated—highly predicated, contracted—and differs structurally from external speech, making it a distinct and philosophically interesting phenomenon.

**Charles Fernyhough, *The Voices Within: The History and Science of How We Talk to Ourselves* (2016)** — An accessible synthesis of the psychology and philosophy of inner speech, covering its phenomenological forms (condensed, expanded, other-voices), its developmental origins, its role in cognition, and its relationship to anomalous experiences such as hearing voices in psychosis. Essential reading for any course on the topic.

**Bénédicte de Boysson-Bardies, *How Language Comes to Children* (1999)** — Provides developmental context for inner speech, covering how children acquire the language capacities that are then internalized. Understanding the developmental emergence of language is essential background for Vygotsky's claim that inner speech has a social origin.

**Rupert Hurlburt and Eric Schwitzgebel, *Describing Inner Experience?: Proponent Meets Skeptic* (2007)** — A unique dialogue between Hurlburt (defender of Descriptive Experience Sampling methodology) and Schwitzgebel (skeptic about the accuracy of introspective reports), focusing especially on inner speech. This book raises fundamental questions about how inner speech can be studied and whether first-person reports are reliable data.

**Brian Alderson-Day and Charles Fernyhough, "Inner Speech: Development, Cognitive Functions, Phenomenology, and Neuroscience" (2015), *Psychological Bulletin*** — A comprehensive review of the empirical literature on inner speech from multiple perspectives. Covers the varieties of inner speech phenomenology, its neural substrates, its roles in working memory and problem-solving, and its relationship to auditory verbal hallucinations.

## Secondary Literature

**Peter Langland-Hassan and Agustín Vicente (eds.), *Inner Speech: New Voices* (2018)** — A state-of-the-art collection of philosophical and empirical essays on inner speech, covering its phenomenology, its linguistic structure, its relationship to thought, and its role in self-knowledge and metacognition. Represents the current best thinking across disciplines.

**Vygotsky, *Mind in Society* (1978)** — Collects key essays on the social origins of higher psychological functions, providing context for understanding inner speech within Vygotsky's broader theoretical framework of internalization and zone of proximal development.

**José Luis Bermúdez, *Thinking Without Words* (2003)** — Though focused on non-linguistic thought, Bermúdez's book illuminates the relationship between verbal and non-verbal cognition by examining what thinking can be achieved without language. Provides an important counterpoint to views that identify thought with inner speech.

**Jean-Rémy Martin and Francesca Della Sala, "Predictive Coding and the Inner Voice" (2016)** — Argues that inner speech is best understood through a predictive processing framework as an action that is predicted but not fully executed. Situates inner speech research within contemporary computational neuroscience.

**Alain Morin, "Possible Links Between Self-Awareness and Inner Speech: Theoretical Background, Underlying Mechanisms, and Empirical Evidence" (2005), *Journal of Consciousness Studies*** — Examines the relationship between inner speech and self-reflective consciousness, arguing that self-directed inner speech plays a central role in the development and maintenance of self-awareness.
