# Nonverbal Thought

The question of whether thought requires language — whether genuine thinking can occur without any kind of linguistic representation — is important for understanding the scope of cognition across species, for developmental accounts of thinking in pre-linguistic infants, and for philosophical accounts of the relationship between language and mind.

**The Case for Nonverbal Thought**

Several lines of evidence suggest that substantial cognition can occur without language.

*Non-human animals*: Many animals exhibit behaviors that require what we would normally call thinking — problem-solving, planning, learning from experience, recognizing individual conspecifics, using tools — without having language in any recognizable sense. Crows can engage in multi-step planning to obtain food; great apes can learn to use sign language or symbol boards and can solve abstract problems; elephants show complex social reasoning. If these behaviors involve anything like thought, it is nonverbal.

*Pre-linguistic infants*: Spelke's core knowledge research shows that infants have sophisticated expectations about objects, numbers, and agents before they have language. The knowledge being exercised in preferential looking experiments appears to be conceptual but is clearly not linguistic.

*Mathematical and spatial thinking*: Mathematicians often report that their most creative insights occur nonverbally — as spatial intuitions, pattern recognitions, or abstract "feelings" about mathematical structures that are only later translated into formal language. Spatial reasoning and navigation seem to involve representations that are better described as imagistic or map-like than as linguistic.

*Expertise and tacit knowledge*: Much expert knowledge is tacit — embodied in skilled performance, pattern recognition, and rapid judgment — rather than explicitly represented in verbal rules. The master chess player who sees promising moves without calculating, the experienced clinician who recognizes a syndrome before articulating the evidence: these suggest that much sophisticated thinking is nonverbal.

**The Linguistic Requirement View**

Some philosophers and researchers hold that genuine propositional thought — thinking that something is the case, making judgments with truth conditions — requires something like language. Donald Davidson argued that thought requires language: to have beliefs (propositional attitudes), you must have a language, because having a language is what provides the framework within which beliefs are distinguished and individuated.

Specifically, Davidson argued that you can have beliefs only if you understand the concept of belief — only if you can have beliefs about what others believe. And having beliefs about beliefs requires language, because the conceptual resources for making belief attributions are linguistic. Without language, you cannot have the kind of beliefs that ground intentional agency.

This strong linguistic requirement view has been widely challenged. It seems to prove too much: it would deny thought to pre-linguistic infants and non-human animals in ways that seem inconsistent with their behavioral competences.

**Animal Cognition Without Language**

The study of animal cognition has revealed increasingly sophisticated nonverbal thought. Chimpanzees in Boesch's studies engage in cooperative hunting strategies that seem to require some form of anticipation and mutual coordination. Clark's nutcrackers remember thousands of food cache locations across months. Cephalopods like octopuses solve novel problems without any obvious language capacity.

The philosophical question is whether these behaviors involve genuine propositional thought (beliefs with truth conditions) or merely something like "proto-beliefs" — functional states that guide behavior in the way beliefs do, without the full logical structure of propositional attitudes.

Stalnaker and others have argued that the difference between propositional attitudes proper and proto-beliefs may be one of degree rather than kind: full propositional thought requires the integration of multiple representational systems with sensitivity to logical relations, and this integration may be present to varying degrees across species.

**Thinking in Images and Space**

A particularly important form of nonverbal thought is imagistic thinking — thinking in visual images, spatial models, or kinesthetic simulations. Architects think spatially; musicians think in patterns of sound; sculptors think in three-dimensional forms.

Whether imagistic thinking constitutes "genuine" thought in a philosophically relevant sense depends on whether images can bear propositional content. Kosslyn argues that images can represent spatial relationships, and that reasoning over images (mental rotation, scanning, size estimation) constitutes genuine spatial reasoning. The question is whether this spatial reasoning is merely a useful heuristic or whether it carries the content that theoretical thought requires.

The evidence suggests that imagistic and linguistic thought are complementary systems that are often used together, with imagistic thought especially suited for spatial, analogical, and creative reasoning, and linguistic thought especially suited for precise, compositional, and communicable reasoning.
