# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Thought Without Language

## Key Arguments

**The Argument from Spatial and Musical Thought**
Cognitive scientists and philosophers point to spatial reasoning (navigating a building, solving a mechanical puzzle, composing a three-dimensional scene) and musical cognition (imagining a melody, recognizing harmonic structure) as paradigm cases of sophisticated thought that is not conducted in natural language and cannot be adequately characterized in propositional terms. These forms of thought exhibit systematic, productive, and inferential structure, suggesting that language is not a universal medium of thought but one cognitive format among several.

**The Argument from Deaf Cognition**
Research on individuals born deaf who have not been exposed to any conventional sign language reveals complex spatial reasoning, numerical cognition, and social understanding that cannot have been mediated by phonological language. Goldin-Meadow's work on "homesigners" — deaf individuals who create gesture-based systems without linguistic input — shows that some representational and communicative structure arises independently of exposure to a linguistic community, arguing against the claim that language is necessary for sophisticated thought.

**The Animal Cognition Argument**
Research on problem-solving in non-human primates, corvids, and cetaceans shows that these animals can engage in causal reasoning, planning, analogical inference, and numerical estimation without access to natural language. Köhler's chimpanzee insight experiments, corvid future planning studies (Clayton), and primate numerical cognition (Matsuzawa) are taken as evidence that the cognitive operations underlying these capacities do not require linguistic representation. This challenges LOTH-style claims that all thought requires a language-like representational format.

**Wittgenstein's Private Language Argument**
Wittgenstein argues that a language whose terms refer to private inner sensations — known only to the speaker and unconnectable to public behavioral criteria — is not a genuine language at all. The argument proceeds by showing that a "private language" lacks the conditions for correct application of its terms: without a publicly checkable standard for correctness, the distinction between thinking one is following a rule and actually following it collapses. If thought requires rule-following, and rule-following requires public criteria, then purely private, non-linguistic thought may be incoherent as a medium of rule-governed inference.

## Core Concepts

**Non-Linguistic Thought**
Non-linguistic thought refers to cognitive processes and representations that are not conducted in, and cannot be adequately captured by, natural language or language-like symbol systems. Paradigm cases include visual imagery, spatial reasoning, musical cognition, and the kinds of practical know-how involved in skilled action. The existence of non-linguistic thought is used to argue against strong versions of the language-of-thought hypothesis and against Whorfian linguistic determinism.

**Homesigning**
Homesigning, studied by Susan Goldin-Meadow, refers to the gesture systems spontaneously developed by deaf children in hearing families who have not been exposed to a conventional sign language. Homesigners create systems with some linguistic properties — combining gestures to express propositions, using gesture order systematically — without having received them from a linguistic community. This natural experiment suggests that some linguistic-like representational capacities emerge from general social-cognitive abilities rather than requiring linguistic input.

**The Private Language Argument (Wittgenstein)**
Wittgenstein's private language argument, developed in the Philosophical Investigations, holds that there cannot be a language whose meaning is grounded solely in private inner ostension — pointing to an inner sensation and labeling it. The argument is that in such a language there would be no standard for distinguishing correct from incorrect use of a term (the same sensation might be named differently on different occasions without any external check), so any apparent "rule" would have no determinate content. The argument bears on whether thought can be conducted in purely private mental symbols.

**Embodied and Enactive Cognition**
Theorists in the embodied cognition tradition (Varela, Thompson, Rosch; Chemero) argue that cognition is not a matter of manipulating inner symbolic representations but of the skillful, sensorimotor engagement of an organism with its environment. On this view, much of what philosophers call "thought" — especially practical know-how and perceptual judgment — does not involve language-like representations at all but is constituted by bodily action patterns and organism-environment coupling. The view challenges both LOTH and Whorfianism by denying that cognition is primarily representational.

**Analogical Reasoning in Animals**
Analogical reasoning — inferring that A is to B as C is to D — has been demonstrated in non-human primates (chimpanzees and baboons trained on relational match-to-sample tasks), implying the capacity for abstract relational thought without natural language. Penn, Holyoak, and Povinelli argue that genuine analogical reasoning requires representing relations-between-relations (second-order relational reasoning), and that the evidence for this in non-humans is more limited. The debate is significant for delimiting the cognitive capacities that require linguistic scaffolding.

**Sign Language and Linguistic Thought**
Research on sign languages (American Sign Language, British Sign Language) shows that they are fully natural languages with rich grammatical structure, processed by the same left-hemisphere language systems as spoken languages. This demonstrates that the capacity for linguistic thought is not tied to a specific sensory-motor modality (auditory-phonological) but to the formal structure of language itself. It also shows that deaf signers have full linguistic thought, confirming that phonological inner speech is not required for linguistic cognition.

## Thought Experiments

**The Blind Sculptor**
Consider a sculptor who, from birth sightless, works in three-dimensional clay, building complex spatial forms from tactile and proprioceptive information alone. She can plan ahead, perceive mistakes, and achieve desired formal relationships — but none of this proceeds in visual imagery or verbal description. The thought experiment is used to argue that spatial reasoning and aesthetic judgment can be fully realized in non-linguistic, non-visual thought, challenging any claim that thought requires a specific representational modality tied to language.

**Animal Problem-Solving (Köhler's Insight)**
Wolfgang Köhler's chimpanzee Sultan famously stacked boxes and used sticks to reach bananas out of reach — solving problems that required multi-step planning and spatial reasoning without any language-mediated instruction or reward-based shaping. The case is philosophically significant as an apparent demonstration of genuine problem-solving — insight — in a non-linguistic animal, challenging the view that structured, flexible problem-solving requires linguistic scaffolding.

**Wittgenstein's Beetle in the Box**
Wittgenstein asks us to imagine that every person has a box containing something they call a "beetle," but no one can look into anyone else's box, and it is possible everyone's box contains something different or nothing at all. If "beetle" gets its meaning from its use in the language game rather than from private ostension of the contents, then the contents of the box — whatever private sensation or inner item is there — "cancels out" of the meaning. The thought experiment illustrates why the meaning of mental terms cannot be grounded in purely private inner items, bearing on whether thought can be constituted by private, non-linguistic representations.
