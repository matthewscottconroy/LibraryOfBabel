# Unit Introduction: Memory and Time-Consciousness

## Central Questions

Memory and time-consciousness are philosophically distinctive because they are the mind's ways of being present to its own past and future. Memory does not merely store information from the past; it presents the past as past — as something that happened to me, at a particular time, in a particular way. This *mnemonic presentness* is what distinguishes genuine remembering (with its phenomenal character of re-experiencing) from merely knowing something that once happened. Similarly, our experience of the present moment is not a knife-edge instant but an extended temporal window within which just-past events are still experienced as just-past and immediately upcoming events are anticipated as imminent.

Edmund Husserl's analysis of time-consciousness — in lectures he gave in 1905 and which were published posthumously — remains the most sophisticated phenomenological account of how consciousness is structured temporally. Husserl distinguished three aspects of every moment of consciousness: the *primal impression* (the experience of the current moment), *retention* (the experience of the just-past as having just occurred), and *protention* (the experience of the immediately expected as about to occur). This three-part structure explains how a melody can be heard as a melody — as a temporal pattern — rather than as a succession of isolated notes: each note is heard against the retained background of previous notes and the protended anticipation of notes to come.

A third set of questions concerns *episodic memory* — memory for personally experienced events — and its relationship to imagination, prospection (imagining the future), and personal identity. Endel Tulving's discovery that patients with hippocampal damage lose not only episodic memory but also the ability to imagine the future (a phenomenon called *mental time travel*) suggests deep connections between remembering the past and projecting into the future.

## Major Positions and Debates

### The Experiential and Non-Experiential Views of Memory

A fundamental debate in the philosophy of memory concerns whether genuine remembering requires an experiential connection to the original event. *Experiential memory* (Locke, Tulving) holds that episodic memory involves re-experiencing the original event in some form — not just knowing that it happened but reliving it from the inside. The phenomenal character of episodic memory — its sense of *pastness*, its egocentric perspective, and its sense of *mineness* — distinguishes it from merely propositional knowledge of past events.

*Simulationism* about memory (Schacter, Addis, Rosenbaum) holds that episodic memory is not a replay of the past but a constructive re-simulation of it. Every act of remembering involves actively reconstructing the episode from stored fragments, which is why memory is subject to distortions, intrusions, and false memories. On the simulationist view, the strong distinction between remembering and imagining (a future or past episode) weakens: both involve the same constructive simulation process, using overlapping neural systems (hippocampus, medial temporal lobe).

### Husserl's Time-Consciousness

Husserl's analysis distinguishes between *objective time* (the time studied by physics and history) and *immanent time* (the inner time of consciousness). The experience of a melody involves not objective time but the immanent temporal structure of consciousness: retention and protention spread out the experienced present across a *temporal horizon*.

Retention is not memory in the ordinary sense: I do not *recall* that a note just sounded; I am still experientially present to it, though in a mode of just-pastness. This immediate presence to the just-past is what distinguishes it from secondary memory (ordinary memory of something that happened earlier). The phenomenological analysis here is subtle and has implications for debates about the specious present (the duration of the experienced present moment) and about the relationship between consciousness and time.

Phenomenologists after Husserl (Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty) developed his account in different directions. Heidegger's analysis of *temporality* in *Being and Time* holds that the fundamental temporal structure of Dasein is not the retention-primal impression-protention of Husserl but the three-dimensional *ecstatic* structure of thrownness (past), facticity (present), and projection (future).

### Episodic Memory and Mental Time Travel

Tulving's distinction between *episodic memory* (memory for personally experienced events, with their subjective context of time and place) and *semantic memory* (factual knowledge without experiential context) has been enormously influential. The distinction is supported by neuropsychological evidence: patients with hippocampal damage lose episodic memory while retaining semantic memory; patients with semantic dementia show the reverse pattern.

The *mental time travel* hypothesis holds that episodic memory and prospection (imagining the future) share a common mechanism: both involve mentally projecting oneself to a different time point and constructing a first-person simulation. Patients who lose episodic memory typically also lose the ability to construct rich images of the future, supporting the idea that remembering and prospecting rely on the same constructive process.

### Temporal Binding and the Specious Present

The *specious present* is the duration of the subjectively experienced present moment — the temporal window within which events are experienced as simultaneous or in immediate succession rather than as past or future. Estimates of the specious present range from a few milliseconds to several seconds, depending on context and modality.

*Temporal binding* is the brain's tendency to associate events that are causally related as simultaneous even when they are separated in time. Voluntary action is temporally bound to its effects: the gap between pressing a key and hearing a tone is subjectively compressed when the tone is caused by the key press. This has implications for agency and free will: the subjective timing of action and its effects may be systematically distorted.

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**Edmund Husserl** (*On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time*, lectures 1905, published 1928) provided the most detailed analysis of temporal experience. His account of retention, protention, and the living present is foundational for phenomenological philosophy of mind.

**Martin Heidegger** developed the temporal structure of Dasein in *Being and Time* (1927): the three ecstases of temporality (having-been, present, future) constitute the structure of care and of Dasein's being-in-the-world.

**Endel Tulving** distinguished episodic from semantic memory in *Elements of Episodic Memory* (1983) and developed the concept of mental time travel. His distinction has organized three decades of neuropsychological and neuroimaging research.

**Daniel Schacter** developed the constructive memory framework (*The Seven Sins of Memory*, 2001; *Remembering the Past to Imagine the Future*, 2007), arguing that the same neural systems that support episodic memory also support future imagination.

**Marya Schechtman** (*The Constitution of Selves*, 1996) developed the narrative account of personal identity with particular attention to the role of memory in constructing a self-narrative.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Husserl, *On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* (1928)**
The foundational phenomenological analysis of temporal experience. Dense but essential. The analysis of retention, primal impression, and protention provides conceptual tools that no other approach has replicated. A good commentary (Zahavi, *Husserl's Phenomenology*) is advisable.

**2. Tulving, *Elements of Episodic Memory* (1983)**
The foundational account of episodic memory and its distinction from semantic memory. Tulving's analysis has driven neuropsychological research for four decades. The concept of *autonoetic consciousness* — the sense of self in time that characterizes episodic memory — is philosophically important.

**3. Schacter, *Searching for Memory* (1996) or *The Seven Sins of Memory* (2001)**
Schacter's work provides the most accessible account of the constructive character of memory and its vulnerabilities. The philosophical implications of the constructive view — for epistemic authority over one's own past, for personal identity, for the relationship between memory and imagination — are significant.

**4. Heidegger, *Being and Time* (1927), Division Two, Chapter 3**
The phenomenological analysis of temporality as the structure of care. Heidegger's account moves beyond Husserl in important ways: temporality is not just the structure of individual experience but the structure of Dasein's existence in the world. The discussion of being-toward-death and the authentic/inauthentic modes of temporality is philosophically distinctive.

**5. Suddendorf and Corballis, "The Evolution of Foresight: What is Mental Time Travel, and is it Unique to Humans?" (2007)**
The most comprehensive review of the mental time travel hypothesis and the evidence bearing on whether non-human animals can imagine the future. Essential for connecting philosophical questions about episodic memory to empirical cognitive science and comparative psychology.

## Open Questions

**1. Is the phenomenology of episodic memory veridical?**
Episodic memory presents itself as re-experiencing a past event from one's own perspective. But memory is constructive and subject to systematic distortions. Does the phenomenology mislead us about the nature of memory? And if memory is thoroughly constructive, can it still play the identity-constituting role that psychological continuity theories assign it?

**2. What is the specious present?**
The question of how long the experienced present moment is — and what determines its duration — remains genuinely open. Different experimental paradigms give very different estimates. Whether the specious present is a single unified phenomenon or a family of related phenomena is unclear.

**3. Can mental time travel be attributed to non-human animals?**
Bees appear to report food locations that they visited earlier; scrub jays seem to plan for future needs. Whether these behaviors involve genuine episodic-like memory and future imagination (with autonoetic awareness) or can be explained by simpler associative mechanisms is contested. The answer has implications for the uniqueness of human temporal cognition and for the moral status of non-human animals.

**4. What is the relationship between memory and the extended mind?**
If cognitive processes can extend into notebooks and smartphones (Clark-Chalmers), then memory can extend into external storage. But episodic memory seems to require a first-person experiential connection to the past that external storage cannot provide. Is extended memory possible, and if so, what would constitute genuine episodic memory in an extended system?

**5. Does temporal binding undermine the sense of agency?**
The brain's tendency to bind voluntary actions to their effects — and to subjectively anticipate those effects — could create an illusion of control. If the temporal order of experience systematically misrepresents the actual causal order of events, what does this mean for our sense of ourselves as agents?
