# Further Reading: Theories of Memory

## Primary Sources

**Henri Bergson, *Matter and Memory* (1896)** — Bergson's foundational distinction between habit memory (the body's motor dispositions) and pure memory (the complete, singular preservation of past experience) remains one of the deepest philosophical treatments of memory's nature. He argues against the localization of memory in the brain, treating genuine recollection as a process of actualization rather than storage-and-retrieval.

**Edmund Husserl, *On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* (1928/1964)** — Husserl's analysis of retention (the just-passed phase of experience still held in consciousness), primal impression (the now), and recollection (secondary memory or re-presentation) distinguishes different temporal modes of consciousness. His account of retention as not a memory but a living tail of the present has been enormously influential for phenomenological treatments of memory.

**Sven Bernecker, *The Metaphysics of Memory* (2008)** — A rigorous analytic treatment of memory's philosophical foundations, covering the causal theory of memory, the epistemic role of memory, memory's relationship to knowledge, and the persistence question. Bernecker defends a simulationist approach against pure epistemic theories and engages carefully with the psychological literature on reconstructive memory.

**Dorothea Frede and Burkhard Reis (eds.), *Body and Soul in Ancient Philosophy* (2009)** — Contains essential background on ancient theories of memory from Plato and Aristotle, important for understanding the long history of philosophical reflection on what memory is and how it relates to perception, knowledge, and the soul.

**John Sutton, *Philosophy and Memory Traces: Descartes to Connectionism* (1998)** — A historical and philosophical examination of memory trace theories from Descartes through the nineteenth century to contemporary connectionism, arguing that the concept of a memory trace is more philosophically loaded than is usually recognized.

## Secondary Literature

**Kourken Michaelian and John Sutton, "Memory" (2017), *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*** — The authoritative overview of the philosophy of memory, covering causal theories, epistemic theories, the simulationist approach, the relationship of memory to imagination, and the social dimensions of memory. An indispensable starting point.

**Kourken Michaelian, *Mental Time Travel: Episodic Memory and Our Knowledge of the Personal Past* (2016)** — A comprehensive philosophical account of episodic memory, arguing for a simulationist view on which remembering involves a constructive, imagination-like process constrained by causal connections to past experience. Engages extensively with empirical work on memory reconstruction.

**Marya Schechtman, *The Constitution of Selves* (1996)** — Chapter 2 develops a philosophical account of how memory constitutes personal identity through self-narrative, showing the connection between memory theory and personal identity theory.

**Merlin Donald, *Origins of the Modern Mind* (1991)** — Donald's evolutionary account of human cognitive development includes an important treatment of external memory systems—the way humans have extended biological memory through cultural and technological means. Provides important context for understanding memory as not purely biological.

**Elizabeth Loftus, "Memory Faults and Fixes" (2003), *Issues in Science and Technology*** — A readable overview by the leading researcher on memory malleability, covering how memories are implanted, modified, and lost, and the practical and legal implications of memory's fallibility.
