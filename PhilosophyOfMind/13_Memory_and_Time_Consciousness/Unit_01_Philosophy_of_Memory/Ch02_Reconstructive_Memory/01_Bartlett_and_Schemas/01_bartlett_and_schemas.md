# Bartlett, Schemas, and Reconstructive Memory

Frederic Bartlett's *Remembering* (1932) is one of the foundational texts of cognitive psychology and philosophy of memory. Through simple but revealing experiments, Bartlett demonstrated that memory is not a passive recording of experience but an active, constructive process shaped by prior knowledge, cultural schemas, and the needs of the present situation.

**The War of the Ghosts**

Bartlett's most famous experiments used a Native American folk tale — "The War of the Ghosts" — which was unfamiliar to his British subjects and contained elements inconsistent with their cultural expectations. Subjects read the story and were asked to recall it at various intervals.

The patterns of distortion in recall were systematic and revealing. Subjects tended to:
- Shorten and simplify the story
- Rationalize unusual elements to fit familiar schemas (a supernatural journey became a more mundane trip)
- Import familiar story conventions absent in the original
- Remember the emotional tone accurately while distorting the specific content
- Increase distortions with the passage of time, with the story becoming more conventional and less unusual with each retelling

Bartlett's conclusion: memory is not a fixed storehouse of impressions. It is an "effort after meaning" — an active process of making sense of past experience using available cognitive resources, including pre-existing knowledge structures he called "schemas."

**The Concept of Schema**

Bartlett borrowed the concept of schema from the neurologist Henry Head, who used it to describe the organized body-image that underlies perception of bodily movement. Bartlett extended it to cover all forms of organized prior knowledge that influence memory.

A schema is a structured set of expectations and knowledge about a domain — what typically happens in a restaurant, what the structure of a story involves, what a doctor's office looks like. Schemas guide encoding (we notice what is schema-consistent and schema-violating), organization (we store information in terms of schema structure), and retrieval (we reconstruct information using schemas to fill gaps).

The contemporary cognitive science concept of schema has been elaborated into frameworks like "frames" (Minsky), "scripts" (Schank and Abelson), and "situation models" (Zwaan). All of these capture the idea that our knowledge of the world is organized into structured templates that guide both understanding and memory.

**Schema-Based Distortions**

Schemas produce characteristic distortions in memory:

*Leveling*: Unusual, schema-violating details are forgotten or normalized. The Native American folk tale's unusual elements were leveled toward more familiar narrative conventions.

*Sharpening*: Some details are selectively retained and emphasized — typically those that are distinctive and schema-relevant.

*Assimilation*: Schema-inconsistent information is reinterpreted to fit existing schemas, or gaps are filled in with schema-typical information.

*False recognition*: We mistakenly "remember" schema-typical elements that were not present, because our reconstruction supplies them from the schema.

These distortions are not random; they follow the structure of the relevant schemas. Memory is not merely degraded over time; it is distorted in systematic, schema-shaped ways.

**The Philosophical Implications**

Bartlett's account has profound philosophical implications.

First, it challenges the "storehouse" metaphor for memory: memories are not stored and retrieved; they are reconstructed from fragments plus schemas. This reconstruction is influenced by current knowledge, current contexts, and current purposes — making memory an intrinsically present-oriented activity rather than a passive window onto the past.

Second, it implies that there is no sharp boundary between memory and inference. When we "remember" an event, we are partly recollecting stored traces and partly inferring what must have happened on the basis of schemas and expectations. The phenomenological sense of "remembering" does not reliably distinguish genuine recollection from schema-based inference.

Third, it suggests that the cultural context of memory is not merely background but is constitutive. Schemas are culturally specific: what is familiar, what is expected, what is worth noting are all culturally shaped. This means that cultural change affects what can be remembered and how — a fact relevant to both individual psychology and collective memory.

**Social and Collective Memory**

Bartlett's account extends naturally to collective memory — the shared memory of groups, communities, and nations. Just as individual memory is shaped by individual schemas, collective memory is shaped by collective schemas: the narratives, symbols, and frameworks through which groups understand their shared past.

Halbwachs's work on collective memory (1925) argued that all individual memory is shaped by social frameworks — that even personal memories are organized in terms of the social groups we belong to. This connects individual and collective memory as two aspects of a single socially embedded phenomenon.

The implications for historical knowledge are significant: what we "remember" about the past — what counts as important, how events are interpreted, what is worth preserving — is shaped by the schemas and narratives of our present cultural community. History is not merely the past as it was; it is the past reconstructed through present schemas.
