# Key Thinkers: Reconstructive Memory

---

**Frederic Bartlett (1886–1969)**
British psychologist at Cambridge University, first professor of experimental psychology at Cambridge. Bartlett's *Remembering* (1932) established the empirical foundations for reconstructive memory theory, showing through his famous "War of the Ghosts" experiments that subjects' recall of culturally alien narratives was systematically transformed by their own cultural schemas and expectations. His concept of "schema" anticipated modern framework theories of cognition, and his emphasis on memory as an active process of reconstruction oriented toward meaning challenged the dominant trace-reproduction model. His work was largely neglected for decades before being revived in cognitive psychology.
*Key works:* *Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology* (1932), *Thinking: An Experimental and Social Study* (1958)

---

**Elizabeth Loftus (1944–)**
American cognitive psychologist at UC Irvine, world authority on the malleability of human memory. Loftus's research has demonstrated that human memory is profoundly susceptible to post-event suggestion: misinformation presented after an event can alter or replace genuine memories, and entirely false memories can be implanted through suggestion and imagination. Her testimony in criminal cases involving eyewitness identification has had direct social impact, and her work was central to the "memory wars" controversy over repressed and recovered memories in the 1990s.
*Key works:* *Eyewitness Testimony* (1979), "The Formation of False Memories" (1995), *Witness for the Defense* (1991)

---

**Daniel Schacter (1952–)**
American cognitive neuroscientist at Harvard University. Schacter has provided the most comprehensive synthesis of cognitive and neuroscientific research on memory's failures and their significance. His "seven sins of memory" framework organizes memory errors by their underlying mechanisms, and his research on the relationship between memory and imagination has been central to developing the "constructive episodic simulation" hypothesis—the view that episodic memory and future imagination share a common neural and cognitive machinery.
*Key works:* *Searching for Memory* (1996), *The Seven Sins of Memory* (2001), "The Future of Memory: Remembering, Imagining, and the Brain" (2012)

---

**Martin Conway (1953–)**
British cognitive psychologist at City, University of London. Conway developed the Self-Memory System model, which holds that autobiographical memories are constructed dynamically from a hierarchical structure of event knowledge (lifetime periods, general events, event-specific knowledge) in service of the current self's goals and concerns. His model captures why autobiographical memory is both reconstructive and self-relevant, and why memories can change as the self develops. His work connects memory research to personal identity and the narrative self.
*Key works:* *Autobiographical Memory: An Introduction* (1990), "The Construction of Autobiographical Memories in the Self-Memory System" (2000, with Pleydell-Pearce)

---

**Ulric Neisser (1928–2012)**
German-American cognitive psychologist, founder of cognitive psychology as a field. Neisser coined the term "cognitive psychology" and brought Bartlett's reconstructive framework back to prominence in the cognitive era. His naturalistic studies of memory—including his research on flashbulb memories and his "Challenger" study of how people's memories for the space shuttle disaster changed over time—showed that even vivid, emotionally significant memories are subject to reconstruction and distortion.
*Key works:* *Cognitive Psychology* (1967), *Memory Observed: Remembering in Natural Contexts* (1982)
