# Prospection and Agency

The capacity for prospection — for representing and anticipating the future — is intimately connected to agency. To act intentionally is to act toward a future goal; to plan is to represent the future and select among possible courses of action. The relationship between prospection and agency is thus not merely contingent but conceptually close: agency, at least in its full human form, requires prospection.

**Agency and Future-Directedness**

Agency, at its most basic, involves action guided by states that represent goals or desired outcomes. Even simple organisms respond to environmental stimuli in goal-directed ways — the bacterium "seeking" nutrients, the cat stalking its prey. But the richer forms of agency associated with rational, deliberate action involve explicit representation of future states and the selection of actions on the basis of anticipated consequences.

Bratman's planning theory (discussed in the chapter on intention) captures the prospective dimension of human agency: we form plans — representations of intended future actions — that coordinate present and future behavior. The capacity for planning is partly a capacity for prospection: it requires the ability to simulate future scenarios, assess their desirability, and select actions that lead to preferred futures.

Deliberation, too, is fundamentally prospective: when I deliberate about what to do, I simulate the likely consequences of different choices, compare their anticipated qualities, and select the action with the best anticipated outcome. Deliberation without prospection — without the capacity to anticipate future states — would be reduced to responding to the immediate situation, not genuine deliberative choice.

**Prospection and Rational Self-Governance**

The relationship between prospection and rational self-governance is a central theme in Parfit's work. Parfit argues that the irrationality of extreme time-preference (caring much less about future suffering than present suffering, simply because it is in the future) can be understood as a failure of prospective appreciation: we fail to fully realize, emotionally and motivationally, that the future suffering will be just as real and just as ours as present suffering.

Vivid episodic future thinking may help correct this bias: when we can simulate future suffering (or future pleasure) vividly, we are more likely to give it appropriate weight in deliberation. The "future self continuity" literature (Hershfield) shows that people who feel less connected to their future selves tend to have higher time preferences — to discount the future more steeply. Vivid episodic future thinking of one's own future life can increase felt continuity and reduce discounting.

**Prospection and Regret**

Prospection also operates in reverse, in a sense: anticipated regret — the simulation of how one would feel after making a choice that turns out badly — is a powerful motivator and a significant source of decision distortion. People often make decisions to minimize anticipated regret rather than to maximize expected utility.

Marcel Zeelenberg's research on the role of anticipated regret in decision-making shows that people choose in ways that reduce their potential regret, sometimes at significant cost to expected outcomes. When the potential for regret is made salient — when people are asked to imagine how they would feel if they made the "wrong" choice — they become more cautious.

This connects prospection to the phenomenology of temporal experience discussed earlier: the anticipated future can color and motivate present experience in ways that alter its quality. Living with an eye to the future — whether in hope, dread, or the determination to avoid regret — is a distinctive feature of temporal human existence.

**Prospection and the Hard Problem**

Does prospection have any special phenomenal character? When we engage in vivid episodic future thinking — imagining ourselves at a future moment, experiencing anticipated pleasures or dreading anticipated pains — there does seem to be a phenomenal quality to the simulation. The imagined future pain is not itself painful, but there is something it is like to anticipate it, which differs from the experience of either present pain or of knowing abstractly that pain will occur.

This phenomenal dimension of prospection connects to broader questions about the relationship between imagination and experience. If imagining a future scenario recruits many of the same neural systems as experiencing the present scenario, and if those neural activations have phenomenal character, then prospective imagination may have genuine (if muted) phenomenal character — something it is like to anticipate a future that has not yet arrived.
