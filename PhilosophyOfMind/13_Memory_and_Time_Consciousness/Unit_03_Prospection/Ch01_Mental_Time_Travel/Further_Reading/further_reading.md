# Further Reading: Mental Time Travel

## Primary Sources

**Endel Tulving, "Episodic and Semantic Memory" (1972), in *Organization of Memory*** — The foundational paper distinguishing episodic memory (memory for specific personal events, located in time and place) from semantic memory (general knowledge). This distinction has been crucial for the study of mental time travel, since it is specifically episodic memory that enables subjective travel to past and future times.

**Endel Tulving, "Memory and Consciousness" (1985), *Canadian Psychology*** — Tulving introduces the concept of "chronesthesia" (mental time travel) and "autonoetic consciousness"—the form of consciousness that accompanies episodic memory and enables awareness of subjective time. This paper establishes the conceptual framework for understanding the distinctive phenomenology of episodic recollection.

**Daniel Schacter, Donna Rose Addis, and Randy Buckner, "Remembering the Past to Imagine the Future: The Prospective Brain" (2007), *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*** — A highly influential paper arguing that episodic memory and future imagination rely on the same neural system (the default mode network) and the same underlying process of constructive episodic simulation. This paper launched a major research program on the relationship between memory and prospection.

**Thomas Suddendorf and Michael Corballis, "The Evolution of Foresight: What is Mental Time Travel, and Is It Unique to Humans?" (2007), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*** — A comprehensive review of the evidence for and against mental time travel in non-human animals, arguing that while animals show some prospective behaviors, genuine mental time travel (involving autonoetic self-projection) may be uniquely human. Includes extensive commentary from researchers across disciplines.

**Kourken Michaelian, *Mental Time Travel: Episodic Memory and Our Knowledge of the Personal Past* (2016)** — Michaelian develops the simulationist account of episodic memory, arguing that remembering past events and imagining future ones both involve the same underlying simulation process. He examines the epistemological implications—can constructive simulation still yield genuine knowledge?—and engages with evidence from amnesic patients.

## Secondary Literature

**Daniel Schacter, *The Seven Sins of Memory* (2001)** — Places mental time travel within a broader account of how memory's constructive character supports adaptive prospection. Chapter 8, "Memory and the Future," is particularly relevant.

**Adam Buckner and Jessica Andrews-Hanna, "The Brain's Default Network: Anatomy, Function, and Relevance to Disease" (2008), *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences*** — Reviews the neuroscience of the default mode network that underlies mental time travel, covering its anatomy, its activation during rest and mind-wandering, and its disruption in various neurological conditions.

**Armin Rashidi-Ranjbar et al., "Episodic Memory" (various)** — For clinical dimensions, patient studies with hippocampal amnesia demonstrate the dependence of both episodic recall and future imagination on intact medial temporal lobe systems, providing crucial evidence for the common-core hypothesis.

**Donna Addis and Daniel Schacter, "The Hippocampus and Imagining the Future: Where Do We Stand?" (2012), *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*** — A focused review of the evidence from neuroimaging and patient studies for shared mechanisms of past and future mental time travel. Clear and current overview of the empirical landscape.

**Thomas Suddendorf, *The Gap: The Science of What Separates Us from Other Animals* (2013)** — A readable exploration of the evidence on what cognitive capacities are uniquely human, with substantial discussion of episodic memory and mental time travel and their role in human uniqueness.
