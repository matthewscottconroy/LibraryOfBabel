# Epicurean Arguments About Death

Epicurus's argument that death cannot be a harm to the one who dies — because when death is, you are not, and when you are, death is not — is one of the most controversial arguments in ancient philosophy. Its apparent conclusion (that death is not bad for the person who dies) conflicts strongly with commonsense attitudes. Evaluating this argument requires careful attention to what death means for the experiencer, for personal identity, and for the value of continued existence.

**Epicurus's Argument**

Epicurus's argument is compact: "Death is nothing to us, since when we exist, death is not yet present, and when death is present, then we do not exist." The argument rests on an experiential account of harm: something is a harm only if there is someone who is harmed by it, someone who is made worse off. But death is the cessation of existence — the person who dies no longer exists. There is no one to be harmed.

This argument is sometimes called the "Epicurean consolation": we need not fear death because death cannot harm us. The proper attitude toward death is indifference, not fear. Time spent in anxious anticipation of death is time wasted.

**Thomas Nagel's Critique**

Thomas Nagel's essay "Death" (1970) is the canonical response. Nagel argues that Epicurus's experiential account of harm is too restrictive. We can be harmed by events or states of affairs even if we do not experience the harm — indeed, even if we never come to know about it.

Nagel's central argument invokes the idea of "comparative" harm: something is bad for a person if it makes them worse off than they would otherwise have been. Death makes a person worse off — it deprives them of the experiences, relationships, and achievements they would otherwise have had. This deprivation is the harm, not any experienced suffering.

The asymmetry between prenatal non-existence and posthumous non-existence is also relevant. We do not typically mourn the vast period before our birth during which we did not exist. Why should we be troubled by our posthumous non-existence if we are not troubled by our prenatal non-existence? Nagel argues that there is an asymmetry: we exist when we die (we are the subjects of the posthumous non-existence), but we did not exist before birth (there was no subject for the prenatal non-existence).

**The Deprivation Account**

Fred Feldman and others have developed a deprivation account of why death is bad: death is bad for a person to the extent that it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have enjoyed. The harm of death is the value of the experiences, relationships, and achievements foregone.

On this account, death at 100 is not typically as bad as death at 20 — the person who dies at 20 is deprived of more prospective goods. Death may not be bad at all if the life remaining would have been one of pure suffering; it is typically bad for people who would have continued to live well.

The deprivation account handles Epicurus's objection by denying that the subject of harm must experience the harm. The relevant comparison is between two worlds: the world in which the person dies and the world in which they live. The person is harmed to the extent that the second world is better for them than the first.

**The Timing Problem**

One puzzle for the deprivation account is the timing of the harm. When is death a harm? Not during death (the person does not exist). Not before death (the death has not yet occurred). Perhaps at no time at all — death is an "atemporal" harm, in Bernard Williams's phrase.

This atemporal harming is philosophically uncomfortable but perhaps acceptable: some harms (the harm of defamation against a person who will never know about it) may have no particular time at which they occur. What matters is that the state of affairs (the death, the defamation) is worse for the person than an alternative state of affairs in which they are not dead, not defamed.

**Williams on the Tedium of Immortality**

Bernard Williams responded to the deprivation account with the observation that immortality would not necessarily be desirable. Williams argued that what makes a life worth continuing is the presence of "categorical desires" — desires for things whose existence depends on the subject continuing to exist. If a person were to live forever, either their desires would have to keep changing (in which case the immortal person is not really the same person who wanted to live forever) or they would eventually exhaust their categorical desires and face endless tedium.

Williams's argument suggests that the appropriate attitude toward death is neither the Epicurean equanimity (death is nothing to us) nor unreserved mourning of all deaths, but a more nuanced assessment that depends on the quality of the life that would continue. Some deaths are grievous deprivations; others may not be.
