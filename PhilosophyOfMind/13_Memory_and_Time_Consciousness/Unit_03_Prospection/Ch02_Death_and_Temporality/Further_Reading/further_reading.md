# Further Reading: Death and Temporality

## Primary Sources

**Epicurus, "Letter to Menoeceus" (c. 300 BCE)** — Epicurus's argument that death cannot harm us because when death is present we are absent ("when death is, I am not; when I am, death is not") remains the starting point for all philosophical discussion of death's badness. His symmetry argument—that we are untroubled by the infinite time before our birth, so we should be equally untroubled by the time after our death—has generated extensive philosophical debate.

**Thomas Nagel, "Death" (1970), in *Mortal Questions*** — Nagel's seminal paper challenges the Epicurean argument, defending the deprivation account: death is bad for the person who dies because it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have enjoyed. He argues that the absence of a subject during posthumous non-existence does not show that death is not bad, since deprivation of goods does not require an experiencer.

**Martin Heidegger, *Being and Time*, Division II (1927)** — Heidegger's analysis of "being-toward-death" holds that authentic Dasein must hold open the possibility of its own death as its ownmost, non-relational, certain, and indefinite possibility. Confronting death with existential honesty—rather than fleeing into das Man's reassurances—individuates Dasein and grounds authentic temporal existence. Death is not an event that occurs but a constant structural feature of existence.

**Jeff McMahan, *The Ethics of Killing: Problems at the Margins of Life* (2002)** — McMahan develops a sophisticated account of the badness of death that combines the deprivation account with psychological continuity theory: the badness of death depends on both what goods would have been experienced and the degree of psychological connectedness between the person who dies and the future person deprived. His account has important implications for the ethics of killing at all life stages.

**Bernard Williams, "The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality" (1973)** — Williams argues that immortality would be undesirable because identity requires categorical desires oriented toward a finite future, and that without the prospect of death our pursuits would lose their point. His argument that death is not simply bad opens philosophical space for understanding what makes mortality appropriate to a human life.

## Secondary Literature

**Stephen Rosenbaum, "How to Be Dead and Not Care: A Defense of Epicurus" (1989), *American Philosophical Quarterly*** — A careful defense of the Epicurean argument against contemporary objections, arguing that the deprivation account fails because there is no subject of deprivation during posthumous non-existence.

**Fred Feldman, *Confrontations with the Reaper: A Philosophical Study of the Nature and Value of Death* (1992)** — A comprehensive philosophical treatment of death, covering the definition of death, the deprivation account, and hedonistic versus desire-satisfaction theories of welfare as they bear on the badness of death.

**Todd May, *Death* (2009)** — A clear, philosophically sophisticated introduction to the topic, engaging with both the analytic tradition (Nagel, Feldman, McMahan) and the phenomenological tradition (Heidegger) in an accessible way. Suitable for advanced undergraduate courses.

**Ben Bradley, *Well-Being and Death* (2009)** — A rigorous technical treatment of the relationship between theories of welfare and the badness of death, examining how different accounts of what makes life go well bear on whether and why death is bad.

**Samuel Scheffler, *Death and the Afterlife* (2013)** — Scheffler develops the provocative thesis that what matters to us is not just our own survival but the continuation of humanity after our death—that the prospect of collective extinction would devastate our individual lives. A creative and important contribution to thinking about death's significance.
