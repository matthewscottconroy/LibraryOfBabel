# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Death and Temporality

## Key Arguments

**Epicurus's Symmetry Argument**
Epicurus argues that death cannot be bad for the person who dies because badness requires a subject who experiences it, and when death occurs the subject no longer exists to be harmed. The argument concludes: "When I am, death is not; when death is, I am not; therefore death is nothing to me." This argument against the badness of death has been enormously influential and remains a touchstone for all subsequent philosophical treatments, forcing defenders of the view that death is bad to explain how non-existence can harm a non-existing subject.

**Lucretius's Mirror Argument**
Lucretius extends the Epicurean argument by noting the symmetry between the prenatal non-existence (the time before we existed) and post-mortem non-existence: we are not troubled by the time before our birth, and if death is bad because of deprivation, prenatal non-existence should be equally bad. Since we are not distressed by the prenatal period, we should not be distressed by the post-mortem period either. Nagel and others respond by arguing that the two periods of non-existence are asymmetric: one can be deprived of a life only if one exists, but we did not exist before our birth in the relevant way.

**Nagel's Deprivation Account**
Thomas Nagel argues, against Epicurus, that death can be bad for the person who dies through deprivation: death deprives its subject of the goods of continued life that would otherwise have been available. The argument does not require the deceased to consciously experience the deprivation; it suffices that the person would have had goods. Nagel accepts that this creates a puzzle about when and where the harm occurs (since the deceased does not exist to bear it) but insists that our evaluative practices and existential fears are not irrational.

**Heidegger's Being-Toward-Death**
Heidegger argues that authentic existence requires owning one's own death as one's ownmost, non-relational, certain, and indefinitely impending possibility — the one possibility that cannot be taken over by another and that individualizes Dasein most completely. The "anticipatory resoluteness" called for by this awareness of mortality transforms how one takes up one's projects: it strips away the distracting securities of the "they-self" (das Man) and summons Dasein to its authentic self. Being-toward-death is not a recommendation to dwell on death but to acknowledge one's finitude as the condition for meaningful existence.

**The Non-Identity Problem for Death (Feldman)**
Fred Feldman argues that the deprivation account faces a challenge: at the time of death, there is no one to be deprived; before death, the person is not yet deprived (they still have their goods). Feldman's own solution involves Epicurean-friendly moves about timeless harm — harm can be real without being experienced at a particular time. This debate about when death harms connects to broader issues in the metaphysics of harm, temporal location of states, and whether interests can be set back without subjective experience.

## Core Concepts

**The Epicurean Argument**
The Epicurean argument against the badness of death runs: (1) something can be bad for a person only if the person can be the subject of that badness; (2) when death occurs, the person no longer exists and therefore cannot be a subject of badness; (3) therefore death cannot be bad for the person who dies. The argument's appeal to the necessity of a conscious subject for harm underpins its force and has generated extensive philosophical debate about the ontology of harm, the subject requirement, and the relevance of non-existence to well-being.

**The Deprivation Account**
The deprivation account holds that death is bad for the person who dies because it deprives that person of the goods — experiences, achievements, relationships — that continued life would have contained. Death's badness is comparative: the person in the possible world where they continue to live has a better life than the person in the actual world where they die. The account is defended by Nagel, Parfit, and many others, and faces the challenge of identifying the subject who is deprived when no subject exists post-mortem.

**Being-Toward-Death (Heidegger)**
Heidegger's existential analytic treats death not as a biological event but as the structural possibility of Dasein's impossibility — the condition in which Dasein's ability-to-be comes to an end. Being-toward-death is the ontological structure that gives Dasein its individuality, urgency, and the possibility of authenticity. Rather than treating death as a future fact to be endured, Heidegger argues that Dasein must take up its mortality as an ever-present structural feature of its existence, enabling a relationship to its own finitude that transforms the character of every project.

**The Symmetry Thesis (Lucretius)**
The symmetry thesis holds that pre-natal non-existence and post-mortem non-existence are metaphysically and evaluatively symmetric: both are periods of non-experience, and if one is not bad the other should not be either. The thesis is used by Epicureans to support the claim that fear of death is irrational. Asymmetric responses argue that our interests and narrative identities are directed differently with respect to past and future: future goods can be anticipated and deprived, while past goods (of a pre-natal life) could not have been ours.

**Anticipatory Resoluteness (Heidegger)**
Heidegger's term for the authentic mode of being that arises from fully acknowledging one's being-toward-death. Anticipatory resoluteness does not mean eagerness for death but a clear-eyed acknowledgment of one's finitude that enables decisive, wholehearted engagement with one's ownmost possibilities, free from the dispersal and inauthenticity of das Man (the anonymous "they-self"). It is the phenomenological correlate of authenticity in the face of death.

**Non-Existence and the Subject Requirement**
The subject requirement for harm holds that a state of affairs can be bad for a person only if there is a subject who is harmed — who occupies some experiential or interest-bearing relationship to the harmful state. Applied to death, the subject requirement seems to preclude posthumous harm (since there is no subject after death), while the deprivation account requires it. This tension generates the central puzzle of the philosophy of death and connects to debates about harm to future generations, dead people's interests, and postmortem duties.

## Thought Experiments

**Lucretius's Mirror**
Lucretius invites us to consider the vast expanse of time before our birth: we were not there, and we are not troubled by it. If death's badness consists in non-existence, the prenatal period of non-existence should trouble us equally. Since it does not, either death is not bad (the Epicurean conclusion), or there is an asymmetry between prenatal and posthumous non-existence that we must explain. The thought experiment is one of the most discussed cases in the philosophy of death, and responses include appeals to asymmetric attitudes to past and future goods (Nagel, Parfit) and to the origins of persons (Parfit, McMahan).

**Nagel's Unfortunate Caesar**
Nagel asks us to consider whether it matters that Julius Caesar never owned a sports car — a good he missed by being born too early. We feel the deprivation is not bad for Caesar because sports cars were not possible for him. Contrast this with death: someone who dies young is deprived of a future that was a live possibility. The thought experiment distinguishes genuine deprivation (missing a possible good) from non-deprivation (lacking an impossible good) and supports the deprivation account by showing that not all forms of non-possession constitute harm.

**Woody Allen's Distinction: The Eternal and the Now**
As a thought experiment in the Epicurean tradition, consider the Epicurean prescription: if you knew that after death you would simply cease to experience — no pain, no regret, nothing — would you fear death? Many people report that they would still fear death even knowing this, because what they fear is not posthumous suffering but the ending of the goods of life. This phenomenological observation is used to argue that the Epicurean argument, even if metaphysically sound, does not dissolve existential death anxiety, because what we fear is the deprivation itself, not posthumous suffering. It reveals the gap between philosophical argument and lived existential attitude that Heidegger's analysis is intended to address.
