# Key Thinkers: Death and Temporality

---

**Epicurus (341–270 BCE)**
Ancient Greek philosopher, founder of the Epicurean school in Athens. Epicurus argued that death cannot harm us because harm requires a subject, and when death has occurred there is no longer a subject to be harmed. His "symmetry argument" challenges us to consider why we are untroubled by the infinite time before our birth but disturbed by the prospect of our death: if pre-natal non-existence is not bad, posthumous non-existence should not be either. This argument frames all subsequent debate about the rationality of death-fear and the badness of dying.
*Key works:* "Letter to Menoeceus" (c. 300 BCE), *Principal Doctrines*

---

**Martin Heidegger (1889–1976)**
German philosopher, one of the most influential figures in twentieth-century continental thought. Heidegger's analysis of "being-toward-death" in *Being and Time* (1927) treats death not as a future event but as a constant existential possibility that individuates and individualizes Dasein. Authentic existence requires "running ahead" to one's death—acknowledging that it is ownmost, non-relational, certain, and indefinitely proximate. This confrontation with finitude is for Heidegger what gives temporal existence its urgency and meaning, making death constitutive of authentic selfhood rather than merely its termination.
*Key works:* *Being and Time* (1927), *The Concept of Time* (1924)

---

**Thomas Nagel (1937–)**
American philosopher at NYU. Nagel's paper "Death" (1970) defends the deprivation account against Epicurean arguments: death is bad for the person who dies because it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have enjoyed—continued experience, relationships, and projects. His reply to the Epicurean is that a deprivation can be bad for a subject even if that subject no longer exists to experience it: what matters is the counterfactual comparison between the life one had and the better life one would have had. Nagel's paper also raises the puzzle of posthumous harm and remains the standard reference point for the contemporary debate.
*Key works:* "Death" (1970), *Mortal Questions* (1979), *The View from Nowhere* (1986)

---

**Bernard Williams (1929–2003)**
British moral philosopher at Cambridge and Berkeley. Williams' "The Makropulos Case" (1973) argues both against Epicurus and against the desirability of immortality. He accepts that death can be bad while arguing that immortal life would be worse—not because we would get bored, but because immortality is incompatible with the kind of categorical desires that give a person their identity. Williams' analysis illuminates how mortality is internal to the structure of a human life rather than simply an evil to be overcome.
*Key works:* "The Makropulos Case" (1973), *Problems of the Self* (1973), *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* (1985)

---

**Jeff McMahan (1954–)**
British moral philosopher at Oxford University. McMahan's deprivation account in *The Ethics of Killing* (2002) combines insights from psychological continuity theory with welfare theory: the badness of death depends on both the quantity of goods foregone and the degree of psychological connectedness between the current person and the future person deprived. This framework generates counterintuitive but important implications—early deaths are worse than late ones, and the deaths of infants may be less bad than those of young adults—while providing a principled basis for thinking about killing at all stages of life.
*Key works:* *The Ethics of Killing* (2002), *Killing in War* (2009)
