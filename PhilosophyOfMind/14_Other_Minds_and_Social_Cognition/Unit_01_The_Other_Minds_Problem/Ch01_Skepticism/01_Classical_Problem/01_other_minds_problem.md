# The Problem of Other Minds

The problem of other minds asks how we can know that other people have minds — that there is something it is like to be them, that they have conscious experiences, beliefs, desires, and feelings. Each of us has direct access to our own mental life, but we can observe only the behavior and physical states of others. How do we bridge the gap from observable behavior to the invisible inner life?

**The Classical Formulation**

The problem is often stated in terms of an asymmetry of access. I know my own mental states directly — I know I am in pain by being in pain, not by observing my behavior or neurological states. But I can observe only the external, public behavior of others. No matter how much behavioral and neural evidence I gather about another person, I can never directly observe their inner experience.

This asymmetry generates a skeptical worry: the behavioral and neural evidence I observe is, in principle, compatible with the hypothesis that the other person is a philosophical zombie — physically and behaviorally identical to a conscious being, but lacking any inner experience. Since I cannot rule out this hypothesis on behavioral evidence alone, the problem of other minds becomes a form of skepticism analogous to the problem of the external world.

The problem was sharpened by Descartes's dualism: if the mind is a non-physical substance, then there is no necessary connection between physical behavior and mental states. Physical behavior could occur without any accompanying mental life. The behavioral "evidence" for other minds is at best indirect and inconclusive.

**Analogy and Argument from Analogy**

The traditional response to the problem of other minds is the argument from analogy: I observe that in my own case, certain physical and behavioral states (facial expressions, vocalizations, approach and avoidance behaviors) are associated with specific mental states (pleasure, pain, fear). By analogy, when I observe similar behaviors in others, I infer that they too are accompanied by similar mental states.

John Stuart Mill gave a classic formulation: I know that fire burns me (pain follows contact with fire in my case); I know that other humans have nervous systems similar to mine; therefore, by analogy, fire probably causes pain in others.

The problem with the argument from analogy is that it is based on a single case — my own. In most inductive inferences, we generalize from a substantial sample; here we generalize from one instance to billions. This seems epistemically weak. Moreover, the inference is from the observable (behavior, neural states) to the unobservable (inner experience), which is a problematic inference pattern.

**Wittgenstein's Response**

Wittgenstein argued that the traditional formulation of the problem of other minds is based on a misunderstanding of the grammar of mental language. Our knowledge of other minds is not based on inference from observed behavior to inferred mental states; it is built into our practices of using mental language.

Wittgenstein's private language argument shows that the very idea of a mental state that is "purely private" — knowable only to the subject who has it, and not connected in principle to any public criteria — is incoherent. If "pain" picks out a purely private state, there would be no way to establish whether the word is being used correctly. The meaning of mental terms is constituted by their connection to public criteria — behavioral criteria — not by pointing to a private inner state.

This does not mean that mental states are just behavior; it means that the behavioral criteria are part of what it is to attribute mental states. To see someone writhing and crying out from an injury is not to observe behavior and then infer pain; it is (in normal circumstances) to see pain directly.

**The Hard Problem and Other Minds**

The problem of other minds connects directly to the hard problem of consciousness. What makes the problem intractable is precisely the phenomenal dimension of mental life: we can imagine all the functional and behavioral criteria being satisfied while the phenomenal experience is absent (the philosophical zombie). If the hard problem has no easy solution, the problem of other minds may likewise resist full resolution.

The residual worry is not merely academic. With the development of AI systems that exhibit increasingly sophisticated behavior, the question of which systems have genuine inner experiences becomes practically urgent. Our ordinary criteria for attributing mental states are behavioral, and AI systems may eventually pass all behavioral tests. Whether this is sufficient for genuine mentality — whether behavior plus appropriate architecture is enough for experience — is a version of the problem of other minds applied to non-biological systems.
