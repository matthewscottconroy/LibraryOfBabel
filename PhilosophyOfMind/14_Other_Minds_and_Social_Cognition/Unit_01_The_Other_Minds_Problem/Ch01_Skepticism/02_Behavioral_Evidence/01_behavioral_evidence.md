# Behavioral Evidence and the Problem of Other Minds

If we cannot directly access others' inner lives, behavioral evidence is what we have. The question is whether behavioral evidence is sufficient — and if not, what more would be needed.

**The Behaviorist Response**

One response to the problem of other minds is behaviorism: the view that mental states are nothing over and above behavioral dispositions. On this view, to be in pain is to be disposed to cry out, to favor the injured part, to seek relief, and so on. If this behavioral characterization exhausts what pain is, then knowledge of other minds through behavior is unproblematic — we can know another is in pain because their behavioral dispositions are precisely what pain consists in.

Logical behaviorism (Ryle, Carnap, Hempel) held that mental state attributions can be translated into statements about behavioral dispositions without loss of content. This view has been largely abandoned: the multiple realizability of mental states (the same behavior can be produced by different mental states, and the same mental state can produce different behaviors in different circumstances) shows that behavioral dispositions are at best defeasible evidence for mental states, not constitutive of them.

**Functionalism and Behavioral Evidence**

Functionalism provides a more sophisticated account. Mental states are defined by their functional roles — their causal-dispositional relationships to inputs, outputs, and other mental states. On the functionalist account, what evidence we have for mental states is evidence about functional organization. Behavior is evidence for underlying functional states; the task is to infer the functional architecture from behavioral data.

This does not require a perfect mapping between behaviors and mental states. The same behavior can be produced by different combinations of beliefs and desires; inferring the correct combination requires modeling the entire functional system. What functionalism provides is a principled account of what we are trying to determine when we attribute mental states to others: we are trying to determine whether the relevant functional organization is present.

**Animal Minds**

The problem of other minds is not limited to other humans. The same arguments that raise doubts about human minds apply, with even more force, to animal minds. Animals cannot communicate their mental states in language; we can observe only their behavior and neural states. Whether animals have consciousness, pain, and emotion is a question of enormous philosophical and practical importance.

The behavioral evidence for animal minds is extensive: nociceptive responses (avoidance of harmful stimuli, wound protection, limb guarding) are present in virtually all vertebrates and some invertebrates. Behavioral flexibility, learning, and problem-solving suggest something like cognition. The neural substrates associated with consciousness in humans (cortical and subcortical structures) are present in varying degrees across the animal kingdom.

The Cambrian Declaration (2012), signed by leading neuroscientists, asserted that non-human animals possess the neurological substrates for conscious states. But whether such substrates are sufficient for phenomenal consciousness — whether there is something it is like to be a fish — remains philosophically contested.

**The Limits of Behavioral Evidence**

The deep problem with behavioral evidence is that it does not directly address the phenomenal dimension of mental life. A system can exhibit all the behaviors associated with consciousness — pain behavior, attention, recognition, communication — without any accompanying phenomenal experience, as the philosophical zombie thought experiment illustrates.

This means that behavioral evidence for mental states is necessarily indirect and defeasible. It is the best evidence we have, and in ordinary social life it is more than sufficient — we do not seriously doubt that our friends and family are conscious. But philosophically, it leaves an epistemic gap between what we can observe and what we claim to know about mental life.

Closing this gap requires either showing that phenomenal consciousness is necessitated by the right kind of functional organization (the functionalist position), or acknowledging a residual uncertainty about which systems are conscious that behavioral evidence alone cannot eliminate. The latter position may be appropriate: confident attribution of mentality to systems most like us, decreasing confidence as the system differs from us in relevant ways, and principled uncertainty about genuinely novel cases.
