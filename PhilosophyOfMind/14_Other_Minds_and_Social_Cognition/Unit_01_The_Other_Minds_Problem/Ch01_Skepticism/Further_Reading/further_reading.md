# Further Reading: Skepticism about Other Minds

## Primary Sources

**John Stuart Mill, *An Examination of Sir William Hamilton's Philosophy*, Ch. 12 (1865)** — Mill presents the classical argument from analogy for knowledge of other minds: because I observe that my own bodily behavior accompanies mental states, and because other human bodies behave similarly, I can infer by analogy that similar mental states accompany their behavior. Though widely criticized for relying on a very small sample (one's own case), this argument remains the default starting point for the debate.

**Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* (1953)** — Wittgenstein's private language argument challenges the view that mental states are inner private objects whose names are learned by ostensive definition. His claim that the criteria for mental state attributions are behavioral and public suggests that skepticism about other minds arises from a confused model of how mental language works, rather than from a genuine gap in our knowledge.

**Gilbert Ryle, *The Concept of Mind* (1949)** — Ryle's logical behaviorism dissolves the other minds problem by arguing that mental state attributions are not descriptions of hidden inner events but of behavioral dispositions and tendencies. If having a belief or intention is nothing over and above certain patterns of behavior, there is no problem in principle about knowing others' minds.

**A.J. Ayer, "One's Knowledge of Other Minds" (1953), *Theoria*** — Ayer examines the argument from analogy, responses to it, and the status of other-minds knowledge within an empiricist framework. He considers whether the problem is genuinely epistemological or whether it arises from conceptual confusion about the nature of mind.

**P.F. Strawson, *Individuals: An Essay in Descriptive Metaphysics*, Ch. 3 (1959)** — Strawson argues that the concept of a person is logically primitive—it is a concept of an entity to whom both physical and psychological predicates are equally applicable. On this view, the skeptical problem is generated by a distorted view of what persons are, and once the primacy of the person concept is recognized, the problem dissolves.

## Secondary Literature

**Ian Ravenscroft, "Folk Psychology as a Theory" (1997), *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*** — Provides a clear survey of the debate between theory-theory and simulation theory as accounts of how we come to know other minds, situating them in relation to the classical other minds problem.

**Peter Strawson, *Skepticism and Naturalism: Some Varieties* (1985)** — Develops the view that skepticism about other minds, like other forms of skepticism, can be met naturalistically: our attribution of mental states to others is a framework belief that cannot coherently be doubted from within. Connects the other minds problem to broader questions in epistemology.

**Alvin Goldman, "Interpretation Psychologized" (1989), *Mind and Language*** — Goldman's early statement of simulation theory as an alternative to both the argument from analogy and theory-theory, arguing that we understand others by running offline simulations of their perspective using our own cognitive resources.

**Colin McGinn, "The Problem of Other Minds" (1984), *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*** — McGinn argues that the other minds problem may be genuinely insoluble in principle given the first-person structure of mental concepts, connecting it to his broader mysterianism about consciousness.

**Jaakko Hintikka, *Knowledge and Belief* (1962)** — A formal epistemological treatment that examines the logical structure of knowledge attributions in the context of other minds, developing possible-worlds semantics for epistemic logic. Useful for understanding the formal dimensions of the problem.
