# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Skepticism about Other Minds

## Key Arguments

**The Argument from Analogy (Mill)**
John Stuart Mill's argument from analogy holds that I can infer the presence of mental states in others by analogical reasoning from my own case: I know from introspection that certain bodily behaviors in me are caused by and accompany mental states; I observe the same behaviors in others; therefore, by analogy, others also have mental states. The argument's strength depends on the reliability of the analogy, and its weakness is that it is an inference from a single case — my own — and so has a very narrow inductive base.

**The Single-Case Problem**
Critics of the argument from analogy point out that it relies on inference from a single observed correlation — the one between my own behavior and my own mental states — and then projects this correlation onto all other beings on the basis of behavioral similarity alone. Unlike standard inductions from multiple instances, this inference rests on an N=1 base. The single-case problem is one of the most powerful objections to the analogical argument and motivates the search for non-analogical solutions to the other minds problem.

**Wittgenstein's Private Language Argument as a Response**
Wittgenstein argues that the other minds problem rests on a misguided picture of mental states as private, inner items accessible only to their subject through a special "inner sense." If mental concepts get their meaning from their role in shared language practices tied to behavior, circumstances, and training, then others' mental states are not hidden entities behind behavior but intelligibly expressed in behavior itself. The "response" dissolves the problem: there is no epistemic gap between behavior and mind because mind is not a private inner theater.

**The Criterion Problem**
A.J. Ayer and others raise the criterion problem: what criterion do we use to apply psychological concepts to others? If the criterion is behavioral, we seem to be defining psychological states in terms of behavior (logical behaviorism), which many find reductive. If the criterion is an inner state, we cannot directly access others' inner states. The criterion problem presses on any account of how we justify applying mental concepts beyond our own case.

**Logical Behaviorism as a Response**
Logical behaviorism (Ryle, early Carnap) holds that to attribute a mental state to someone just is to say something about their behavioral dispositions — nothing more is being claimed. If this is right, there is no other-minds problem because mental attributions are not claims about hidden inner entities but about observable behavioral tendencies. Critics object that this analysis seems to leave out the qualitative, phenomenal character of experience (qualia), suggesting that logical behaviorism does not so much solve the other minds problem as deny that there is a phenomenon to explain.

## Core Concepts

**The Other Minds Problem**
The other minds problem is the epistemological puzzle of how any individual can have knowledge (or even justified belief) that beings other than themselves have mental states — consciousness, experience, feelings, thoughts. The problem arises because mental states, on many views, are directly accessible only from the first-person perspective: I have direct access to my own mental states but must infer the existence of others' mental states from their behavior, which logically underdetermines their inner life.

**Argument from Analogy**
The argument from analogy is the classical empiricist response to the other minds problem: one reasons from the known connection between one's own bodily behavior and one's own mental states, via the perceived similarity of another's behavior to one's own, to the conclusion that the other has similar mental states. The argument treats the inference to other minds as a case of analogical induction, though its reliability is contested because of the single-case limitation.

**Logical Behaviorism**
Logical behaviorism is the view, associated with Gilbert Ryle and early Wittgenstein interpreters, that mental state attributions are logically equivalent to attributions of behavioral dispositions: to say that someone is in pain just is to say they are disposed to behave in certain ways under certain conditions. If correct, this dissolves the other minds problem by denying that mental states are hidden inner entities; it faces the objection that it leaves out the qualitative "feel" of mental states.

**The Criterion Problem**
The criterion problem asks: by what criterion do we apply mental concepts to other beings? If we appeal to behavior, we seem to define mental states in behavioral terms (reductively). If we appeal to the inner states themselves, we cannot access them in others. Any satisfactory account of other minds must specify a criterion for mental attribution that is neither question-begging (assuming the very access it needs to justify) nor trivially reductive.

**Wittgenstein's Language Games and Other Minds**
Wittgenstein's approach to other minds denies that the problem arises once we understand how mental concepts get their meaning: they are embedded in shared practices, including characteristic behaviors, circumstances, and training, that make mental attribution intelligible without requiring direct access to a private inner world. "My attitude towards him is an attitude towards a soul" (Philosophical Investigations II, iv) captures the idea that relating to others as minded is a primitive form of life, not a theoretical inference.

**The Privacy of Experience**
The privacy of experience is the phenomenological claim that one's own experiences are directly accessible to oneself in a way they are not to any other person: I know my pain from the inside, while others can only observe its behavioral manifestations. This asymmetry generates the other minds problem on the assumption that knowledge requires direct access. Responses either challenge the privacy claim (Wittgenstein), accept it and develop inference strategies (analogical argument), or argue that privacy does not preclude knowledge (phenomenological approaches).

## Thought Experiments

**The Zombie World**
The philosophical zombie thought experiment — a world physically identical to ours but containing beings with no inner experience — dramatizes the other minds problem by showing that no amount of behavioral evidence can logically entail the presence of consciousness. If zombies are conceivable, then even a perfect behavioral twin of a conscious being might lack experience entirely. While Chalmers deploys this primarily against physicalism, it also sharpens the other minds problem by making vivid the logical gap between behavior and inner life.

**The Inverted Spectrum**
Imagine that your internal color experience when you see red is qualitatively identical to what I experience when I see green, and vice versa, but that we have learned to apply color words to the same objects so our behavior is indistinguishable. This thought experiment illustrates that behavioral evidence is insufficient to determine the qualitative character of another's inner experience, suggesting that the other minds problem has depth beyond mere mental-state attribution and extends to the qualitative character of those states.

**Robinson Crusoe and the Discovery of Friday**
Consider Robinson Crusoe discovering a human footprint. He has, in his history, observed only his own footprints made by himself. The discovery of Friday's footprint prompts an inference to another mind on the basis of behavioral evidence (the footprint, then later gestures and vocalizations). This Millian case illustrates the analogical inference in a simplified setting, making vivid both its appeal and its single-case limitation: Crusoe's inference is plausible but rests on the assumption that human behavior is a reliable sign of human mentality.
