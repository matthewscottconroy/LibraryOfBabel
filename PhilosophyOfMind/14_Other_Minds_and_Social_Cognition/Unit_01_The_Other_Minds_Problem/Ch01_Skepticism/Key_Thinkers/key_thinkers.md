# Key Thinkers: Skepticism about Other Minds

---

**John Stuart Mill (1806–1873)**
British utilitarian philosopher and logician. Mill's argument from analogy in his examination of William Hamilton's philosophy represents the canonical attempt to justify belief in other minds within an empiricist framework. He argued that because my own mental states correlate with bodily states and behavior, and because other human bodies show similar states and behavior, I may infer by analogy the existence of similar mental states in others. This argument has been criticized for relying on a sample of one, but it remains the standard starting point for epistemological discussions of other minds.
*Key works:* *An Examination of Sir William Hamilton's Philosophy* (1865), *A System of Logic* (1843), *Utilitarianism* (1863)

---

**Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951)**
Austrian-British philosopher, one of the most influential figures in twentieth-century philosophy. Wittgenstein's private language argument challenges the view that mental terms refer to inner, intrinsically private objects accessible only to their possessor. He argues that the meaning of mental terms must be grounded in public criteria and practices, rather than private ostensive definition. This dissolves the other minds problem (on Wittgenstein's view) by showing that it arises from a confused picture of how mental language works: there is no hidden private realm to be inaccessible from outside.
*Key works:* *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), *On Certainty* (1969), *The Blue and Brown Books* (1958)

---

**Gilbert Ryle (1900–1976)**
British analytic philosopher at Oxford, editor of *Mind*. Ryle's *The Concept of Mind* (1949) attacks what he called the "Official Doctrine" or "the myth of the ghost in the machine"—the Cartesian view of the mind as a private inner theater. By arguing that mental state attributions are reports of behavioral dispositions rather than descriptions of hidden inner events, Ryle's logical behaviorism dissolves the other minds problem: if having a belief just is a disposition to behave in certain ways, then others' behavior is direct evidence for their mental states.
*Key works:* *The Concept of Mind* (1949), "Knowing How and Knowing That" (1945)

---

**P.F. Strawson (1919–2006)**
British analytic philosopher at Oxford. Strawson's *Individuals* (1959) argues that the concept of a person is ontologically basic—a concept of an entity to which both physical and psychological predicates apply as equally fundamental. On this view, skepticism about other minds rests on a prior distortion: the assumption that persons are fundamentally bodies to which mental attributes are only contingently or inferentially connected. Strawson's approach suggests that the problem of other minds is dissolved by correcting this conceptual error rather than solved by argument from analogy.
*Key works:* *Individuals* (1959), *The Bounds of Sense* (1966), *Skepticism and Naturalism* (1985)

---

**Alvin Goldman (1938–)**
American philosopher at Rutgers University, working in epistemology and philosophy of mind. Goldman developed simulation theory as a response to both the argument from analogy and theory-theory: rather than inferring others' mental states from a tacit theory or reasoning by analogy, we understand others by simulating their perspective—running their situation through our own cognitive and emotional systems "offline." His work has connected the epistemology of other minds to cognitive science, particularly to research on the neural bases of social cognition.
*Key works:* "Simulation and Interpersonal Utility" (1992), *Simulating Minds* (2006)
