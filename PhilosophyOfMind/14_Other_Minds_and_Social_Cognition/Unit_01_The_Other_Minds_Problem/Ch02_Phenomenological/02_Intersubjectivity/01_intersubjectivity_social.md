# Intersubjectivity and the Social Constitution of Mind

Intersubjectivity — the shared, mutually acknowledged character of social experience — is not merely a solution to the problem of other minds. It is, according to many philosophers, a constitutive feature of mind itself. On this view, being a subject is not a private, self-enclosed condition that then reaches out to other subjects; it is from the beginning a condition that involves and is shaped by other subjects.

**Hegel's Recognition**

Hegel's account of self-consciousness in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) is the classic statement of the social constitution of the self. In the famous master-slave dialectic, Hegel argues that self-consciousness cannot achieve genuine self-understanding in isolation. To recognize oneself as a self-conscious subject — as a subject who is free, autonomous, and deserving of recognition — requires recognition from another subject. Self-consciousness is constitutively dependent on mutual recognition between subjects.

The master-slave relationship, for Hegel, is a pathological case: the master achieves recognition from the slave, but this recognition is unsatisfying because the slave is not recognized as a free subject whose recognition would be worth having. Genuine recognition requires a relationship of mutual acknowledgment between free and equal subjects.

This Hegelian insight — that subjectivity requires recognition — has been enormously influential in social philosophy (Marx, Sartre, Honneth, Butler) and in phenomenology (Merleau-Ponty's intercorporeality, Zahavi's shared experiential spaces).

**Developmental Intersubjectivity**

Empirical developmental psychology supports the philosophical case for primordial intersubjectivity. Colwyn Trevarthen's work on "primary intersubjectivity" showed that newborn infants are responsive to and coordinated with other human faces and voices from birth — they engage in proto-conversations, imitating facial expressions and coordinating with the rhythms of adult speech.

This early responsiveness to others is not learned and is not a product of inference; it appears to be a basic feature of the human perceptual and motor system. The infant is constitutively oriented toward other minds from the beginning — not as a mind that then encounters others, but as a being whose orientation toward the social world is primary.

Trevarthen distinguishes primary intersubjectivity (direct, dyadic, affective engagement with others) from secondary intersubjectivity (triadic engagement: infant, caregiver, and shared object). The development of secondary intersubjectivity — joint attention, pointing, following gaze — around 9-12 months marks a crucial development in social cognition and is associated with the emergence of communicative language.

**The Social Constitution of Thought**

Vygotsky's account (mentioned in the context of inner speech) extends the intersubjectivity thesis to cognition itself: higher cognitive functions are first enacted in social interaction before being internalized as individual capacities. The child's ability to regulate her own behavior through inner speech derives from the social regulation of behavior through interpersonal speech.

This Vygotskian thesis implies that the mind is not first individual and then social; higher mental functions are essentially social in their origins, and the "individual mind" is a product of the internalization of social practices. Individual thought is, in an important sense, internalized dialogue.

This connects to the dialogical self-theory (Hermans) and to Brandom's social inferentialism (discussed in the context of inferential role semantics): the normative dimension of thought — the fact that thought is answerable to standards of correctness — is constituted by social practices of giving and asking for reasons.

**The Limits of Intersubjectivity**

The intersubjective constitution of mind has limits. Even if higher cognitive functions are socially constituted, there remains a first-person dimension of experience — the "mineness" of experience — that is not socially constituted. Zahavi argues that while the self is partly constituted through recognition and social interaction, the minimal phenomenal self (the basic first-person character of experience) is prior to and not constituted by intersubjectivity.

The debate is between two complementary truths: the self is constitutively social (one cannot become a fully developed person without other persons) and the self has an irreducibly first-person dimension (there is something it is like to be me that is not constituted by others). Both may be true, and the task is to understand how these two dimensions of selfhood relate to each other.
