# Folk Psychology and Theory of Mind

"Theory of mind" (ToM) is the capacity to attribute mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions, emotions — to other people, and to use these attributions to explain and predict their behavior. It is called "theory of mind" because having this capacity appears to involve having a theory about how minds work — a framework for understanding mental causation, belief formation, and desire satisfaction.

**Folk Psychology as a Cognitive Achievement**

Folk psychology — the everyday practice of explaining behavior in mental terms — is an extraordinarily powerful and universally human capacity. We use it automatically and successfully: tracking what others believe and want, predicting what they will do, explaining why they did what they did, and coordinating our behavior with theirs. This practice is so deeply embedded in social life that its complexity and sophistication is easy to overlook.

The "theory of mind" label, coined by Premack and Woodruff (1978), reflects the hypothesis that human social cognition is structured by an implicit theoretical framework analogous to scientific theories. The framework posits mental states (beliefs, desires), links them to observable inputs (perception, testimony) and outputs (behavior), and generates predictions about what people will do.

The evidence that this framework is theoretical in a psychologically real sense comes in part from developmental research. Children develop theory of mind capacities in a characteristic developmental sequence that has been extensively studied through the "false belief task."

**The False Belief Task**

The classic false belief task (Wimmer and Perner, 1983) is designed to detect whether a child can attribute to another person a belief that the child knows to be false. In the standard version, a character (Maxi) places chocolate in one location and then leaves; while he is gone, the chocolate is moved to a different location. The question: where will Maxi look for the chocolate when he returns?

Children under about 3.5-4 years of age typically say Maxi will look where the chocolate actually is — they fail to represent Maxi's false belief. Children above this age correctly predict that Maxi will look where he put the chocolate (where he believes it to be), demonstrating the capacity to attribute false beliefs.

This developmental milestone marks the emergence of a key theory of mind capacity: understanding that others can hold beliefs that differ from one's own and from reality. Before this milestone, children seem to treat belief-talk as transparent to reality — what people "believe" is simply what is the case.

**Autism and Theory of Mind**

Baron-Cohen, Leslie, and Frith's hypothesis (1985) that children with autism have a specific deficit in theory of mind had an enormous impact on autism research and on the study of social cognition more broadly. The hypothesis proposed that the social and communicative difficulties characteristic of autism stem from an impaired capacity to attribute mental states — to "mind-read."

The evidence: children with autism (matched for verbal mental age) failed the false belief task at rates far higher than typically developing children or children with intellectual disabilities from other causes. This suggested a specific impairment in the cognitive system underlying theory of mind, rather than a general intellectual impairment.

The theory of mind hypothesis has been refined and challenged in subsequent decades: many adults with autism pass advanced theory of mind tests while still showing social difficulties in real-world contexts. Current understanding recognizes that autism involves a complex pattern of social cognitive differences that is not captured by simple absence of ToM capacity.

**Theory Theory vs. Simulation Theory**

The debate between "theory theory" and "simulation theory" of mindreading is one of the central debates in the cognitive science of social cognition.

*Theory theory* holds that mindreading involves a tacit theory of mind — a set of representations about mental states and their causal relationships. When I predict what you will do, I apply this theory to a description of your beliefs and desires to generate a prediction.

*Simulation theory* (Gordon, Goldman) holds that mindreading does not require a theory. Instead, I use my own cognitive and motivational system as a model of yours, "pretending" to be you and using my own reactions to generate predictions about yours. I understand your anger by simulating what would make me angry in your situation, not by applying a theory of anger.

These two approaches connect to the empirical debate about mirror neurons and embodied simulation theory discussed in the context of empathy. The debate between them is active, and the current consensus is that both processes — both "cold," theory-like computations and "hot," simulation-like resonance — contribute to mindreading in different contexts.
