# Further Reading: Mindreading and Theory of Mind

## Primary Sources

**Heinz Wimmer and Josef Perner, "Beliefs about Beliefs: Representation and Constraining Function of Wrong Beliefs in Young Children's Understanding of Deception" (1983), *Cognition*** — The classic false-belief task paper, showing that children below age four typically fail to attribute false beliefs to others—a finding taken as evidence that young children lack a theory of mind. The Maxi task became the paradigm for developmental research on theory of mind over the following decades.

**Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie, and Uta Frith, "Does the Autistic Child Have a 'Theory of Mind'?" (1985), *Cognition*** — This landmark study used the false-belief task with autistic children, showing that they disproportionately failed compared to typically developing children and children with Down syndrome. The paper supported the "mindblindness" hypothesis—that autism involves a specific deficit in mentalizing—and was enormously influential in both autism research and theory of mind debates.

**Alvin Goldman, *Simulating Minds: The Philosophy, Psychology, and Neuroscience of Mindreading* (2006)** — Goldman's comprehensive defense of simulation theory as an account of mindreading, arguing that we understand others by simulating their mental states using our own cognitive systems offline. He engages with both philosophical objections and neuroscientific evidence from mirror neurons.

**Shaun Gallagher, "The Practice of Mind: Theory, Simulation, or Primary Interaction?" (2001), *Journal of Consciousness Studies*** — Gallagher's defense of "interaction theory" as an alternative to both theory-theory and simulation theory, arguing that direct embodied interaction provides the primary mode of social understanding and that both standard theories misplace the locus of social cognition.

**Ian Apperly, *Mindreaders: The Cognitive Basis of "Theory of Mind"* (2011)** — A cognitive scientist's treatment that distinguishes two-system theory of mind: implicit, fast mindreading (present from infancy) and explicit, reflective mindreading (dependent on conceptual development). This distinction challenges the standard narrative from the false-belief task data and reconciles apparently contradictory developmental findings.

## Secondary Literature

**Chris Frith, *Making Up the Mind: How the Brain Creates Our Mental World* (2007)** — Frith's account of how the brain builds models of other minds and how this capacity is disrupted in schizophrenia. Provides accessible neuroscientific context for theory of mind research.

**Rebecca Saxe and Simon Baron-Cohen (eds.), *Theory of Mind: A Special Issue of Social Neuroscience* (2007)** — Collects key neuroscientific papers on the brain basis of theory of mind, particularly the role of the temporoparietal junction and medial prefrontal cortex.

**Josef Perner, *Understanding the Representational Mind* (1991)** — A developmental account of how children acquire the concept of mental representation, arguing that the false-belief task marks the acquisition of a genuine representational theory of mind at around age four.

**Peter Carruthers and Peter Smith (eds.), *Theories of Theories of Mind* (1996)** — An important anthology collecting key papers in the theory-theory vs. simulation theory debate, with contributions from Goldman, Gordon, Leslie, Perner, and others.

**Daniel Hutto and Erik Myin, *Radicalizing Enactivism: Basic Minds Without Content* (2013)** — A radical challenge to both standard accounts of social cognition, arguing that basic social understanding involves neither theories nor simulations but skilled, embodied engagement with others.
