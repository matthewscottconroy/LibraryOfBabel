# Key Thinkers: Mindreading and Theory of Mind

---

**Simon Baron-Cohen (1958–)**
British clinical psychologist and neuroscientist at Cambridge University. Baron-Cohen's "mindblindness" hypothesis holds that autism spectrum disorder involves a specific deficit in the theory of mind module—the cognitive system that attributes mental states to others. His 1985 paper with Leslie and Frith using the false-belief task with autistic children was among the most influential in cognitive science, generating decades of research on the neural and developmental basis of mentalizing. His later work on the "extreme male brain" hypothesis and on the systemizing/empathizing dimension has been influential if controversial.
*Key works:* "Does the Autistic Child Have a Theory of Mind?" (1985), *Mindblindness* (1995), *The Essential Difference* (2003)

---

**Alan Leslie (1951–)**
British cognitive scientist at Rutgers University. Leslie proposed that the capacity for metarepresentation—representing representations as representations—is an innate modular system that underpins both pretend play and theory of mind. His account of how children "decouple" representations from their normal truth-evaluable function to engage in pretend play provides a unified explanation of the emergence of both capacities at around 18 months. His theory of "ToMM" (Theory of Mind Mechanism) has been influential in developmental and clinical research.
*Key works:* "Pretense and Representation" (1987), "ToMM, ToBy, and Agency: Core Architecture and Domain Specificity" (1994)

---

**Josef Perner (1948–)**
Austrian developmental psychologist at the University of Salzburg. Perner, with Wimmer, designed the original false-belief task that became the paradigm for theory of mind research. His developmental account holds that children around age four acquire a genuine representational understanding of mind—an understanding that mental states represent the world in ways that can be false or mismatched with reality. This is a genuine conceptual achievement rather than the acquisition of a modular mechanism, on his view, and his emphasis on conceptual development distinguishes his position from Leslie's nativist approach.
*Key works:* "Beliefs about Beliefs" (1983, with Wimmer), *Understanding the Representational Mind* (1991)

---

**Alvin Goldman (1938–)**
American philosopher at Rutgers University. Goldman is the principal philosophical defender of simulation theory as an account of mindreading. On his account, we understand others by running their situation through our own cognitive and emotional systems "offline"—simulating their perspective rather than applying a theoretical framework. His later work incorporated mirror neuron evidence to support simulation theory, arguing that the automatic resonance mirror neurons provide constitutes a form of "low-level simulation." His comprehensive treatment in *Simulating Minds* (2006) engages with both philosophical objections and neuroscientific evidence.
*Key works:* "Interpretation Psychologized" (1989), "Simulation and Interpersonal Utility" (1992), *Simulating Minds* (2006)

---

**Shaun Gallagher (1948–)**
American philosopher at the University of Memphis. Gallagher's "interaction theory" of social cognition challenges both theory-theory and simulation theory by arguing that direct, embodied interaction—perception of others' actions, emotional expressions, and bodily comportment—provides the primary mode of social understanding. He draws on Merleau-Ponty's intercorporeality and on developmental research showing that even neonates engage in immediate, non-inferential responses to other bodies. Interaction theory has been influential in connecting phenomenology to empirical social neuroscience.
*Key works:* "The Practice of Mind" (2001), *How the Body Shapes the Mind* (2005), "Two Problems of Intersubjectivity" (2009)
