# Key Thinkers: Mirror Neurons

---

**Giacomo Rizzolatti (1937–)**
Italian neuroscientist at the University of Parma, discoverer of mirror neurons. Rizzolatti's laboratory made the serendipitous discovery in the early 1990s that individual neurons in macaque premotor cortex fire both during action execution and during observation of the same action performed by another. He and his colleagues identified this "mirror" property as a neural substrate for understanding actions by "simulating" them internally. Rizzolatti has subsequently argued for the importance of mirror systems in action understanding, imitation, language evolution, and social cognition, though he has been more cautious than some interpreters about the scope of mirror neuron functions.
*Key works:* "Premotor Cortex and the Recognition of Motor Actions" (1996), *Mirrors in the Brain* (2008, with Sinigaglia)

---

**Vittorio Gallese (1959–)**
Italian neuroscientist and philosopher at the University of Parma, one of the original mirror neuron researchers. Gallese has been the most philosophically ambitious interpreter of mirror neuron research, developing the "shared manifold" hypothesis: mirror systems constitute a basic level of intersubjectivity in which the motor representations underlying others' actions are directly shared. He collaborated with Goldman to connect mirror neurons to simulation theory and has extended the framework to empathy, aesthetic experience, and language comprehension. His philosophical claims have been influential but also criticized for overextension beyond the neuroscientific evidence.
*Key works:* "Mirror Neurons and the Simulation Theory of Mind-Reading" (1998, with Goldman), "The Roots of Empathy" (2003), "Before and Below 'Theory of Mind'" (2007)

---

**Alvin Goldman (1938–)**
American philosopher at Rutgers University. Goldman embraced mirror neuron research as empirical support for his philosophical simulation theory, arguing that the automatic motor resonance of mirror neurons implements the "low-level simulation" that underlies action understanding. His collaboration with Gallese was influential in connecting neuroscience and philosophy of mind on social cognition. His *Simulating Minds* (2006) includes detailed engagement with mirror neuron evidence while maintaining philosophical rigor about what the neuroscience actually establishes.
*Key works:* *Simulating Minds* (2006), "Mirror Systems and Prior Intentions" (2008)

---

**Gregory Hickok (1963–)**
American cognitive neuroscientist at UC Irvine. Hickok is the most prominent scientific critic of mirror neuron theories of action understanding, arguing in detail that the neuroscientific evidence is inconsistent with the strong claim that mirror neurons underlie action understanding. His "eight problems" paper identifies specific empirical challenges: mirror neurons exist in motor areas without comprehension capacity, patients with motor system damage can understand actions normally, and the predictions of the mirror neuron hypothesis have not been confirmed. His critical work has been important in separating genuine neuroscientific findings from philosophical speculation.
*Key works:* "Eight Problems for the Mirror Neuron Theory" (2009), *The Myth of Mirror Neurons* (2014)

---

**Christian Keysers (1975–)**
Dutch-German neuroscientist at the Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience. Keysers has extended mirror system research to include somatosensory and emotional domains, studying how the brain responds to others' pain, touch, and disgust with activations resembling those of first-person experience. His work on "vicarious neural responses" provides broader evidence for the resonance systems underlying empathy, while his measured interpretation of this evidence—arguing for a role in social understanding without overclaiming—represents a more philosophically careful position than early mirror neuron enthusiasm.
*Key works:* *The Empathic Brain* (2011), "A Touching Sight: SII/PV Activation During the Observation and Experience of Touch" (2004)
