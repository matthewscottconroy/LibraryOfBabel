# We-Intentions and Collective Intentionality

The concept of we-intentions — discussed in the context of collective intention in Topic 08 — is relevant here as a form of social cognition and as a philosophical account of how individual minds participate in collective action. Collective intentionality raises distinctive questions about the relationship between individual minds and social phenomena.

**The Irreducibility of We-Intentionality**

Searle's argument that we-intentions cannot be reduced to I-intentions plus beliefs about others connects to the broader argument that social facts are not reducible to individual psychological facts. When a group acts together — when a symphony orchestra plays together, when a team wins together — the intentionality involved is irreducibly we-intentional: each member intends "we are playing" rather than "I am playing and I believe others will too."

This we-intentional form cannot be constructed from I-intentionality alone, because the content of the we-intention is essentially collective: to intend "we are doing A" is to represent the action as a shared action, not merely as my action that happens to be coordinated with yours. No conjunction of I-intentions captures this shared character.

**Joint Attention**

Joint attention — the shared focus of two or more people on a common object or event — is one of the most basic forms of collective intentionality. When a caregiver and infant both attend to the same toy, they are engaged in a simple form of we-intentionality: both are aware of the toy, both are aware of each other's awareness of the toy, and this mutual awareness is mutually acknowledged.

Joint attention emerges developmentally around 9-12 months, marked by the ability to follow gaze, to point, and to share reference. Its development is associated with the emergence of secondary intersubjectivity and is a prerequisite for language acquisition and for many forms of cooperative activity.

Joint attention is philosophically significant because it involves a form of shared representation: both parties have a common object of attention, and each is aware of the other's attention as directed at the same object. This shared representation is a primitive form of the mutually acknowledged intentionality that characterizes richer forms of collective action.

**Common Knowledge**

The concept of common knowledge — developed in game theory and the philosophy of language — captures a dimension of collective intentionality: to act together on a plan, each participant must know the plan, know that others know it, know that others know that they know it, and so on (potentially ad infinitum).

This infinite regress in the definition of common knowledge is philosophically puzzling: no finite computation can verify an infinite chain of mutual knowledge. David Lewis proposed that common knowledge is established not by explicit mutual verification but by salient common salience: some things are mutually known because they are salient and it is mutually known that they are salient. Lewis's concept of convention draws on this: conventions are regularities maintained by mutual expectation, grounded in the common knowledge that everyone is following the convention.

**The Social Ontology of Collective Intentionality**

Collective intentionality connects to broader questions about social ontology: what kinds of things are social institutions, roles, and practices, and how do they exist in relation to individual mental states?

Searle's account of social reality holds that institutions (money, marriage, government) are constituted by collective intentional acceptance of status functions: through collective agreement that this piece of paper counts as money in this context, and through the mutual acceptance of this status, the institution of money is created and maintained.

Searle calls these "institutional facts" — facts that depend on collective acceptance for their existence, in contrast with "brute facts" (physical facts independent of any acceptance). Institutional facts require language for their constitution: you need a representation (a sentence or its equivalent) of the form "X counts as Y in context C" to create an institutional fact.

This account of social ontology through collective intentionality gives individual minds a constitutively social dimension: to be a person in human society is to participate in networks of collective intentionality that constitute the social world one inhabits. The capacity for we-intentionality is not an optional feature of human cognition but is bound up with what it is to be a fully social, culturally embedded human being.
