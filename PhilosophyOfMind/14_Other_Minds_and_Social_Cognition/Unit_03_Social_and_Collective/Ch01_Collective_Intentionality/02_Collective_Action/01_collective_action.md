# Collective Action and Group Agency

Collective action — action performed by groups as groups, not merely as aggregates of individuals acting in parallel — raises questions about how individual minds combine to produce group-level agency. When a corporation makes a decision, when a legislature passes a law, when a team wins a game, is there a genuine group agent whose actions are not reducible to the actions of its members?

**Aggregative Views**

Aggregative views hold that collective action is simply the sum of individual actions, coordinated in appropriate ways. When a group acts together, there is no additional "group action" over and above the coordinated individual actions. The group's causal power is just the sum of its members' causal powers.

This view has the advantage of ontological simplicity — it does not require positing group minds or group intentionality over and above individual minds. Corporate decisions, legislative acts, and team victories are all, on this view, analyzable in terms of the decisions, acts, and performances of individuals.

The aggregative view faces challenges from cases where group outcomes are not simply aggregations of individual contributions — where emergent properties of the group make a difference to what happens. A deliberating group may reach a conclusion that no individual member would have reached alone; a market may produce outcomes that no individual market participant intended.

**Non-Aggregative Views**

Philip Pettit and Christian List have developed a "group agency" view that grants groups a form of genuine agency not reducible to their members' agency. Their argument focuses on the phenomenon of "judgment aggregation" — the aggregation of individual judgments into group judgments.

The key insight: if a group aggregates individual members' judgments on propositions independently, the resulting "group judgment" may be logically inconsistent, even if each member's individual judgments are consistent. This "doctrinal paradox" shows that the group's position cannot simply be the sum of individual positions; maintaining consistency at the group level requires a form of group-level reasoning that transcends individual reasoning.

On Pettit and List's view, a genuine group agent is one that has a capacity for rational agency — the ability to form consistent beliefs and purposes and to act on them. Human institutions (corporations, courts, legislatures) can be organized to have this capacity, in which case they are genuine agents, with moral and practical properties that are not simply those of their members.

**Responsibility and Attribution**

The question of group agency connects to questions of responsibility and attribution. When a corporation harms the environment, who is responsible? When a government commits a wrong, who can be held accountable?

The aggregative view tends to attribute responsibility to the individual human beings who made the relevant decisions. The group agency view allows responsibility to be attributed at the group level: the corporation is responsible, even if no individual can be singled out as fully responsible for the outcome.

This has practical implications for law, ethics, and policy. If groups can be genuine agents, they can be the proper targets of moral and legal responsibility, and institutions can be designed to be responsible in ways that transcend the individual accountability of their members.

**Group Cognition vs. Group Mind**

An important distinction is between group cognition (the cognitive processes that emerge from group interaction) and group mind (a unified subjective perspective at the group level). Distributed cognition (Hutchins) and collective intelligence research show that groups can achieve cognitive outcomes that exceed individual capabilities — through division of cognitive labor, external artifacts, and interactive processes.

But this group cognition does not require a group mind in the sense of a unified subjective perspective. There is no "group consciousness" accompanying the cognitive achievements of well-organized teams, and the group experience does not have a phenomenal character over and above the experiences of the individual members.

This suggests a useful distinction: groups can have functional agency (organized to achieve collective goals through systematic processes) and even moral status (corporate responsibility) without having phenomenal consciousness or subjective experience at the group level. Group minds in the phenomenal sense remain philosophically problematic; group agents in the functional sense are a real and important social phenomenon.
