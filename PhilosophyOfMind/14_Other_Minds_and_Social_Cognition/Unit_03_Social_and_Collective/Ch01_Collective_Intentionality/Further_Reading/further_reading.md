# Further Reading: Collective Intentionality

## Primary Sources

**John Searle, "Collective Intentions and Actions" (1990), in *Intentions in Communication*** — Searle's foundational paper argues that collective intentionality (we-intentions) is a primitive, irreducible phenomenon—not reducible to combinations of individual I-intentions plus mutual beliefs. He maintains that collective intentionality is biologically primitive and can exist in the heads of individual members of a group, without requiring any literal sharing between minds.

**Michael Bratman, "Shared Cooperative Activity" (1992), *Philosophical Review*** — Bratman develops a reductionist account of shared intention and shared cooperative activity, arguing that these can be analyzed in terms of individual intentions plus meshing sub-plans and mutual responsiveness. His approach preserves individual agency while capturing the distinctive normativity of cooperative action, and has been highly influential in practical philosophy.

**Margaret Gilbert, *On Social Facts* (1989)** — Gilbert argues that social groups have a distinctive form of existence grounded in "plural subjects"—entities constituted by joint commitment rather than by individual attitudes. Her account of social facts as requiring the kind of commitment that creates obligations between members of the group represents a strong anti-reductionist position.

**Raimo Tuomela, *The Philosophy of Sociality: The Shared Point of View* (2007)** — Tuomela's comprehensive treatment distinguishes we-mode intentions (intentions taken from the perspective of a group as a group) from I-mode intentions (intentions of individuals as members of groups), arguing that genuine collective action requires we-mode attitudes that are not reducible to I-mode ones.

**David Velleman, "How to Share an Intention" (1997), *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*** — Velleman argues against both Bratman and Searle, developing an account of shared intention that does not require either irreducible we-intentions or individual intentions to participate in collective action. His account focuses on the normative structure of planning rather than the psychological structure of intention.

## Secondary Literature

**Kirk Ludwig, *From Individual to Plural Agency: Collective Action* (2016)** — A systematic treatment of collective intentionality arguing for a "singularist" position: all apparently plural agency can be fully analyzed in terms of individual agents with appropriately related individual intentions. This strongly reductionist view offers an alternative to both Searle's and Gilbert's anti-reductionism.

**Günter Schmid (ed.), *Collective Intentionality* (2009)** — A collection of essays by leading researchers covering all major positions in the debate, from Bratman, Gilbert, Tuomela, and Searle to more recent empirical and evolutionary approaches.

**Matthew Tomasello, *A Natural History of Human Morality* (2016)** — Develops an evolutionary and developmental account of how shared intentionality—uniquely developed in humans—underlies cooperative activity, social norms, and moral reasoning. Bridges philosophical and comparative cognitive science.

**Hans Bernhard Schmid, *Plural Action: Essays in Philosophy and Social Science* (2009)** — A philosophically sophisticated treatment of collective agency that engages with both the analytic tradition (Bratman, Searle, Gilbert) and the phenomenological tradition (Husserl, Heidegger), arguing that the full structure of collective intentionality requires both.

**Frederick Schmitt (ed.), *Socializing Epistemology: The Social Dimensions of Knowledge* (1994)** — Extends collective intentionality themes to epistemology, covering collective belief, testimony, and social knowledge. Relevant for understanding how the philosophy of collective intentionality connects to broader social epistemology.
