# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Collective Intentionality

## Key Arguments

**Searle's Irreducibility Argument**
John Searle argues that collective intentionality — "we intend," "we believe," "we perceive" — is a primitive, irreducible form of intentionality that cannot be analyzed in terms of individual intentions with mutual beliefs about one another's intentions. The key move is that collective intentionality is a biological phenomenon instantiated in individual brains, but its content (the "we" component) cannot be decomposed into a complex of "I intend...and I believe you intend...and I believe you believe I intend..." without infinite regress or mismatch with actual cases. Genuine collective action requires genuine collective intention.

**Bratman's Shared Cooperative Activity**
Michael Bratman argues for a reductive analysis of shared cooperative activity (SCA) in terms of individual intentions with the right structure: we act together (paint the house, play a duet) when each of us has an intention to do our part of the joint activity that is (1) appropriately meshed with the others' sub-plans, (2) publicly known, and (3) causally interconnected in the right way. Bratman's account is explicitly reductive and non-mysterious: collective action emerges from individual intentions with the right relational properties, without requiring a sui generis "we intention."

**Gilbert's Plural Subject Theory**
Margaret Gilbert argues that genuine joint commitments — "we promised," "we believe as a group," "we intend together" — involve plural subjects: two or more persons who are jointly committed to doing something as a body. Joint commitments are not reducible to the aggregate of individual commitments or to mutual beliefs about one another's commitments; they create genuine obligations and entitlements between the parties that individual intentions do not. The we is constituted as a subject by the joint commitment itself.

**Tuomela's We-Mode vs. I-Mode**
Raimo Tuomela distinguishes between the I-mode, in which individuals act as private agents pursuing individual goals, and the we-mode, in which individuals act as members of a group, taking the group's goals, beliefs, and norms as their own operational reasons. We-mode action requires a distinctive form of intentionality — the agent acts as a group member, where the group's perspective governs — that is irreducible to I-mode action under mutual belief. The we-mode concept is used to explain the distinctive phenomenology and practical significance of group membership.

## Core Concepts

**Collective Intentionality**
Collective intentionality refers to the intentional states — intentions, beliefs, desires, perceptions — that are shared by or belong to groups of agents: "we intend to build a city," "we believe the economy is in trouble," "we see the ball." The concept is contested at every level: whether it is real, whether it is irreducible to individual intentionality, and whether it requires a sui generis form of subjecthood or can be constructed from individual mental states with the right relational properties.

**We-Intention**
A we-intention, in Searle's and Tuomela's usage, is a first-person plural intentional state — "I intend that we do X" — that is constitutively distinct from a first-person singular intention with mentalistic addenda ("I intend to do my part, and I believe you intend to do your part..."). A genuine we-intention includes the group as the subject of the intended action in a way that cannot be fully captured by any finite conjunction of individual intentions with mutual beliefs. Searle holds that we-intentions are biologically instantiated in individual brains but have irreducibly collective content.

**Joint Commitment (Gilbert)**
Gilbert's joint commitment is a normative social fact that arises when two or more persons collectively express their readiness to be jointly committed to a goal, belief, or norm. Unlike a sum of individual commitments, a joint commitment generates obligations and entitlements between the parties: if we are jointly committed to walking together, you have a right to call me to account if I walk ahead without consulting you. Joint commitments are central to Gilbert's analysis of social groups, promises, conventions, and collective belief.

**Shared Cooperative Activity (Bratman)**
Bratman's shared cooperative activity is a reductive analysis of joint action in terms of individual intentions with the right relational properties: each participant intends to do their part in the joint action, each intends that the joint action proceed via the others' intentions (an interdependence condition), and the participants' sub-plans are appropriately meshed. The analysis aims to show that the ontology of joint action requires only individual mental states in the right relational configuration, with no irreducible collective entity.

**The We-Mode (Tuomela)**
Tuomela's we-mode is the practical and intentional stance of an agent who acts as a member of a group, taking the group's goals, beliefs, and norms as operative reasons for action. Acting in the we-mode is not simply acting for the benefit of the group (which could be I-mode altruism) but acting from a perspective that is constitutively that of the group — where the relevant first person is not "I" but "we." The we-mode is argued to underlie the distinctive obligations, satisfactions, and phenomenology of collective action.

**The Reducibility Debate**
The central dispute in the theory of collective intentionality concerns whether collective intentional states are reducible to individual intentional states — states of individual minds — or whether they are irreducible sui generis phenomena. Searle, Gilbert, and Tuomela argue (for different reasons) for irreducibility; Bratman, Miller, and others defend reduction. The debate has implications for social ontology, the metaphysics of groups, the status of group agents, and the possibility of corporate moral responsibility.

## Thought Experiments

**The Orchestra**
An orchestra performs a symphony together. The musicians play their individual parts, but what they are doing is not merely each playing their own melody simultaneously: they are playing the symphony together. Searle uses this to illustrate that the performance constitutes a genuine collective action that cannot be described as a mere sum of individual actions without residue. The thought experiment motivates the irreducibility of "we play the symphony" to the conjunction of individual "I play my part" intentions.

**Gilbert's Walk**
Gilbert's central example: two people are walking together. If one suddenly stops or turns, the other can reasonably say "Hey, where are you going?" — implying an entitlement to an explanation and an obligation on the other to give one. But this obligation cannot arise if the two walkers merely happen to be walking in the same direction with individual intentions to do so. The joint commitment that constitutes walking together creates genuine normative relations between the parties, not just coordination. The thought experiment is used to argue for the reality and irreducibility of joint commitments.

**The Difference Between a Mob and an Army**
Consider the difference between a crowd that happens to disperse when a fire starts (aggregated individual fleeing) and a military unit that advances under orders (collective action). Even if the individual actions are physically similar, what makes the second a collective action and the first not? The contrast is used to explore what is added when individual actions are organized by collective intentions, joint commitments, or we-mode reasoning — and whether that addition is irreducibly collective or constructible from individual ingredients.
