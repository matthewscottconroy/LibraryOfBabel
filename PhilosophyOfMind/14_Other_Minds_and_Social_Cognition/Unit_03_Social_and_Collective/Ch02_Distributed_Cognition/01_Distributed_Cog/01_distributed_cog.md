# Distributed Cognition

Distributed cognition, developed by Edwin Hutchins, is the view that cognitive systems can extend beyond individual minds to include groups of people, external artifacts, and the environment in which they are embedded. Cognition is not just something that happens inside skulls; it is a process that can be spread across people, tools, and physical arrangements.

**Hutchins's Research**

Hutchins's ethnographic study of navigation on a US Navy ship, reported in *Cognition in the Wild* (1995), is the foundational empirical case for distributed cognition. Hutchins showed that the cognitive process of navigation — determining the ship's position and plotting a course — was not accomplished by any single navigator but by a system of people, instruments, charts, and procedures.

Different people contributed different pieces of information and performed different operations: the helmsman steered to heading, the quartermasters took bearings, the plotter recorded positions on the chart, the navigator integrated information and made decisions. No individual could accomplish the full navigational task alone, and the cognitive system as a whole achieved something that no individual member could achieve independently.

Hutchins argued that the appropriate unit of cognitive analysis is not the individual mind but the cognitive system — the functional system that processes information to achieve the relevant outcome. This system includes people, their interactions, their tools, and their physical environment.

**Properties of Distributed Cognitive Systems**

Distributed cognitive systems have several properties that distinguish them from individual cognition.

*Representation media*: Distributed systems use multiple, diverse representation media — spoken language, written records, charts, instruments, physical arrangements of objects. Different media have different properties: charts allow spatial reasoning about position; spoken commands allow rapid real-time communication; written records provide persistence.

*Propagation of representational states*: Cognition in distributed systems involves the propagation of representational states through the system — information encoded in one medium is transformed and re-encoded in another as it passes through the cognitive process. The chart's spatial representation of position is transformed into a bearing reading, then into a coordinate pair, then into a plotted position — each transformation is a cognitive operation.

*Division of cognitive labor*: Distributed systems can divide cognitive tasks among members and tools in ways that exploit the specific strengths of each. Tasks that are difficult for individuals (maintaining continuous temporal records) can be offloaded to artifacts (clocks, logs). Tasks that require rapid judgment are kept with human members.

**Distributed Cognition and the Extended Mind**

The relationship between distributed cognition and Clark-Chalmers's extended mind thesis is important. Both views challenge the individualist assumption that cognitive processes occur entirely within individual skulls.

The extended mind thesis focuses on the individual-artifact couple: when an individual's cognitive processes extend into an artifact (like Otto's notebook), the artifact becomes part of the individual's cognitive system. Distributed cognition focuses on the group-artifact-environment system: cognitive processes are distributed across multiple agents, artifacts, and environments, and the unit of analysis is the system, not any individual.

The two views are complementary rather than competing: extended mind shows how individual cognition can extend into the environment; distributed cognition shows how group cognition operates through environment-spanning systems. Together, they support a thoroughly anti-individualist picture of cognition.

**Cultural Scaffolding**

Hutchins extends the distributed cognition framework to cultural history: cognitive systems are not just created by individuals but are inherited from cultural history. The tools, practices, and procedures that support navigation (charts, compasses, coordinate systems) were developed over centuries by many individuals and cultures, and they represent accumulated cognitive work that is now available to any practitioner.

This cultural scaffolding of cognition — the way each individual's cognition is supported by inherited cognitive tools and practices — is central to understanding human intelligence. Human cognitive capacities are not fully explained by individual brain architecture; they depend essentially on the cultural and technological heritage that individuals are born into and trained in.

This perspective challenges an individualist account of cognitive achievements: much of what we consider individual intellectual achievement is better understood as the momentary instantiation of a culturally and materially scaffolded process — one that draws on tools, practices, and knowledge that are the products of collective cognitive history.
