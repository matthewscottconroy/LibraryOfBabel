# Further Reading: Distributed Cognition

## Primary Sources

**Edwin Hutchins, *Cognition in the Wild* (1995)** — Hutchins' foundational study of navigation on a US Navy vessel argues that cognitive processes are not confined to individual minds but are distributed across people, tools, representations, and practices. His analysis of how the ship's navigation system functions as a cognitive system in which individual cognitive contributions are coordinated shows that the appropriate unit of cognitive analysis is often the sociotechnical system rather than the individual.

**Andy Clark and David Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (1998), *Analysis*** — This influential paper argues that mental states—including beliefs—can extend beyond the skull and skin of the individual. Using the case of "Otto" (who uses a notebook as an external memory system), Clark and Chalmers argue that if a cognitive process would be counted as mental if it occurred inside the head, the same process should count as mental when it occurs partly outside, provided appropriate conditions are met.

**Andy Clark, *Supersizing the Mind: Embodiment, Action, and Cognitive Extension* (2008)** — Clark's comprehensive defense and elaboration of the extended mind thesis, responding to critics and developing the cognitive coupling and parity principles. He argues that human cognition is essentially hybrid—incorporating biological and non-biological elements in a deeply integrated way.

**Merlin Donald, *Origins of the Modern Mind: Three Stages in the Evolution of Culture and Cognition* (1991)** — Donald's evolutionary account proposes that modern human cognition emerged through three stages of cognitive evolution, each introducing new forms of external representation. His concept of "exograms"—external memory traces—extends the notion of cognitive architecture to include cultural and material media.

**Mark Johnson, *The Meaning of the Body: Aesthetics of Human Understanding* (2007)** — Develops an embodied cognition approach that connects Dewey's pragmatism with cognitive science, arguing that meaning, understanding, and reasoning are grounded in bodily experience and extend into the environment through action and perception.

## Secondary Literature

**Richard Menary (ed.), *The Extended Mind* (2010)** — An excellent anthology collecting the key papers in the extended mind debate, including Clark and Chalmers' original paper, critics (Adams and Aizawa, Rupert), and defenders. The editor's introduction provides a clear map of the terrain.

**Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa, *The Bounds of Cognition* (2008)** — The most sustained philosophical critique of extended mind, arguing that cognition requires intrinsic content and is bounded by the skull and skin. Their "mark of the cognitive" argument has been central to the debate.

**Lambros Malafouris, *How Things Shape the Mind: A Theory of Material Engagement* (2013)** — An archaeological and cognitive science approach arguing that material culture and artifacts are not merely external supports for cognition but constitutive participants in cognitive processes. Extends distributed cognition to the archaeological record.

**John Sutton, "Exograms and Interdisciplinarity: History, the Extended Mind, and the Civilizing Process" (2010)** — Applies extended mind concepts to historical and cultural memory, arguing that external representations are not merely tools but transformative cognitive resources that change what kinds of thinking are possible.

**David Kirsh and Paul Maglio, "On Distinguishing Epistemic from Pragmatic Actions" (1994), *Cognitive Science*** — Distinguishes actions done to transform the world for practical purposes from "epistemic actions"—actions done to change one's cognitive situation, simplify problems, or aid memory. This distinction is foundational for understanding how the environment participates in cognition.
