# Intentionality in Husserl

Husserl's analysis of intentionality is the philosophical core of his phenomenology. His detailed account of how consciousness is directed toward objects — and of the structure of this directedness — provided the conceptual vocabulary for phenomenology's subsequent development and influenced analytic philosophy of mind through Frege's contemporaneous work on sense and reference.

**The Noetic-Noematic Structure**

After the phenomenological reduction, every act of consciousness is found to have a two-sided structure. Husserl calls the "subjective" side the noesis (the act of intending) and the "objective" side the noema (the intended object as it appears).

The noesis is the intentional act itself — the perceiving, remembering, imagining, judging — with all the subjective features that characterize how the object is intended. The noema is the "meant object" in the precise way in which it is meant — what the French call the "sens" and what contemporary philosophers call content. The noema is not the real object in the world but the object as it is presented to consciousness: the tree as perceived, or the remembered event as recalled.

The distinction between noesis and noema is Husserl's answer to a puzzle about intentionality: how can consciousness be directed toward objects that do not exist? If I imagine a golden mountain, there is no golden mountain that is the target of my imagining. Husserl's answer: the target of consciousness is always the noema — the object as intended — not the real object. Whether the real object exists is a further question (one that the natural attitude assumes but the reduction brackets).

**Sense and Reference**

Husserl's noema parallels Frege's distinction between "Sinn" (sense) and "Bedeutung" (reference). Frege distinguished between the sense of an expression (its mode of presentation, the way it picks out its referent) and its reference (the object it picks out). "The morning star" and "the evening star" have the same reference (Venus) but different senses.

Husserl's noema is like Fregean sense: it is the mode of presentation, the way in which the intended object is given to consciousness. Two acts of consciousness can have the same noema (intend the same object in the same way) or the same real object but different noemata (the same tree perceived from different angles involves different noemata but the same referent).

This parallel was developed explicitly by later phenomenologists (Gurwitsch, Sokolowski) and by analytic philosophers influenced by both traditions (Parsons, Dreyfus).

**Fulfillment and Evidence**

A central feature of Husserl's account of intentionality is the concept of fulfillment. Intentional acts are typically not fully satisfied by their noemata alone; they aim at an object that transcends what is immediately given. When I see a cup, I see its front side immediately but intend the cup as a whole — the back, the inside, the handle. The full cup is intended through "empty intentions" that anticipate but do not yet present its further features.

Fulfillment occurs when an empty intention is "filled in" by an intuition — a perception, memory, or imagination that presents the intended features. Evidence (in Husserl's technical sense) is the degree to which an intention is fulfilled: adequate evidence is the complete fulfillment of an intention; apodictic evidence is the fulfillment that excludes any possible doubt.

This account of fulfillment connects Husserl's phenomenology to epistemology: evidence (in the philosophical sense of justification) is the phenomenological category of intentional fulfillment. Knowledge claims are the products of intentions that have been fulfilled in appropriate ways.

**Horizon Intentionality**

A distinctive feature of Husserl's mature account is his analysis of "horizon" intentionality. Every object is given against a horizon of further determinations that are anticipated but not yet presented — the inner horizon (the further features of the object itself) and the outer horizon (the broader context of objects in which it appears).

When I see the cup, I implicitly anticipate its further features and its context — the table it stands on, the room it is in, the world beyond. This horizon intentionality gives perception a richness and implicit reference that goes far beyond what is immediately presented. Consciousness is always already ahead of itself, anticipating more than it has yet received.

Horizon intentionality connects to the "background" discussed by Searle and the "implicit understanding" emphasized by Dreyfus: behind any explicit intentional act, there is an implicit background of understandings and anticipations that gives the act its sense and context.
