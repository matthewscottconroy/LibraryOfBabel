# Existential Structures: Thrownness, Projection, and Care

Heidegger's analysis of Dasein in *Being and Time* proceeds by identifying the fundamental "existential structures" — what Heidegger calls "existentialia" — that constitute the being of Dasein. These are not empirical features that Dasein might or might not have, but the ontological conditions for the possibility of being Dasein at all. Among the most important are thrownness, projection, fallenness, and the overarching structure of care that unifies them.

**Thrownness**

Dasein always finds itself already in a situation it did not choose. Heidegger calls this thrownness (Geworfenheit): we are thrown into a world — a particular time, culture, language, family, body — before we have any say in the matter. The thrownness of Dasein is not simply the contingency of empirical facts about where and when we were born; it is an ontological structure. To be Dasein at all is to find oneself already underway, already in a situation with a background of meanings, capabilities, and orientations that one did not choose and cannot fully survey.

Thrownness is revealed affectively, through what Heidegger calls Stimmungen — moods or attunements. Mood is not merely a coloring added to experience; it is the way in which we are disclosed to ourselves as thrown into a particular situation. Anxiety, boredom, joy, depression — these moods do not merely tell us how we feel about our situation; they open up the situation in a particular way, making certain features salient and others invisible. Anxiety has a special status for Heidegger: unlike fear, which is directed at a specific object or threat, anxiety has no specific object. In anxiety, the world as a whole becomes strange and uncanny (unheimlich — literally "not-at-home"), and Dasein is confronted with its own thrown condition, its "not-being-at-home" in the world.

**Projection**

If thrownness captures Dasein's past-oriented condition — the fact that we always already are somewhere — projection (Entwurf) captures Dasein's future-oriented character. Dasein is not a fixed substance that then does things; it is constitutively ahead of itself, oriented toward possibilities that it has taken up or failed to take up. To exist is to project: to be understood in terms of where one is going, what one is for, how one takes up the possibilities that one's situation makes available.

This does not mean that Dasein explicitly deliberates about its future and then acts accordingly. Most of the time, projection is not thematic — it shows up in the way things matter, in the engaged, goal-oriented structure of ordinary activity. The carpenter projecting toward a completed house does not pause to deliberate about the house; the projection is operative in the focused, purposive quality of her work.

Heidegger's analysis of projection challenges the common picture of action as originating in explicit intentions or desires. The intentional structure of action is not primarily explicit propositional attitudes but the lived orientation toward possibilities that characterizes Dasein's engaged existence. This connects to discussions of action in contemporary philosophy of mind, where there is growing recognition that skilled, habitual action is not well-described as the execution of explicitly represented intentions.

**Fallenness**

A third existential structure is fallenness (Verfallenheit). Heidegger observes that Dasein tends to understand itself and its possibilities in terms drawn from the social world — from what "one" does, what "people" say, what is "done." This anonymous public interpretation of existence is das Man, the "they-self" — the way the individual's possibilities are pre-interpreted and standardized by the social world.

Fallenness is not a moral failing; it is an ontological tendency. To be Dasein is to be always in danger of losing oneself in the social world, of taking one's possibilities as given rather than owned, of evading the confrontation with one's own finitude and uniqueness that authentic existence requires. Das Man provides comfort, familiarity, and social coordination, but it also insulates us from the existential challenges that, Heidegger argues, are essential to genuine self-understanding.

**Care**

Thrownness, projection, and fallenness are not three independent structures; they are moments of a unified structure that Heidegger calls care (Sorge). The formal structure of care is: being-already-in (thrownness) as being-ahead-of-oneself (projection) in the midst of entities (fallenness). Care is Heidegger's answer to the question of what it is to be Dasein — not a list of properties but a dynamic structural unity that captures how the different temporal dimensions of Dasein's existence — past, future, and present — are unified in the being of Dasein.

Care is not a specific feeling or attitude; it is the ontological structure that makes possible all the specific ways in which we engage, avoid, pursue, and relate to things and people and ourselves. That we care — that things matter to us, that we are invested in our existence and its possibilities — is not a contingent fact about us; it is constitutive of our being as Dasein.

**Significance for Philosophy of Mind**

Heidegger's existential analytic provides resources for philosophy of mind that have been extensively mined. The primacy of practical engagement over theoretical contemplation anticipates 4E cognitive science. The role of mood and attunement in disclosing the world anticipates the cognitive role of affect. The analysis of das Man and social self-understanding connects to work on social cognition and the construction of self. The concept of care as the fundamental structure of human existence provides an alternative to both desire-based and belief-based accounts of motivation and action.
