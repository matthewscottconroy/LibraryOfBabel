# Language and Thinking in Later Heidegger

Among the most distinctive claims of the later Heidegger is that language is not an instrument that human beings use to express pre-formed thoughts. Rather, language speaks through us; it is the "house of Being" in which we dwell. This is not a merely rhetorical point but a substantive claim about the relationship between mind, language, and world that connects to live debates in philosophy of mind and cognitive science.

**Language as the House of Being**

In the traditional picture — which Heidegger sees as one more consequence of the metaphysical tradition's error — language is a tool that we wield for communication and expression. We first have thoughts, and then we find words to encode them; words are signs pointing to prior mental contents. This picture treats language as external to thought and treats speaking and writing as practical activities for transmitting already-formed meanings.

Heidegger's late work inverts this picture. Language is not a tool we use; it is a structure we inhabit. The possibilities for thought available to us are always already shaped by the language we speak — not in the sense of the weak Sapir-Whorf hypothesis (that vocabulary affects how we categorize things), but in the deeper sense that language discloses a world. To learn a language is to come to inhabit a world — to encounter things as certain kinds of things, with certain possibilities and resistances, against a background of significance that is carried by the grammar, vocabulary, and idioms of the language.

This makes language not a transparent medium for representing thought but a constitutive condition for the kind of thinking that is possible for us. Different languages do not merely name the same world differently; they disclose different dimensions of the world, make different aspects salient, enable different forms of questioning.

**The Claim of Language**

Heidegger makes the radical claim that "language speaks" — not that human beings speak using language, but that language itself is the speaking. Human speaking is authentic when it is genuinely responsive to what language itself is saying — when we do not merely deploy familiar phrases and conventional formulas but attend to what is being said and allowed to be said by the words we use.

This claim has been interpreted in various ways. The least radical interpretation is phenomenological: authentic thinking requires careful attention to the words we use, since the history and resonances of words carry dimensions of meaning that explicit definitions cannot capture. The more radical interpretation, which Heidegger seems to intend, is that language is not under our control in the way a tool is; it is more like a medium we find ourselves within, and our thinking is always already shaped by what the language allows us to say.

**Thinking and Technology**

In his later reflections on thinking, Heidegger distinguishes between "calculative thinking" and "meditative thinking." Calculative thinking — the thinking of science, technology, and everyday planning — operates within a fixed framework of means and ends, calculating how to achieve pre-given goals. It is the dominant form of thinking in the technological world, and it has achieved remarkable successes. But it is, Heidegger argues, the most superficial form of thinking, because it never questions its own framework or the goals it serves.

Meditative thinking, by contrast, is what philosophy and poetry at their best achieve: a thinking that questions the assumptions of calculative thought, attends to what is concealed rather than disclosed by the technological world, and holds open the question of Being. Meditative thinking is not opposed to science but operates in a different register — it asks not "how does this work?" or "how do we achieve X?" but "what does it mean that this is the world we live in?" and "what is opened and closed by our way of engaging with things?"

This distinction maps onto the contrast that contemporary cognitive scientists draw between System 1 and System 2 thinking, or between explicit deliberation and background understanding — though Heidegger would resist any assimilation to cognitive science's framework, which he would see as one more expression of calculative thought.

**Significance for Philosophy of Mind**

Heidegger's treatment of language challenges any simple picture of thought as prior to and independent of linguistic expression. This connects to debates about the language of thought hypothesis: is thinking necessarily linguistic? Heidegger's answer is nuanced — he does not claim that thought is propositional language, but he does claim that the disclosure of a world through which thinking is possible is constitutively linguistic.

This also connects to debates about artificial intelligence. Current large language models are extraordinarily fluent in language — but whether they understand language in Heidegger's sense, whether they dwell in the world disclosed by language, is a profound open question. They generate and transform linguistic content with great facility, but whether they are responsive to "what language is saying" — whether they have the background understanding and world-engagement that for Heidegger is the condition for genuine thinking — remains deeply uncertain.
