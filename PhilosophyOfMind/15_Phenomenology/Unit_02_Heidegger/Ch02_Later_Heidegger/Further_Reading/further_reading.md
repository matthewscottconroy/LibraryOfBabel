# Further Reading: Later Heidegger

## Primary Sources

**Martin Heidegger, "The Question Concerning Technology" (1954), in *The Question Concerning Technology and Other Essays*** — Heidegger's most famous analysis of modern technology argues that technology is not merely a tool but a way of "revealing" or disclosing Being—specifically, the modern "Enframing" (Gestell) that discloses everything as "standing reserve" (Bestand) available for human ordering and disposal. This technological disclosure threatens to conceal other, richer forms of revealing. The essay raises fundamental questions about the relationship between modern technology and human self-understanding.

**Martin Heidegger, "Building Dwelling Thinking" (1951) and "The Thing" (1950), in *Poetry, Language, Thought*** — These essays develop Heidegger's later account of dwelling—being at home in a world—and of things as "gathering" the fourfold of earth, sky, mortals, and divinities. They represent his late concern with understanding the relationship between human existence and its material and spatial environment.

**Martin Heidegger, "The Origin of the Work of Art" (1935/1950), in *Poetry, Language, Thought*** — Heidegger argues that great art works open up a world—a whole horizon of significance—rather than merely representing it. Art is truth "setting itself to work" by establishing a conflict between world and earth. This essay has been enormously influential in aesthetics and in understanding Heidegger's account of disclosure.

**Martin Heidegger, "Letter on Humanism" (1947)** — Written in response to Sartre's existentialism, Heidegger here articulates his conception of language as the "house of Being" and develops his critique of humanism as a form of subjectivism that forgets the question of Being. The letter introduces the concept of thinking as "dwelling" in the openness of Being.

**Hubert Dreyfus, "Heidegger on the Connection between Nihilism, Art, Technology, and Politics" (1993)** — An accessible treatment of Heidegger's later philosophy that connects his technology critique to questions about meaning, nihilism, and the possibility of a post-technological relationship to Being. Dreyfus's interpretation emphasizes the connections between later Heidegger and contemporary concerns.

## Secondary Literature

**Albert Borgmann, *Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life* (1984)** — A philosophical engagement with Heidegger's technology critique that develops the concept of the "device paradigm"—the way modern devices conceal the machinery that produces their benefits—and argues for "focal practices" as a response. One of the most practically useful developments of Heidegger's later thought.

**Don Ihde, *Technics and Praxis* (1979)** — Develops a phenomenology of human-technology relations influenced by both Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, providing a more empirical and less dramatic account of technology than Heidegger's own work.

**Mark Wrathall (ed.), *Heidegger and Unconcealment: Truth, Language, and History* (2011)** — Examines Heidegger's later conception of truth as alētheia and language as the house of Being, providing careful scholarly analysis of these difficult late texts.

**Richard Polt, *The Emergency of Being: On Heidegger's "Contributions to Philosophy"* (2006)** — An examination of Heidegger's *Beiträge* (1936–1938), his most important unpublished work from the transitional period, which provides the internal development of his turn from *Being and Time*.

**Iain Thomson, *Heidegger on Ontotheology: Technology and the Politics of Education* (2005)** — Examines the connections between Heidegger's critique of technology and his account of ontotheology, with implications for questions about education, nihilism, and meaning in modernity.
