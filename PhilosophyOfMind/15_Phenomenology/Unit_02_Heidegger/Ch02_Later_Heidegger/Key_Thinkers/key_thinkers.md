# Key Thinkers: Later Heidegger

---

**Martin Heidegger (1889–1976)**
German philosopher whose "turn" (Kehre) from the Dasein-centered analysis of *Being and Time* to a thinking of Being itself as giving and withdrawing produced his distinctive later philosophy. His technology critique holds that modern technology's "Enframing" (Gestell) reduces everything—including humans—to standing reserve available for ordering and disposal, crowding out other forms of disclosure. His claim that language is the "house of Being"—that Being discloses itself through and as language—grounds his late meditations on poetry, thing, and dwelling. His critique of calculative thinking in favor of "meditative thinking" has been influential in philosophy of technology and environmental thought.
*Key works:* "The Question Concerning Technology" (1954), *Poetry, Language, Thought* (1971), "Letter on Humanism" (1947), *Contributions to Philosophy* (1936–1938/1989)

---

**Hubert Dreyfus (1929–2017)**
American philosopher at UC Berkeley. Dreyfus drew on both early and later Heidegger to develop a critique of AI and classical cognitive science, arguing that these approaches fail because they attempt to model the mind as rule-following computation and thereby ignore the background of pre-theoretical coping that Heidegger identified as fundamental. His work connecting Heideggerian phenomenology to AI and cognitive science has been enormously influential, making later Heidegger accessible and relevant to Anglo-American philosophy.
*Key works:* *What Computers Can't Do* (1972), *Being-in-the-World* (1991), "Heidegger on the Connection between Nihilism, Art, Technology, and Politics" (1993)

---

**Albert Borgmann (1937–)**
German-American philosopher at the University of Montana. Borgmann extended Heidegger's technology critique into practical philosophy, developing the concept of the "device paradigm"—the tendency of modern technology to deliver commodities while concealing the machinery that produces them—and contrasting devices with "things" that gather engagement and practice. His account of "focal practices" as responses to technological disengagement represents one of the most practically useful applications of Heideggerian ideas.
*Key works:* *Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life* (1984), *Holding On to Reality* (1999)

---

**Hans-Georg Gadamer (1900–2002)**
German philosopher at Heidelberg University. Gadamer was Heidegger's student and the inheritor and transformer of his hermeneutic philosophy. His *Truth and Method* (1960) develops Heideggerian historicity and the disclosure of being-in-language into a philosophical hermeneutics, arguing that understanding is always situated in a tradition and that the "fusion of horizons" between text and interpreter is the basic model of all understanding. Language as the medium of all human experience—not just poetry—is central to Gadamer's account.
*Key works:* *Truth and Method* (1960), *Philosophical Hermeneutics* (1976), *The Gadamer Reader* (2007)

---

**Don Ihde (1934–)**
American philosopher at Stony Brook University. Ihde developed a phenomenology of human-technology relations—covering "embodiment relations" (technology taken into the body), "interpretive relations" (technology that must be interpreted), and "alterity relations" (technology as quasi-other)—that extends and concretizes Heidegger's analysis in a more empirically tractable way. His work has been foundational for philosophy of technology as a discipline and has influenced science and technology studies.
*Key works:* *Technics and Praxis* (1979), *Technology and the Lifeworld* (1990), *Postphenomenology and Technoscience* (2009)
