# The Chiasm and the Flesh

In his final, unfinished work *The Visible and the Invisible* (published posthumously in 1968), Merleau-Ponty pushed beyond the phenomenological framework of *Phenomenology of Perception* toward a more radical ontological vision. The central concept of this late work is the chiasm — the intertwining or reversibility of perceiver and perceived, seer and seen, toucher and touched — and the associated concept of "the flesh" (la chair) as the fundamental ontological category underlying this reversibility.

**The Reversibility of Touch**

Merleau-Ponty begins from a phenomenological observation that his *Phenomenology of Perception* had already noted: when I touch my left hand with my right, something strange happens. I can take either hand as the touching subject or as the touched object, and in a certain remarkable way, the hand that is being touched also feels itself touching. There is a reversibility of touching and being touched that is not present in my contact with inanimate objects: when I touch the table, the table does not touch me back in the relevant sense, but my two hands touch each other in a way that confounds the subject-object division.

This reversibility is not symmetrical — I cannot fully occupy both positions simultaneously; there is always a gap, a delay, a crossing rather than a coincidence. But it is a genuine reversibility that reveals something fundamental about the body's relationship to the world: the body is not a purely subjective center of experience confronting an objective world of things. The body is itself part of the perceptual world; it is visible, tangible, subject to the same textures and resistances as the things it perceives.

**The Flesh**

From this phenomenological observation, Merleau-Ponty draws an ontological conclusion. The body and the world are not two fundamentally different kinds of substance (mind and matter, subject and object). They are made of the same "stuff" — what Merleau-Ponty calls the flesh (la chair). This is not biological flesh; it is an ontological category: the reversible fabric of perceiver and perceived, the medium in which seeing and being seen, touching and being touched, are intertwined.

The concept of flesh represents Merleau-Ponty's attempt to transcend both subjectivism (which makes the world a construction of consciousness) and objectivism (which makes consciousness an object in the world) by finding a more fundamental ontological ground that precedes and underlies both. The flesh is neither mind nor matter but the condition for both — the reversible element from which both the seeing subject and the visible world are carved.

This is a demanding and somewhat obscure idea, and Merleau-Ponty himself acknowledged that he was working at the edge of what language and concept can easily accommodate. But the basic move is clear: to ground the relationship between consciousness and world not in a subject-object relation but in a more primitive "intercorporeal" tissue that subtends both.

**Intersubjectivity and the Chiasm**

The chiasm extends beyond the individual body to intersubjectivity. Other people are not objects I observe from the outside and then project consciousness onto by analogy. They are other flesh — bodies engaged in the same perceptual world that I am engaged in, with the same kind of reversibility that characterizes my own relation to my body. To encounter another person is to encounter the same flesh from a different perspective, not a different substance.

This provides an original solution to the problem of other minds. The problem arises when we assume that minds are enclosed within individual bodies and that we only have access to the body, not the mind, from the outside. But if the flesh is the fundamental category — if subjectivity and world are aspects of a single reversible fabric — then the other's body is not a mere object that hides a mind; it is already an expression of subjectivity in the same sense that my own body is.

Merleau-Ponty's late ontology connects to phenomenological traditions in intersubjectivity (Husserl's analogical apperception, Stein's empathy) but also anticipates certain themes in contemporary social neuroscience: the role of shared bodily resonance, motor mimicry, and bodily synchrony in interpersonal understanding.

**Legacy**

The chiasm and flesh have been enormously productive concepts in post-Merleau-Pontian philosophy. Feminist phenomenologists have used them to explore the gendered and racialized body — the way socially constructed meanings are literally incorporated into flesh, shaping perception and engagement. Phenomenologists of disability have used them to explore how altered embodiment changes the structure of world-experience. And philosophers of nature have appealed to the flesh concept to develop an ecologically sensitive ontology in which the living body and its environment are understood as co-constitutive dimensions of a single process.
