# Further Reading: Perception and World

## Primary Sources

**Maurice Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception*, Part II (1945)** — The second part of *Phenomenology of Perception* covers the phenomenology of the perceived world, including the analysis of space, the "lived" nature of perception's figure-ground structure, and the pre-objective world that underlies scientific accounts of nature. Merleau-Ponty's rejection of the constancy hypothesis—the empiricist assumption that there is a fixed correspondence between stimulus and sensation—grounds his account of perception as active and engaged.

**Maurice Merleau-Ponty, *The Visible and the Invisible* (1968)** — Merleau-Ponty's unfinished late work develops the ontology of "the flesh" (la chair)—not as material substance but as a texture of being common to both sensing body and sensed world. The chiasm between flesh-of-the-body and flesh-of-the-world, exemplified in the reversibility of touch (the hand can be both touching and touched), grounds a new ontology of intertwining that overcomes the subject-object dichotomy.

**Merleau-Ponty, *The Primacy of Perception* (1964)** — A collection of important essays, including "Eye and Mind"—Merleau-Ponty's meditation on the phenomenology of vision and painting, developing the flesh ontology—and "The Philosopher and His Shadow," which engages with the late Husserl. "The Primacy of Perception" essay itself presents the most direct case for taking perception rather than thought as the foundation of experience.

**Hubert Dreyfus and Sean Dorrance Kelly, *All Things Shining* (2011)** — Develops a Merleau-Ponty-influenced account of how things can "shine"—show up as mattering—in modern secular life without requiring religious foundations. Applies phenomenological insights about skilled perception to questions of meaning in contemporary culture.

**Alva Noë, *Action in Perception* (2004)** — Develops an "enactive" theory of perception heavily influenced by Merleau-Ponty, arguing that perceiving is a form of skilled bodily engagement with the environment rather than the passive reception of sensory data. Noë argues that perception essentially involves sensorimotor knowledge—knowing how the appearance of things changes as one moves around them.

## Secondary Literature

**Sean Dorrance Kelly, "Seeing Things in Merleau-Ponty" (2005)** — An analytic reconstruction of Merleau-Ponty's account of perceptual experience that engages with philosophy of mind literature, arguing that Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology of perception provides a superior account to both representationalist and disjunctivist alternatives.

**Lawrence Hass, *Merleau-Ponty's Philosophy* (2008)** — A thorough and philosophically careful treatment of Merleau-Ponty's later philosophy, including the flesh ontology and the chiasm. Particularly valuable for students who have already read the secondary literature on *Phenomenology of Perception*.

**Ted Toadvine and Leonard Lawlor (eds.), *The Merleau-Ponty Reader* (2007)** — A well-selected anthology of Merleau-Ponty's writings, covering the full range of his work from the early *Structure of Behavior* through the late flesh ontology, with helpful editorial introductions.

**Glen Mazis, *Earthbodies: Rediscovering Our Planetary Senses* (2002)** — Develops Merleau-Ponty's perceptual ontology in an ecological and environmental direction, arguing for a more expansive understanding of how embodied perception is embedded in natural and social environments.

**Richard Shusterman, *Body Consciousness: A Philosophy of Mindfulness and Somaesthetics* (2008)** — Develops a "somaesthetics" that engages with Merleau-Ponty's body phenomenology while also arguing for the role of conscious attention to the body in improving perceptual and motor skills—a point on which he differs from Merleau-Ponty.
