# Sartre: Consciousness and Nothingness

Jean-Paul Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943) is the founding document of French existentialism and one of the most ambitious philosophical analyses of consciousness produced in the twentieth century. Sartre develops his account of consciousness in explicit dialogue with Husserl and Heidegger, radicalizing certain of their claims while arguing that they did not take their insights far enough. The result is a distinctive ontology built around the absolute difference between the being of consciousness and the being of things.

**Being-in-Itself and Being-for-Itself**

Sartre's ontology begins with a fundamental distinction between two modes of being. Being-in-itself (l'en-soi) is the being of things — opaque, dense, self-identical, contingent, simply there. A stone is what it is; it has no perspective on its own being; it is not directed toward anything; it simply exists. Being-for-itself (le pour-soi) is the being of consciousness — transparent, nihilating, always other than itself, free.

The for-itself is what Sartre means by consciousness. The crucial feature of consciousness, for Sartre, is that it is always already beyond itself — directed toward something, never simply coinciding with itself. Following Husserl, Sartre holds that consciousness is always consciousness of something. But he goes beyond Husserl by arguing that this intentional structure entails a fundamental nothingness at the heart of consciousness.

**Consciousness as Nothingness**

Sartre's most radical claim is that consciousness is nothing — or more precisely, that consciousness involves a fundamental nihilation. When I am conscious of the table, consciousness is not itself something; it is purely a revelation of the table. Consciousness has no contents of its own; it is through and through directedness toward the world. The Husserlian idea of immanent mental contents — the noema as a real component of the act of consciousness — is, for Sartre, a category error. Consciousness does not contain representations; it is a transparency pointing at the world.

This nihilation has radical implications for freedom. Because consciousness is always a distance from itself — always "not-yet" what it could be, always able to step back from its situation and take a stance on it — consciousness is inherently free. This freedom is not a property that some conscious beings happen to have; it is constitutive of what consciousness is. To be conscious is to be condemned to freedom, as Sartre's famous phrase has it.

**Bad Faith**

If freedom is constitutive of consciousness, why does it so often feel like we are unfree? Sartre's answer is the concept of bad faith (mauvaise foi): the attempt by consciousness to flee its freedom by taking itself to be a thing — to have a fixed nature, a stable identity, predetermined roles that determine what it must do. Bad faith is not simple self-deception; it is a peculiar structure in which consciousness knows its freedom but pretends to itself not to know it.

Examples abound: the waiter who plays the role of waiter so thoroughly that he seems to have no existence outside the role; the woman who pretends not to understand the romantic implications of her companion's hand-holding, treating her own hand as a mere object; the person who says "I can't help it, that's just the way I am." In each case, consciousness is evading the burden of its freedom by pretending to have a fixed essence — by treating itself as if it were being-in-itself rather than being-for-itself.

Bad faith is distinguished from lying to others precisely because the one who is in bad faith is both the deceiver and the deceived. This is possible, Sartre argues, because consciousness has a peculiar structure of self-presence that is not the same as transparent self-knowledge: consciousness is present to itself without coinciding with itself, and this gap makes it possible for consciousness to simultaneously know and not-know its own freedom.

**The Look**

Sartre's analysis of intersubjectivity takes the form of an analysis of "the look" (le regard). When I become aware of being looked at — when another's gaze falls upon me — I experience a sudden transformation: I become an object for another's consciousness. I am, from the other's perspective, a thing among things, with a fixed nature and position in the world. The experience of shame is paradigmatic: to be ashamed before another is to experience myself as seen and defined, reduced from subject to object by the other's look.

This means that intersubjectivity is, for Sartre, fundamentally conflictual. The other is a constant threat to my freedom — every other consciousness can objectify me, reduce me to an object in their world. Unlike Merleau-Ponty's intercorporeal intersubjectivity, Sartre's interpersonal world is characterized by an irreducible tension between the free subjects who each threaten to reduce the other to an object. The fundamental structures of love, desire, masochism, sadism, and indifference are all analyzed as strategies for managing this conflict.

**Assessment**

Sartre's analysis of consciousness has been enormously influential but also widely criticized. His insistence on absolute freedom has seemed psychologically unrealistic and politically problematic, neglecting the ways in which class, race, gender, and bodily situation constrain the possibilities available to individuals. Merleau-Ponty argued that Sartre's for-itself was too disembodied — a consciousness without a body cannot make sense of the ways that embodied habit and skill shape and constrain experience. Feminist and postcolonial critics have argued that Sartre's universalization of freedom ignores the ways that social structures produce genuinely constrained subjectivities.

Despite these criticisms, Sartre's analysis of consciousness — its nihilating structure, its freedom, its bad faith, its intersubjective conflict — remains a powerful and provocative framework for understanding the peculiarities of human existence.
