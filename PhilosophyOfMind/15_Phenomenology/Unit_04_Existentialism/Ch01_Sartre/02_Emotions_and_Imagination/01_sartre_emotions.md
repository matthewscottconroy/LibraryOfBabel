# Sartre on Emotions and Imagination

Alongside his major ontological work, Sartre produced important focused analyses of specific mental phenomena. His early essays *Sketch for a Theory of the Emotions* (1939) and *The Imaginary* (1940) develop views about emotion and imagination that connect to his broader ontology while raising distinctive philosophical issues. These works are philosophically interesting in their own right and have had significant influence on phenomenology of mind.

**The Magical Transformation Theory of Emotion**

Sartre's theory of emotion is explicitly critical of the James-Lange theory, which holds that emotions are the perception of bodily changes. For Sartre, this gets the relationship between emotion and bodily change backwards and misses the intentional, meaning-laden character of emotion entirely.

Sartre's positive account begins from the observation that emotions arise in situations where our ordinary, practical means of dealing with the world have failed or are unavailable. When I cannot open a door through ordinary means, I may become angry and beat on it — not because anger is an automatic response to frustration, but because anger transforms the relationship between me and the world. The angry beating expresses a shift in how the world is understood: it becomes something that resists me, that is hostile, that deserves to be struck.

Emotion, for Sartre, is a "magical transformation" of the world. When we cannot achieve our aims through the ordinary practical means that define the rational order of the world, we transform the world emotionally — we make it appear in a different register, under a different aspect that makes our response appropriate or relieving. Fear is not simply a bodily trembling; it is a way of dealing with a threat by transforming the relationship to the world — by taking flight in a way that makes the threat seem to disappear. Fainting from fear is an extreme case: consciousness literally escapes from an intolerable situation by eliminating itself from it.

This is not a calculation or a deliberate strategy; it is a spontaneous, unreflective transformation of the lived relationship to the world. Emotion is a form of consciousness — intentionally directed at the world — but one that apprehends the world in a way that bypasses ordinary practical engagement.

**The Imaginary: Imagination versus Perception**

In *The Imaginary*, Sartre develops an analysis of the imagination that is deeply illuminating about the nature of consciousness. His central claim is that imagination and perception are fundamentally different modes of consciousness, not variations on the same basic act.

The image, for Sartre, is not a weak copy of a perception. When I imagine a friend, the imagined friend is present to me in a distinctive way: imaginatively present, as opposed to perceptually present. These are not degrees of vividness or clarity; they are different modes of intentionality. In perception, I am confronted with something that presents itself to me in its own terms — the perceived object has properties I can discover, aspects I haven't yet seen, resistances I must accommodate. In imagination, by contrast, I intend what I already know — the imagined object contains no more information than I put into it. I cannot discover the number of columns on the imagined Pantheon by examining my mental image more carefully; I can only imagine a number of columns.

This is what Sartre calls the "imaginary's essential poverty." The imagined object is thin, depleted — it has only the features I have given it. The perceived object is rich, inexhaustible, always offering more than I have yet taken in. This difference is not a matter of degree but of kind: imagination and perception are categorially different modes of consciousness.

**Imagination and Freedom**

The poverty of the imaginary is, paradoxically, connected to freedom. Imagination is the power of consciousness to nihilate — to step back from what is actually the case and consider what is not the case, what might be, what could be different. When I imagine, I surpass the actual world toward a non-actual one; I stand in a relationship of distance and freedom from what is given in perception.

For Sartre, this is why imagination is fundamental to consciousness as such. The capacity for imagination is not a special faculty added to a non-imagining consciousness; it is an expression of the nihilating character of consciousness itself. A consciousness that could not imagine — could not surpass the actual toward the possible — would be consciousness trapped in the in-itself, which is to say, it would not be consciousness at all. This connects directly to Sartre's analysis of freedom: to be free is to be able to imagine things otherwise, and this capacity is constitutive of consciousness as being-for-itself.

**Critical Reception**

Sartre's magical transformation theory of emotion has been criticized as overly intellectualized — it does not capture the brute, overwhelming character of intense emotion — and as making emotions seem more controlled and functional than they often feel. Contemporary appraisal theories of emotion (Lazarus, Nussbaum) share Sartre's insight that emotions involve a meaningful assessment of one's situation, but they develop this within a more empirically grounded framework.

Sartre's analysis of imagination has been more positively received. His distinction between the mode of presence of imagined and perceived objects anticipates important work in the philosophy of imagination and mental imagery: whether mental images are depictive (picture-like) or descriptive (more like propositional representations), and how the intentionality of imagination differs from that of belief and perception.
