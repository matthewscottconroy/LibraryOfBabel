# Further Reading: Sartre on Consciousness

## Primary Sources

**Jean-Paul Sartre, *Being and Nothingness* (1943, trans. Barnes 1958)** — Sartre's magnum opus develops a phenomenological ontology distinguishing being-in-itself (être-en-soi: the massive, opaque self-identity of things) from being-for-itself (être-pour-soi: consciousness as the nihilating negation that introduces lack, possibility, and freedom). Consciousness is characterized as "a nothing in the middle of being"—it has no positive content of its own but exists only as the negation and projection of possibilities. The analysis of bad faith, the look, and the body's for-others dimension are among the most important sections.

**Jean-Paul Sartre, *The Psychology of Imagination* (1940)** — Sartre's phenomenological account of imagining argues that the image is not a faint perception but an intentional act that posits its object as absent or nonexistent. This account is important for understanding consciousness's freedom—its capacity to negate the given—and for Sartre's distinctive account of how imagination differs from perception.

**Jean-Paul Sartre, "Existentialism is a Humanism" (1945)** — Sartre's accessible public lecture presenting the core ideas of his existentialism: that existence precedes essence, that humans are "condemned to be free," that radical freedom entails radical responsibility, and that bad faith is the flight from acknowledging this freedom. A useful entry point but not a substitute for *Being and Nothingness*.

**Jean-Paul Sartre, *The Transcendence of the Ego* (1936)** — Sartre's early critique of Husserl's transcendental ego, arguing that consciousness does not need an ego as its owner or underlying subject—consciousness is "impersonal" or "pre-personal." The ego appears at the level of reflection, not at the level of pre-reflective consciousness. This essay is essential for understanding how Sartre's account of consciousness differs from Husserl's.

**Christina Howells (ed.), *The Cambridge Companion to Sartre* (1992)** — An excellent scholarly collection covering all aspects of Sartre's philosophy, including his accounts of consciousness, bad faith, the look, freedom, and ethics, as well as his political philosophy and literary work.

## Secondary Literature

**Joseph Catalano, *A Commentary on Jean-Paul Sartre's Being and Nothingness* (1974)** — The most detailed and reliable English-language commentary on *Being and Nothingness*, following the argument of the text section by section. Indispensable for students studying the text closely.

**Thomas Baldwin, "Sartre, Husserl, and the Problem of Other Minds" (1987)** — Examines Sartre's distinctive response to the problem of other minds through the concept of "the look" (le regard), arguing that Sartre's account captures the threatening and objectifying character of being seen better than Husserlian intersubjectivity.

**Gregory McCulloch, *Using Sartre: An Analytical Introduction to Early Sartrean Themes* (1994)** — An analytic reconstruction of Sartre's early philosophy that makes his account of consciousness and bad faith accessible to readers trained in analytic philosophy.

**Mark Rowlands, *The New Science of the Mind: From Extended Mind to Embodied Phenomenology* (2010)** — Engages with Sartre's account of consciousness as negation in the context of contemporary philosophy of mind, showing its continued relevance to debates about the relationship between intentionality and nihilation.

**Hazel Barnes, *Sartre* (1973)** — An accessible and sympathetic introduction to Sartre's entire philosophy by the translator of *Being and Nothingness*, covering both the philosophical and literary dimensions of his work.
