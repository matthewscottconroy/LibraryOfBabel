# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Sartre on Consciousness

## Key Arguments

**The Ontological Argument: Consciousness as Nothing (Néant)**
Sartre argues in Being and Nothingness that consciousness (the for-itself, pour-soi) is characterized by non-being: it is "what it is not and is not what it is." Unlike things (the in-itself, en-soi), which are simply what they are (a stone is a stone), consciousness is perpetually distanced from itself by the nothingness that constitutes it — the gap between consciousness and its objects, and between consciousness and its own past self. This non-being is not a lack but the condition of possibility for freedom, negation, and the capacity to imagine things other than they are.

**The For-Itself/In-Itself Distinction**
Sartre's fundamental ontological distinction separates the in-itself (en-soi) — the mode of being of things, which simply are what they are, fully determined, dense with being — from the for-itself (pour-soi) — the mode of being of consciousness, which is never fully determined, always projecting beyond itself, always haunted by nothingness. The for-itself is "what it is not" because it is temporally extended (its past is what it was, not what it is), negating (it can negate the given), and free (its future is always undetermined).

**The Argument for Radical Freedom**
Sartre argues that because consciousness is constituted by nothingness — a perpetual non-coincidence with itself — it is radically free: there is no determining cause (biological, psychological, historical) that fixes what it will do, because consciousness can always negate the causal influence by choosing to act otherwise. This freedom is not a gift but a burden ("condemned to be free"): one cannot escape the responsibility of choice, even choosing not to choose is a choice. The argument grounds Sartre's ethics and his account of bad faith.

**Bad Faith as Self-Deception About Freedom**
Sartre argues that bad faith (mauvaise foi) is the fundamental form of self-deception by which consciousness flees its freedom by pretending to be determined — by treating itself as an in-itself (a thing with fixed properties and no freedom). The waiter who plays at being a waiter, the woman who pretends not to notice her companion's advances — both act as if they were things with fixed natures rather than free projects. Bad faith is possible only because consciousness is perpetually non-coincident with itself; its possibility reveals the structure of the for-itself.

**The Look and the Objectification of the Self**
Sartre argues that the encounter with the Other's gaze (le regard) is a fundamental existential event: when I catch another looking at me, I am abruptly transformed from a free subject into an object in another's world. The Other's look "steals" my world and reveals me as a thing with fixed properties and a point of view external to my own. Shame — the pre-reflective awareness of being caught — reveals this structure. The encounter with the Other's look is constitutive of a domain of being (being-for-others) that is irreducibly distinct from being-for-itself.

## Core Concepts

**Being-For-Itself (Pour-Soi)**
The for-itself is Sartre's term for the mode of being characteristic of consciousness: a being that is never simply what it is, that perpetually escapes complete determination by virtue of the nothingness at its core. The for-itself is temporal (it exists ecstatically, projecting toward the future and having been its past), negating (it can negate the given and imagine alternatives), and free (no causal chain fully determines its choices). The for-itself is "haunted by being" — it desires to coincide with itself as an in-itself would, but this impossible desire is the source of all human projects.

**Being-In-Itself (En-Soi)**
The in-itself is the mode of being of things that are simply what they are: fully determined, opaque, non-relational, uncreated, without nothingness. A stone, a table, a mountain — these are in-itself: they do not project, negate, or transcend their given nature. The in-itself is not "conscious of" anything and is not characterized by temporality or freedom. Sartre uses the concept to define human consciousness by contrast: human reality aspires (impossibly) to the solidity of the in-itself while remaining for-itself.

**Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi)**
Bad faith is Sartre's term for the fundamental form of self-deception in which consciousness denies its own freedom by treating itself as fully determined — as a thing with a fixed nature. Bad faith is not simple lying to others but lying to oneself, and it is possible because consciousness is always non-coincident with itself. Examples include the waiter who has become his role (denying his transcendence) and the woman who denies her freedom in a romantic situation (treating her hand as a thing rather than owning its withdrawal). Bad faith is the root form of inauthenticity.

**Radical Freedom**
Sartre's radical freedom is the claim that consciousness is always free to negate the given and choose otherwise: there are no determining causes that make a particular choice necessary, because consciousness can always distance itself from its situation by the nihilating power of nothingness. This freedom is not empirical liberty (the absence of obstacles) but ontological: it is the very structure of the for-itself. Radical freedom entails radical responsibility — one can never appeal to one's nature, upbringing, or circumstances to excuse one's choices.

**The Look (Le Regard)**
The look (le regard) is Sartre's term for the Other's gaze as experienced by the subject. The look is not primarily a visual event but an existential encounter: when I experience being-looked-at, I suddenly find myself as an object in another's world, with fixed properties and a perspective external to my own. The look reveals the fundamental ontological relation between for-itselfs: each consciousness is a threat to the other's freedom, because each tends to reduce the other to an object. This permanent conflict — "hell is other people" — is the structure of intersubjectivity for Sartre.

**Being-For-Others**
Being-for-others is the dimension of one's existence that is constituted by others' perspectives on oneself: the properties, character, and identity attributed to one by others, which one cannot directly control or know. It is distinct from being-for-itself (one's own first-personal existence) and from being-in-itself (the objective, material body). Being-for-others is experienced as the alienation of one's freedom: one's "ego" as seen from the outside is a kind of in-itself — a fixed character — that one is and yet can never be, since one is always for-oneself the nothingness of free projection.

## Thought Experiments

**The Waiter**
Sartre describes a waiter whose gestures are "a little too precise, a little too rapid" — who has become his role so thoroughly that his freedom has been absorbed into the character of "waiter." The waiter is in bad faith: he treats himself as a thing whose being is to be a waiter, denying the transcendence that makes him free to quit. The thought experiment reveals the structure of bad faith as the denial of transcendence: the waiter acts as if he were an in-itself with a fixed nature rather than a for-itself who is always free to be otherwise.

**The Woman on a Date**
Sartre describes a woman on a date who knows her companion intends a seduction but pretends not to notice. When he takes her hand, she "leaves it" — neither withdrawing it (acknowledging the situation) nor responding to it (accepting it). She treats her hand as a thing, dissociating from her own embodied freedom. This bad faith consists in failing to own one's transcendence — denying that one is the kind of being who must choose. The case illustrates bad faith as the flight from freedom under the pressure of social situations.

**Caught in the Keyhole**
Sartre describes being caught by another while looking through a keyhole: absorbed in curiosity (a mode of being in which there is no self, only the object), one suddenly hears a footstep and realizes one is being watched. In that instant, shame arises — not from the content of what one was doing, but from the pre-reflective awareness of being an object for another. This thought experiment reveals the structure of the look: the Other's gaze does not merely add a new fact to one's situation but transforms one's ontological mode of being from for-itself to for-others.
