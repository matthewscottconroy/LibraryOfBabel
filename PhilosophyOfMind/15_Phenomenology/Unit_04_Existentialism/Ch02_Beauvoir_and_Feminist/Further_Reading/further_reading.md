# Further Reading: Beauvoir and Feminist Phenomenology

## Primary Sources

**Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex* (1949, trans. Constance Borde and Sheila Malovany-Chevallier 2010)** — De Beauvoir's founding text of feminist philosophy argues that woman is not born but made—that femininity is a social construction that situates women as "Other" to man's self-defined transcendence. Drawing on existentialism, psychoanalysis, and Marxism, she analyzes how economic dependence, embodied experience, and cultural mythology conspire to deprive women of authentic freedom. The 2010 translation restores many passages omitted from the earlier Parshley translation.

**Iris Marion Young, "Throwing Like a Girl: A Phenomenology of Feminine Body Comportment, Motility, and Spatiality" (1980), *Human Studies*** — Young's landmark essay applies Merleau-Ponty's body phenomenology to the analysis of gendered embodiment, arguing that feminine motor comportment is characterized by an "ambiguous transcendence"—partial inhibition and hesitancy—that reflects the social positioning of women as objects of the gaze. The essay established embodied feminism as a major philosophical approach.

**Frantz Fanon, *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952)** — Fanon's analysis of racialized embodiment uses Merleau-Ponty's body schema to analyze how racism disrupts the Black person's bodily self-experience. The "epidermal racial schema" imposes an alien layer on the body schema, producing what Fanon calls "historic-racial schema"—an experience of one's body as perpetually visible, fixed, and overdetermined by racial categories.

**Susan Wendell, *The Rejected Body: Feminist Philosophical Reflections on Disability* (1996)** — Wendell develops a feminist phenomenology of disability, arguing that the social model of disability—which attributes disabled people's disadvantages entirely to social barriers—ignores the genuine bodily suffering that disability involves. Her account of the "rejected body" analyzes how illness and disability are experienced as a betrayal of normal bodily self-experience.

**Linda Martín Alcoff, *Visible Identities: Race, Gender, and the Self* (2006)** — Develops a phenomenological account of how racial and gender identities are lived and experienced, drawing on Merleau-Ponty, Heidegger, and Fanon. Alcoff argues for a "positive" realism about identity categories that neither essentializes nor deflates them.

## Secondary Literature

**Elizabeth Grosz, *Volatile Bodies: Toward a Corporeal Feminism* (1994)** — Develops a feminist theory of embodiment that draws on both phenomenology (Merleau-Ponty) and poststructuralism (Deleuze), arguing for a "corporeal feminism" that takes the specificity of female embodiment seriously without reducing it to biology.

**Sonia Kruks, *Retrieving Experience: Subjectivity and Recognition in Feminist Politics* (2001)** — Develops and defends de Beauvoir's phenomenological feminism against poststructuralist criticisms, arguing that the concept of lived experience remains essential for feminist theory.

**Rosalyn Diprose, *The Bodies of Women: Ethics, Embodiment and Sexual Difference* (1994)** — A phenomenological feminist treatment of sexual difference, bodily generosity, and the ethics of giving. Draws on both de Beauvoir and Levinas to articulate an ethics of embodied alterity.

**Shannon Sullivan, *Living Across and Through Skins: Transactional Bodies, Pragmatism, and Feminism* (2001)** — Develops a Dewey-influenced account of racialized and gendered embodiment that complements phenomenological approaches, focusing on the transactional character of bodily habits across persons and environments.

**Sara Ahmed, *Queer Phenomenology: Orientations, Objects, Others* (2006)** — Applies phenomenological concepts—especially Husserlian orientation, Merleau-Ponty's body, and Heidegger's dwelling—to queer theory, arguing that sexual orientation is a matter of how bodies are oriented in space and toward certain objects. An innovative and influential synthesis.
