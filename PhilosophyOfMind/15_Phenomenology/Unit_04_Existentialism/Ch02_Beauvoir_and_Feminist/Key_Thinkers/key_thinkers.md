# Key Thinkers: Beauvoir and Feminist Phenomenology

---

**Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986)**
French philosopher, novelist, and feminist. De Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) is the founding text of contemporary feminist philosophy, applying existentialist concepts to analyze women's oppression. Her central claim—"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman"—identifies femininity as an imposition rather than a natural essence, achieved through socialization that systematically denies women the transcendence that existentialism identifies as constitutive of authentic human existence. Her analysis of woman as "the Other"—constituted as immanence, matter, and passivity against the masculine norm of activity and freedom—remains foundational for feminist theory.
*Key works:* *The Second Sex* (1949), *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947), *Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter* (1958)

---

**Iris Marion Young (1949–2006)**
American feminist philosopher and political theorist at the University of Chicago. Young's "Throwing Like a Girl" (1980) transformed feminist philosophy by applying Merleau-Ponty's body phenomenology to the analysis of gendered motor comportment. She argued that women's characteristic bodily hesitancy and circumscribed use of space reflect not biological difference but the social experience of being positioned as an object of the gaze. Her subsequent work on feminist politics, spatial justice, and the "politics of difference" extended these phenomenological insights into social and political philosophy.
*Key works:* "Throwing Like a Girl" (1980), *Justice and the Politics of Difference* (1990), *On Female Body Experience* (2005)

---

**Frantz Fanon (1925–1961)**
Martinican psychiatrist and revolutionary theorist. Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) applies Merleau-Ponty's body schema to the phenomenology of race, arguing that colonialism and racism impose a "historic-racial schema" that disrupts the Black person's spontaneous bodily engagement with the world, forcing a hyperawareness of the racially marked body. His analysis of how the white gaze fixes and overdetermines Black experience—making the body an object of stereotypes rather than a vehicle of action—remains the foundational text for critical race phenomenology.
*Key works:* *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961)

---

**Linda Martín Alcoff (1955–)**
Panamanian-American philosopher at the CUNY Graduate Center. Alcoff has developed a phenomenological account of race and gender as lived identities—neither fictional constructs nor biological essences but real features of the social world that are experienced in bodily perception and everyday practice. Drawing on Merleau-Ponty, Heidegger, and Fanon, she argues for a "positive" philosophical realism about social identities that acknowledges their construction without reducing them to mere social conventions.
*Key works:* *Visible Identities* (2006), *Rape and Resistance* (2018)

---

**Sara Ahmed (1969–)**
British feminist philosopher and queer theorist at Kingston University. Ahmed's *Queer Phenomenology* (2006) applies phenomenological concepts of orientation, habituation, and dwelling to queer experience, arguing that sexual orientation is fundamentally a matter of how bodies orient themselves in space—toward certain objects, away from others. Her work extends body phenomenology into new domains while also critically examining how phenomenological categories are themselves inflected by normative assumptions about gender, race, and sexuality.
*Key works:* *Queer Phenomenology* (2006), *The Cultural Politics of Emotion* (2004), *Willful Subjects* (2014)

---

**Susan Wendell (1947–)**
Canadian philosopher at Simon Fraser University. Wendell developed a feminist phenomenology of illness and disability, arguing that mainstream society and philosophy systematically "reject" bodies that do not conform to norms of productivity and able-bodiedness. Her account of the lived experience of chronic illness—the way it disrupts the body schema and forces a confrontation with the body as a resistant, suffering presence—provides important resources for disability studies and medical ethics.
*Key works:* *The Rejected Body* (1996), "Toward a Feminist Theory of Disability" (1989)
