# Unit Introduction: Philosophy of Neuroscience

## Central Questions

Philosophy of neuroscience examines the conceptual foundations, methodological assumptions, and theoretical implications of neuroscience as a scientific discipline. It does not merely apply philosophical tools to neuroscientific findings; it interrogates what neuroscientific explanation consists in, what the relationship is between neural descriptions and psychological descriptions, and what the neural findings actually tell us about the nature of mind.

Three central questions organize this unit. First, the *levels* question: neuroscience describes the brain at multiple levels — the molecular, the cellular, the circuit, the systems, and the behavioral — and it is not always clear how these levels relate to each other. David Marr's three-levels framework provides one influential account; the *multiple realizability* and *mechanistic explanation* frameworks provide others. How we understand the levels structure of neuroscience bears on questions about reduction and emergence in the science of mind.

Second, the *neural correlates* question: neuroscience has made remarkable progress in identifying neural correlates of mental phenomena — the neural activity that is reliably associated with specific mental states. But what does a neural correlate *tell us*? Correlation is not identity, and identifying the neural correlate of a mental state does not automatically explain the mental state. How NCC research bears on the mind-body problem — whether it supports identity theory, functionalism, or merely maps the physical accompaniments of the mental — is a conceptual question that neuroscience alone cannot answer.

Third, the *explanation* question: what counts as a neuroscientific explanation of a mental phenomenon? Is it sufficient to identify the neural mechanism? Or does a full explanation require connecting the neural description to a functional or computational description, and then to a psychological or phenomenological description? The question of what explanatory adequacy requires for neuroscience connects to debates about reduction, multiple levels, and the relationship between neuroscience and psychology.

## Major Positions and Debates

### Marr's Three Levels

David Marr's *Vision* (1982) proposed that any information-processing system must be understood at three levels: the *computational level* (what the system does and why — the problem it solves, specified in terms of its inputs and outputs); the *algorithmic level* (how it does it — the procedure or algorithm it implements); and the *implementational level* (the physical substrate in which the algorithm is realized). The three levels are relatively independent: the same computation can be implemented by many algorithms; the same algorithm can be implemented in many physical substrates.

Marr's framework has been enormously influential but is also contested. The cleanest objection is that the three levels are not as independent as Marr suggests: in neural systems, computational constraints often determine algorithmic features, and algorithmic features constrain implementation. The framework also does not cleanly apply to phenomena like consciousness, where the relevant computation may not be characterizable independently of its phenomenal properties.

Philosophers of neuroscience have developed alternative frameworks. The *mechanistic* explanation model (Machamer, Darden, and Craver) holds that neuroscientific explanation consists in identifying mechanisms — organized systems of entities and activities that produce the phenomenon to be explained. Mechanisms are not described purely computationally but in terms of the real entities (neurons, synapses, circuits) and their activities (firing, releasing neurotransmitter, activating).

### Neural Correlates of Consciousness (NCC)

The NCC research program, launched by Crick and Koch in 1990, seeks to identify the minimal neural conditions sufficient for a given conscious state. The search for NCCs has produced an extensive literature on the neural basis of visual awareness, attention, binocular rivalry, and other phenomena.

The conceptual status of NCCs is disputed. Francis Crick and Christof Koch defined an NCC as the minimal set of neuronal events jointly sufficient for a specific conscious percept. But *sufficient* under what conditions? If we hold behavior constant, the NCC is the neural activity that is sufficient for consciousness given normal behavioral context. If we do not hold other neural activity constant, the NCC might just be the neural activity correlated with the conscious state, without being sufficient in any strong sense.

More fundamentally: what does identifying an NCC tell us about consciousness? Correlation is not identity; correlation is not explanation. Finding that V5 activity correlates with motion perception does not show that V5 activity *is* motion perception, or that it *explains* why motion is experienced as it is. The hard problem (Unit 03) is not touched by NCC research unless we supplement it with a theory of why neural activity should give rise to phenomenal experience.

Koch's later work with Tononi on *integrated information theory* (IIT) is an attempt to go beyond NCC identification to a theory of why certain kinds of neural organization give rise to consciousness. IIT holds that consciousness is identical to integrated information (Φ); identifying the NCC is identifying the region of the brain with the highest Φ in a given state. Whether IIT is correct is disputed, but it represents the most developed attempt to connect neuroscience to a theory of consciousness.

### Reduction and Emergence in Neuroscience

The relationship between neuroscience and psychology raises questions about *intertheoretic reduction*. Can psychology be reduced to neuroscience? The reductionist holds that psychological laws and explanations can be derived from, and in principle replaced by, neuroscientific explanations. The non-reductionist holds that psychological explanations pick out patterns (functional patterns, computational patterns) that are not captured by any finite neuroscientific description.

Jaegwon Kim's arguments (Unit 02) have implications here: if mental properties are multiply realizable, they cannot be reduced to neural properties, and Kim's exclusion argument threatens the causal efficacy of psychological properties. The *mechanistic* account of reduction proposes an alternative: psychological phenomena are reduced to neuroscientific mechanisms not by derivation from lower-level laws but by showing that the mechanism underlying the psychological phenomenon is constituted by neural entities and activities.

Emergence is the flip side of reduction: properties that are not reducible to lower-level properties are *emergent*. Strong emergence — where emergent properties genuinely transcend the underlying physical properties — is usually regarded as philosophically suspicious; weak emergence — where emergent properties supervene on lower-level properties — is compatible with physicalism. Whether consciousness is strongly or weakly emergent from neural activity is a central question.

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**David Marr** (*Vision*, 1982) provided the three-levels framework and demonstrated what a multi-level analysis of a neural system looks like in practice. His account of visual processing — from the computational level (what the visual system computes) through the algorithmic level (how it computes it) to the implementational level (what neural machinery carries out the computation) — is a model of integrative neuroscience.

**Carl Craver** (*Explaining the Brain*, 2007) developed the mechanistic explanation framework and applied it systematically to neuroscience. Craver's account of constitutive explanation (explaining a phenomenon by identifying the mechanism that underlies it) clarifies the relationship between neuroscientific and psychological explanation.

**Francis Crick and Christof Koch** launched the NCC research program and identified the claustrum and various areas of the visual cortex as candidate NCCs. Koch's *The Feeling of Life Itself* (2019) provides the most accessible overview of the IIT account of consciousness and its neuroscientific implications.

**Patricia Churchland** (*Neurophilosophy*, 1986) argued for a thoroughgoing naturalism in philosophy of mind, holding that philosophical questions about the mind should be informed by (and potentially dissolved by) neuroscientific findings. Churchland's work has been enormously influential in establishing the field of philosophy of neuroscience.

**Ned Block** has argued persistently that the NCC program faces conceptual difficulties — in particular, that it faces the *cognitive/phenomenal* confusion: experiments designed to isolate NCCs often confound phenomenal consciousness with the cognitive access to it.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Marr, *Vision: A Computational Investigation into the Human Representation and Processing of Visual Information* (1982)**
A foundational text in both computational neuroscience and philosophy of neuroscience. The three-levels framework, developed through a detailed computational account of vision, is the starting point for thinking about levels of explanation in neuroscience.

**2. Craver, *Explaining the Brain: Mechanisms and the Mosaic Unity of Neuroscience* (2007)**
The most philosophically careful account of mechanistic explanation in neuroscience. Craver's distinction between explaining by mechanism (constitutive explanation) and explaining by laws (covering law explanation) clarifies the structure of neuroscientific explanation and its relationship to reduction.

**3. Churchland, *Neurophilosophy: Toward a Unified Science of the Mind-Brain* (1986)**
The founding document of neurophilosophy. Churchland argues for a thoroughgoing integration of philosophy and neuroscience, holding that traditional philosophical questions about mind and knowledge can and should be informed by what neuroscience tells us.

**4. Koch, *The Feeling of Life Itself: Why Consciousness Is Widespread but Can't Be Computed* (2019)**
Koch's mature statement of his views on consciousness, NCC research, and IIT. The book is accessible, covers the major theories and evidence, and is frank about the limits of current knowledge.

**5. Block, "Consciousness, Accessibility, and the Mesh Between Psychology and Neuroscience" (2007)**
Block's argument that neuroscience conflates phenomenal consciousness with cognitive access. Available in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 30(5); the target article and the extensive commentaries are essential for understanding the methodological challenges of consciousness neuroscience.

## Open Questions

**1. Does the NCC program make progress on the hard problem?**
The NCC program identifies neural correlates of consciousness. But even a complete NCC map — knowing which neural activity is necessary and sufficient for every type of conscious experience — would not explain why those neural states are conscious. The hard problem asks why any physical process should be accompanied by experience; NCC research tells us which processes are, not why they are. Whether there is a form of neuroscientific explanation that could address the hard problem is deeply unclear.

**2. Is Marr's three-levels framework still adequate?**
Marr's framework was developed for the visual system, which processes relatively structured inputs according to well-characterized computations. For less structured cognitive systems — reasoning, planning, social cognition — the computational level is harder to specify. Whether Marr's framework extends to the whole brain, and whether the three levels remain as independent as Marr suggested, is contested.

**3. Can psychiatric conditions be fully naturalized?**
The Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) program aims to characterize psychiatric conditions in terms of neural systems and their dysfunctions rather than symptom clusters. But psychiatric conditions are also social, cultural, and interpersonal phenomena. Whether a purely neurobiological account of mental illness is possible, or whether it always requires a broader social and psychological context, is a major open question in philosophy of psychiatry.

**4. What does the connectome tell us about cognition?**
Connectomics — the systematic mapping of neural connections — is one of the major research programs of contemporary neuroscience. The complete connectome of *C. elegans* (302 neurons, ~7,000 connections) has been known since 1986 but has not generated a full understanding of the worm's behavior. Whether mapping the human connectome will unlock the neural basis of cognition, or whether cognition requires information about activity, dynamics, and plasticity that the connectome does not provide, is an open question.

**5. How should we interpret null results in neuroimaging?**
fMRI studies routinely fail to find activity differences between experimental conditions. These null results are typically unreported (publication bias). But null results can be informative: if a task requires neural computation, finding no fMRI activity difference might show that the task is performed by small local circuits below fMRI resolution, or that the computation is distributed and the experimental contrast is not isolating the relevant activity. How to interpret neuroimaging evidence — including null results — requires philosophical attention that the field has not yet fully given it.
