# Further Reading: Reduction and Levels of Explanation

## Primary Sources

**Peter Machamer, Lindley Darden, and Carl Craver, "Thinking About Mechanisms" (2000), *Philosophy of Science*** — The foundational paper for the new mechanist philosophy of science, arguing that scientific explanation in the life sciences consists in describing mechanisms—organized entities and activities that produce phenomena. Their account of how mechanisms are organized hierarchically in levels has become the standard framework for understanding levels of explanation in neuroscience and cognitive science.

**David Marr, *Vision: A Computational Investigation into the Human Representation and Processing of Visual Information* (1982)** — Marr's framework distinguishing three levels of analysis—computational (what is computed and why), algorithmic (how it is computed), and implementational (how the algorithm is physically realized)—has been enormously influential in cognitive science. His argument that each level must be understood in its own terms, and that computational explanation is not reducible to neural implementation, provides a model for explanatory pluralism in cognitive science.

**Carl Craver, *Explaining the Brain: Mechanisms and the Mosaic Unity of Neuroscience* (2007)** — A rigorous philosophical treatment of mechanistic explanation in neuroscience, developing the MDC framework into a full-scale philosophy of neuroscience. Craver examines how mechanisms are discovered, how they explain at multiple levels, and how explanation in neuroscience differs from covering-law explanations in physics.

**Patricia Churchland, *Neurophilosophy: Toward a Unified Science of the Mind-Brain* (1986)** — A foundational text in philosophy of neuroscience, arguing that genuine understanding of the mind requires co-evolutionary development of philosophy and neuroscience. Churchland defends a reductionist program—ultimately psychology should be reduced to neuroscience—while acknowledging the methodological complexity of this reduction.

**Jaegwon Kim, *Mind in a Physical World* (1998)** — Kim's arguments for causal exclusion—that if mental properties are causally efficacious they must be reducible to physical properties or be causally inert—provide the sharpest philosophical argument for why reduction in cognitive science is a genuine issue.

## Secondary Literature

**Kenneth Schaffner, *Discovery and Explanation in Biology and Medicine* (1993)** — An important treatment of reductionism and levels of explanation in biology and medicine, engaging with how Nagel-style reduction applies and fails in life sciences.

**William Bechtel and Robert Richardson, *Discovering Complexity: Decomposition and Localization as Strategies in Scientific Research* (1993)** — Examines how scientists discover mechanisms by decomposing complex phenomena into component parts and their interactions. Essential for understanding the methodology of mechanistic neuroscience.

**Robert McCauley and William Bechtel, "Explanatory Pluralism and Heuristic Identity Theory" (2001), *Theory and Psychology*** — Defends explanatory pluralism as consistent with ontological monism: different levels of explanation can all be genuine without any being eliminable.

**Evan Thompson, *Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind* (2007)** — Argues for an enactivist alternative to reductionist neuroscience, connecting phenomenological levels of analysis to biological and cognitive levels through the concept of autopoiesis.

**Max Coltheart, "What Has Functional Neuroimaging Told Us About the Mind (So Far)?" (2006), *Cortex*** — A critical assessment of what neuroimaging findings actually establish about psychological processes, arguing for more caution about inferring cognitive function from neural activation patterns.
