# Key Thinkers: Reduction and Levels of Explanation

---

**David Marr (1945–1980)**
British neuroscientist and psychologist, cut short by leukemia at 35. Marr's three-level framework for understanding information-processing systems—computational, algorithmic, and implementational—became one of the most influential methodological proposals in cognitive science. His argument that each level has its own explanatory autonomy, and that the computational level asks what problem is being solved before asking how, grounds a principled form of multiple realization and supports explanatory pluralism. His *Vision* (1982) demonstrates the framework in practice, analyzing visual processing from computational principles through algorithms to neural implementation.
*Key works:* *Vision* (1982)

---

**Peter Machamer (1942–2016), Lindley Darden (1945–), and Carl Craver (1967–)**
American philosophers of science. Machamer, Darden, and Craver's "new mechanism" paper (2000) established mechanistic explanation as the dominant paradigm in philosophy of biology and neuroscience. Their framework defines mechanisms as organized entities and activities that produce phenomena through their organization, and argues that scientific explanation consists in discovering and describing such mechanisms. This account supports a levels-based, non-reductive approach to explanation in the life sciences that is neither eliminativist nor vitalist.
*Key works:* "Thinking About Mechanisms" (2000); Craver, *Explaining the Brain* (2007)

---

**Carl Craver (1967–)**
American philosopher of science at Washington University in St. Louis. Craver has developed the new mechanist framework most comprehensively for neuroscience, arguing that explanation in neuroscience consists in multi-level mechanistic descriptions where higher-level phenomena are explained by the activities and organization of lower-level components. His work has also addressed the nature of levels themselves, arguing that constitutive levels are not spatial or compositional but are defined by mechanistic relevance relations.
*Key works:* *Explaining the Brain* (2007), "Mechanisms and Natural Kinds" (2009)

---

**Patricia Churchland (1943–)**
Canadian-American philosopher and neuroscientist at UC San Diego. Churchland's *Neurophilosophy* (1986) launched the field by arguing that genuine philosophy of mind must be informed by neuroscience, and that co-evolutionary development of neuroscience and philosophy can solve mind-body problems. She has been a consistent defender of reductionism—or at least of a "smoothed and revised" reduction—arguing that eventually psychological categories should be replaced by or reduced to neural ones as neuroscience matures.
*Key works:* *Neurophilosophy* (1986), *Brain-Wise* (2002), *Touching a Nerve* (2013)

---

**Jaegwon Kim (1934–2019)**
Korean-American philosopher of mind at Brown University. Kim's causal exclusion argument poses the sharpest challenge for anyone who wants to preserve both the reality of mental causation and the causal closure of physics. If every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and mental events are not identical to physical events, then mental events are causally excluded—a conclusion that threatens non-reductive physicalism and supports either elimination or reduction of mental to physical. His arguments have forced contemporary philosophers of mind to confront the relationship between mental causation and reductionism.
*Key works:* *Supervenience and Mind* (1993), *Mind in a Physical World* (1998), *Physicalism, or Something Near Enough* (2005)
