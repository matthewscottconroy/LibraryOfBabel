# Neuroscience and Folk Psychology

One of the most consequential and contested questions in the philosophy of neuroscience concerns the relationship between neuroscience and folk psychology — the commonsense framework of beliefs, desires, intentions, emotions, and other mental states through which ordinary people explain behavior. Does neuroscience support folk psychology, threaten to replace it, or is the relationship more complex? The stakes are high: the answer determines whether the mental states we care most deeply about — our beliefs, preferences, intentions — have any place in a scientifically adequate picture of the mind.

**Folk Psychology as Explanatory Framework**

Folk psychology — sometimes called "the intentional stance" (Dennett) or "belief-desire psychology" — is the commonsense explanatory framework that attributes beliefs, desires, intentions, emotions, and other mental states to people in order to explain and predict their behavior. We explain why Maria ran for the bus by saying she wanted to catch it and believed it would leave in a minute. We predict that John will reach for the salt by knowing that he wants more flavor and believes the salt shaker is nearby.

This framework is extraordinarily powerful. It enables coordination, communication, and social life. It applies across vast differences in underlying biology — we use the same framework to explain the behavior of people from different cultures, with different neurological profiles, across different developmental stages. Its explanatory vocabulary is flexible, compositional, and apparently universal.

The philosophical question is what the relationship is between this framework and the neural processes that actually govern behavior. Three broad positions can be distinguished.

**The Vindication View**

On the vindication view, folk psychology is essentially correct, and neuroscience will ultimately vindicate it by identifying the neural mechanisms that correspond to beliefs, desires, and other folk psychological states. The basic categories of folk psychology carve nature at its joints — they pick out real patterns in human cognition — and neuroscience's job is to fill in the neural details.

Defenders of this view (Fodor, many "common sense" realists) point to the predictive and explanatory success of folk psychology as evidence for its accuracy. The patterns it identifies — systematic relationships between mental states and behavior — are genuine patterns in nature. The fact that these patterns may be multiply realizable, that they may not correspond to specific neural types, does not undermine their reality; it just means that psychological patterns are at a different level from neural patterns.

**The Revision View**

A more moderate position holds that folk psychology will need revision in light of neuroscience, but not wholesale elimination. The basic idea that behavior is influenced by information-bearing states that represent the world (something like beliefs), and states that represent desirable outcomes (something like desires), is likely to survive — but the specific folk categories may need refinement.

Neuroscience already suggests that folk psychology oversimplifies. The unified belief-desire unit of folk psychology does not map cleanly onto any single neural system. "Memory" in folk psychology is actually implemented by multiple, dissociable systems. "Decision making" involves parallel computations in multiple brain systems that can conflict with each other. "Attention" refers to a family of distinct, only partially overlapping processes. Neuroscience prompts us to revise the boundaries and individuation of folk psychological kinds, not to abandon the enterprise.

**The Neurolaw and Applied Dimensions**

The relationship between neuroscience and folk psychology has immediate practical implications in law, medicine, and ethics. Legal systems attribute responsibility on the basis of folk psychological concepts — the defendant acted with intent, had the capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of the action, was unable to control their behavior. Neuroscientific evidence about brain damage, addiction, impulse control disorders, and developmental abnormalities is increasingly introduced in legal proceedings.

How should folk psychological concepts of responsibility, intention, and control interact with neuroscientific findings? If neuroscience shows that a defendant's prefrontal cortex was significantly abnormal, does this bear on their responsibility for their actions? The answer depends on contested philosophical questions about the relationship between neural abnormality, folk psychological incapacity, and moral responsibility. Neurolaw — the emerging field at the intersection of neuroscience and law — is grappling with these questions with practical urgency.

The general lesson is that neuroscience neither straightforwardly confirms nor simply replaces folk psychology. The relationship is one of interaction, revision, and mutual constraint — a long-term project of working out how the folk categories and the neural facts fit together, and how both should be revised in light of the other.
