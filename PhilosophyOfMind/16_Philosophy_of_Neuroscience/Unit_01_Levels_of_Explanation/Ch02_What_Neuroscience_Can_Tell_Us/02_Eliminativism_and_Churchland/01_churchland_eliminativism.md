# The Churchlands and Eliminative Materialism

Paul and Patricia Churchland represent the most radical and sustained philosophical engagement with neuroscience's implications for our conception of mind. Their eliminative materialism — the view that folk psychology is a fundamentally false theory that will eventually be eliminated and replaced by a mature neuroscience — is the most provocative position in the philosophy of neuroscience. Whether or not one is persuaded, engaging with their arguments is essential for understanding what is at stake in the debate about mind and brain.

**Folk Psychology as a Theory**

The Churchlands' starting move is to insist that folk psychology is a theory — not a set of observed facts but an explanatory framework that makes theoretical claims about the causal structure of human behavior. Like any theory, folk psychology can be evaluated for its truth, its predictive success, its theoretical virtues, and its prospects for successful integration with the rest of science.

On this evaluation, Paul Churchland argues, folk psychology does not fare well. It is essentially unchanged from ancient frameworks of common sense. It has made little progress in explaining the phenomena it purports to explain — the mechanisms of memory, learning, sleep, mental illness, creativity, and development remain opaque to folk psychological analysis. It has failed to achieve productive integration with biology, chemistry, and physics. And it faces a theoretical rival — neuroscience — that promises to explain behavior from the bottom up in terms of neural processes.

The proper historical analogy, Churchland suggests, is not the reduction of chemistry to physics but the elimination of phlogiston from chemistry, the elimination of vital force from biology, the elimination of caloric from thermodynamics. These were not cases where the old theory was shown to be approximately true and was reduced to the new framework; they were cases where the old categories failed to pick out real natural kinds and were abandoned. Churchland argues that beliefs and desires may be more like phlogiston than like temperature — categories that will be abandoned rather than reduced when neuroscience matures.

**Patricia Churchland's Neurophilosophy**

Patricia Churchland's *Neurophilosophy* (1986) is a foundational text of the field. Her project is to bring philosophy and neuroscience into genuine dialogue — not to apply philosophical categories to neuroscientific findings, but to allow neuroscientific findings to inform and transform philosophical questions about the mind.

Her approach challenges the "armchair" methodology of much analytic philosophy of mind: arguments developed independently of empirical neuroscience, relying on conceptual analysis and thought experiments. Churchland argues that philosophical claims about consciousness, mental representation, knowledge, and rationality are empirical hypotheses, not conceptual necessities, and they should be evaluated against the empirical evidence. Neuroscience has already revealed that many philosophical intuitions about the unity of consciousness, the reliability of introspection, and the architecture of cognition are simply incorrect.

Patricia Churchland has also been a proponent of the view that consciousness will eventually be explained in neural terms. She is skeptical of the hard problem as Chalmers formulates it — the claim that there is something that purely physical explanation will necessarily miss — arguing that this intuition may reflect our current ignorance rather than a genuine explanatory gap. Just as "vital force" seemed necessary before biochemistry showed how living processes are possible, the "explanatory gap" between neural processes and conscious experience may close as neuroscience matures.

**Responses to Eliminativism**

Eliminative materialism has provoked extensive philosophical response.

The "self-refutation" objection notes that eliminativism is itself a set of beliefs (about folk psychology, about neuroscience) — but if beliefs do not exist, the eliminativist cannot coherently hold eliminativism. The Churchlands respond that this objection assumes what is at issue: whether the states involved in accepting or rejecting theories are best described as "beliefs" in the folk psychological sense, or whether a better vocabulary will eventually supersede this one.

Fodor and others have argued that folk psychology's predictive success is strong evidence for its accuracy: a systematically false theory cannot explain systematic predictive success. The Churchlands respond that predictive success establishes that there is some real pattern being tracked, not that folk categories accurately describe the underlying mechanism — Newtonian mechanics predicted many things accurately while being fundamentally false.

Phenomenological critics argue that the Churchlands underestimate what needs to be explained: even if they are right that folk categories are not accurately describing neural mechanisms, the phenomenal character of conscious experience — what it is like to believe, to see, to feel — is not something that can be eliminated without remainder. Whether a more complete neuroscience will vindicate or undermine this phenomenological objection remains an open question.
