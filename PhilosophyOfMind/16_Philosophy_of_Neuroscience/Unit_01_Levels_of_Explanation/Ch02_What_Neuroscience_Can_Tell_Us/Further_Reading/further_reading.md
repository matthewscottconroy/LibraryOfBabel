# Further Reading: What Neuroscience Can Tell Us

## Primary Sources

**Paul Churchland, *Scientific Realism and the Plasticity of Mind* (1979)** — Churchland's early argument for eliminative materialism about folk psychology, holding that the ordinary psychology of beliefs and desires is a folk theory that will be superseded by mature neuroscience. He argues that folk psychological categories—belief, desire, intention—may not correspond to natural kinds that feature in a completed neuroscience.

**Patricia Churchland, *Neurophilosophy: Toward a Unified Science of the Mind-Brain* (1986)** — The foundational work of philosophy of neuroscience, arguing for co-evolutionary development of philosophy and brain science. Churchland surveys the neuroscience of her time and argues that progress in understanding mind depends on abandoning the assumption that folk psychology provides a privileged access to mental kinds.

**Paul Churchland, *Matter and Consciousness* (3rd ed., 2013)** — A clear and accessible presentation of Churchland's neurophilosophy, including his arguments for eliminative materialism, his account of connectionist models, and his critique of folk psychology. Widely used as a teaching text.

**Benjamin Libet, "Unconscious Cerebral Initiative and the Role of Conscious Will in Voluntary Action" (1985), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*** — Libet's famous experiment reporting that the "readiness potential" in the brain precedes the conscious decision to act by several hundred milliseconds, raising questions about whether conscious will plays a causal role in action. This experiment has been enormously influential in neuroscience and philosophy of action, though its interpretation is contested.

**John-Dylan Haynes and Geraint Rees, "Decoding Mental States from Brain Activity in Humans" (2006), *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*** — Reviews the methods and findings of multivariate pattern analysis in neuroimaging, showing that mental states can be "decoded" from brain activity patterns. Raises philosophical questions about what such decoding shows about the relationship between mental and neural states.

## Secondary Literature

**Maxwell Bennett and Peter Hacker, *Philosophical Foundations of Neuroscience* (2003)** — A rigorous critique of what Bennett and Hacker call the "mereological fallacy" in neuroscience—the attribution of mental properties to brains or parts of brains rather than to persons. Their close attention to how neuroscientific language can mislead philosophical interpretation has been influential though also controversial.

**Eric Roskies, "Neuroscientific Challenges to Free Will and Responsibility" (2006), *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*** — A careful analysis of what neuroscientific findings—especially Libet's experiments—do and do not show about free will. Argues for more caution than popularizers typically display.

**Adina Roskies, "Are Neuroimages Like Photographs of the Brain?" (2007), *Philosophy of Science*** — Examines the epistemic status of neuroimages, arguing that they are not straightforwardly photographs of brain states but are theory-laden representations that require careful philosophical analysis.

**Scott Sagan, *What Neuroscience Cannot Tell Us About Ourselves* (2013)** — Argues against "neuroscientism"—the claim that neuroscience can answer all philosophical questions about mind—while acknowledging the genuine importance of neuroscientific findings.

**Nancey Murphy and Warren Brown, *Did My Neurons Make Me Do It? Philosophical and Neurobiological Perspectives on Moral Responsibility and Free Will* (2007)** — A balanced treatment of what neuroscience does and does not show about moral responsibility, written jointly by a philosopher and a neuroscientist.
