# Key Thinkers: What Neuroscience Can Tell Us

---

**Paul Churchland (1942–)**
Canadian philosopher at UC San Diego. Paul Churchland is the leading proponent of eliminative materialism—the view that folk psychology (the ordinary theory of beliefs, desires, and intentions) is a false theory that will be replaced by a mature neuroscience. He argues that just as phlogiston theory and caloric theory were eliminated rather than reduced when chemistry matured, folk psychological categories may not survive the development of neuroscience. His positive vision involves a "neurocomputational" cognitive architecture in which learning and cognition are understood in terms of connectionist networks rather than propositional attitudes.
*Key works:* *Scientific Realism and the Plasticity of Mind* (1979), *A Neurocomputational Perspective* (1989), *Matter and Consciousness* (1984)

---

**Patricia Churchland (1943–)**
Canadian-American philosopher at UC San Diego, Paul Churchland's collaborator and intellectual partner. Patricia Churchland's *Neurophilosophy* (1986) launched the interdisciplinary field of philosophy of neuroscience. While sympathetic to eliminativism about folk psychology, she has been more cautious than Paul Churchland about strong eliminativist claims, arguing instead for a "neurophilosophy" in which philosophical and neuroscientific questions are addressed jointly. Her work on the neuroscience of decision-making, moral psychology, and the self has influenced debates about free will and moral responsibility.
*Key works:* *Neurophilosophy* (1986), *Brain-Wise* (2002), *Touching a Nerve* (2013), *Conscience: The Origins of Moral Intuition* (2019)

---

**Benjamin Libet (1916–2007)**
American neurophysiologist at UC San Francisco. Libet's readiness potential experiment (1985) reported that unconscious brain activity precedes the conscious decision to act by several hundred milliseconds, apparently showing that the brain "decides" before the person consciously does. This finding has been interpreted as challenging libertarian free will and has generated enormous philosophical controversy about the relationship between neural causation, conscious awareness, and voluntary action. Many philosophers and neuroscientists have questioned the experiment's design and interpretation.
*Key works:* "Unconscious Cerebral Initiative and the Role of Conscious Will in Voluntary Action" (1985), *Mind Time: The Temporal Factor in Consciousness* (2004)

---

**Maxwell Bennett (1939–) and Peter Hacker (1939–)**
Australian neuroscientist (Bennett) and British philosopher (Hacker). Together they developed the most sustained critique of what they call the "mereological fallacy" in neuroscience—the attribution of psychological predicates (seeing, knowing, deciding) to the brain or its parts rather than to the whole organism. Their *Philosophical Foundations of Neuroscience* (2003) argues that much neuroscientific language is philosophically confused and that clarity requires insisting on the distinction between person-level and sub-personal-level descriptions.
*Key works:* *Philosophical Foundations of Neuroscience* (2003), *History of Cognitive Neuroscience* (2008)

---

**David Papineau (1947–)**
British philosopher of science at King's College London. Papineau is a prominent defender of philosophical naturalism—the view that philosophy should be continuous with and answerable to natural science. His argument for physicalism about mind rests on the causal closure of the physical and the reality of mental causation. He has also argued that consciousness does not pose a distinctive hard problem once we understand how concepts of consciousness work, making him a significant voice in debates about what neuroscience can and cannot tell us about the mind.
*Key works:* *Philosophical Naturalism* (1993), *Thinking About Consciousness* (2002)
