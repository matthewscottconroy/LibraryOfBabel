# Key Thinkers: Brain Systems

---

**Andy Clark (1957–)**
Scottish philosopher and cognitive scientist at the University of Edinburgh. Clark's predictive processing account—most fully developed in *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016)—holds that the brain is fundamentally a hierarchical prediction machine that continuously generates predictions of sensory input and uses prediction errors to update its models. This framework, which builds on Friston's free energy principle, unifies perception, action, and cognition within a single computational architecture. Clark argues that this view supports and extends his earlier extended mind thesis, since the brain's predictions are designed to be corrected by real-world engagement.
*Key works:* *Being There* (1997), *Supersizing the Mind* (2008), *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016)

---

**Karl Friston (1959–)**
British neuroscientist at University College London. Friston's free energy principle proposes that the brain—and indeed all living systems—resist entropy by minimizing the free energy (variational surprise) of their sensory inputs through perceptual inference and action. His mathematical framework provides the rigorous theoretical foundation for predictive processing and active inference as a universal principle of brain function. Friston's work has had enormous influence on computational neuroscience and has been embraced by philosophers of mind seeking a unifying framework.
*Key works:* "The Free-Energy Principle: A Unified Brain Theory?" (2010), "Active Inference and the Free Energy Principle" (2009)

---

**Marcus Raichle (1937–)**
American neuroscientist at Washington University in St. Louis. Raichle's identification of the default mode network (2001) revealed that the brain maintains organized activity during rest that is suppressed during task performance—a finding that transformed understanding of the brain's resting state. The DMN has been linked to self-referential processing, autobiographical memory, prospection, and social cognition, making it central to discussions of how the brain implements the sense of self and of temporal consciousness.
*Key works:* "A Default Mode of Brain Function" (2001), "The Brain's Default Mode Network" (2015)

---

**Alan Baddeley (1934–)**
British psychologist at the University of York. Baddeley's multi-component model of working memory—comprising the phonological loop, visuospatial sketchpad, and central executive—has been the dominant framework for understanding how information is temporarily held and manipulated in the service of cognition. His later addition of the "episodic buffer" connected working memory to long-term episodic memory. His work has been fundamental for understanding prefrontal contributions to cognition and executive function.
*Key works:* *Working Memory* (1986), *Working Memory, Thought, and Action* (2007)

---

**Jakob Hohwy (1969–)**
Danish philosopher at Monash University. Hohwy has provided the most philosophically rigorous development of the predictive processing framework, examining its implications for perception, agency, consciousness, and the self. He argues that the predictive brain model has radical implications for our understanding of perception as fundamentally inferential and for our understanding of how the brain constructs the experience of a unified self from probabilistic modeling. His engagement with both neuroscience and analytic philosophy makes his work the key philosophical bridge to the predictive processing literature.
*Key works:* *The Predictive Mind* (2013), "The Predictive Coding Account of Psychosis" (2013)
