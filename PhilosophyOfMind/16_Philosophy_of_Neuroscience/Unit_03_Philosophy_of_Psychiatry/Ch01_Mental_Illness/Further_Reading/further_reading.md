# Further Reading: Mental Illness

## Primary Sources

**Jerome Wakefield, "The Concept of Mental Disorder: On the Boundary Between Biological Facts and Social Values" (1992), *American Psychologist*** — Wakefield's influential "harmful dysfunction" analysis defines mental disorder as a condition that (a) involves a genuine biological dysfunction—a failure of a mechanism to perform the function for which it was naturally selected—and (b) causes harm to the individual. This analysis attempts to reconcile the naturalist demand for a biological basis with the normativist insistence on the role of values in disorder judgments.

**Christopher Boorse, "On the Distinction Between Disease and Illness" (1975), *Philosophy and Public Affairs*** — Boorse's biostatistical theory defines disease as a statistically subnormal functional ability relative to the species-typical design, independent of social value judgments. He distinguishes disease (a purely biological concept) from illness (disease plus suffering or inability to perform roles), arguing that medicine deals in both but that the basic disease concept is value-free.

**Rachel Cooper, *Classifying Madness: A Philosophical Examination of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders* (2005)** — A rigorous philosophical analysis of the conceptual foundations of the DSM, arguing that psychiatric classification is not straightforwardly empirical but involves normative and theoretical commitments that deserve philosophical scrutiny.

**Thomas Szasz, *The Myth of Mental Illness* (1961)** — Szasz's provocative argument that mental illnesses are not genuine diseases but "problems in living"—patterns of behavior that violate social norms—presented as medical conditions for political and social control purposes. Though widely criticized, his challenge forced clearer thinking about what distinguishes mental from physical illness.

**Peter Zachar and Rachel Cooper (eds.), *Psychiatry and the Human Condition* (2018)** — A collection of recent essays on the conceptual and philosophical issues in psychiatry, covering the nature of mental disorder, the validity of psychiatric categories, and the relationship between psychiatry and neuroscience.

## Secondary Literature

**Nick Haslam, "Essentialism and Its Discontents in Psychiatry: Mental Disorder Categories as Natural Kinds" (2014)** — Examines whether psychiatric diagnostic categories correspond to natural kinds, arguing for a moderate view that acknowledges the heterogeneity of most psychiatric conditions while preserving the importance of classification.

**Derek Bolton, *What Is Mental Disorder? An Essay in Philosophy, Science, and Values* (2008)** — A nuanced treatment arguing that mental disorder involves both biologically grounded failure and social-normative harm, broadly supporting Wakefield's analysis while engaging with the complexity of psychiatric nosology.

**Allan Horwitz and Jerome Wakefield, *The Loss of Sadness: How Psychiatry Transformed Normal Sorrow into Depressive Disorder* (2007)** — Argues that the DSM's symptom-based criteria for major depression fail to distinguish genuine disorder from normal sadness in response to life circumstances, resulting in systematic over-diagnosis.

**George Graham, *The Disordered Mind: An Introduction to Philosophy of Mind and Mental Illness* (2010)** — An accessible philosophical introduction to the nature of mental illness, covering debates about the concepts of disorder, rationality, and agency in relation to psychiatric conditions. Suitable for students.

**Dominic Murphy, *Psychiatry in the Scientific Image* (2006)** — Develops a naturalistic account of psychiatric disorder as involving abnormalities in the mechanisms underlying mental functioning, engaging with both the philosophy of science and the science of psychiatry.
