# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Mental Illness

## Key Arguments

**Wakefield's Harmful Dysfunction Analysis**
Jerome Wakefield argues that a mental disorder is a harmful dysfunction: "dysfunction" is defined as the failure of a mental mechanism to perform its biologically designed function (in the evolutionary sense), and "harmful" means causing harm as judged by current cultural norms. The analysis attempts to combine a naturalistic (biological dysfunction) element with a normative (harm) element, capturing both the medical and the social dimensions of mental disorder. Critics argue that the "designed function" criterion is unclear for complex psychological traits and that the naturalistic component underdoes too much normative work.

**Boorse's Biostatistical Theory**
Christopher Boorse argues for a purely naturalistic theory of disease and disorder: a condition is a disease if and only if it involves a statistically subnormal functioning of a biological mechanism relative to the reference class of age-matched members of the same species. On this value-free account, whether something is a disease is a purely empirical question about species-typical functioning; whether it is also an illness (bad for the patient) is a separate evaluative question. Critics argue that what counts as "statistically normal" for a species is itself value-laden and that the theory cannot explain why we care about diseases.

**The Categorical vs. Dimensional Debate (DSM)**
The DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual) uses a categorical model of mental disorder: you either have Major Depressive Disorder or you do not, based on meeting a threshold number of criteria. Critics (including Widiger, Trull, and dimensional researchers) argue that most psychopathology is better modeled dimensionally — along continua of severity and trait variation — than as discrete natural kinds. This debate has ontological implications (are mental disorders discrete kinds or points on a distribution?) and practical implications (diagnosis, treatment selection, research design).

**Szasz's Social Constructivism**
Thomas Szasz argues in The Myth of Mental Illness that mental illnesses are not genuine biological diseases but metaphors: problems in living, deviant behaviors, or social nonconformity that have been medicalized without adequate biomedical justification. The only genuine illnesses, on his view, are those with demonstrable biological lesions. While most psychiatrists reject Szasz's strong thesis, his critique has sharpened debates about the legitimacy of psychiatric diagnosis, the role of values in diagnosis, and the politics of medicalization.

**The Role of Values in Diagnosis**
Fulford and colleagues argue that values are inextricably embedded in psychiatric diagnosis — not only in the harm component (as Wakefield acknowledges) but in the very identification of dysfunction. What counts as a failure to function "properly" depends on value judgments about what kinds of function are important, normal, or desirable. This "values-based practice" approach acknowledges that psychiatric diagnosis involves clinical interpretation that cannot be reduced to the application of value-free biological criteria.

## Core Concepts

**Harmful Dysfunction Analysis**
Wakefield's harmful dysfunction (HD) analysis defines mental disorder as the joint presence of a harmful condition (causing harm as culturally judged) and a dysfunction (failure of a biological mechanism to perform its evolutionary function). The analysis is designed to exclude mere deviance (unusual but not dysfunctional behavior), pure value judgments (labeling nonconformity as disorder), and unharmful biological dysfunctions. Critics debate whether the evolutionary function criterion is scientifically tractable and whether the harm criterion does too little to distinguish disorder from mere misfortune.

**Biostatistical Theory of Disease (Boorse)**
Boorse's biostatistical theory (BST) defines disease as a statistically subnormal biological function relative to an appropriate reference class (age- and sex-matched members of the species). Health is species-typical functioning; disease is deviation from it. The BST is meant to be entirely value-free — what counts as disease is an empirical, statistical question. The theory faces objections from: (1) cases where species-typical functioning is harmful (e.g., aging), (2) cases where subnormal functioning by BST standards is not harmful (e.g., infertility in a post-reproductive individual), and (3) the argument that statistical norms are themselves constructed by value-laden choices about what to measure.

**The DSM Categorical Model**
The DSM (currently DSM-5-TR) organizes mental disorders into discrete diagnostic categories, each with specified criteria, symptom thresholds, and duration requirements. The categorical model is operationalized for clinical use and research consistency but faces empirical challenges: high comorbidity between categories, lack of sharp natural boundaries between disorders and normal variation, and poor correspondence between DSM categories and biological or genetic findings. The debate over categories vs. dimensions has driven proposals for dimensional alternatives like the HiTOP (Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology) model.

**Medicalization**
Medicalization is the process by which human conditions and problems come to be defined and treated as medical (or psychiatric) problems. Critics of medicalization (including Szasz, Conrad, and others) argue that psychiatric diagnosis can extend pathologizing to socially deviant behavior, political dissent, or normal variation, conferring the social authority of medicine on value judgments. Defenders argue that medicalization can also reduce stigma and enable access to effective treatment. The debate concerns the appropriate boundaries of psychiatric classification.

**Values-Based Practice**
Values-based practice (Fulford) is an approach to psychiatric decision-making that explicitly acknowledges the irreducible role of values — not only patients' values but clinicians' values and socially shared values — in all clinical encounters, including diagnosis. It is offered as a complement to evidence-based medicine: where evidence-based medicine identifies what is true about a condition, values-based practice identifies whose values and what values are implicated in a clinical decision. The approach acknowledges that diagnosis in psychiatry involves interpretation of meaning and significance, not just measurement of biological variables.

**Natural Kinds in Psychiatry**
The question of whether psychiatric categories correspond to natural kinds — real, mind-independent groupings of patients that share causal mechanisms and treatment responses — is a central issue in philosophy of psychiatry. Kendell and Jablensky argue for the "validity" of psychiatric diagnoses in terms of consistent predictive utility; Hacking argues that psychiatric categories are "interactive kinds" that affect the self-understanding and behavior of those diagnosed; and Haslanger distinguishes questions about the reality of the category from questions about its normative significance.

## Thought Experiments

**The Unhappy But Adaptive Individual**
Consider a person who is profoundly unhappy, chronically dissatisfied, and unable to form stable relationships, but whose psychological mechanisms are all functioning as they were designed to: they accurately perceive their environment, form correct beliefs, and behave in ways that reflect their values (which happen to produce unhappiness). Does this person have a mental disorder? Wakefield's analysis says no (no dysfunction); the harmful condition criterion says potentially yes (there is harm). The case probes the HD analysis's division of labor between dysfunction and harm.

**The Healthy Sadistic Surgeon**
Boorse's theory implies that a surgeon who derives great pleasure from cutting people — a functional state that, while morally problematic, does not involve subnormal biological functioning — does not have a disease by the BST. This result challenges the intuition that psychiatric diagnosis should track moral or social harm: the theory's commitment to value-freedom seems to exclude some cases intuitively felt to be pathological. The case is used to argue that purely naturalistic theories of disorder are inadequate.

**The Classification of Homosexuality**
The removal of homosexuality from the DSM in 1973, following political pressure and a review of the evidence, is a historical case that functions as a thought experiment about the value-ladenness of psychiatric classification. Before 1973, it was classified as a disorder; after 1973, it was not. Did homosexuality stop being a disorder in 1973, or did psychiatry correct an error? The case is used to argue that psychiatric diagnosis involves irreducibly normative judgments — about which kinds of psychological variation constitute dysfunction — that cannot be resolved by purely empirical means.
