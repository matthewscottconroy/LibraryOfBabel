# Key Thinkers: Mental Illness

---

**Jerome Wakefield (1947–)**
American social worker and philosopher at New York University. Wakefield's "harmful dysfunction" analysis of mental disorder has become the dominant philosophical account, attempting to combine biological naturalism (disorder involves genuine dysfunction of a naturally selected mechanism) with normativist recognition (the dysfunction must cause harm, as judged by social standards). His subsequent empirical work with Allan Horwitz showed that DSM diagnostic criteria for major depression fail to exclude normal sadness, generating systematic over-diagnosis. His conceptual work has been enormously influential in debates about the validity of psychiatric classification.
*Key works:* "The Concept of Mental Disorder" (1992), *The Loss of Sadness* (2007, with Horwitz)

---

**Christopher Boorse (1946–)**
American philosopher at the University of Delaware. Boorse's biostatistical theory holds that health and disease are value-free biological concepts defined by statistical deviation from species-typical functioning. A disease is a statistically subnormal functional ability—a deviation from what members of the species do well enough to contribute to survival and reproduction. This naturalist account has been the main target of normativist critics like Nordenfelt and Fulford, and has been revised and defended by Boorse through decades of philosophical debate.
*Key works:* "On the Distinction Between Disease and Illness" (1975), "A Rebuttal on Health" (1997), "Goals of Medicine" (various)

---

**Thomas Szasz (1920–2012)**
Hungarian-American psychiatrist at SUNY Upstate. Szasz argued in *The Myth of Mental Illness* (1961) that most psychiatric diagnoses are not genuine diseases—since there is no identifiable physical lesion or dysfunction—but rather judgments that someone's behavior or experience deviates from social norms. He contended that psychiatry functions as a tool of social control, using the medical model to pathologize non-conformity. While his most radical claims have been widely rejected, his challenge stimulated important clarifications about what distinguishes mental from physical illness.
*Key works:* *The Myth of Mental Illness* (1961), *The Manufacture of Madness* (1970)

---

**Rachel Cooper (1975–)**
British philosopher of science at Lancaster University, specializing in philosophy of psychiatry. Cooper's work on psychiatric classification examines the conceptual foundations of the DSM and ICD, arguing that psychiatric categories are not straightforwardly natural kinds but are partly constructed by the diagnostic systems themselves. Her historical and philosophical analyses of how psychiatric categories have changed and been contested illuminate the complex interplay between theory, evidence, and social values in psychiatric nosology.
*Key works:* *Classifying Madness* (2005), *Psychiatry and Philosophy of Science* (2007)

---

**Bill (K.W.M.) Fulford (1942–)**
British psychiatrist and philosopher at Oxford University. Fulford has developed "values-based medicine" as a complement to evidence-based medicine, arguing that good psychiatric practice requires explicit attention to the diverse values of patients, clinicians, and communities rather than assuming a single authoritative standard. His "reverse view" of mental illness—that mental illness is primarily about negative values, while physical illness is primarily about facts—has been influential in shifting the philosophy of psychiatry toward explicit normativism.
*Key works:* *Moral Theory and Medical Practice* (1989), *Essential Philosophy of Psychiatry* (2006)
