# Psychosis, Reality, and Mind

Psychosis — particularly as it appears in schizophrenia — raises among the most profound philosophical questions about perception, belief, reality, and the structure of mind. Psychotic experiences challenge ordinary assumptions about what it means for a belief to be irrational, what it means for a perception to be veridical, and how the sense of reality is constructed and maintained. The philosophy of psychosis has become an important area connecting philosophy of mind, psychopathology, and phenomenology.

**What Psychosis Is**

Psychosis is characterized by a loss of contact with shared reality, manifested in symptoms including hallucinations (perceptions without external stimuli), delusions (fixed, false beliefs that are maintained despite counter-evidence), disorganized thinking, and in some presentations, severe disturbances of emotion and motivation. Schizophrenia is the most studied psychotic condition, though psychotic symptoms appear across a range of diagnoses including bipolar disorder, severe depression, and various medical and substance-related conditions.

The phenomenological experience of psychosis is extraordinarily varied, but characteristic themes include: a sense that ordinary events have hidden meanings or significance specially directed at oneself (ideas of reference); the experience that one's thoughts are being broadcast to others or inserted into one's mind by external agents; hearing voices that comment on one's actions or issue commands; a pervasive sense that the world has become strange, unreal, or transformed (what Karl Jaspers called "delusional atmosphere").

**Delusion and the Philosophy of Belief**

Delusions pose a severe challenge to standard philosophical accounts of belief. The standard picture treats beliefs as representations of how things are, formed on the basis of evidence and sensitive to counter-evidence. Delusions seem to violate these norms systematically: they are maintained despite obvious counter-evidence, they are often bizarre (incompatible with background knowledge), and they are not reliably connected to action in the way that ordinary beliefs are (a person who believes they are being persecuted may not take obvious protective actions).

There is debate about whether delusions are best understood as genuine beliefs (formed through a deviant process, but genuine beliefs nonetheless), doxastic states of a distinctive kind (not quite beliefs, not quite something else), or imaginings that have been misidentified as beliefs. Philosophers including Lisa Bortolotti have argued for the "doxastic" view — that delusions are beliefs because they are integrated into the patient's cognitive life, influence behavior (selectively), and are maintained with the tenacity of committed convictions. On this view, delusions are irrational beliefs, not something categorically different.

Others, including Phil Reed and Bill MacEwan, have argued that some delusions are better understood as anomalous perceptual or experiential states that have been interpreted and endorsed rather than as standard beliefs. The Capgras delusion — the belief that a close relative has been replaced by an identical impostor — may arise from a dissociation between facial recognition and the emotional response that normally accompanies it (the face is recognized as familiar but the feeling of recognition is absent), which the patient then rationalizes through the impostor hypothesis. On this view, the delusion is a rational response to an anomalous experience, not a straightforward epistemic failure.

**Hallucination and Perception**

Auditory verbal hallucinations — hearing voices — are among the most philosophically interesting symptoms of psychosis. They share the phenomenology of ordinary perception: they are experienced as coming from outside the self, as having the same kind of presence and reality as heard speech, as not under voluntary control. Yet they arise in the absence of the external stimuli that would ordinarily produce such experiences.

Computational models propose that voices arise when internally generated speech signals (inner speech, imagination) are misattributed to external sources — when the "efference copy" mechanism that normally tags internal activity as self-generated fails to function correctly, so that internal content is experienced as externally sourced. This provides a mechanistic story that connects to the predictive processing framework: hallucinations may reflect the pathological dominance of prior expectations over sensory input.

Phenomenologically, hearing voices is not simply "like hearing a real voice" in every respect. Many voice-hearers report that the voices have distinctive characteristics — particular tones, attitudes, relationships — that reflect and express the voice-hearer's psychology. Marius Romme and Sandra Escher's work with voice-hearers demonstrated that many people hear voices without distress and without meeting diagnostic criteria for psychosis, suggesting that the hearing of voices per se is not the pathology — rather, it is the relationship to voices (whether they are understood as hostile, overwhelming, uncontrollable) that determines whether the experience is distressing and disabling.

**Schizophrenia and the Self**

Phenomenologists of schizophrenia, particularly Josef Parnas and Louis Sass, have argued that the core of schizophrenic experience is a disturbance of ipseity — the pre-reflective sense of being oneself, the basic first-person character of experience. This disturbance underlies not just specific symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, thought disorder) but the pervasive strangeness and fragmentation that characterizes schizophrenic experience across its many manifestations.

The ipseity disturbance is not a disturbance of explicit self-concept (believing you are a different person) but of the most basic level of selfhood: the immediate, pre-reflective givenness of experience as one's own. When this is disturbed, the boundary between self and world becomes uncertain, experiences feel foreign or unreal, and the ordinary sense of being the author of one's thoughts and actions is undermined. This phenomenological analysis connects to the broader philosophy of the minimal self and to debates about the relationship between self-awareness and consciousness.
