# Key Thinkers: Specific Conditions

---

**Matthew Ratcliffe (1973–)**
British philosopher at the University of York, specializing in phenomenological psychopathology. Ratcliffe's phenomenological analysis of depression argues that the condition involves a fundamental change in existential feelings—the background sense of belonging to a world and having a future—rather than simply negative affect overlaid on normal experience. His distinction between emotion (a figure against a background) and existential feeling (the background itself) illuminates why depression is not merely sadness but a transformation of the entire experiential horizon. His work bridges phenomenological philosophy and clinical psychiatry with meticulous attention to first-person accounts.
*Key works:* *Feelings of Being* (2008), *Experiences of Depression* (2015)

---

**Louis Sass (1943–)**
American psychologist at Rutgers University. Sass's work on schizophrenia develops the "hyperreflexivity" hypothesis: that schizophrenic symptoms arise from an exaggerated reflective turning of attention on mental processes that normally operate pre-reflectively. This hyperreflexivity disrupts the automatic, taken-for-granted quality of normal experience, producing characteristic symptoms such as thought insertion (noticing one's own thoughts as alien intrusions) and passivity experiences (perceiving one's own actions as externally caused). His approach draws on Wittgenstein, Heidegger, and Husserl to analyze schizophrenic experience from the inside.
*Key works:* *Madness and Modernism* (1992), "Schizophrenia, Consciousness, and the Self" (2003, with Parnas)

---

**Josef Parnas (1950–)**
Danish psychiatrist at the University of Copenhagen. Parnas has developed the concept of "ipseity disturbance" as the core of schizophrenic experience—a disruption of the first-person character of experience, the sense of "mineness" that normally makes experience self-given without requiring explicit reflection. He has operationalized this phenomenological concept in clinical assessment tools (the EASE: Examination of Anomalous Self-Experiences) and has argued that ipseity disturbance is more specifically schizophrenic than the symptoms emphasized in current diagnostic manuals.
*Key works:* "Schizophrenia, Consciousness, and the Self" (2003, with Sass), *Examining for Anomalous Self-Experience* (2005)

---

**Thomas Fuchs (1958–)**
German psychiatrist and philosopher at Heidelberg University. Fuchs has provided the most systematic account of how psychiatric conditions alter lived temporality: in depression, time stagnates; in mania, it accelerates; in schizophrenia, the temporal flow of experience fragments. His phenomenological-biological approach treats these temporal disorders as reflecting disruptions of the embodied rhythms and intercorporeal synchrony that normally constitute lived time. His clinical phenomenology has been influential in both psychiatry and philosophy of mind.
*Key works:* *Ecology of the Brain* (2018), "Psychopathology of Lived Time" (2013)

---

**George Graham (1945–)**
American philosopher of mind at Georgia State University. Graham's philosophical account of psychosis examines how conditions involving delusion and hallucination challenge our understanding of what it means to be in touch with reality. He develops a philosophical framework that distinguishes different failures of reality contact in psychosis, connecting these to broader questions about the nature of belief, perception, and rational agency. His work is notable for combining philosophical analysis with genuine engagement with clinical psychiatry.
*Key works:* *The Disordered Mind* (2010), "Self-Ascription, Phenomenal Concepts, and Pathologies of Self-Knowledge" (2013)
