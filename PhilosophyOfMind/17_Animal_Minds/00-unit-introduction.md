# Unit Introduction: Animal Minds and Consciousness

## Central Questions

Do non-human animals have conscious experiences? Do they feel pain? Do they have beliefs and desires? Can they reason, plan, and recognize themselves in mirrors? These questions are not merely academic: their answers bear directly on how we are permitted to treat the billions of animals in agriculture, research, and captivity. Philosophical analysis of animal minds therefore has unusual practical urgency.

The core epistemological challenge is the *other minds problem* in its sharpest form: we cannot directly access any other mind, but at least for humans we have strong analogical and linguistic evidence. For animals, the linguistic evidence is absent (with some partial exceptions for great apes and cetaceans), and the analogical evidence varies by phylogenetic proximity. The challenge is to develop a methodology for attributing mental states to non-human creatures that is neither overcritical (denying minds to creatures with elaborate social, exploratory, and problem-solving behaviors) nor overgenerous (anthropomorphizing in ways that distort the actual nature of animal cognition).

Morgan's canon — attributed to Lloyd Morgan (1894) — holds that "in no case is an animal activity to be interpreted in terms of higher psychological processes if it can be fairly interpreted in terms of processes which stand lower in the scale of psychological evolution and development." This principle of parsimony has been enormously influential, but it is also double-edged: if the relevant comparison is not between lower and higher psychological processes but between psychological and non-psychological processes, the canon might require attributing conscious experience to animals whenever a conscious explanation is as parsimonious as a non-conscious one.

## Major Positions and Debates

### Behaviorism and Its Legacy

Classical behaviorism (Watson, Skinner) denied that internal mental states — beliefs, desires, consciousness — have any role in psychological explanation. Behavior is fully explained by stimulus-response regularities, and mentalistic explanation is either a loose way of talking about behavioral regularities or a conceptual confusion. For animal psychology, this meant explaining all animal behavior in terms of conditioning and reinforcement without appeal to mentalistic concepts.

Behaviorism has been largely abandoned in human cognitive science, but its influence persists in animal psychology, where the pressure to avoid anthropomorphism can lead to an implicit operationalism: attribute to animals only what is directly observable in their behavior. Contemporary animal cognition researchers like Cecilia Heyes have argued for a form of methodological restraint that is behaviorist in spirit: attribute mental states to animals only when the behavior cannot be explained by simpler associative mechanisms.

### Comparative Cognition and the Cognitive Ethology Approach

Cognitive ethology (Griffin, 1976) argued that the methods of ethology (naturalistic observation, evolutionary analysis) and cognitive science should be combined to study animal cognition in natural settings. Animals' behavior in their natural environments gives better evidence for their cognitive capacities than behavior in artificial laboratory settings, and evolutionary analysis can generate specific predictions about which cognitive capacities should be present in which species.

Key findings in comparative cognition include: caching behavior in Clark's nutcrackers (evidence for episodic-like memory); mirror self-recognition in great apes, dolphins, and elephants (evidence for a form of self-awareness); causal cognition in corvids (evidence for analogical reasoning); deceptive behavior in primates (evidence for theory of mind); and numerical cognition across species from bees to crows to mammals. Whether these findings establish genuine mentalistic properties or can be explained by simpler mechanisms is consistently contested.

### Fish Pain and the Moral Status of Invertebrates

The question of whether fish feel pain has been particularly controversial. Fish have nociceptors and show behavioral responses to noxious stimuli that look like pain avoidance. But the neocortex — the brain region associated with conscious pain processing in mammals — is absent in fish. Whether a fish's brain can support phenomenal pain without a neocortex depends on contested theories of consciousness.

The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (2012), signed by a prominent group of neuroscientists, affirmed that non-human animals, including fish, possess the neurological substrates necessary for consciousness. But the declaration does not resolve the philosophical question: what matters is not the presence of any particular neural substrate but whether the relevant neural organization can give rise to phenomenal experience.

The question extends to invertebrates: cephalopods (octopuses, squid) show remarkable problem-solving behavior and have large, complex brains relative to their body size. The UK Animal (Scientific Procedures) Act was extended to include cephalopods in 2010. Whether decapod crustaceans (crabs, lobsters) are capable of suffering has become a policy question: the UK Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 includes decapods. These policy decisions depend on contested philosophical and empirical questions about the conditions for phenomenal experience.

### The Evolution of Consciousness

If consciousness is a biological phenomenon, it presumably evolved — it was selected for. But this raises questions: what functional role does consciousness play that explains its selection? If zombies are possible (Unit 03), then any functional role can be played without consciousness; but then consciousness could not have been selected for its functional contribution. This is the *epiphenomenalism worry* for evolutionary accounts of consciousness.

The most common response is that the evolutionary argument provides strong evidence against epiphenomenalism: if consciousness had no effects on behavior, it would not have been selected for (at the metabolic cost of the neural machinery it requires), and yet it is present in at least the most behaviorally sophisticated animals. This is not a proof but a strong prior against epiphenomenalism.

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**Peter Singer** (*Animal Liberation*, 1975; *Practical Ethics*, 1979) is the most influential philosopher on the ethical implications of animal consciousness. Singer argues on utilitarian grounds that the capacity to suffer, not species membership, is the relevant criterion for moral status; and that animals capable of suffering have interests that must be counted equally with those of humans.

**Donald Griffin** (*The Question of Animal Awareness*, 1976) founded cognitive ethology and argued that animals have rich mental lives that are accessible to behavioral investigation.

**Daniel Dennett** argues that questions about animal consciousness are genuinely difficult because we cannot read off phenomenal consciousness from behavioral or neural evidence; he recommends skepticism about rich attributions of animal phenomenal consciousness while acknowledging that some form of proto-conscious experience may be present.

**David DeGrazia** (*Taking Animals Seriously*, 1996) provides the most philosophically careful account of the moral implications of animal consciousness and the case for animal liberation.

**Cecilia Heyes** (*Cognitive Gadgets*, 2018) argues against the nativist view that sophisticated cognitive capacities in animals are evolutionarily innate, holding instead that they are cultural products transmitted by social learning.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Singer, *Animal Liberation* (1975)**
The founding document of the modern animal liberation movement. Singer's philosophical argument (from equal consideration of interests) and his empirical survey of factory farming and animal experimentation are both philosophically important and practically significant.

**2. Griffin, *Animal Minds: Beyond Cognition to Consciousness* (1992)**
An accessible survey of cognitive ethology and the evidence for animal consciousness. Griffin is an advocate for liberal attributions of consciousness to animals; his willingness to go beyond the evidence is a useful foil for more cautious accounts.

**3. Allen and Bekoff, *Species of Mind: The Philosophy and Biology of Cognitive Ethology* (1997)**
A more philosophically careful treatment of cognitive ethology, addressing the methodology questions directly. Allen and Bekoff develop the case for using evolutionary considerations to guide attributions of mental states to animals.

**4. DeGrazia, *Taking Animals Seriously* (1996)**
The most philosophically careful treatment of the moral implications of animal cognition. DeGrazia develops a framework for attributing degrees of consciousness and cognitive sophistication to animals and draws the ethical implications with care.

**5. Heyes, *Cognitive Gadgets: The Cultural Evolution of Thinking* (2018)**
A provocative argument that sophisticated cognitive capacities — including imitation, mindreading, and language — are cultural products, not genetic inheritances. Important for thinking about what animal-human cognitive comparisons show.

## Open Questions

**1. What is the minimal neural organization for phenomenal consciousness?**
If phenomenal consciousness requires a neocortex, fish and invertebrates are not conscious. But there is no consensus that the neocortex is necessary rather than merely sufficient; IIT (for instance) implies that phenomenal consciousness depends on integrated information, which could be realized in non-mammalian nervous systems. Settling this question requires a theory of consciousness, not just neuroscience.

**2. Do the great apes have a genuine theory of mind?**
Chimpanzees pass some false-belief tasks and show behavior consistent with attributing goals and visual perspectives to others. But whether this constitutes genuine theory of mind — understanding others as having beliefs that represent the world — or whether it can be explained by more primitive behavior-reading mechanisms is actively contested.

**3. Can fish suffer?**
The behavioral and neurological evidence on fish pain is genuinely ambiguous. Fish have nociceptors and show avoidance behavior, but they lack the neural architecture typically associated with conscious pain processing. Whether the absence of a neocortex is evidence against phenomenal pain or merely shows that fish experience pain through a different neural mechanism is unclear.

**4. Is there a meaningful distinction between sentience and sapience in non-human animals?**
The distinction between merely feeling (sentience) and thinking (sapience) has been used to draw moral distinctions between different kinds of animal. But the empirical evidence suggests that the relevant capacities (emotion, reasoning, self-awareness) come in degrees and are distributed across species in complex ways. Whether there is a principled line between sentient and sapient animals, and whether this line has moral significance, is contested.

**5. Is Morgan's canon sound?**
Morgan's canon requires attributing the simplest cognitive explanation consistent with the behavioral evidence. But "simplest" is theory-relative: whether a behavioral repertoire is better explained by associative learning or by mentalistic cognition depends on what cognitive architecture we attribute to the animal. Whether parsimony is a good guide to truth in cognitive ethology — or whether it systematically underdescribes animal minds — is a methodological question that has not been resolved.
