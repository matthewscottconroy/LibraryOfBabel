# Behavioral Evidence for Animal Minds

The question of which animals have minds, and in what sense, is one of the oldest in philosophy and one of the most practically urgent in contemporary ethics. Behavioral evidence has been the primary basis for attributing mental states to animals, but interpreting behavioral evidence requires philosophical care — the evidence underdetermines which mental states (if any) underlie the behavior, and different theoretical frameworks lead to very different conclusions from the same observations.

**Morgan's Canon and the Skeptical Tradition**

C. Lloyd Morgan's canon — "In no case is an animal activity to be interpreted in terms of higher psychological processes, if it can be fairly interpreted in terms of processes which stand lower in the scale of psychological evolution and development" — has been the default methodological principle in comparative psychology for over a century. Morgan's canon reflects an appropriate scientific caution: the simpler the explanation, the better, unless the more complex explanation is required by the evidence.

Applied to animal behavior, the canon warns against attributing rich mental states — consciousness, reasoning, emotion — when simpler processes like conditioning, habituation, or reflexive response would suffice. A dog that appears to mourn its dead owner may simply be responding to the absence of stimuli that normally trigger certain behaviors, without anything that deserves to be called grief.

Morgan's canon is valuable, but it has been criticized for resting on a questionable assumption: that more complex mental processes are always less likely than simpler ones. In humans, we routinely and correctly attribute complex mental states based on behavioral evidence; why should the default be different for closely related species? The canon may reflect a human exceptionalism that needs to be earned through evidence rather than assumed as default.

**Self-Recognition and the Mirror Test**

Gordon Gallup's mirror test is one of the most widely used and philosophically discussed behavioral tests for self-awareness in animals. The test: animals are anesthetized and marked with a non-irritating dye on a part of the body they cannot see directly. After recovery, they are given access to a mirror. Animals that use the mirror to examine and touch the mark on their own body — rather than treating the mirror image as another animal — are said to pass the test and are attributed a form of self-recognition.

Great apes (chimpanzees, bonobos, orangutans), bottlenose dolphins, Asian elephants, European magpies, and arguably manta rays have passed the mirror test. Dogs, cats, and most other mammals and birds do not. Gorillas give mixed results. Human infants reliably pass around 18 months of age — the same age at which they begin to show other signs of self-concept development.

The mirror test is philosophically contested. Critics argue that passing the test requires not just self-awareness but also a particular set of visual-spatial processing abilities and motivational patterns that may be necessary but not sufficient for self-awareness. Olfactory animals like dogs may have a different but equally valid form of self-recognition that the test doesn't capture. And failing the test does not establish absence of self-awareness — it establishes absence of a specific response in a specific paradigm.

**Grief, Play, and Emotional Behavior**

Ethologists have accumulated extensive observations of what appear to be grief, play, compassion, jealousy, and other emotion-like behaviors in a wide range of species. Elephants appear to mourn their dead: they return to the bones of deceased family members, touch them repeatedly, and may remain near the body for extended periods. Crows cache food and show what appears to be distress when watched — suggesting anticipation of theft. Rats show reduced willingness to press levers that deliver shocks to other rats, and in some experiments will pass up food to avoid shocking a conspecific — suggesting something like empathy or social sensitivity.

These observations are genuinely difficult to explain without attributing something like emotional states to the animals involved. The philosophical challenge is to specify what kinds of mental states these observations establish. They are consistent with rich, phenomenally conscious emotional states; they are also consistent with functional states that have the causal-functional role of emotions without phenomenal character. The behavioral evidence does not by itself settle the phenomenal question.

**The Asymmetry Problem**

A fundamental philosophical problem in interpreting behavioral evidence for animal minds is the asymmetry of access. For other human beings, we attribute mental states on the basis of behavioral evidence, but our interpretation is also shaped by the assumption that other humans are structurally similar to us — that the same states that produce certain behaviors in us produce similar states in others. For animals, especially those distant from us phylogenetically, this assumption of structural similarity is weaker.

This asymmetry does not justify skepticism about animal minds, but it does require developing more sophisticated methods for assessing animal mental states than simple behavioral observation — which is where neuroscience becomes essential.
