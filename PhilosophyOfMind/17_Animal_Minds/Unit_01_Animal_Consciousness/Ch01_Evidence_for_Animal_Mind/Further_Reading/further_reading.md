# Further Reading: Evidence for Animal Mind

## Primary Sources

**Philip Low et al., "The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness" (2012)** — The statement signed by a prominent group of neuroscientists at the Francis Crick Memorial Conference declaring that non-human animals possess the neurological substrates that generate conscious states. While not a peer-reviewed paper, the declaration represents a scientific consensus statement on animal consciousness with significant philosophical implications.

**Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974), *Philosophical Review*** — Nagel's classic paper argues that consciousness has an essentially subjective character that cannot be captured by any objective physical account. Using the example of bat echolocation, he argues that we cannot know what it is like to be a bat even if we have complete objective knowledge of bat neuroscience. This argument establishes the conceptual framework for discussions of animal consciousness and the limits of our knowledge of other minds.

**Donald Griffin, *Animal Minds: Beyond Cognition to Consciousness* (2001)** — Griffin, the discoverer of bat echolocation, was a pioneer in arguing that animals have genuine cognitive processes and potentially consciousness. His "cognitive ethology" approach advocates for inferring mental states from animal behavior as the best scientific approach to animal minds, against Morgan's Canon's deflationary tendencies.

**C. Lloyd Morgan, *An Introduction to Comparative Psychology* (1894)** — The classic source for "Morgan's Canon"—the principle that animal behavior should not be attributed to a higher psychological process when it can be explained in terms of a lower one. Morgan's principle has been both influential and contested: while it guards against anthropomorphism, critics argue it systematically underestimates animal cognition.

**Jonathan Balcombe, *What a Fish Knows: The Inner Lives of Our Underwater Cousins* (2016)** — A synthesis of evidence for sophisticated cognitive and affective capacities in fish, including pain, learning, social cognition, and tool use, challenging popular and scientific assumptions about fish cognition and moral status. Useful for extending the discussion of animal mind beyond mammals.

## Secondary Literature

**Gary Varner, *Personhood, Ethics, and Animal Cognition* (2012)** — A philosophical treatment that combines the philosophy of mind and ethics, arguing that different levels of mental complexity—sentience, autonomy, and biographical agency—ground different levels of moral status. Engages rigorously with the empirical literature on animal cognition.

**Marian Stamp Dawkins, *Why Animals Matter: Animal Consciousness, Animal Welfare and Human Well-Being* (2012)** — Argues that animal welfare science should be grounded in the question of animal consciousness, reviewing the behavioral and neuroscientific evidence and its implications for how we treat animals.

**Peter Godfrey-Smith, *Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness* (2016)** — A philosophical and scientific exploration of consciousness in cephalopods, arguing that the distributed intelligence of octopuses represents an alternative form of mentality that challenges assumptions about the relationship between consciousness and neural centralization.

**Allen Dickel, "Morgan's Canon and Animal Psychology" (1991)** — A critical examination of Morgan's Canon as a methodological principle, arguing that it can be interpreted in multiple ways and that its most deflationary interpretations are not well justified.

**Lynn Sneddon, Victoria Braithwaite, and Michael Gentle, "Do Fish Have Nociceptors? Evidence for the Evolution of a Vertebrate Sensory System" (2003), *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*** — The empirical paper that established the presence of nociceptors in fish, raising the question of whether fish feel pain and contributing to the evidence for a much wider distribution of sentience than previously assumed.
