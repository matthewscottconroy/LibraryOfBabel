# Key Thinkers: Evidence for Animal Mind

---

**Thomas Nagel (1937–)**
American philosopher at NYU. Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974) established the framework for thinking about animal consciousness by arguing that consciousness has an irreducibly subjective character—there is something it is like to be a bat—that cannot be captured by any objective physical description. While arguing for the reality of animal experience, the paper also establishes its epistemic inaccessibility from the outside, making the problem of animal consciousness both serious and perhaps insoluble in principle. The paper is essential for understanding why animal consciousness is philosophically distinctive.
*Key works:* "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974), *Mortal Questions* (1979), *Mind and Cosmos* (2012)

---

**Donald Griffin (1915–2003)**
American zoologist and biologist at Harvard, Rockefeller, and Cornell Universities. Griffin discovered bat echolocation and was the pioneer of "cognitive ethology"—the scientific study of animal minds. His *Animal Thinking* (1984) and *Animal Minds* (2001) argued against Morgan's Canon's deflationary tendencies, advocating for attributing genuine cognitive processes and potentially consciousness to animals when the behavioral evidence supports it. He helped legitimize the scientific study of animal cognition as a serious research program.
*Key works:* *The Question of Animal Awareness* (1976), *Animal Thinking* (1984), *Animal Minds* (1992/2001)

---

**C. Lloyd Morgan (1852–1936)**
British psychologist and animal behaviorist at the University of Bristol. Morgan's Canon—"In no case may we interpret an action as the outcome of the exercise of a higher psychical faculty, if it can be interpreted as the outcome of the exercise of one which stands lower in the psychological scale"—became one of the most influential methodological principles in comparative psychology. Intended as a guard against anthropomorphism, the canon has been variously interpreted as a logical principle, a scientific method, and a substantive claim, and its relationship to parsimony and scientific inference has been extensively debated.
*Key works:* *An Introduction to Comparative Psychology* (1894), *Animal Behaviour* (1900)

---

**Philip Low (1975–)**
American neuroscientist, lead author of the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness. Low organized the conference at which the Declaration was signed, and his own research on thalamocortical systems and consciousness has been important for understanding the neural bases of conscious experience in vertebrates. The Declaration—stating that non-human animals possess the neurological substrates necessary for conscious states—represents the first major public scientific consensus statement on animal consciousness and has had significant implications for animal welfare policy.
*Key works:* The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (2012, lead author)

---

**Peter Godfrey-Smith (1965–)**
Australian philosopher at the University of Sydney and City University of New York. Godfrey-Smith's *Other Minds* (2016) uses the extraordinary intelligence of cephalopods—octopuses, squid, and cuttlefish—to explore the origins and nature of consciousness from an evolutionary perspective. Because cephalopod intelligence evolved independently from vertebrate intelligence, he argues, cephalopods represent a natural experiment in the evolution of mind that illuminates what is and is not essential to consciousness. His work is philosophically sophisticated and empirically grounded.
*Key works:* *Other Minds* (2016), *Metazoa: Animal Life and the Birth of the Mind* (2020)
