# Degrees of Consciousness and the Consciousness Continuum

The binary picture — organisms are either conscious or they are not — is increasingly viewed as inadequate. Both theoretical considerations and empirical evidence point toward a picture in which consciousness admits of degrees, comes in different types, and varies in richness and complexity across the spectrum of life. This shift has important implications for how we study animal minds and for the ethical frameworks we use to evaluate animal welfare.

**Why Degrees?**

Several independent lines of reasoning support a graded view of consciousness.

First, evolutionary considerations. If consciousness is a biological phenomenon with adaptive value, it plausibly evolved gradually, from simpler to more complex forms, rather than appearing suddenly at a particular point in evolutionary history. The brain systems associated with consciousness are among the most ancient in the nervous system's evolutionary history. It would be extraordinary if consciousness had a sharp threshold — if creatures just below a certain level of neural complexity had zero consciousness while creatures just above had full-blown phenomenal experience.

Second, considerations from theoretical frameworks. Integrated Information Theory (IIT) explicitly proposes that consciousness is identical to integrated information (phi), which is a continuously variable quantity. Every system has some phi; the question is how much. Even a thermostat has some phi, and hence some minimal consciousness on this view. Biological systems with rich, recurrent neural connectivity have much higher phi. IIT entails that consciousness is everywhere, in degrees — panpsychism, though of a structured rather than mere primitive-property kind.

Global Workspace Theory is less committed to graded consciousness — either information achieves global broadcast or it doesn't — but even here, researchers recognize that different conditions of global broadcast (partial, unstable, full) correspond to different degrees of access consciousness.

Third, clinical evidence. In humans, consciousness varies continuously: from full wakefulness to drowsy, to light sleep, to deep sleep, to varying depths of anesthesia, to various disorders of consciousness (minimally conscious state, vegetative state, unresponsive wakefulness). Electrophysiological and neuroimaging measures track these transitions and show that the neural signatures of consciousness vary continuously, not discretely.

**Primary and Higher-Order Consciousness**

Gerald Edelman developed an influential distinction between primary consciousness and higher-order consciousness. Primary consciousness is the capacity for a mental scene — an integrated, present-moment awareness of perceptual experience that is available for action guidance. It requires neural mechanisms for perceptual categorization and their linkage to value and memory, and Edelman argued that many mammals possess it.

Higher-order consciousness — the capacity for reflective awareness, for awareness of one's own mental states, for the construction of a sense of past and future, for linguistic self-description — is a further development that depends on primary consciousness but requires additional neural machinery, particularly the capacity for self-referential narrative. Higher-order consciousness in Edelman's sense is uniquely developed in humans, though other great apes may have rudimentary versions.

This distinction maps onto Ned Block's phenomenal versus access consciousness: primary consciousness is more closely related to phenomenal consciousness (raw experience), while higher-order consciousness is more closely related to access consciousness (availability for reasoning and report). But the mapping is not exact — primary consciousness, for Edelman, is not purely phenomenal; it involves the integration of perception with value and memory in a way that already involves access to a certain kind of global representation.

**Phenomenal Richness and Complexity**

Even among creatures that are clearly phenomenally conscious, there is likely to be variation in the richness and complexity of experience. A mouse's visual experience of a hawk may be phenomenally real — it may involve genuine fear and a genuine felt sense of threat — but it is unlikely to involve the elaborated context that a human would bring to the same perception: memories of similar encounters, conceptual categorization, narrative self-placement, anticipation of future consequences, and linguistic processing.

The concept of phenomenal richness — the complexity and integration of phenomenal experience — is different from the mere presence or absence of phenomenality. A creature's experiences can be genuinely phenomenal without being as rich, integrated, or self-reflective as human experience. This distinction matters for ethics: we might hold that all phenomenal experience has some moral weight while also recognizing that richer, more self-aware experiences have additional dimensions of moral significance (anticipatory suffering, narrative disruption, self-concept harm) that simpler experiences lack.

**The Ethical Relevance**

The graded picture of consciousness has direct practical implications. If consciousness comes in degrees, then the obligation to minimize animal suffering applies across a vast range of species, but with different weights proportional to the richness and degree of consciousness involved. Factory farming's treatment of sentient mammals raises different — and arguably more serious — ethical concerns than commercial fishing's treatment of fish, which raises different concerns than pesticide use's effects on insects.

These are not comfortable conclusions; they require confronting enormous industries and practices with significant social and economic inertia. But they follow fairly directly from taking seriously the evidence for graded consciousness across the animal kingdom.
