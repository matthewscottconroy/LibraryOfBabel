# Further Reading: Theories of Animal Consciousness

## Primary Sources

**Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974), *Philosophical Review*** — Nagel's classic argument that consciousness is irreducibly subjective and that we cannot in principle know what it is like to be another kind of creature. This establishes the philosophical problem of animal consciousness and why it resists purely objective approaches.

**Daniel Dennett, *Kinds of Minds: Towards an Understanding of Consciousness* (1996)** — Dennett's skeptical treatment of animal consciousness argues against attributing "full" consciousness to animals on methodological grounds, using his heterophenomenological method. While acknowledging animal intelligence and perhaps sentience, he cautions against inferring rich inner life from behavioral complexity.

**Marc Bekoff and Jane Goodall, *The Smile of a Dolphin: Remarkable Accounts of Animal Emotions* (2000)** — A collection of scientists' first-person accounts of emotional experiences in the animals they study, providing anecdotal but rich evidence for the diversity of animal emotional life. Bekoff argues for the scientific legitimacy of attributing emotions to animals.

**Allen and Bekoff, *Species of Mind: The Philosophy and Biology of Cognitive Ethology* (1997)** — A philosophical and scientific treatment arguing that cognitive ethology—the study of animal mental states—is a legitimate scientific enterprise and that mental state attributions to animals can be scientifically evaluated.

**Antonio Damasio, *The Feeling of What Happens: Body and Emotion in the Making of Consciousness* (1999)** — Damasio's account of core consciousness and its relationship to bodily feeling states is directly relevant to animal consciousness: if consciousness requires bodily emotion regulation rather than sophisticated cognition, then many animals may have genuine consciousness.

## Secondary Literature

**José Luis Bermúdez, *Thinking Without Words* (2003)** — Argues that non-linguistic creatures can have genuine beliefs and desires, establishing a philosophical framework for attributing mental states to animals without requiring language.

**Kristin Andrews, *The Animal Mind: An Introduction to the Philosophy of Animal Cognition* (2015)** — An accessible and rigorous philosophical introduction to animal cognition, covering the nature of animal mental states, methodology in comparative psychology, and the philosophical implications of animal cognition research. An excellent course text.

**Peter Singer, *Animal Liberation* (1975)** — While primarily an ethics text, Singer's utilitarian argument for animal liberation rests on the claim that animals are sentient—that they have the capacity to suffer—and this capacity grounds their moral consideration. The philosophical connections between consciousness, sentience, and moral status are made explicit.

**David DeGrazia, *Taking Animals Seriously: Mental Life and Moral Status* (1996)** — A philosophical examination of animal mental life and its moral implications, arguing that animals have richer inner lives than common wisdom supposes and that this requires taking their interests much more seriously.

**Victoria Braithwaite, *Do Fish Feel Pain?* (2010)** — A rigorous scientific examination of the evidence for fish nociception and its implications for whether fish experience pain, showing how the question of animal consciousness has genuinely practical implications for animal welfare.
