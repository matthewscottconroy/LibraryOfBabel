# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Theories of Animal Consciousness

## Key Arguments

**Nagel's Bat Argument Applied to Animals**
Thomas Nagel's argument that we cannot know what it is like to be a bat is not merely about bats: it generalizes to all animals with phenomenal experience different from our own. The bat's sonar-based experience — a form of perception that has no analog in human experience — is inaccessible to human imagination; we can describe its functional role but not its phenomenal character. Nagel uses this to argue for the irreducibility of phenomenal consciousness, but it also establishes that animals have genuine phenomenal experience while making its specific character unknowable to us.

**Dennett's Skepticism About Animal Qualia**
Daniel Dennett argues that we should be skeptical about attributing rich subjective experience to animals not because animals lack information-processing states but because the concept of qualia — irreducible phenomenal properties — is itself confused. His heterophenomenological method holds that attributions of experience should track behavioral evidence and the subject's own reports; non-reporting animals cannot be subjects of heterophenomenology, so claims about their qualia are methodologically unsupportable. Dennett is not denying that animals have complex, valenced states but denying that these states involve irreducible phenomenal properties.

**Global Workspace in Animals (Dehaene)**
Stanislas Dehaene argues that the global workspace — the neural architecture of widely distributed broadcasting that he identifies with consciousness — is present, to varying degrees, in mammals and birds. The key neural features of the global workspace (long-range corticocortical connections, ignition dynamics, late cortical responses to conscious stimuli) are present in some form in many vertebrates. This suggests that basic access consciousness — the global availability of information for behavioral control — is phylogenetically widespread, though its presence does not settle questions about phenomenal consciousness.

**Higher-Order Theories Applied to Animals**
Higher-order theories (Rosenthal, Lycan) hold that a mental state is conscious only if it is accompanied by a higher-order representation of that state. Applied to animals, this implies that most animals — lacking the neural substrates for higher-order representation (the developed prefrontal cortex) — lack genuine conscious states, even if they have information-processing states that influence behavior. Critics of this application argue that higher-order representation is an overly demanding criterion and that it would implausibly deny consciousness to infants and many non-human mammals.

## Core Concepts

**What It Is Like (Nagel)**
Nagel's famous phrase "there is something it is like to be" a creature captures the phenomenal character of consciousness: for any conscious organism, there is a subjective character to its experience — an inner "what-it's-likeness" — that is not captured by any functional or third-person description. Applied to animals, the claim is that (at least some) animals have phenomenal experience with a character that is genuinely their own, even if that character is inaccessible to human imagination.

**Minimal Phenomenal Experience**
The concept of minimal phenomenal experience refers to the simplest, most primitive form of phenomenal consciousness — a bare "something it is like" without the rich intentional structure and self-awareness of human experience. Philosophers like Ned Block and Thomas Metzinger use this concept to argue that consciousness can exist in very simple forms and is likely widespread in the animal kingdom. The concept challenges higher-order theories that set the bar for consciousness at the level of explicit self-representation.

**Global Workspace in Animals**
Dehaene's evidence for a global workspace architecture in animals draws on studies of late cortical activity, cross-species comparisons of corticocortical connectivity, and the behavioral signatures of global broadcast (such as the "ignition" of widespread neural activity when a stimulus becomes consciously reportable). Animals with extensive long-range cortical connectivity — particularly mammals and corvids — are argued to have sufficient substrate for a rudimentary global workspace, suggesting that access consciousness extends beyond humans.

**Higher-Order Representation (HOT) and Animal Consciousness**
Higher-order thought (HOT) theories require that a mental state be conscious only when accompanied by a higher-order thought that represents it. Applied to animals, this would require that animals have prefrontal-like systems capable of forming representations of their own mental states. Rosenthal explicitly argues that most animals probably lack conscious experience in the relevant sense, making the HOT theory notably restrictive. Defenders of animal consciousness argue that this makes HOT theory implausible as a theory of phenomenal consciousness.

**The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness**
The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (Low et al., 2012) is a scientific consensus statement asserting that the neurological substrates of conscious experience are not unique to humans: all mammals, birds, and many other creatures, including octopuses, possess the neurological substrates for conscious states. The declaration is significant as a statement of scientific consensus rather than a philosophical argument, and it has been influential in arguments for animal welfare reform and in philosophical debates about the default assumption regarding animal consciousness.

**Phenomenal Overflow vs. Access Consciousness**
Ned Block's distinction between phenomenal consciousness (P-consciousness: what it's like) and access consciousness (A-consciousness: availability for use in reasoning, report, and action) is significant for animal consciousness debates. If phenomenal consciousness can exist without access consciousness (as Block's "overflow" argument suggests), then animals that cannot report on their states might still have rich phenomenal experience. If phenomenal consciousness requires access (as global workspace theorists maintain), then the presence of access-consciousness mechanisms in an animal is sufficient for consciousness.

## Thought Experiments

**What Is It Like to Be a Bat?**
Nagel asks us to imagine what it is like to experience the world primarily through sonar echolocation: to navigate by emitting ultrasonic pulses and processing the returning echoes. No amount of third-person description of echolocation captures the phenomenal character of that experience. The thought experiment establishes both that bats (probably) have genuine phenomenal experience and that this experience is cognitively inaccessible to beings with a fundamentally different perceptual modality — making the subjective character of animal consciousness genuinely unknowable from a human perspective.

**The Lobster and Phenomenal Pain**
Consider whether a lobster placed in boiling water has a phenomenal experience of pain. The lobster has a nociceptive system and shows escape behavior, but it lacks a cerebral cortex. HOT theorists say no; global workspace theorists say unclear; proponents of minimal phenomenal experience suggest yes. The thought experiment is philosophically significant because it presses on the exact conditions necessary for phenomenal experience and reveals that our intuitions diverge precisely where the theory-dependent differences become most important.

**The Pigeon Mirror Test Variant**
The standard mirror self-recognition test (showing a red spot on an animal's forehead visible in a mirror) is typically passed only by great apes, dolphins, elephants, and magpies. Pigeons can be trained to pass a version of the task with extensive conditioning. The thought experiment of comparing the trained pigeon to the spontaneously passing chimpanzee asks: does the cognitive process of spontaneous self-recognition require a different kind of consciousness than the trained recognition? The case tests higher-order theories (which predict self-recognition requires explicit self-representation) against simpler accounts.
