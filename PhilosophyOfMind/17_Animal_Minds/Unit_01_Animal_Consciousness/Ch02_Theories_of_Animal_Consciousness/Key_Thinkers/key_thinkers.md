# Key Thinkers: Theories of Animal Consciousness

---

**Thomas Nagel (1937–)**
American philosopher at NYU. Nagel's argument that there is something it is like to be a bat—that bat consciousness is genuinely subjective and not capturable by objective description—establishes both the reality and the inaccessibility of animal consciousness from the outside. For Nagel, this irreducible subjectivity is what makes consciousness philosophically distinctive and makes the problem of animal minds both serious and epistemically challenging. His work frames all subsequent theoretical discussion of what animal consciousness is and why it resists reductive accounts.
*Key works:* "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974), *Mortal Questions* (1979), *The View from Nowhere* (1986)

---

**Daniel Dennett (1942–)**
American philosopher at Tufts University. Dennett's heterophenomenological method holds that we should attribute mental states to animals (and other systems) on the basis of their behavioral profile, without assuming that these attributions commit us to full-blown phenomenal consciousness. His "intentional stance"—treating systems as rational agents in order to predict their behavior—does not require that the attributed beliefs and desires correspond to real phenomenal states. Dennett's deflationary approach is influential but has been criticized for being unable to account for the distinctive features of animal experience.
*Key works:* *Kinds of Minds* (1996), *Consciousness Explained* (1991), *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* (1995)

---

**Marc Bekoff (1945–)**
American ethologist and cognitive ethologist at the University of Colorado. Bekoff has been one of the most active advocates for taking animal emotions and consciousness seriously as scientific topics. His research on play behavior, social emotions, and moral behavior in animals argues that these phenomena can be studied scientifically without requiring either anthropomorphism or the denial of animal experience. His collaboration with Jane Goodall and his public advocacy have made him a prominent voice for animal consciousness in both science and popular culture.
*Key works:* *Species of Mind* (1997, with Allen), *The Smile of a Dolphin* (2000, with Goodall), *The Emotional Lives of Animals* (2007)

---

**Antonio Damasio (1944–)**
Portuguese-American neuroscientist at USC. Damasio's account of consciousness as grounded in the organism's representation of its own bodily states has direct implications for animal consciousness: if consciousness requires the kind of bodily self-representation that all vertebrates possess rather than higher cognitive functions associated with language, then animal consciousness may be much more widespread than previously thought. His concept of "core consciousness"—the immediate awareness of the interaction between organism and object—is specifically intended to apply across species.
*Key works:* *The Feeling of What Happens* (1999), *Self Comes to Mind* (2010)

---

**Kristin Andrews (1972–)**
Canadian philosopher at York University in Toronto. Andrews has been one of the most philosophically rigorous contributors to the debate about animal minds, arguing that we need new conceptual frameworks—not just extensions of human psychological concepts—for understanding animal cognition. Her work on animal folk psychology, animal norm-following, and the methodology of comparative cognition has advanced the philosophical study of animal minds beyond the question of whether animals have "theory of mind" (in the human sense) to richer questions about what kinds of minds animals actually have.
*Key works:* *The Animal Mind* (2015), *Do Apes Read Minds?* (2012)
