# Tool Use, Culture, and Animal Intelligence

Tool use was once considered a uniquely human capacity, then a capacity shared only with chimpanzees, and then — as research expanded — a property of an increasingly wide range of species including corvids, dolphins, sea otters, and even some fish. The expanding documentation of animal tool use has pushed philosophers and scientists to ask deeper questions: What kind of intelligence does tool use require? What does cultural transmission of tool use reveal about animal cognition? And how does the capacity to create and use tools relate to the kind of intelligence we most value in humans?

**New Caledonian Crows**

New Caledonian crows (Corvus moneduloides) have become the most extensively studied non-primate tool users. In the wild, they manufacture tools from leaves and branches — cutting leaves into hook shapes, snapping and trimming sticks — and use them to extract invertebrates from holes in wood. The manufacture of tools (as opposed to their use) is a key cognitive indicator: the crow must represent not only the current situation but the affordances of the material and the desired product.

Laboratory research by Alex Taylor, Russell Gray, and colleagues has revealed remarkably sophisticated causal and physical reasoning in New Caledonian crows. These crows can solve multi-step sequential problems (getting a short tool to get a long tool to get food), can select tools based on their suitability for the task, and show evidence of planning ahead — caching tools in locations where they will be needed later.

Particularly striking are experiments on causal understanding. Ravens and crows have shown evidence of understanding water displacement (dropping stones into water to raise the level and access floating food), the causal role of supports, and the relationship between their actions and their effects. In some paradigms, corvid performance rivals and occasionally surpasses chimpanzee performance on the same tasks.

**Cultural Transmission and Social Learning**

Perhaps the most philosophically significant aspect of tool use in chimpanzees is its cultural transmission. Different chimpanzee populations in the wild have different tool-using traditions — different techniques for nut-cracking, different types of tools used for ant-dipping, different honey-extraction methods — that are passed down within populations and acquired by young individuals through social learning. These traditions have been maintained for at least as long as chimpanzees have been systematically studied (decades) and are presumably older.

The cultural variation in chimpanzee tool use is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for genuine culture (in the sense of socially transmitted behavioral traditions) in non-human animals. It raises the question of what mechanism underlies this cultural transmission. True imitation — copying the actions of a model, as opposed to merely learning through individual exploration of the same problem — is more cognitively demanding and requires representing the model's actions as a template for one's own. Research on imitation fidelity shows that human children and chimpanzees differ: chimpanzees tend to "emulate" (copy the goal without copying the specific action method) while children tend to "imitate" (copy the specific action, including causally irrelevant steps), a phenomenon called "overimitation."

This distinction may be relevant to cultural transmission fidelity: high-fidelity imitation (of the kind human children show) enables cumulative culture, where innovations are preserved precisely and can be built upon by subsequent generations. Chimpanzee emulation may be less precise, limiting the accumulation of cultural complexity. This has been proposed as one explanation for the gap between human cultural complexity and the more limited cultural traditions of other species.

**Intelligence and the Manufacture of Novelty**

A recurring question is whether animal intelligence is genuinely productive — whether it can generate genuinely novel solutions — or whether it is essentially reconstructive, recombining previously learned elements without truly generating the new. This distinction matters for understanding the relationship between animal intelligence and human creativity.

Research on insight in animals — paradigmatically, Wolfgang Köhler's original demonstrations of Sultan the chimpanzee arranging boxes to reach food — suggests that at least some animals can solve structurally novel problems in ways that go beyond simple trial-and-error learning. But the evidence for genuinely productive, creative intelligence — the kind that generates conceptual novelty rather than recombining behavioral repertoires — is more limited and contested in non-human species.

The philosophical significance of this distinction connects to broader questions about the nature of intelligence: is intelligence fundamentally recombinative, working with existing elements, or can it be genuinely generative, producing conceptual novelty? If human intelligence is in some important sense generative in a way that animal intelligence is not, understanding the neural and cognitive differences that account for this may be essential for understanding what is distinctive about human minds.
