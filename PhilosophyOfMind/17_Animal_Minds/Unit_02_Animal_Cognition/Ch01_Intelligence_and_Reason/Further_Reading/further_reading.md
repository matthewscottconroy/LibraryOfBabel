# Further Reading: Intelligence and Reason in Animals

## Primary Sources

**Alex Taylor, Russell Gray, Gavin Hunt, and Russell Grayson, "New Caledonian Crows Reason About Hidden Causal Agents" (2009), *PNAS*** — A landmark study showing that New Caledonian crows use causal reasoning to infer the presence of hidden agents from indirect evidence (moving sticks). This finding—one of several demonstrating sophisticated tool use and causal cognition in crows—sparked major debate about the distribution of reason-like cognitive capacities across vertebrates.

**Michael Tomasello, *The Cultural Origins of Human Cognition* (1999)** — Tomasello's comparative account argues that what is distinctive about human cognition is not raw intelligence but shared intentionality—the capacity for genuinely joint attention and cultural learning—which underlies cumulative cultural evolution. His careful comparison of human and non-human primate cognition establishes what non-human primates can and cannot do.

**Nicola Clayton and Anthony Dickinson, "Episodic-Like Memory During Cache Recovery by Scrub Jays" (1998), *Nature*** — The influential study demonstrating that western scrub jays remember what, where, and when they cached food, providing evidence for episodic-like memory—previously thought to be uniquely human. This paper stimulated major research into episodic memory and mental time travel in animals.

**Wolfgang Köhler, *The Mentality of Apes* (1917/1927)** — Köhler's classic experiments demonstrating insight learning in chimpanzees—their ability to solve novel problems (reaching bananas using sticks) without extended trial and error—established that non-human primates can engage in flexible, intelligent problem-solving. This foundational work remains relevant for debates about whether animals engage in genuine reasoning.

**Irene Pepperberg, *The Alex Studies* (1999)** — Pepperberg's comprehensive account of her research with Alex the grey parrot, showing object classification, numerical reasoning, understanding of abstract concepts ("same"/"different"), and apparently spontaneous communication. Alex's capacities challenged assumptions about what cognitive abilities require linguistic or social learning environments.

## Secondary Literature

**Daniel Povinelli, *Folk Physics for Apes: The Chimpanzee's Theory of How the World Works* (2000)** — A careful examination of chimpanzee tool use and causal understanding, arguing that chimpanzees understand causal relationships in terms of surface features rather than hidden causal mechanisms—a finding that challenges strong claims about chimpanzee intelligence while acknowledging their genuine capacities.

**Sarah Brosnan and Frans de Waal, "Monkeys Reject Unequal Pay" (2003), *Nature*** — The famous study showing that capuchin monkeys refuse to accept less valuable food when they observe a conspecific receiving more valuable food for the same task. This finding provided evidence for a sense of fairness in non-human primates and has been important for evolutionary accounts of cooperation and justice.

**Alex Taylor, "Corvid Cognition" (2014), *WIREs Cognitive Science*** — A review of cognitive research on corvids—crows, jays, and ravens—covering their tool use, causal reasoning, social cognition, and memory. A useful synthesis of what is one of the most productive comparative cognition research programs.

**Carel van Schaik, *The Primate Origins of Human Nature* (2016)** — An evolutionary account of how primate social intelligence, culture, and technology paved the way for distinctively human cognition, drawing on decades of field research on orangutans and other primates.

**W. Tecumseh Fitch, *The Evolution of Language* (2010)** — A comprehensive treatment of language evolution that engages with comparative cognition, covering what language requires cognitively and what non-human animals can and cannot do in the domain of communication and symbolic representation.
