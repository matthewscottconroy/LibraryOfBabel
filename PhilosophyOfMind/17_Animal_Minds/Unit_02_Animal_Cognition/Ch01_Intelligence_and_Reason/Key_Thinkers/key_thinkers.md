# Key Thinkers: Intelligence and Reason in Animals

---

**Wolfgang Köhler (1887–1967)**
German Gestalt psychologist, director of the anthropoid research station at Tenerife. Köhler's *The Mentality of Apes* (1917) demonstrated insight learning in chimpanzees—their ability to solve novel problems (using sticks to reach bananas, stacking boxes to reach overhead food) without extended trial and error. He argued that this demonstrated genuine intelligence involving the perception of relationships, not merely conditioned responses. His Gestalt approach—emphasizing the perception of structure and form—influenced later cognitive ethology and challenged behaviorist accounts of animal learning.
*Key works:* *The Mentality of Apes* (1917/1927), *Gestalt Psychology* (1929)

---

**Nicola Clayton (1962–)**
British comparative psychologist at Cambridge University. Clayton's research on western scrub jays demonstrated that these corvids have episodic-like memory—they remember what, where, and when they cached food—providing the first clear evidence for episodic memory outside humans. Her subsequent research on mental time travel, flexible planning, and prospective cognition in corvids has been central to debates about whether episodic memory and future planning are uniquely human capacities or more widely distributed.
*Key works:* "Episodic-Like Memory During Cache Recovery by Scrub Jays" (1998, with Dickinson), *The Philosopher's Dog* (2003, popular)

---

**Alex Taylor**
New Zealand comparative psychologist at the University of Auckland. Taylor's research on New Caledonian crows has pushed the boundaries of what we thought non-primate birds could do cognitively. His experiments demonstrating causal reasoning about hidden agents—crows modifying their behavior based on the inferred presence of unseen observers—represent some of the most compelling evidence for reasoning-like cognitive processes in non-mammalian species. His work has been important in challenging mammal-centric assumptions in comparative cognition.
*Key works:* "New Caledonian Crows Reason About Hidden Causal Agents" (2009), "Of Crows and Caches: The Interplay Between Memory and Attention in Corvids" (2012)

---

**Irene Pepperberg (1949–)**
American comparative psychologist at Harvard University. Pepperberg's three decades of research with Alex the African grey parrot showed that a bird could master complex cognitive tasks—categorizing objects by color, shape, and material; understanding numerical concepts; expressing concepts of sameness and difference—through social, interactive teaching. Her work challenged the assumption that the cognitive prerequisites for such abilities are limited to primates or require mammalian brain architecture.
*Key works:* *The Alex Studies* (1999), *Alex and Me* (2008)

---

**Daniel Povinelli (1963–)**
American comparative psychologist at the University of Louisiana. Povinelli's careful experimental work on chimpanzee cognition has consistently argued against strong attributions of physical and psychological understanding to non-human primates. He argues that chimpanzees are sophisticated behavioral readers but lack understanding of unobservable causes—hidden forces, mental states—and that apparent causal understanding in apes reflects pattern learning rather than genuine causal reasoning. His methodologically rigorous challenges to more generous interpretations have been important in clarifying what comparative cognition research actually establishes.
*Key works:* *Folk Physics for Apes* (2000), *The Mind's Past* (2001)
