# Theory of Mind in Animals

Whether non-human animals attribute mental states to others — whether they have something like a theory of mind — is one of the most contested questions in comparative cognition. The evidence is complex, the experimental paradigms are difficult, and the theoretical interpretations are sharply divided. But the question matters: if some animals have genuine theory of mind, this has profound implications for our understanding of social cognition, the evolution of human intelligence, and the moral status of animals.

**The Original Question and Its Operationalization**

Premack and Woodruff (1978) asked whether chimpanzees attribute beliefs, desires, and intentions to others — whether they represent others as having mental states that causally explain behavior. They used behavioral tasks to assess this, finding suggestive evidence. The subsequent debate has centered on operationalizing "theory of mind" in ways that can be meaningfully tested in non-verbal subjects.

The standard paradigm in developmental psychology is the false-belief task: does the subject predict behavior based on the agent's false belief about the world (the belief, not the world), or based on the actual state of the world? If the child predicts that the agent will look for the object where the agent last saw it (even though it has been moved), they are attributing beliefs to the agent and tracking those beliefs rather than just tracking the world.

Adapting this paradigm to non-human animals is methodologically challenging: you cannot instruct a chimpanzee to predict where a competitor will look. Early attempts (using behavioral choices, gaze direction) gave ambiguous results, largely because subjects could solve the tasks without attributing beliefs, by tracking behavioral regularities or perceptual access.

**The Anticipatory Looking Paradigm**

Krupenye, Call, and colleagues (2016) adapted the paradigm developed for human infants (using anticipatory gaze as a behavioral measure rather than explicit prediction) and applied it to great apes. In their study, apes watched videos in which a human agent observed an object being hidden in one location. While the agent was absent, the object was moved to a different location. When the agent returned, the apes' gaze anticipated where the agent would look — and importantly, they anticipated the agent would look where the agent had seen the object hidden, not where it actually was.

This result is significant because anticipatory gaze is a more implicit measure than explicit behavioral choice — it is less likely to be driven by explicit strategy or behavioral rule-following. The authors argue that the apes were tracking the agent's false belief about the object's location, providing evidence for genuine belief attribution.

The study has been replicated with some variation, but remains controversial. Critics argue that the anticipatory gaze could reflect tracking of the agent's behavioral history (the agent always went to that location before) or spatial-temporal regularities, rather than tracking the agent's mental state. Separating genuine belief attribution from sophisticated behavioral prediction is methodologically very difficult.

**Minimal Theory of Mind**

A productive middle ground in the debate is the concept of "minimal theory of mind" (Apperly and Butterfill). On this view, humans have two systems for understanding others' minds: an implicit, automatic system (system 1-like) that tracks basic mental state features (what the agent can see, what the agent expects) efficiently but with limited representational flexibility; and an explicit, deliberate system (system 2-like) that represents full propositional beliefs and reasons with them flexibly.

The implicit system can solve many theory-of-mind tasks without full propositional belief representation — it operates on more limited representations like "agent's anticipated location" or "agent's registered object." Great apes and human infants may have the implicit system without the explicit one; this would explain both their successes (anticipatory looking, gaze following, competitive food-seeking based on what a dominant can see) and their failures (explicit false-belief prediction in verbal tasks, complex belief-desire reasoning).

This framework suggests that theory of mind is not a single capacity that organisms either have or lack, but a family of capacities that may be differentially present in different species and developmental stages.

**Social Knowledge and Competitive Intelligence**

Even setting aside the false-belief question, the social cognition of great apes is clearly sophisticated in ways that go beyond simple behavioral conditioning. Chimpanzees in competitive food-seeking tasks adjust their behavior based on what a dominant competitor can see — they preferentially take food that the dominant cannot see, switch to new food when the dominant approaches what they have, and use novel routes to avoid detection. This requires tracking the visual perspective of a competitor and using this information strategically.

This competitive social cognition has been proposed as a driver of the evolution of theory of mind: in species where competition for resources depends on understanding what competitors know and do not know, social intelligence is under strong selection pressure. The "Machiavellian intelligence hypothesis" (Byrne and Whiten) proposes that the evolution of large primate brains was driven primarily by the demands of complex social reasoning and competitive social manipulation.
