# Language in Non-Human Animals

The question of whether non-human animals can acquire language has been one of the most dramatic in twentieth-century science, producing spectacular claims, bitter controversies, and lessons that remain relevant for understanding what language is and what kind of mind it requires. The history of animal language research is a case study in the philosophical difficulty of specifying what we are looking for when we look for "language."

**Early Ape Language Projects**

Beginning in the 1960s, researchers attempted to teach language to chimpanzees, working around the animals' vocal anatomy by using American Sign Language (Washoe, Gardner and Gardner), plastic tokens (Sarah, Premack), and computer keyboards (Lana, Rumbaugh; Kanzi, Savage-Rumbaugh). The results were impressive by certain measures: apes learned vocabularies of hundreds of symbols, combined symbols in productive ways, appeared to generate novel combinations, and some seemed to engage in genuine communicative exchange.

The controversy erupted with Herbert Terrace's project with a chimpanzee named Nim Chimpsky (a pun on Noam Chomsky). Terrace had initially reported that Nim had acquired language-like capacities in ASL, but a careful frame-by-frame analysis of training interactions revealed that a high proportion of Nim's "sentences" were imitations or expansions of trainer utterances, and that Nim showed little evidence of the syntactic structure or genuine communicative intent that characterizes human language. Terrace's 1979 reversal — concluding that apes do not acquire language — triggered widespread skepticism about the earlier positive results.

**Kanzi and Bonobo Language**

The most compelling case for ape language acquisition is Kanzi, a bonobo who was not formally trained but acquired lexigrams (symbols on a computerized keyboard) by observing his mother's training sessions. Kanzi's acquisition was more spontaneous and naturalistic than previous ape language subjects, and his comprehension of spoken English was remarkable — he could respond appropriately to novel spoken sentences with complex structure, distinguishing "put the ball in the water" from "put the water in the ball."

Savage-Rumbaugh and colleagues argued that Kanzi's comprehension demonstrated genuine linguistic understanding of syntactic structure. Critics pointed out that comprehension tasks are easier to pass by using context and semantic associations rather than parsing syntactic structure, and that the test sentences were not perfectly controlled for semantic plausibility. The debate continues.

**What Ape Language Research Reveals**

Whatever the final verdict on specific claims, ape language research has been philosophically productive. It has revealed several genuine capacities: referential communication (using symbols to refer to objects, not just to elicit behavior), symbolic representation (the ability to use arbitrary symbols in a way that is generative rather than merely conditioned), and productive combination (generating novel symbol combinations). These are genuine achievements that behaviorism denied were possible in non-human animals.

What the research has not convincingly demonstrated is the full structure of human language: recursive syntax (sentences embedded within sentences), displacement (referring to events far removed in time and space), generative novelty (generating genuinely new semantic content rather than recombining familiar elements), and free word order with syntactic rather than positional semantics. These may be the features of language that require the specifically human neural and cognitive resources — including the language faculty Chomsky and others have proposed.

**Animal Communication Systems**

Beyond ape language research, studies of natural animal communication systems have revealed that some animals have communication systems with more structure than simple signal-response conditioning. Bee waggle dance is a classic example: it encodes information about the direction and distance of food sources through the pattern of movements in the dance. It has something like displacement (it refers to food not currently present), but it lacks productivity and generativity.

More relevant to the language debate are studies of primate vocalizations. Vervet monkeys have distinct alarm calls for different predators (eagle, leopard, snake) that function as crude semantic categories — specific calls reliably produce specific responses in conspecifics. Chimpanzees combine calls in specific sequences that have consistent meanings. Some researchers have proposed that these combinatorial calls constitute a primitive form of syntax.

The study of animal communication systems is an active research area with significant implications for the evolution of language. If rudimentary syntactic and semantic capacities are present in our primate relatives, the evolution of full human language may have built on a pre-existing foundation rather than arising de novo. This evolutionary continuity would connect to broader arguments about the continuity of mind between humans and other animals.
