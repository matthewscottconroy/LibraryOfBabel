# Further Reading: Social Cognition in Animals

## Primary Sources

**David Premack and Guy Woodruff, "Does the Chimpanzee Have a Theory of Mind?" (1978), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*** — The paper that introduced the concept of "theory of mind" to comparative psychology, asking whether chimpanzees can attribute mental states to others. Premack and Woodruff showed that a chimpanzee could identify what a human would need to do to solve a problem, suggesting some capacity to understand intentional action, though the interpretation of these results has been contested.

**Christopher Krupenye, Fumihiro Kano, Satoshi Hirata, Josep Call, and Michael Tomasello, "Great Apes Anticipate that Other Individuals Will Act According to False Beliefs" (2016), *Science*** — Using a non-verbal anticipatory looking paradigm, this study showed that great apes anticipate where others will look based on the others' false beliefs—providing the strongest evidence yet for false-belief understanding in non-human primates and challenging earlier negative findings.

**Richard Byrne and Andrew Whiten (eds.), *Machiavellian Intelligence: Social Expertise and the Evolution of Intellect in Monkeys, Apes and Humans* (1988)** — The collection that introduced the "Machiavellian intelligence hypothesis"—the claim that primate intelligence evolved primarily in response to the demands of living in complex social groups. The hypothesis has been enormously productive in comparative cognition research.

**Brian Hare, Josep Call, and Michael Tomasello, "Do Chimpanzees Know What Conspecifics Know?" (2001), *Animal Behaviour*** — Showed that chimpanzees can use information about what dominant individuals have seen in food competition contexts, providing evidence for gaze following and some social knowledge attribution.

**Frans de Waal, *Chimpanzee Politics: Power and Sex among Apes* (1982)** — De Waal's careful field observations of chimpanzee political life in Arnhem Zoo, documenting alliance formation, deception, reconciliation, and the complexity of chimpanzee social strategy. This book established chimpanzee social intelligence as a major research topic.

## Secondary Literature

**Michael Tomasello, *A Natural History of Human Morality* (2016)** — Examines the evolution of cooperation, prosociality, and morality from shared intentionality, comparing human and non-human primate social cognition to identify what is specifically human about moral norms.

**Josep Call and Michael Tomasello, "Does the Chimpanzee Have a Theory of Mind? 30 Years Later" (2008), *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*** — A comprehensive review of what 30 years of research has established about chimpanzee social cognition, arguing that chimpanzees understand goals, perceptions, and knowledge but probably not beliefs.

**Carel van Schaik and Gavin Isler, "Brain Size and Intelligence in Vertebrates: Proximate and Ultimate Causes" (2012)** — Examines the relationship between brain size, social complexity, and cognitive ability across vertebrates, providing evolutionary context for understanding the relationship between intelligence and social cognition.

**Kristin Andrews, *Do Apes Read Minds? Toward a New Folk Psychology* (2012)** — A philosophical analysis of what comparative cognition research shows about the attribution of mental states to non-human primates, arguing for a "mindreading" framework that is neither as demanding as human theory of mind nor as deflationary as behavioral conditioning accounts.

**Robin Dunbar, *Grooming, Gossip, and the Evolution of Language* (1996)** — Dunbar's social brain hypothesis argues that language evolved as a form of social bonding in large human groups—a "vocal grooming" system—providing an evolutionary account of the relationship between social cognition, language, and brain size.
