# Key Thinkers: Social Cognition in Animals

---

**David Premack (1925–2015)**
American psychologist at the University of Pennsylvania and the University of Paris. Premack, with Woodruff, introduced the concept of "theory of mind" to comparative psychology in their 1978 paper asking whether chimpanzees can attribute mental states to others. His earlier work on language learning in chimpanzees (with Sarah the chimp) established comparative cognition as a serious research enterprise. While his initial results suggested theory of mind in chimpanzees, subsequent decades of research have produced a more qualified picture.
*Key works:* "Does the Chimpanzee Have a Theory of Mind?" (1978, with Woodruff), "Minds without Language" (1988)

---

**Frans de Waal (1948–2019)**
Dutch-American primatologist and ethologist at Emory University. De Waal was the foremost advocate for the depth and complexity of primate social life, documenting alliance formation, reconciliation, empathy, cooperation, and fairness in chimpanzees, bonobos, and other primates. His concept of "chimpanzee politics" and his ethological observations of Machiavellian social strategies showed that primate social cognition is far more sophisticated than behaviorist accounts acknowledged. His broader work on animal empathy and the origins of human morality has influenced ethics and evolutionary biology.
*Key works:* *Chimpanzee Politics* (1982), *Good Natured: The Origins of Right and Wrong in Humans and Other Animals* (1996), *The Age of Empathy* (2009)

---

**Richard Byrne (1948–)**
British primatologist and comparative psychologist at the University of St. Andrews. Byrne co-edited the influential *Machiavellian Intelligence* volume (1988) that introduced the Machiavellian intelligence hypothesis, and his research on primate tactical deception, imitation, and hierarchical action planning has been important for understanding the scope and character of primate social cognition. His work on how gorillas and chimpanzees imitate complex sequences of action has contributed to understanding the evolution of cultural learning.
*Key works:* *Machiavellian Intelligence* (1988, with Whiten), *The Thinking Ape: Evolutionary Origins of Intelligence* (1995)

---

**Michael Tomasello (1950–)**
American developmental and comparative psychologist at Duke University. Tomasello's comprehensive comparative program has been the most systematic in identifying what distinguishes human from non-human primate social cognition. He argues that the key difference is shared intentionality—genuine joint attention and cooperative communication—rather than raw intelligence. His research on the false-belief task in chimpanzees (largely negative) and in human infants (positive) has been central to debates about what understanding mental states requires, and his later work using non-verbal paradigms has produced more mixed results.
*Key works:* *The Cultural Origins of Human Cognition* (1999), *Becoming Human* (2019), *Does the Chimpanzee Have a Theory of Mind? 30 Years Later* (2008, with Call)

---

**Christopher Krupenye**
American comparative psychologist at Johns Hopkins University. Krupenye's 2016 Science paper with colleagues demonstrated that great apes anticipate where others will search based on the others' false beliefs—a non-verbal paradigm that avoided the verbal demands of standard false-belief tasks. This finding reopened the debate about false-belief understanding in non-human primates and suggested that earlier negative findings may have underestimated ape social cognition due to methodological confounds.
*Key works:* "Great Apes Anticipate that Other Individuals Will Act According to False Beliefs" (2016)
