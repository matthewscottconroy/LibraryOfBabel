# Key Thinkers: Animal Ethics and Mind

---

**Peter Singer (1946–)**
Australian utilitarian philosopher at Princeton University. Singer's *Animal Liberation* (1975) applied utilitarian principles to argue for radical revision of the human treatment of animals. His key move was to argue that the capacity to suffer—not rationality, species membership, or linguistic ability—is the morally relevant criterion, and that ignoring animal suffering because it is animal suffering is "speciesism" analogous to racism and sexism. His preference utilitarian framework requires that we give equal weight to equal interests regardless of species, generating strong obligations to minimize animal suffering in food production, experimentation, and other practices.
*Key works:* *Animal Liberation* (1975), *Practical Ethics* (1979/2011), *The Expanding Circle* (1981)

---

**Tom Regan (1938–2017)**
American philosopher at North Carolina State University. Regan's rights-based approach to animal ethics argues that all "subjects-of-a-life"—creatures with a complex mental life including beliefs, desires, memory, emotion, and an experiential welfare—have inherent value that entitles them to rights. Unlike Singer's utilitarian calculus, Regan's rights framework is absolutist: the rights of subjects-of-a-life cannot be overridden by aggregate utility. He argued for the abolition rather than the regulation of animal use, generating practical conclusions more radical than Singer's while using a different theoretical framework.
*Key works:* *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983), *Empty Cages: Facing the Challenge of Animal Rights* (2004)

---

**David DeGrazia (1961–)**
American philosopher at George Washington University. DeGrazia's work carefully examines the evidence for animal mental life—including consciousness, self-awareness, emotion, social cognition, and autonomy—and derives moral implications from these findings. His account of "multiple selves" and graduated moral status resists both the view that animals have no moral status and the view that they have the same status as humans, arguing instead that moral status tracks cognitive and experiential complexity. His rigorous engagement with both philosophy and cognitive science makes his work the best bridge between animal cognition research and animal ethics.
*Key works:* *Taking Animals Seriously* (1996), *Human Identity and Bioethics* (2005)

---

**Christine Korsgaard (1952–)**
American Kantian philosopher at Harvard University. Korsgaard's *Fellow Creatures* (2018) develops a Kantian argument for animal ethics that does not depend on attributing full rational autonomy to animals. She argues that animals have a perspective on what is good or bad for them—a form of subjective valuing—and that this is sufficient to generate obligations toward them. Her account gives theoretical grounding for animal moral status within a Kantian framework that has traditionally been seen as excluding animals.
*Key works:* *Creating the Kingdom of Ends* (1996), *Self-Constitution: Agency, Identity, and Integrity* (2009), *Fellow Creatures* (2018)

---

**Martha Nussbaum (1947–)**
American philosopher of law and ethics at the University of Chicago. Nussbaum's capabilities approach, originally developed for human development ethics, has been extended to animals in *Upholding Animal Dignity* and *Justice for Animals* (2023). The framework holds that each animal species has characteristic forms of flourishing and capabilities, and that we have obligations—grounded in dignity rather than sentience alone—to support rather than impede the flourishing of each species. Her approach is more sensitive to species differences and less demanding of sentience than utilitarian accounts.
*Key works:* *Frontiers of Justice* (2006), *Justice for Animals* (2023)
