# The Turing Test and What It Tests

Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" opens with a proposal: instead of asking "Can machines think?" — a question Turing considered ill-formed — we should ask whether a machine can pass the "Imitation Game." A human interrogator conducts text-based conversations with two participants, one human and one machine, and attempts to identify which is which. If the machine can fool the interrogator as often as the human can, the machine passes. Turing's proposal has been enormously influential but also persistently misunderstood.

**The Original Proposal**

Turing's original formulation was actually a three-party game: a man (A), a woman (B), and an interrogator (C) who cannot see the others and communicates by written text. The interrogator tries to determine which is the man and which the woman; the man tries to deceive; the woman tries to help the interrogator. Turing then asks: what happens if a machine takes the man's role? Can it substitute for a human in a task that requires flexible, intelligent, deceptive communication?

Several features of this framing are important. The test is behavioral and third-personal: it asks about what the machine does, not what it experiences. It is not a test for consciousness, genuine understanding, or inner life; it is a test for behavioral indistinguishability from intelligent human performance in open-ended conversational exchange. Turing himself was explicitly aware of the hard problem: he bracketed questions about machine consciousness as unanswerable and proposed instead to focus on what could be tested.

Turing's own view was that a sufficiently sophisticated machine would pass the test, and he thought this would be practically equivalent to intelligence: if it walks like a duck and quacks like a duck, what more are we looking for? But this inference from behavioral equivalence to mental equivalence is exactly what Searle's Chinese Room Argument challenged.

**The Turing Test in Practice**

The Loebner Prize has been awarded annually since 1991 for chatbot performance in the Turing Test, with a Gold Prize (never yet awarded) for a system that could pass the full test. The prize competitions have revealed interesting things about both the difficulty of the test and the possibility of gaming it.

Early competition winners generally did not achieve genuine conversational intelligence but rather exploited the expectations and biases of human judges: using humor, deflection, apparent emotional responses, and tactical topic changes to seem more human than they were. This "Turing trapping" — winning the test not by being intelligent but by exploiting cognitive biases in the judges — suggests that the test is not a perfectly reliable indicator of intelligence.

In 2014, a chatbot named Eugene Goostman was widely reported as "passing the Turing Test" in a competition at the Royal Society, London. Eugene, presenting as a 13-year-old Ukrainian boy (which gave it cover for imperfect English and limited knowledge), fooled 33% of judges — above the threshold set by the competition. This was controversial: most researchers felt that Eugene's performance reflected clever self-presentation and judge confusion rather than genuine intelligence.

**What the Test Does and Does Not Establish**

The Turing Test has been criticized on several grounds.

The "wrong question" objection (including Searle's Chinese Room): behavioral indistinguishability from intelligent performance does not establish intelligence, understanding, or consciousness. A perfect behavioral mimic that had no inner life would pass the test; the test is too easily fooled by surface behavior.

The "too hard" objection: the full unrestricted Turing test, conducted with sophisticated human interlocutors across a wide range of topics, is an extraordinarily demanding test. It tests not just cognitive performance but the accumulated knowledge, common sense, personality, and conversational style of a lifetime. Passing the full test may be harder than most aspects of human cognitive performance, making it a poor practical test of "intelligence."

The philosophical significance of the test is not primarily practical — it is the clarification of what "intelligence" means when stripped of assumptions about biological substrate. Turing's move was to ask: what is it that matters about intelligence, and can it be separated from the specific biological and phenomenological form in which we happen to encounter it? This is still the right question, and the Turing Test's limitations are philosophically revealing about what a full answer to it would require.
