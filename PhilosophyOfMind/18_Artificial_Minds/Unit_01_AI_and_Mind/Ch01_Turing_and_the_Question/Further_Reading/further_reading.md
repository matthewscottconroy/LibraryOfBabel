# Further Reading: Turing and the Question of Machine Intelligence

## Primary Sources

**Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), *Mind*** — The foundational paper proposing the "imitation game" as a test for machine intelligence and addressing nine objections to machine intelligence including theological, mathematical, and consciousness objections. Turing's "Polite Convention" suggests that if a machine can fool us into thinking it is human in conversation, the question of whether it is "really" intelligent is merely semantic—a position that has generated decades of debate.

**John Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*** — The Chinese Room argument: a person who does not understand Chinese could follow rules for manipulating Chinese symbols to produce appropriate outputs, passing any functional test without understanding anything. Searle argues that syntax (formal symbol manipulation) is not sufficient for semantics (genuine meaning and understanding), challenging computational functionalism about mind.

**Hubert Dreyfus, *What Computers Can't Do: A Critique of Artificial Reason* (1972)** — Dreyfus's phenomenological critique of AI argued that classical AI—based on rule-following symbol manipulation—could not capture the background practical understanding, embodied skill, and context-sensitivity that human cognition depends on. His critique, drawing on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, was initially dismissed but has been substantially vindicated.

**Jack Copeland, *Artificial Intelligence: A Philosophical Introduction* (1993)** — A balanced and rigorous philosophical introduction to AI and mind, covering the Turing test, the Chinese Room argument, connectionism, and the philosophical implications of AI research. Suitable for students seeking an accessible overview.

**Margaret Boden, *The Philosophy of Artificial Intelligence* (ed., 1990)** — An excellent anthology collecting key papers from Turing through Dreyfus, Searle, and connectionism debates. Includes Turing's original paper, Searle's Chinese Room, and important responses.

## Secondary Literature

**Jack Copeland (ed.), *The Essential Turing: Seminal Writings in Computing, Logic, Philosophy, Artificial Intelligence, and Artificial Life* (2004)** — Collects Turing's foundational papers with editorial commentary, providing context for understanding Turing's contributions to computability theory, the imitation game, and the philosophy of mind.

**Ned Block, "Psychologism and Behaviorism" (1981), *Philosophical Review*** — Block's "blockhead" argument against the Turing Test as a criterion for intelligence: a system could pass the Turing Test by table-lookup rather than genuine thought, showing that behavioral equivalence is not sufficient for mentality.

**Daniel Dennett, "Can Machines Think?" (1985)** — Dennett's response to Searle and other skeptics, arguing that the Turing Test is a better criterion for machine intelligence than critics suppose. His defense engages with both the philosophical and practical dimensions of the question.

**Margaret Boden, *Computer Models of Mind: Computational Approaches in Theoretical Psychology* (1988)** — Examines how computational models in cognitive science illuminate human cognition, covering both symbolic AI and connectionist approaches.

**Luciano Floridi and J.W. Cowls, "A Unified Framework of Five Principles for AI in Society" (2019), *Harvard Data Science Review*** — Develops ethical principles for AI (beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, explicability) that presuppose thoughtful distinctions between AI capabilities and limitations.
