# Key Thinkers: Turing and the Question of Machine Intelligence

---

**Alan Turing (1912–1954)**
British mathematician, logician, and computer scientist. Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" proposed the imitation game as a test for machine intelligence, sidestepping the philosophical question "Can machines think?" in favor of the behavioral question of whether a machine could fool an interrogator into thinking it was human. His paper also addressed nine objections to machine intelligence—including the theological objection, the argument from consciousness, and the argument from mathematical limitations—and proposed a learning machine program as the path to machine intelligence. His theoretical work on computability (Turing machines) provided the mathematical foundations for computer science.
*Key works:* "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), "On Computable Numbers" (1936)

---

**John Searle (1932–)**
American philosopher of mind and language at UC Berkeley. Searle's Chinese Room argument (1980) challenged computational functionalism by arguing that formal symbol manipulation—however sophisticated—cannot constitute genuine understanding. A person who follows rules for producing Chinese responses from Chinese inputs without understanding Chinese does not thereby understand Chinese; so neither does a computer. His argument claims to show that syntax is never sufficient for semantics, and that genuine intentionality requires biological implementation. The Chinese Room has been the most discussed thought experiment in AI philosophy.
*Key works:* "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980), *Intentionality* (1983), *The Rediscovery of the Mind* (1992)

---

**Hubert Dreyfus (1929–2017)**
American philosopher at UC Berkeley. Dreyfus's phenomenological critique of classical AI, developed through successive editions of *What Computers Can't Do* (1972) and *What Computers Still Can't Do* (1992), argued that AI's failure to produce human-level performance in a wide range of tasks reflects fundamental architectural limitations. Drawing on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty, he argued that human cognition depends on embodied, practical understanding—the ability to cope skillfully with a situation—that cannot be captured in explicit rules or representations. His predictions have been substantially confirmed by the historical arc of classical AI's limitations.
*Key works:* *What Computers Can't Do* (1972), *What Computers Still Can't Do* (1992), *Being-in-the-World* (1991)

---

**Margaret Boden (1936–)**
British cognitive scientist and philosopher of AI at the University of Sussex. Boden has provided some of the most rigorous and balanced philosophical treatments of AI, examining both its capacities and limitations. Her work on computational creativity—asking whether computers can be genuinely creative—distinguishes combinatorial, exploratory, and transformational creativity, arguing that computers can exhibit some forms but perhaps not all. Her comprehensive histories of cognitive science and AI have also been influential.
*Key works:* *The Creative Mind: Myths and Mechanisms* (1990), *Computer Models of Mind* (1988), *Mind as Machine: A History of Cognitive Science* (2006)

---

**Ned Block (1942–)**
American philosopher at NYU. Block's "Blockhead" argument against the Turing Test argues that a system could pass the test by table lookup—storing canned responses to all possible inputs—without thereby possessing anything like genuine understanding or intelligence. His argument that behavioral indistinguishability from human intelligence does not establish genuine intelligence has been important for evaluating what the Turing Test can and cannot establish about machine minds.
*Key works:* "Psychologism and Behaviorism" (1981), "The Mind as the Software of the Brain" (1990)
