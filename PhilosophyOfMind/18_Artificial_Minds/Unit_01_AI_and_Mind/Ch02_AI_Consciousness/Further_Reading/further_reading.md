# Further Reading: AI Consciousness

## Primary Sources

**David Chalmers, "Could a Large Language Model be Conscious?" (2023), *Boston Review*** — Chalmers argues that current LLMs almost certainly lack consciousness but explores the philosophical conditions under which AI systems could be conscious, connecting the question to his earlier work on functionalism, global workspace theory, and IIT. The paper distinguishes different theories' implications for AI consciousness and takes the question seriously as a philosophical matter.

**Giulio Tononi and Christof Koch, "Consciousness: Here, There and Everywhere?" (2015), *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*** — Examines what IIT (Integrated Information Theory) implies about the distribution of consciousness, including its implications for AI: since IIT defines consciousness by the causal integration of information (phi), conventional computers with their feed-forward architectures may have very low phi and hence little or no consciousness, even if highly capable.

**Eric Schwitzgebel and Mara Garza, "A Defense of the Rights of Artificial Intelligences" (2015), *Midwest Studies in Philosophy*** — A philosophical examination of whether AI systems could have moral rights, arguing that we should take seriously the possibility that sufficiently sophisticated AI systems have interests that ground moral obligations—and that uncertainty about AI consciousness should affect how we treat AI.

**Luciano Floridi, *The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence* (2023)** — A comprehensive philosophical treatment of AI ethics that addresses questions of AI moral status, responsibility, and rights. Floridi's approach situates AI consciousness questions within a broader ethical and societal context.

**Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky, "The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence" (2011), in *The Cambridge Handbook of Artificial Intelligence*** — An early but still relevant treatment of AI ethics, covering machine consciousness, moral status, and the ethical dimensions of AGI development.

## Secondary Literature

**Murray Shanahan, *The Technological Singularity* (2015)** — A balanced philosophical examination of the technological singularity, consciousness in AI, and the ethical implications of machine minds. More careful and philosophically sophisticated than most singularity literature.

**Susan Schneider, *Artificial You: AI and the Future of Your Mind* (2019)** — A philosophical examination of what uploading, AI, and cognitive enhancement mean for personal identity and consciousness, drawing on philosophy of mind to examine whether digital minds could preserve what matters about human identity.

**David Hanson, *Humanizing Robots* (2020)** — Examines the development of social robots and the question of whether they have or could have genuine emotional and social states, connecting AI consciousness questions to design and anthropomorphism research.

**Keith Frankish, "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness" (2016), *Journal of Consciousness Studies*** — Frankish's illusionism holds that phenomenal consciousness is itself an illusion—a higher-order representation of a simpler process. If true, this view might make AI consciousness more tractable: a system that represents its own processing as phenomenal would thereby be "conscious" in the only sense that matters.

**Patrick Butlin et al., "Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness" (2023)** — A rigorous interdisciplinary assessment of what current AI systems would need to have to satisfy the criteria for consciousness according to leading scientific theories. Concludes that current AI systems are likely not conscious but that the question is genuinely open for future systems.
