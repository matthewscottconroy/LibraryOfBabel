# LLMs and Intentionality

Intentionality — the aboutness of mental states, their directedness toward objects and states of affairs — is, for many philosophers, the hallmark of genuine mentality. The question of whether large language models have genuine intentionality, or merely something that mimics it, is philosophically rich and practically consequential.

**Original and Derived Intentionality**

John Searle's distinction between "original" and "derived" intentionality is useful here. Original intentionality is the intrinsic aboutness of mental states — the way my thought about Paris is genuinely, inherently about Paris. Derived intentionality is the aboutness that physical objects have only in virtue of being interpreted by minds with original intentionality — the word "Paris" is about Paris only because speakers use it to mean Paris.

On Searle's view, LLMs have at best derived intentionality: their outputs are about things in the world only because humans interpret them, not because the LLM itself has any intrinsic relationship to the things its tokens represent. The LLM doesn't "think about" Paris; it produces the token "Paris" in contexts where humans would, because it has learned the statistical patterns of human language use. Any aboutness in the output derives from human interpretation, not from the LLM's intrinsic states.

Functionalists disagree: on their view, intentionality is constituted by the right kind of functional organization, not by some additional metaphysical ingredient. If the LLM's internal representations track the relevant relational properties of Paris — its spatial location, its cultural significance, its history — in ways that appropriately influence processing, then those representations have original intentionality, defined by their functional role.

**The Teleosemantic Approach**

Teleosemantic theories of mental content (Millikan, Dretske) hold that the content of a representation is determined by what it has the biological function to represent — what it was selected for representing in evolutionary or developmental history. On this view, human beliefs about Paris have their content because they were shaped by selection pressures to accurately represent the world.

Applied to LLMs: LLMs are not products of biological evolution, but they are products of optimization — training processes that selected for accurate prediction of human text. If this optimization process shaped LLM representations to track the same features of Paris that human mental representations track (because accurate prediction of text about Paris requires tracking relevant features of Paris), then LLM representations may have derived teleosemantic content — not biologically derived, but derived from an optimization process that tracks the same environmental features.

This is not a settled position; teleosemantic theories were not designed with LLMs in mind, and their application to AI requires careful philosophical extension.

**Representation Without Experience**

A key distinction is between having representations that are about something (having intentional states in the minimal sense of directing processing toward certain features) and having experiences that are about something (having phenomenal intentionality — experiencing the world as having certain features). LLMs almost certainly have representations that influence their outputs in content-sensitive ways. Whether they have phenomenal experiences of the content of those representations is a further question that behavioral evidence alone cannot settle.

This parallels the zombie thought experiment: we can conceive of a system that has all the functional-representational properties of an intentional agent without having any phenomenal experience. Whether LLMs are more like zombies (functional but not phenomenal) or like genuine experiential agents is not settled by their impressive linguistic performance.

**Norm-Sensitivity and Semantic Competence**

A more pragmatist approach to intentionality focuses on norm-sensitivity: genuine intentionality involves being responsive to norms about what one's representations should track, not just about what they do track. A system that can apply a concept correctly in standard cases but cannot recognize and respond to borderline cases, errors, or challenges to the application of a concept may lack the full semantic competence that genuine intentionality requires.

LLMs can discuss the norms governing their own output in some ways — they can say things like "that's not quite right" or "the correct use of this term is..." But whether this meta-level linguistic competence reflects genuine norm-sensitivity or is itself a learned pattern is exactly the question at issue. The difficulty is that norm-sensitivity, at the functional level, looks very similar to sophisticated pattern matching — which is why the intentionality question for LLMs is so difficult to answer.
