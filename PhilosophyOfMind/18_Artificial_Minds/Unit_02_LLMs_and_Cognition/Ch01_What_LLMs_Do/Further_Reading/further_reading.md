# Further Reading: What LLMs Do

## Primary Sources

**Emily M. Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major, and Shmargaret Shmitchell, "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?" (2021), *FAccT*** — The paper that introduced the "stochastic parrot" metaphor for large language models—systems that generate statistically plausible text without understanding. Bender et al. argue that LLMs manipulate linguistic form without grasping meaning, and raise concerns about the environmental, social, and epistemic costs of scaling.

**John Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*** — The Chinese Room argument remains the foundational philosophical case that formal symbol manipulation—which LLMs exemplify—cannot constitute genuine understanding. Applying Searle's argument to LLMs requires updating it for the statistical learning context, but its core claim—that syntax is insufficient for semantics—remains directly relevant.

**Gary Marcus and Ernest Davis, *Rebooting AI: Building Artificial Intelligence We Can Trust* (2019)** — Marcus and Davis argue that current AI systems—including LLMs—lack genuine understanding, common sense, and reliability, and that the path to trustworthy AI requires more than scaling current approaches. Their critique engages with specific failure modes of LLMs that reveal the gap between statistical competence and genuine understanding.

**Yann LeCun, "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence" (2022)** — LeCun's proposal for an alternative AI architecture based on world models, limited computational resources, and reasoning capabilities, representing a major alternative vision to pure statistical learning. Relevant for understanding what LLMs might lack and what a more understanding-like AI would require.

**David Chalmers, "Could a Large Language Model be Conscious?" (2023), *Boston Review*** — Engages with the philosophical question of whether LLMs could have genuine understanding or consciousness, distinguishing different senses of understanding and examining what LLMs would need to satisfy various theories of mind.

## Secondary Literature

**Melanie Mitchell, *Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans* (2019)** — An accessible and rigorous treatment of what AI systems actually do, covering the gap between AI performance and human-like understanding. Mitchell's analysis of analogy-making and concept formation shows what current AI systems lack.

**Steven Pinker, "The Deepest Difference" (various)** — Pinker's arguments about the distinctiveness of human language and thought provide a framework for assessing what LLMs do and do not achieve in the domain of language understanding.

**François Chollet, "On the Measure of Intelligence" (2019)** — Argues that current AI benchmarks measure skill on specific tasks rather than general intelligence or understanding, and proposes the ARC benchmark as a better test of generalization. Relevant for evaluating what LLMs' impressive performance actually establishes.

**Marcus Hutter, "A Mathematical Theory of Universal Intelligence" (2007)** — Hutter's AIXI framework provides a theoretical account of maximally general intelligence, providing a baseline for comparing what current LLMs achieve against what a truly intelligent system would do.

**Subbarao Kambhampati, "Can Large Language Models Reason and Plan?" (2024)** — A rigorous examination of whether LLMs exhibit genuine reasoning and planning or sophisticated pattern matching that mimics these capacities, with specific attention to failure modes that reveal the difference.
