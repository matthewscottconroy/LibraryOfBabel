# Key Thinkers: What LLMs Do

---

**Emily M. Bender (1969–)**
American computational linguist at the University of Washington. Bender's "stochastic parrots" paper (2021), co-authored with Timnit Gebru and colleagues, argued that large language models generate statistically plausible text without grasping meaning—they "parrot" statistical patterns without understanding. The Octopus Thought Experiment in her earlier "Climbing towards NLU" paper (with Koller, 2020) distinguishes form-based language processing from genuine semantic grounding, arguing that LLMs cannot acquire meaning from form alone. Her work has been important for maintaining philosophical rigor in discussions of what LLMs actually do.
*Key works:* "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" (2021), "Climbing towards NLU: On Meaning, Form, and Understanding in the Age of Data" (2020, with Koller)

---

**Gary Marcus (1970–)**
American cognitive scientist and AI critic at NYU. Marcus has been one of the most prominent critics of pure statistical learning approaches to AI, arguing that LLMs and similar systems lack the systematic, compositional understanding required for reliable reasoning and common sense. His work distinguishes between narrow statistical competence and genuine cognitive flexibility, arguing that current AI approaches will not scale to AGI without fundamentally different architectures that incorporate structured, symbolic representations alongside neural learning.
*Key works:* *Rebooting AI* (2019, with Davis), *The Algebraic Mind* (2001), various public essays on AI limitations

---

**John Searle (1932–)**
American philosopher at UC Berkeley. Searle's Chinese Room argument (1980) is the most widely discussed philosophical challenge to the view that formal symbol manipulation constitutes genuine understanding. Applied to LLMs, the argument suggests that statistical text generation—however sophisticated—is a form of symbol manipulation that lacks the semantic grounding that constitutes genuine understanding. The debate about whether LLMs constitute counterexamples to the Chinese Room has reinvigorated philosophical discussion of intentionality and meaning.
*Key works:* "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980), *Intentionality* (1983)

---

**Melanie Mitchell (1969–)**
American cognitive scientist at the Santa Fe Institute. Mitchell's research on analogy-making and her critical analyses of AI capabilities have been important for understanding what current AI systems lack. Her book *Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans* (2019) provides some of the most rigorous and accessible analyses of the gap between AI performance on benchmarks and genuine human-like understanding, drawing on her expertise in cognitive science and AI research.
*Key works:* *Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for Thinking Humans* (2019), *Analogy-Making as Perception* (1993)

---

**Timnit Gebru (1983–)**
Ethiopian-American computer scientist and AI ethics researcher, former co-lead of Google's Ethical AI team, founder of DAIR (Distributed AI Research). Gebru co-authored the "stochastic parrots" paper and has been a prominent voice for attention to the social harms of large AI systems—including environmental costs, bias, and the displacement of meaningful work. Her work connects technical questions about what LLMs do to broader ethical and societal questions about the costs and consequences of deploying these systems at scale.
*Key works:* "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" (2021), various papers on bias in facial recognition and NLP
