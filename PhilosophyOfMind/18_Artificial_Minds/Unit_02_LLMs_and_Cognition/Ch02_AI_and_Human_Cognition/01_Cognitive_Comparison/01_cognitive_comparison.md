# Comparing AI and Human Cognition

As AI systems have become more capable, the project of comparing AI and human cognition has become both more tractable and more philosophically rich. Such comparisons reveal both striking similarities and striking differences — and working out the significance of each advances our understanding of both human and artificial cognition.

**Areas of Similarity**

LLMs and humans show striking similarities in some areas of linguistic and cognitive performance. Both exhibit sensitivity to semantic context, both can engage in some forms of analogical reasoning, and both produce language that reflects complex patterns of discourse coherence. LLMs have been shown to exhibit effects analogous to certain human cognitive phenomena: they show something like "priming" (earlier context influences later outputs), something like "anchoring" (initial information biases subsequent processing), and something like the availability heuristic (outcomes that are well-represented in training data are treated as more likely).

These parallels are philosophically interesting. They could reflect two possibilities: either the similarities arise because both systems have implemented similar cognitive mechanisms (suggesting something deep in common), or they arise because LLMs have learned to mimic the surface statistical patterns of human language, which reflects human cognitive patterns, without implementing the underlying mechanisms. Distinguishing these possibilities is methodologically difficult.

Research in computational cognitive science has found that the internal representations of LLMs often align with representations found in human neural activity. Neural network models of language processing predict patterns of brain activation measured by fMRI and EEG better than many explicit cognitive models. This "neural alignment" suggests that the statistical structure of language itself may be deeply shaped by cognitive structure, and that learning this structure may involve developing representations that partly mirror those in human brains — even when the learning algorithm (backpropagation through a transformer) is very different from the biological learning processes.

**Areas of Difference**

The differences between AI and human cognition are equally significant. Human cognition is embodied, situated, and motivated in ways that AI cognition is not. Humans learn about objects by interacting with them, not just by reading about them; they understand pain, pleasure, hunger, and social connection through lived experience, not text description. The grounding of human concepts in embodied experience may give them a richness and coherence that LLM concepts lack.

Human cognition is also continuous, lifelong, and self-updating: humans accumulate experience over time, update their beliefs in light of new evidence, form stable preferences and values that evolve over their lives. Current LLMs lack persistent memory across conversations (in standard deployment), do not update their parameters through ongoing experience (outside of retraining), and lack the motivational structure of biological agents.

Human cognition is evolutionarily adapted: the cognitive capacities humans have were shaped by selection pressures that LLMs do not face. The result is that humans have specific cognitive biases, emotional responses, and social cognitive tendencies that are not arbitrary — they reflect a long evolutionary history of problem-solving. LLMs have no such evolutionary history; they have statistical biases from training data and optimization pressures from the training objective.

**What the Comparison Reveals**

The comparison between AI and human cognition is philosophically productive because it separates what might seem to be necessarily connected. Human cognition is typically embodied, motivated, continuous, and biological. When AI systems achieve some aspects of human cognitive performance without sharing these features, we learn which aspects of cognition require embodiment, motivation, or biological substrate — and which do not.

For instance: LLMs achieve impressive performance on many language tasks without embodiment, suggesting that some aspects of language competence do not require embodied experience. This is consistent with the view that language is partly autonomous from embodiment (though it leaves open whether the deepest aspects of meaning require embodied grounding). Conversely, the failures of LLMs — on tasks requiring causal reasoning, physical understanding, or genuine novelty — may indicate which aspects of cognition do require something that LLMs lack: embodied experience, evolutionary adaptation, or genuine intentionality.
