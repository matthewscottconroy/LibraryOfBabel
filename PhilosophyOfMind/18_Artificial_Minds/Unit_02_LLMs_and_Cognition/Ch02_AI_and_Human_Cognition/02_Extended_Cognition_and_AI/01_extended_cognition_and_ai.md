# Extended Cognition and AI

The Clark-Chalmers extended mind thesis holds that cognitive processes can extend beyond the brain and body to include external tools, devices, and artifacts when these are appropriately coupled with the brain's processes. The emergence of AI as a cognitive tool raises new and challenging questions for this thesis: When humans use AI systems as cognitive tools — for writing, reasoning, remembering, or deciding — does the AI system become part of the human's extended cognitive system? And if so, what are the philosophical and ethical implications?

**The Parity Principle Applied to AI**

Recall the parity principle: if an external process plays the same functional role in cognition that an internal process would play, we should count the external process as cognitive. Clark and Chalmers applied this to Otto's notebook: because the notebook plays the same functional role in guiding Otto's actions that his internal memory would, it is part of his cognitive system.

Applied to AI: when a person uses an LLM to help reason through a problem — offloading working memory, exploring implications, generating options — the LLM is playing a functional role that internal reasoning processes would otherwise play. By the parity principle, the LLM should count as part of the person's extended cognitive system during this interaction.

This is an interesting and potentially radical conclusion. It suggests that a person reasoning with an LLM has a cognitive system that is not bounded by their skin, but extends into the AI model. Their cognitive processes are partly constituted by the AI's computations. The thoughts they arrive at through AI-assisted reasoning are, in some sense, their thoughts — even though a significant part of the process occurred outside their brain.

**Critiques Applied to AI Integration**

The standard critiques of the extended mind thesis apply with particular force to AI integration. The coupling-constitution fallacy (Adams and Aizawa): just because the AI system is causally coupled to the person's reasoning does not mean it is constitutively part of their cognitive system. External tools causally influence cognition without being cognitively internal.

The mark of the cognitive objection: not all external processes that influence cognition deserve to count as cognitive. The AI system may be doing something that is very different from the human cognitive processes it assists — something that does not have the same kind of intentional directedness, phenomenal character, or rational coherence. If so, it should not count as cognitive even if it plays a similar functional role.

The trust and reliability concern: Clark and Chalmers's conditions for extended memory include that the information be reliably accessible and automatically endorsed. AI outputs do not straightforwardly satisfy these conditions: LLMs hallucinate, produce confident falsehoods, and may not reliably track truth. The human using an LLM must critically evaluate its outputs, which is a significant disanalogy from retrieving memories from one's notebook.

**Cognitive Enhancement and Dependence**

Even setting aside the strict extended mind thesis, AI as a cognitive tool raises important questions about cognitive enhancement and cognitive dependence. If people increasingly rely on AI for writing, reasoning, calculation, and memory, what happens to the corresponding internal capacities? The analogy with previous cognitive technologies (writing, calculators, GPS navigation) is instructive: there is evidence that GPS use reduces development of spatial navigation skills, and that calculator dependence can impair mental arithmetic.

But the analogy has limits. Writing enabled forms of reasoning that would be impossible without it, not merely a crutch for pre-existing capacities. Calculator use enables mathematical practice that human mental arithmetic cannot reach. The question is whether AI assistance enhances human cognition by enabling new forms of thinking, or diminishes it by reducing the exercise of existing capacities — or, as is probably the case, both simultaneously in different domains.

**Identity, Authorship, and AI**

Extended mind applied to AI raises specific questions about identity, authorship, and autonomy. If an AI system is part of my extended cognitive system when I use it, then the thoughts and products that emerge from that system are mine — I am their author, not the AI. But this claim sits uncomfortably with intuitions about what authorship requires.

When an LLM generates text that I use, did I write it? If I substantially directed the generation through prompting, edited and revised it, and take intellectual responsibility for it, the answer seems closer to yes. If I simply accepted the first output without engagement, the answer seems closer to no. The question of authorship in AI-assisted work is not just philosophical but has immediate practical significance for academic integrity, intellectual property law, and journalistic standards.
