# Further Reading: AI and Human Cognition

## Primary Sources

**Andy Clark and David Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (1998), *Analysis*** — Clark and Chalmers' extended mind thesis holds that cognitive processes can extend beyond the skin into the environment. Applied to AI tools, this suggests that AI assistants could become genuine parts of the cognitive systems of users who rely on them—not merely external tools but constitutive components of thought.

**Andy Clark, *Supersizing the Mind: Embodiment, Action, and Cognitive Extension* (2008)** — Clark's comprehensive development of the extended mind thesis argues that the boundaries of cognition are neither fixed nor anatomical but depend on functional integration. This framework naturally extends to AI tools, raising the question of when AI systems become cognitive partners rather than mere instruments.

**Frank Pasquale, *New Laws of Robotics: Defending Human Expertise in the Age of AI* (2020)** — Pasquale's argument for maintaining human oversight and expertise in AI-augmented domains is philosophically informed, engaging with questions about what human expertise contributes that AI cannot substitute. Connects cognitive philosophy to policy questions about AI deployment.

**Luciano Floridi and Massimo Chiriatti, "GPT-3: Its Nature, Scope, Limits, and Consequences" (2020), *Minds and Machines*** — A careful philosophical analysis of GPT-3 that examines what the system does and does not do, covering its formal intelligence, lack of semantic understanding, and implications for the relationship between AI tools and human cognition.

**Evan Selinger and Brett Frischmann, *Re-Engineering Humanity* (2018)** — Examines how technological systems—including AI—can gradually degrade human cognitive capacities through over-reliance, raising questions about cognitive autonomy and what kinds of thinking we should preserve independently of technology.

## Secondary Literature

**Stiegler, *Technics and Time*, Vol. 1 (1994)** — Stiegler's philosophical account of how technology constitutes human memory and cognition provides a historical and ontological framework for thinking about AI's relationship to human cognitive capacities.

**David Krakauer, "Will AI Harm Us? Better to Ask How" (2016), *Scientific American*** — Krakauer's distinction between complementary and competitive cognitive artifacts: some technologies enhance human cognition by augmenting it, while others diminish human cognition by replacing it. The distinction is philosophically and practically important for evaluating AI.

**Cathy O'Neil, *Weapons of Math Destruction: How Big Data Increases Inequality and Threatens Democracy* (2016)** — Examines how algorithmic systems—including AI—can amplify biases, reduce human judgment, and produce systematic injustice, raising questions about epistemic dependence on AI.

**Nick Bostrom, "The Superintelligent Will: Motivation and Instrumental Rationality in Advanced Artificial Agents" (2012), *Minds and Machines*** — Examines what goals and motivations we should expect advanced AI systems to have, which is relevant for understanding how AI tools might eventually affect human agency and cognition.

**Shannon Vallor, *Technology and the Virtues: A Philosophical Guide to a Future Worth Wanting* (2016)** — Develops a virtue ethics approach to technology that examines how AI and other technologies affect human character, practical wisdom, and moral agency.
