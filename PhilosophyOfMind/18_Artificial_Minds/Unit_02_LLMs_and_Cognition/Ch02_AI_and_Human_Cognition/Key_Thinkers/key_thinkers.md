# Key Thinkers: AI and Human Cognition

---

**Andy Clark (1957–)**
Scottish philosopher and cognitive scientist at the University of Edinburgh. Clark's extended mind thesis provides the key philosophical framework for understanding how AI tools relate to human cognition: if Otto's notebook counts as part of his cognitive system, then a sufficiently integrated AI assistant might similarly become a cognitive partner rather than merely an external tool. Clark has engaged directly with questions about how AI extends human cognition, arguing that the parity principle—if it would be cognitive if internal, it is cognitive when external—applies to AI systems that users rely on in the right ways.
*Key works:* *Being There* (1997), *Supersizing the Mind* (2008), "Extended Mind Thesis" (1998, with Chalmers)

---

**Evan Selinger (1972–)**
American philosopher of technology at the Rochester Institute of Technology. Selinger, with Brett Frischmann, has argued that technological "re-engineering" of humanity through cognitive offloading can degrade human capabilities and autonomy. His concern about AI tools is not just that they might go wrong but that they might work too well—that our reliance on them might erode the cognitive skills and capacities that make us genuinely human. His work connects philosophy of technology to political philosophy and ethics of AI.
*Key works:* *Re-Engineering Humanity* (2018, with Frischmann), *Outside Expertise* (2012)

---

**Luciano Floridi (1964–)**
Italian philosopher of information at Oxford University. Floridi has developed a comprehensive philosophy of information and AI that situates AI tools within a broader account of how information technologies transform human cognitive environments. His analysis of what AI systems do—treating them as "inforg" agents operating in the "infosphere"—provides a philosophical framework for understanding human-AI cognitive interaction. His work on AI ethics has been influential in policy discussions about the regulation of AI.
*Key works:* *The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence* (2023), *The Logic of Information* (2019), *The Onlife Manifesto* (ed., 2015)

---

**Shannon Vallor (1972–)**
American philosopher at Edinburgh University, specializing in technology ethics. Vallor's virtue ethics approach to technology examines how AI tools and practices affect human character, practical wisdom, and the capacity for moral agency. Her concern is not primarily about dramatic AI risks but about the more subtle ways that reliance on AI systems shapes the cognitive and moral habits of users, potentially undermining the virtues—attentiveness, care, practical wisdom—that make good judgment possible.
*Key works:* *Technology and the Virtues* (2016), "AI and the Future of Human Virtue" (2017)

---

**Yochai Benkler (1964–)**
Israeli-American legal scholar at Harvard Law School. Benkler's work on networked information economies provides philosophical and political context for understanding how AI tools affect the distribution of cognitive labor and expertise. His analysis of how internet-mediated collaboration changes the structure of knowledge production is relevant for understanding how AI tools like LLMs change who does what cognitive work and with what consequences for epistemic autonomy.
*Key works:* *The Wealth of Networks* (2006), *The Penguin and the Leviathan* (2011)
