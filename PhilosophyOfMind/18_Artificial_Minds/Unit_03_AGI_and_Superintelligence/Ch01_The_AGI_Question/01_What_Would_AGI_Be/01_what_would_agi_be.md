# What Would Artificial General Intelligence Be?

Artificial general intelligence (AGI) refers to AI systems that have the breadth, flexibility, and general cognitive competence associated with human intelligence — as opposed to narrow AI systems that excel at specific tasks but lack generality. The concept is central to contemporary AI discourse, but it is philosophically underspecified: what exactly would it mean for an AI system to be generally intelligent? What is "general" about general intelligence?

**The Narrow-General Distinction**

Current AI systems are mostly "narrow" in the sense that they achieve extraordinary performance in specific domains while failing outside those domains. AlphaGo plays Go at superhuman level but cannot play chess. GPT-4 generates fluent text but cannot navigate a physical environment. These systems lack the flexible generalization that allows humans to apply knowledge and skills across novel domains, to learn from minimal examples, and to transfer competencies developed in one context to entirely different contexts.

Human intelligence, by contrast, is characterized by general learning: the same cognitive system that learns to drive a car can learn to appreciate music, manage social relationships, reason about mathematics, and adapt to radically novel situations. This generality is not just behavioral breadth but cognitive flexibility: the capacity to represent abstract principles and apply them across domains, to reason analogically, to recognize deep structural similarities between superficially different problems.

What would it take for an AI system to have this kind of generality? Several proposals:
- **Domain-general learning algorithms**: algorithms that can learn any task from sufficient experience, without domain-specific pre-built knowledge or inductive biases
- **Compositional generalization**: the ability to combine learned representations in novel ways to handle genuinely new situations
- **Meta-learning**: the ability to learn how to learn, acquiring new capabilities efficiently from minimal examples
- **Common sense reasoning**: the background knowledge about the physical and social world that enables flexible reasoning in everyday situations

**The Problem of Common Sense**

Common sense knowledge — the massive amount of mundane information about how the world works that humans acquire through development and use effortlessly in everyday reasoning — has proven to be one of the most challenging aspects of AGI. AI systems fail at tasks that are trivially easy for humans: understanding that a full cup of water placed upside down will spill, that people have internal states that differ from their expressed behavior, that two objects cannot be in the same place at the same time.

This failure reveals that human cognition is grounded in an enormous, largely implicit knowledge base about the physical world, social world, and causal regularities that we have acquired through embodied, social development. This knowledge is not easily encoded as explicit rules or learned from text alone — it seems to require the kind of experience that only embodied, situated agents develop.

The most ambitious AI safety and AGI research programs are grappling with this: how to build systems that have genuine common sense, not just statistical competence at common-sense tasks. Yann LeCun's proposals for "world models" that represent the causal structure of the physical and social world, and for learning through self-supervised interaction with the environment, address this directly.

**AGI and Consciousness**

An interesting philosophical question is whether AGI would require consciousness. Human general intelligence is clearly associated with consciousness: our flexible, adaptive, creative reasoning takes place in conscious experience, and the richness of our conscious inner life — our ability to imagine alternatives, reflect on our own states, attend to what matters — seems deeply connected to our cognitive generality.

Would an AGI system need to be conscious to be generally intelligent? This is contested. Functionalists might argue that consciousness is not required for AGI — the relevant functional organization could in principle be implemented without phenomenal experience. But the phenomenological tradition suggests that consciousness — particularly the embodied, world-engaged consciousness analyzed by Merleau-Ponty and Heidegger — is not separable from the kind of intelligent, flexible engagement with the world that we associate with genuine understanding. On this view, AGI that lacked phenomenal consciousness might lack the kind of understanding that underlies genuine general intelligence.

This question connects to the hard problem in a new context: if consciousness is required for AGI, then building AGI requires building conscious systems — which is a profound and potentially ethically perilous undertaking.
