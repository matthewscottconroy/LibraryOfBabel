# Further Reading: The AGI Question

## Primary Sources

**Nick Bostrom, *Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies* (2014)** — Bostrom's analysis of the risks posed by superintelligent AI remains the most influential philosophical treatment of the AGI question. He argues that an AI system significantly more intelligent than humans across all relevant domains could pose an existential risk if its goals are not aligned with human values—the "control problem" or "alignment problem." His orthogonality thesis holds that intelligence and terminal goals are independent, so a superintelligent system could have any goals whatsoever.

**Stuart Russell, *Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control* (2019)** — Russell argues that standard AI systems are dangerous because they are designed to achieve objectives—goals—rather than to be uncertain about what humans actually want. His proposed solution is to design AI systems that are explicitly uncertain about human preferences and that defer to human judgment, providing a more constructive response to the alignment problem than Bostrom.

**Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), *Mind*** — Turing's original paper addressed the question of machine intelligence and included discussion of a "child machine" that could learn and develop intelligence over time, anticipating the learning approach to AGI. His framing of the question in terms of behavioral equivalence to human intelligence remains the implicit standard against which AGI definitions are measured.

**Max Tegmark, *Life 3.0: Being Human in the Age of Artificial Intelligence* (2017)** — A physicist's examination of the long-term trajectory of AI development, covering scenarios from beneficial AGI to existential risk, and discussing the philosophical implications of different possible futures with artificial superintelligence.

**Eliezer Yudkowsky, "Artificial Intelligence as a Positive and Negative Factor in Global Risk" (2008), in *Global Catastrophic Risks*** — Yudkowsky's early statement of the case for treating AGI as an existential risk and for the importance of solving the alignment problem before building AGI. Represents the view that has been most influential in effective altruism and AI safety communities.

## Secondary Literature

**Ben Goertzel and Cassio Pennachin (eds.), *Artificial General Intelligence* (2007)** — A technical and philosophical collection examining different approaches to AGI, covering symbolic, connectionist, evolutionary, and hybrid approaches. Provides the technical background for philosophical discussion.

**José Hernández-Orallo, *The Measure of All Minds: Evaluating Natural and Artificial Intelligence* (2017)** — A rigorous account of how to define and measure general intelligence across natural and artificial systems, addressing the question of what a genuinely general intelligence would be and how we would recognize it.

**David Chalmers, "The Singularity: A Philosophical Analysis" (2010), *Journal of Consciousness Studies*** — Chalmers' careful philosophical examination of the singularity hypothesis, arguing that intelligence explosion is possible but raising questions about identity, consciousness, and value for post-singularity entities.

**Toby Ord, *The Precipice: Existential Risk and the Future of Humanity* (2020)** — Examines AGI as a potential existential risk alongside other long-run risks to humanity, providing a broader context for evaluating the importance of AI safety.

**Ryan Calo, A. Michael Froomkin, and Ian Kerr (eds.), *Robot Law* (2016)** — Examines the legal and philosophical implications of increasingly capable AI and robotic systems, covering questions of responsibility, liability, and the legal status of AI agents—including hypothetical AGI.
