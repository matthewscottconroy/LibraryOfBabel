# Key Thinkers: The AGI Question

---

**Nick Bostrom (1973–)**
Swedish philosopher at Oxford University, founder of the Future of Humanity Institute. Bostrom's *Superintelligence* (2014) provided the most systematic philosophical analysis of the risks posed by superintelligent AI. His orthogonality thesis holds that intelligence and final goals are logically independent—a superintelligent system could have any goal whatsoever—and his instrumental convergence thesis argues that most goals would lead a superintelligent system to seek self-preservation, resource acquisition, and goal-content integrity. These theses combine to generate the "control problem": how to ensure a superintelligent system has goals aligned with human values.
*Key works:* *Superintelligence* (2014), "The Superintelligent Will" (2012), *Anthropic Bias* (2002)

---

**Stuart Russell (1962–)**
British AI researcher at UC Berkeley. Russell's *Human Compatible* (2019) argues that the standard model of AI—designing systems to achieve specified objectives—is fundamentally flawed because it assumes perfect knowledge of what humans want. His alternative: design AI systems that are explicitly uncertain about human preferences and that defer to human judgment as the best available evidence about those preferences. His proposal for "Assistance Games" as a formal framework for beneficial AI provides a constructive response to the alignment problem.
*Key works:* *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach* (1995, with Norvig), *Human Compatible* (2019)

---

**Eliezer Yudkowsky (1979–)**
American AI safety researcher, co-founder of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI). Yudkowsky has been the most influential early advocate for treating AGI alignment as an existential priority, arguing that even a small probability of unaligned superintelligence justifies enormous investment in alignment research. His writing on decision theory, agent foundations, and the challenges of specifying human values precisely enough for an AI system to pursue them has been enormously influential in the AI safety community, even when criticized as speculative.
*Key works:* *Rationality: From AI to Zombies* (2015), "Artificial Intelligence as a Positive and Negative Factor in Global Risk" (2008)

---

**David Chalmers (1966–)**
Australian philosopher at NYU. Chalmers' philosophical analysis of the singularity (2010) examines whether an intelligence explosion is possible, what it would entail, and what the philosophical implications would be for consciousness, identity, and value. He takes the singularity seriously as a philosophical possibility while raising important questions about whether a "mind uploading" future would preserve personal identity and whether artificial superintelligence would be conscious. His contribution is distinctive in bringing rigorous philosophy of mind to a topic that usually receives more technological than philosophical treatment.
*Key works:* "The Singularity: A Philosophical Analysis" (2010), *The Conscious Mind* (1996), *Reality+* (2022)

---

**Max Tegmark (1967–)**
Swedish-American cosmologist and AI safety advocate at MIT. Tegmark's *Life 3.0* (2017) examines the long-term trajectory of AI development from a scientific perspective, covering scenarios ranging from beneficial AGI to existential risk and discussing what humanity should do to navigate toward the best outcomes. While not a philosopher by training, his analysis of the relationship between intelligence, goal-pursuit, and consciousness draws on philosophy of mind and has been widely read as an accessible introduction to the AGI landscape.
*Key works:* *Life 3.0* (2017), *Our Mathematical Universe* (2014)
