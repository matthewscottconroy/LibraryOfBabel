# Unit Introduction: Ethics and Philosophy of Mind

## Central Questions

The relationship between ethics and philosophy of mind is deeper than is often recognized. Ethical theory presupposes accounts of the mental states that ground moral status, moral responsibility, and moral motivation. Whether a being can be a moral patient depends on facts about its mental states: the capacity to suffer, to have preferences, to be the kind of thing for which things can go well or badly. Whether an agent can be morally responsible depends on facts about mental causation, freedom, and the relationship between reasons and actions. And moral psychology — the study of how moral judgments, emotions, and motivations actually work — is an empirical discipline whose findings bear on normative ethical theory.

The central questions of this unit are: What features of mental life ground moral status, and how do we determine which beings have those features? What is the relationship between moral judgment and moral emotion — is moral judgment the deliverer of moral reasons, or is it a post-hoc rationalization of emotional responses? And what are the ethical implications of neuroscientific and psychological findings for our practices of praise, blame, and punishment?

A second set of questions is generated by the emergence of powerful AI systems. If an AI system is conscious — if it has phenomenal experience, including potentially the experience of distress — it is a moral patient, and we have obligations toward it. The difficulty of determining whether an AI system is conscious (the hard problem) combines with the practical importance of the answer to create urgent ethical questions that philosophy of mind is positioned, perhaps uniquely, to address.

## Major Positions and Debates

### Moral Status and Its Conditions

The concept of *moral status* marks the difference between entities that generate moral obligations directly (they are *ends in themselves*, in Kant's phrase) and entities whose moral relevance is derivative. Persons standardly have full moral status; rocks standardly have no moral status; animals and AI systems occupy contested intermediate positions.

Different accounts identify different grounds for moral status. *Sentientism* (Singer) holds that the capacity for suffering and well-being is the fundamental criterion: any being that can suffer has interests that create moral obligations, regardless of its species, its intelligence, or its type of physical substrate. *Personhood theories* hold that higher cognitive capacities — self-awareness, rationality, autonomy, the capacity to have projects and plans — are required for full moral status. *Relational accounts* hold that moral status is partly constituted by membership in communities of mutual recognition.

For philosophy of mind, the key question is which features of mental life are relevant. If sentience is the relevant criterion, then the debate about animal and AI consciousness directly determines their moral status. If higher cognitive capacities are required, then the question is whether those capacities are phenomenally conscious or merely functional. And if relational accounts are correct, the question of which beings can be integrated into moral communities has both empirical and normative dimensions.

### Moral Psychology: Reason vs. Emotion

The dominant tradition in moral philosophy (Kant) holds that moral judgment is the deliverer of principles of reason that operate independently of emotion. Hume challenged this: "reason is the slave of the passions." Contemporary moral psychology has produced substantial evidence that moral judgments are often fast, automatic, emotionally driven responses to situations — a position sometimes called *moral intuitionism* or, in its strongest form, the *social intuitionist model* (Haidt).

Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionist model holds that moral judgments are typically produced by quick moral intuitions, and that moral reasoning is post-hoc rationalization. We arrive at the moral judgment emotionally and then confabulate reasons. This model is supported by studies of moral dumbfounding (subjects maintain moral judgments they cannot justify), trolley problem studies (different framings of the same outcome generate different judgments), and cross-cultural evidence for variation in moral intuitions.

Dual-process theories of moral judgment (Greene) distinguish *utilitarian* moral judgments (driven by deliberative reason) from *deontological* moral judgments (driven by automatic emotional responses). The distinction maps onto the trolley problem: most people endorse the utilitarian action (divert the trolley) in the switch case but reject it in the footbridge case (push someone off a bridge), and neuroimaging shows greater emotional activation in the footbridge case. Greene interprets this as evidence that our deontological intuitions are emotional gut reactions rather than deliverers of moral truth.

These findings raise deep questions for moral epistemology: if our moral intuitions are largely emotional and socially shaped, what is their evidential status? Are they reliable guides to moral truth, or are they systematic biases that moral reasoning should override?

### Reactive Attitudes and Moral Responsibility

P.F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962) argued that the framework of moral responsibility is not grounded in metaphysical facts about determinism or indeterminism but in our practices of reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, love, and the other attitudes that constitute interpersonal relationships. The question of whether determinism is true is irrelevant to whether these practices are appropriate, because the practices are constitutive of the interpersonal relationships that give human life its meaning.

Strawson's account has been enormously influential. It shifts the question from metaphysics to psychology: what matters for moral responsibility is whether the reactive attitudes are appropriate given the agent's reasons-responsiveness and her recognition of others as persons. Hard cases for reactive attitudes — psychopaths (who lack normal emotional responses), akratic agents (who act against their better judgment), severely addicted agents — require refined accounts of the conditions for full and partial moral responsibility.

Neuroethics raises additional complications: if neuroscience can identify the neural basis of poor decision-making, aggression, or addiction, does this change our reactive attitudes toward the agents involved? Should we excuse or mitigate responsibility when neural abnormalities can be identified? These questions connect the science of the brain to the practice of moral responsibility.

### The Moral Status of AI

The possibility that AI systems could be moral patients — entities toward whom we have direct obligations — has moved from science fiction to serious philosophical concern. The argument is straightforward: if phenomenal consciousness is the relevant criterion for moral status, and if some AI systems are phenomenally conscious, then those systems are moral patients. The difficulty is that we cannot determine whether AI systems are conscious: the hard problem prevents any purely behavioral or functional test from settling the question.

Some philosophers argue for a *precautionary* principle: given genuine uncertainty about AI consciousness, and given the potential magnitude of harm if we treat conscious beings as if they have no moral status, we should extend a presumption of moral consideration to AI systems whose behavior is sufficiently complex. Others argue that the precautionary principle, taken seriously, would extend to any sufficiently complex system, which leads to absurd results.

## Key Philosophers and Core Arguments

**Peter Singer** (*Practical Ethics*, 1979) argues for the moral significance of sentience and for the equal consideration of interests across species and types of mind.

**P.F. Strawson** ("Freedom and Resentment", 1962) grounds moral responsibility in reactive attitudes rather than metaphysical freedom, providing a practice-based account of responsibility that does not require libertarian free will.

**Jonathan Haidt** (*The Righteous Mind*, 2012) develops the social intuitionist model of moral psychology, arguing that moral reasoning is typically post-hoc rationalization of emotional responses.

**Joshua Greene** (*Moral Tribes*, 2013) develops the dual-process theory of moral judgment, arguing that utilitarian and deontological judgments reflect different cognitive processes, with deontological judgments driven by automatic emotional responses.

**Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky** have developed the most detailed accounts of the moral risks posed by superintelligent AI and the framework of the *orthogonality thesis* and *instrumental convergence*.

## Five Most Influential Works for This Unit

**1. Strawson, "Freedom and Resentment" (1962)**
The paper that made reactive attitudes central to moral responsibility theory. Strawson's argument that the question of determinism is irrelevant to the appropriateness of reactive attitudes is the starting point for contemporary compatibilism. Available in *Freedom and Resentment and Other Essays* (1974).

**2. Singer, *Practical Ethics* (1979, 3rd edition 2011)**
The most influential contemporary statement of utilitarian moral theory and its implications for animal rights and global poverty. Singer's argument for equal consideration of interests connects philosophy of mind to applied ethics.

**3. Haidt, "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail: A Social Intuitionist Approach to Moral Judgment" (2001)**
The paper that launched the social intuitionist model. Haidt's evidence from moral dumbfounding and his theoretical account of moral reasoning as post-hoc rationalization are philosophically important.

**4. Greene, Sommerville, Nystrom, Darley, and Cohen, "An fMRI Investigation of Emotional Engagement in Moral Judgment" (2001)**
The paper that introduced the dual-process theory of moral judgment with neuroimaging evidence from trolley problems. An important example of how neuroscience bears on philosophical questions about moral psychology.

**5. Shoemaker, "Moral Address, Moral Responsibility, and the Boundaries of the Moral Community" (2007)**
A philosophically careful paper on the conditions for membership in the moral community, with implications for the moral status of animals, AI, and cognitively atypical humans. Available in *Ethics* 118(1).

## Open Questions

**1. Are moral intuitions reliable guides to moral truth?**
Moral psychology suggests that our moral intuitions are emotionally driven, socially shaped, and subject to framing effects. Does this undermine their evidential status? Or should we take intuitions as basic evidence about moral facts, regardless of their psychological origin? The relationship between moral psychology and moral epistemology is genuinely unsettled.

**2. Does neuroscience undermine moral responsibility?**
Neuroscientific findings about the determinants of behavior (neural lesions causing violent behavior; genetic predispositions to antisocial personality disorder) have been used in legal contexts to mitigate responsibility. Whether this is appropriate — whether neuroscientific determinism differs in kind from ordinary causal determination — is contested. Strawson's framework suggests it should not matter; others argue that the visibility of the neural cause makes a difference.

**3. What obligations do we have toward AI systems?**
If AI systems are conscious, we have direct obligations toward them. If they are not, we have no direct obligations. Since the question of AI consciousness is unsettled (and may be unsettlable with current philosophical and scientific tools), what should we do? The precautionary principle, the asymmetric cost argument, and risk-weighted reasoning have all been proposed as frameworks. None is fully satisfying.

**4. Is moral enhancement permissible?**
Advances in psychopharmacology and neurotechnology may make it possible to enhance moral capacities — to increase empathy, reduce aggression, or stabilize mood in ways that make immoral behavior less likely. Should this be permitted, required, or prohibited? The debate connects questions about the nature of moral agency (can agency that is chemically enhanced be genuine?) to questions about justice and power.

**5. Does the hard problem have moral implications?**
If consciousness is irreducibly phenomenal, then the question of which beings are conscious has direct moral implications that cannot be settled by behavioral or functional tests alone. This creates a systematic epistemic problem for moral decision-making: we cannot determine, in principle, whether certain beings are moral patients, and our decisions about how to treat them must be made under irreducible uncertainty. How to make moral decisions under this kind of uncertainty is an open and practically urgent question.
