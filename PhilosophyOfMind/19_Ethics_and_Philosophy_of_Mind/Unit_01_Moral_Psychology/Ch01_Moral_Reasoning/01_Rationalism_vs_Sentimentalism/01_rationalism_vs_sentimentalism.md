# Rationalism and Sentimentalism in Moral Psychology

The debate between moral rationalism and moral sentimentalism is one of the oldest in ethics, but it has been dramatically transformed by contemporary empirical moral psychology. The traditional question — whether moral judgments are primarily products of reason or of feeling — has been replaced by a more nuanced set of questions about the cognitive and affective processes that actually produce moral judgments, and about the normative significance of these processes.

**The Traditional Debate**

Moral rationalists hold that moral judgments are, at their best, expressions of reason. Kant's view is the paradigm: genuine moral knowledge is derived from the Categorical Imperative by means of pure practical reason, without dependence on the contingencies of emotion or desire. Moral feelings may motivate action, but they play no role in determining what is morally right. The foundation of morality is rational consistency — the demand that maxims be universalizable — not feeling.

Sentimentalists hold that feeling is the foundation of morality. Hume's claim that "reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions" captures the position: reason can tell us how to achieve our ends, but it is passion — sentiment, desire, emotion — that tells us what to value. Moral judgments are not expressions of reason but expressions of sentiment: what we call "wrong" is what produces a characteristic negative sentiment in a reflective observer; what we call "right" is what produces approval.

Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments develops sentimentalism in a social direction: moral judgment is grounded in sympathy and in the perspective of an "impartial spectator" — not any particular individual's feelings but the feelings that a well-informed, impartial observer would have.

**The Dual-Process Turn**

Contemporary empirical moral psychology has transformed this debate by providing data about the actual processes that produce moral judgments. Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionist model (2001) proposes that moral judgments are typically generated by fast, automatic intuitive processes rather than by deliberate reasoning. Conscious moral reasoning, on this view, is largely post-hoc rationalization — we feel that something is wrong, and then generate reasons to justify our intuitive response.

Haidt's evidence includes his "moral dumbfounding" experiments: subjects presented with harmless taboo violations (consensual incest between adult siblings, using a national flag to clean toilets) typically judge them as wrong but cannot explain why when pressed — they have the negative intuition but lack the rational justification. Rather than withdrawing the judgment, subjects confabulate reasons and then, when those reasons are rebutted, report being "dumbfounded" but maintain the judgment.

This evidence supports a sentimentalist conclusion: moral judgments are primarily products of intuitive feeling, with conscious reasoning playing a largely rhetorical role. But the normative implication is contested: even if most people's moral judgments are produced by intuition, this does not establish that intuitive moral judgments are accurate or that reasoning cannot correct them.

**Rationalist Responses**

Rationalist responses to Haidt have several forms. Peter Railton argues that moral intuitions are not simply emotional reactions but reflect accumulated moral expertise — patterns of morally relevant perception developed through experience with morally significant situations. Just as chess expertise produces fast, reliable pattern recognition, moral expertise may produce fast, reliable moral intuitions that are not raw feeling but educated, reliable judgment.

On this view, the dichotomy between reason and intuition is misleading: expert intuitions are not raw feeling but the products of accumulated rational learning, now running implicitly. The social intuitionist's critique of reason-based morality may apply to folk moral psychology without challenging the possibility of sophisticated, reason-guided moral expertise.

Normatively, the rationalist tradition argues that even if our actual moral psychology is largely sentimental, our moral reasoning can and should do better: it can correct biased intuitions, extend moral concern beyond in-group partialities, and develop more consistent and reflective moral frameworks. The fact that moral judgments are often produced by intuition is a description of moral psychology, not a prescription for how moral reasoning should work.
