# Dual-Process Theory and Moral Reasoning

The dual-process framework — the distinction between fast, automatic, intuitive processing (System 1) and slow, deliberate, effortful reasoning (System 2) — has been applied to moral psychology with significant results and significant controversy. Joshua Greene's influential account proposes that different moral theories — deontological and consequentialist — reflect the different outputs of these two processing systems, and that understanding this neurological fact has important implications for normative ethics.

**Greene's Neural Dual-Process Account**

Greene and colleagues used fMRI to study neural activity during moral judgment, comparing personal moral dilemmas (the trolley "footbridge" problem, where you must push someone onto tracks to save five) with impersonal dilemmas (the standard trolley problem, where you pull a lever). They found that personal dilemmas — involving direct physical harm to an identifiable person — activated emotional processing regions (medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate, amygdala) more strongly than impersonal dilemmas.

Greene's interpretation: the strong emotional response to personal harm scenarios generates the deontological intuition (it is wrong to push the person, even to save five), while utilitarian judgment (push the person if the numbers favor it) requires deliberate cognitive effort to override the emotional response. Deontological intuitions reflect System 1 emotional reactions; utilitarian judgments reflect System 2 deliberate reasoning.

This led Greene to the controversial normative conclusion: consequentialism is the more rationally justified moral framework because it reflects careful deliberation, while deontological rules are artifacts of emotional responses that evolved for different circumstances. We should be suspicious of strong deontological intuitions in high-stakes cases because they may reflect emotional reactions rather than sound moral reasoning.

**Critiques of Greene's Account**

Greene's account has been extensively criticized on both empirical and philosophical grounds.

Empirically, the claim that deontological intuitions are simply emotional reactions has been challenged. Judgment in trolley dilemmas correlates with factors other than personal/impersonal: whether the action is a means versus a side-effect, whether the victim is the cause of the problem, whether the act violates the victim's rights. These structural features do not map cleanly onto the personal/impersonal dimension, and the emotional activation in personal dilemmas may reflect morally relevant features (rights violation, using a person as a means) rather than mere squeamishness.

Philosophically, the argument from emotional origin to normative conclusion is a genetic fallacy: the fact that deontological intuitions are produced by emotional processes does not establish that they are unreliable or normatively worthless. Utilitarian conclusions can also be produced by motivated reasoning, insensitivity to the particularity of persons, or other biased processes. The genetic story does not settle normative questions.

Judy Lichtenberg and others have pointed out that strong moral intuitions — including deontological ones — may serve as the data that moral theory needs to explain and respect, not aberrations to be explained away. A moral theory that requires us to push people onto trolley tracks may be demonstrating its own limits, not the irrationality of our reluctance to comply.

**Integrating Emotion and Reason**

The most philosophically productive response to the dual-process debate in ethics is to reject the simple dichotomy between emotion and reason and ask how the two systems interact. Antonio Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis suggests that emotional responses are not just biases on reasoning but essential inputs to good practical reasoning — providing fast, reliable valuations that guide deliberation toward relevant considerations.

Jesse Prinz's "emotional dog" view (building on Haidt's analogy) holds that moral judgments are fundamentally emotional but that emotions can be educated, refined, and corrected through reasoning and exposure to new information. The goal of moral development is not to override emotion with reason but to develop well-calibrated moral emotions — emotions that respond appropriately to morally relevant features of situations.

This integrative view suggests that both sentimentalism and rationalism capture something true: moral judgment is typically produced by emotion, but emotion can and should be shaped and corrected by reason. The interaction between System 1 and System 2 in moral reasoning is not a battle between feeling and thought but a collaborative, mutually corrective process.
