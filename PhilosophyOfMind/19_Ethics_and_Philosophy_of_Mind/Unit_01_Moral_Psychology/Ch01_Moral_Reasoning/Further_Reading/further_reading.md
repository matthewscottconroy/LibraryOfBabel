# Further Reading: Moral Reasoning

## Primary Sources

**David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, Book III (1739)** — Hume's argument that reason alone cannot motivate action—that "reason is and ought only to be the slave of the passions"—is the foundational statement of moral sentimentalism. His "is-ought" distinction challenges the derivation of normative conclusions from purely descriptive premises and grounds his view that moral judgments express sentiment rather than describe facts.

**Jonathan Haidt, "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail: A Social Intuitionist Model of Moral Judgment" (2001), *Psychological Review*** — Haidt argues that moral judgments are primarily driven by quick emotional intuitions and that reasoning is typically post-hoc rationalization. His "moral dumbfounding" studies show that people can maintain strong moral convictions even when they cannot provide any rational justification for them. This paper launched a major research program in moral psychology.

**Joshua Greene, "From Neural 'Is' to Moral 'Ought': What Are the Moral Implications of Neuroscientific Moral Psychology?" (2003), *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*** — Greene's dual-process theory of moral reasoning argues that consequentialist thinking engages cognitive, deliberative processes while deontological judgments are driven by emotional reactions. His trolley problem research uses neuroimaging to show differential brain activation in impersonal versus personal moral dilemmas.

**Immanuel Kant, *Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785)** — The foundational text for rationalist moral philosophy, arguing that moral requirements derive from pure practical reason through the categorical imperative. Kant's insistence that genuine moral motivation requires acting from duty (rather than inclination) represents the sharpest contrast to Humean sentimentalism.

**John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice* (1971)** — Rawls's reflective equilibrium method—moving between moral principles and considered judgments about particular cases until they cohere—represents an influential rationalist account of moral reasoning that is neither purely deductive nor merely intuitive.

## Secondary Literature

**Jesse Prinz, *The Emotional Construction of Morals* (2007)** — Prinz defends an empiricist, sentimentalist metaethics, arguing that moral properties are constructed from emotional responses and that reason plays only an instrumental role in moral judgment. His engagement with moral psychology and anthropological evidence makes his account more empirically grounded than earlier sentimentalist positions.

**Joshua Greene, *Moral Tribes: Emotion, Reason, and the Gap Between Us and Them* (2013)** — Greene's book-length treatment of dual-process moral psychology, arguing that consequentialist reasoning is the correct approach to moral conflict between groups while emotional responses are appropriate within groups. Engages with both psychology and normative ethics.

**Walter Sinnott-Armstrong (ed.), *Moral Psychology*, 3 vols. (2008)** — A comprehensive and authoritative collection covering all aspects of moral psychology, from the evolution of morality and the role of emotion to moral development and situationist challenges to virtue.

**Peter Railton, "Moral Realism" (1986), *Philosophical Review*** — Railton's sophisticated naturalist account of moral realism argues that moral facts are facts about what would promote human flourishing, providing a bridge between natural science and moral rationalism.

**Shaun Nichols, *Sentimental Rules: On the Natural Foundations of Moral Judgment* (2004)** — Examines the psychological foundations of moral judgment, arguing that moral norms rest on a combination of emotional systems and learned rules, providing an empirically informed account of moral cognition.
