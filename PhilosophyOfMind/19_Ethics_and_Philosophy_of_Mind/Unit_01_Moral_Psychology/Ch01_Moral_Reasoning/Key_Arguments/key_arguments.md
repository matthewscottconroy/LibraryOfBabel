# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Moral Reasoning

## Key Arguments

**Haidt's Social Intuitionist Model**
Jonathan Haidt argues that moral judgment is primarily driven by rapid, automatic moral intuitions, with moral reasoning serving primarily as post-hoc rationalization. His "moral dumbfounding" experiments showed that subjects who judged a harmless but taboo act (consensual incest between adult siblings, eating a family dog) as wrong could not articulate principled reasons and yet refused to change their judgment when their stated reasons were refuted — they were "morally dumbfounded." Haidt concludes that moral reasoning follows moral emotion rather than driving it.

**Greene's Dual-Process Theory of Moral Judgment**
Joshua Greene argues that the distinction between characteristically utilitarian and characteristically deontological moral judgments reflects the operation of two different cognitive systems: a fast, automatic, emotionally driven system (associated with the vmPFC and amygdala) that generates aversion to harmful personal acts regardless of their utilitarian logic, and a slow, deliberative, controlled reasoning system (associated with dlPFC) that performs utilitarian calculation. The trolley problem vs. footbridge problem distinction illustrates the conflict between these systems.

**Kant's Rationalist Account**
For Kant, moral reasoning is the application of the categorical imperative — universalizability (act only on maxims you could will to be universal laws), humanity formula (treat rational beings always as ends in themselves, never merely as means), and kingdom of ends — to determine the permissibility of actions. Emotion plays no constitutive role in genuine moral judgment: acting from inclination, even benevolently, is not acting morally. Moral worth requires acting from duty — from rational respect for the moral law — alone. Kant's rationalism remains the most sophisticated version of the view that moral reasoning is genuinely rational.

**Hume's Sentimentalism**
Hume argues that reason alone cannot motivate action; it can only determine means to ends, not ends themselves. Moral distinctions are "derived from the moral sentiments" — from natural emotional responses of approval and disapproval that are responsive to character traits useful or agreeable to the agent and others. Reason informs us of the facts; sentiment evaluates them morally. Contemporary neo-Humean accounts (Blackburn, Gibbard) develop this sentimentalist tradition in sophisticated ways, connecting it to expressivism and quasi-realism.

## Core Concepts

**Moral Dumbfounding**
Moral dumbfounding, demonstrated by Haidt, is the phenomenon where subjects maintain moral judgments even after their stated justifications are refuted and they cannot produce new reasons. The classic cases involve harmless but taboo acts: most subjects judge them wrong, cannot explain why when pressed, and yet refuse to revise their judgment. Haidt interprets dumbfounding as evidence that moral judgments are caused by intuitions and that reasoning is post-hoc justification rather than the cause of the judgment.

**The Social Intuitionist Model**
The social intuitionist model (SIM), developed by Haidt, holds that: (1) moral judgments are caused primarily by rapid, automatic moral intuitions; (2) moral reasoning is primarily engaged post-hoc to justify those intuitions; (3) moral change happens primarily through social persuasion that triggers new intuitions, not through logical argument. The model represents a challenge to rationalist accounts of morality and connects moral psychology to social psychology.

**Dual-Process Theory in Moral Psychology**
Dual-process theory in moral psychology (Greene) posits two systems underlying moral judgment: a fast, intuitive, emotionally driven System 1 that generates gut reactions to moral situations, and a slow, deliberate, analytically driven System 2 that performs cost-benefit reasoning. The tension between these systems explains inconsistencies in moral judgment (personal vs. impersonal trolley cases), individual variation (psychopaths show reduced System 1 moral emotions), and the possibility of deliberate moral change through training in System 2 reasoning.

**The Categorical Imperative**
Kant's categorical imperative is the supreme principle of morality — the principle that commands unconditionally (categorically) rather than hypothetically (if you want X, do Y). Kant articulates it in several formulations, including: (1) the universalizability formula: act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law; (2) the humanity formula: act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means. These formulations are meant to express a single underlying principle.

**Moral Sentiment (Hume)**
For Hume, moral sentiments are the natural affective responses — approval and disapproval — that constitute our moral evaluations. They are not arbitrary but track character traits that are genuinely useful or agreeable to agents and others, making moral judgments non-arbitrary while remaining empirical and sentiment-based rather than purely rational. Contemporary expressivism develops a Humean picture in which moral judgments express rather than describe moral sentiments.

**Moral Emotions**
Moral emotions are emotions that have specifically moral objects or that are constitutively involved in moral judgment: guilt, shame, indignation, contempt, elevation, admiration, sympathy, and empathy. Research shows that moral emotions are evolutionarily ancient (homologs in other primates), culturally variable in their specific objects, and causally powerful in motivating moral action. The role of moral emotions in moral judgment is central to debates between rationalist and sentimentalist accounts of morality.

## Thought Experiments

**The Incest Scenario (Haidt)**
Haidt's most famous dumbfounding scenario: Julie and Mark are adult siblings who, on a vacation, decide once to have consensual sex with contraception, enjoy it, and never tell anyone. Most respondents judge this wrong but cannot articulate principled reasons that withstand scrutiny (inbreeding risks don't apply; it's not harmful to them; it's consensual; it's private). When their stated reasons are refuted, they maintain the judgment but admit they cannot say why. The thought experiment is designed to induce dumbfounding and thereby demonstrate the priority of intuition over reasoning in moral judgment.

**The Trolley Problem (Thomson after Foot)**
A runaway trolley will kill five people unless you divert it onto a sidetrack where it will kill one. Most people judge it permissible to pull the lever. Now imagine you are on a bridge over the tracks: you could stop the trolley by pushing a large man off the bridge onto the tracks, killing him but saving the five. Most judge this impermissible. The two cases are structurally equivalent in utilitarian terms (one life for five) but generate different intuitions. Greene argues that the difference reflects the activation of personal emotional aversion in the footbridge case, illustrating the dual-process structure of moral judgment.

**Kant's Perfect Murderer**
Kant's famous case: a would-be murderer comes to your door asking where your friend is. You know your friend is hiding in your house. Kant argues that lying is impermissible even here: lying treats the murderer merely as a means and violates the universalizability formula (a world in which lying when convenient were universal would undermine the institution of truth-telling). The verdict — you must tell the truth — is widely regarded as a reductio of Kant's rigorism, but defenders argue it reveals a genuine tension between competing perfect duties (not to lie, not to be complicit in murder) rather than a mandate to tell the murderer where your friend is.
