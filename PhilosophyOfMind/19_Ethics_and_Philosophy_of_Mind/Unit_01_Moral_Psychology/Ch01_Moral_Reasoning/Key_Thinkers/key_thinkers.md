# Key Thinkers: Moral Reasoning

---

**Jonathan Haidt (1963–)**
American social psychologist at NYU, known for his work on moral psychology and political psychology. Haidt's social intuitionist model holds that moral judgments are primarily products of quick, automatic emotional intuitions rather than deliberate reasoning. His "moral dumbfounding" experiments—in which people maintain strong moral convictions about cases they cannot rationally justify—support the view that reasoning typically rationalizes intuitions rather than generating them. His subsequent work identified five (later six) moral foundations—harm, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and liberty—as the evolved psychological bases of human moral systems.
*Key works:* "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail" (2001), *The Righteous Mind* (2012)

---

**Joshua Greene (1974–)**
American philosopher and psychologist at Harvard University. Greene's dual-process theory of moral judgment, developed through his influential trolley problem research, holds that personal moral dilemmas engage emotional brain systems while impersonal dilemmas engage deliberative systems. He argues that emotional reactions generate deontological-seeming judgments (which he regards as evolutionary heuristics) while deliberative reasoning generates consequentialist conclusions (which he regards as more defensible). His work has been both influential and controversial in connecting neuroscience to normative ethics.
*Key works:* "An fMRI Investigation of Emotional Engagement in Moral Judgment" (2001), *Moral Tribes* (2013)

---

**David Hume (1711–1776)**
Scottish empiricist philosopher. Hume's moral sentimentalism argues that moral judgments are not deliverances of reason but expressions of sentiment—specifically of approval and disapproval. His argument that reason alone cannot motivate action (since it can only establish means-ends relations) and his is-ought gap challenge the rationalist account of morality. His positive account identifies sympathy—the capacity to share others' feelings—as the foundation of moral sentiment. Hume's influence on contemporary moral psychology has been enormous, particularly on Haidt's social intuitionist model.
*Key works:* *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751)

---

**Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)**
German philosopher of the Enlightenment. Kant's rationalist moral philosophy holds that morality is grounded in pure practical reason through the categorical imperative—the requirement to act only on maxims that could be universalized, to treat rational beings always as ends and never merely as means. His insistence that genuine moral worth requires acting from duty alone—not from inclination or sympathy—represents the sharpest rationalist alternative to sentimentalism and has generated both admiration and critique in moral psychology.
*Key works:* *Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), *The Metaphysics of Morals* (1797)

---

**Jesse Prinz (1972–)**
American philosopher at CUNY Graduate Center. Prinz's "emotionist" metaethics defends a sentimentalist position on empirical grounds, arguing that anthropological and psychological evidence supports the view that moral properties are constructions from emotional responses and that moral reasoning is instrumental rather than moral in itself. His engagement with moral psychology, empirical anthropology, and metaethics makes his sentimentalism more empirically grounded than Hume's and an important reference point for connecting philosophy and psychology.
*Key works:* *The Emotional Construction of Morals* (2007), *Beyond Human Nature* (2012)
