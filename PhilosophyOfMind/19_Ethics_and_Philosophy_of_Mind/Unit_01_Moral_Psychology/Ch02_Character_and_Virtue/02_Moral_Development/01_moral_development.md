# Moral Development

How do human beings develop into moral agents? The psychology of moral development examines the cognitive, emotional, and social processes through which children acquire moral concepts, moral emotions, and moral agency. The philosophical significance of this research is considerable: it illuminates the nature of moral knowledge, the relationship between development and reason, and the question of whether morality is innate, constructed, or socially transmitted.

**Kohlberg's Stage Theory**

Lawrence Kohlberg's stage theory of moral development, building on Piaget, proposes that moral reasoning develops through a universal sequence of stages, each representing a qualitatively different way of understanding moral requirements.

Pre-conventional morality (stages 1-2) is self-oriented: right action is what avoids punishment (stage 1) or what serves one's own interests, with reciprocal exchange ("I'll scratch your back if you scratch mine," stage 2). Conventional morality (stages 3-4) is group-oriented: right action is what earns approval from others and fulfills social roles (stage 3) or what upholds social order and legitimate authority (stage 4). Post-conventional morality (stages 5-6) is principle-oriented: right action is what respects social contracts and individual rights (stage 5) or what conforms to universal ethical principles arrived at through rational reflection (stage 6).

Kohlberg proposed that the highest stages are characterized by Kantian-style respect for persons and universal principles — suggesting a picture in which moral development is the development of moral rationality, with emotion playing an increasingly subordinate role.

**Gilligan's Critique and the Ethics of Care**

Carol Gilligan's critique of Kohlberg in *In a Different Voice* (1982) is among the most influential in feminist philosophy of psychology. She argued that Kohlberg's theory was biased by its use of an exclusively male research sample, and that women's moral development followed a different but equally valid trajectory — one oriented around care, relationships, and context-sensitivity rather than abstract rules and universal principles.

The ethics of care (Nel Noddings, Virginia Held) proposes that moral life is fundamentally about caring relationships rather than compliance with universal principles. The paradigmatic moral context is the caring relationship between parent and child, carer and dependent, in which the needs of the particular person matter in their particularity — not as instances of a universal rule but as the needs of this specific person in this specific relationship.

This is not relativism — the ethics of care has its own standards (genuine attention to the other's needs, responsiveness to the other as particular rather than generic) — but it is a different framework from Kantian universalism. Its implications for moral psychology are that moral development involves not just the development of abstract reasoning but the development of attentiveness, empathy, and the skills of caring relationships.

**The Nativist Turn**

Contemporary developmental psychology has documented impressive moral competence in very young infants, suggesting that some foundations of moral cognition are innate rather than constructed through development. Kiley Hamlin and Karen Wynn's research found that 6-month-old infants prefer helpers over hinderers (characters who assist versus those who obstruct another character's goals) — suggesting that the most basic distinction between helping and harming is available very early in development, before language or explicit moral instruction.

Jonathan Haidt has argued that the basic moral modules (harm, fairness, loyalty, authority, purity) are evolutionary adaptations — innate foundations of moral psychology that appear across cultures, with cultural variation in how they are expressed and weighted. This nativist picture suggests that moral psychology has a biological foundation that learning and culture elaborate but do not create from scratch.

The nativist evidence does not settle the normative questions — the fact that we have innate tendencies toward in-group favoritism and disgust-based purity judgments does not mean these are morally justified — but it reshapes our understanding of what moral development is and where it starts from.

**Moral Development and Philosophical Implications**

The empirical research on moral development has several philosophical implications. It suggests that moral intuitions are not arbitrary — they are grounded in developmental processes, evolutionary pressures, and social learning that give them genuine epistemic significance. It shows that moral competence involves both cognitive and emotional development — that mature moral agents need both accurate moral knowledge and well-calibrated moral emotions. And it reveals that moral development is a lifelong project, not a completion of a fixed developmental schedule — that adult moral learning through experience, reflection, and the challenge of moral difficulties continues throughout life.
