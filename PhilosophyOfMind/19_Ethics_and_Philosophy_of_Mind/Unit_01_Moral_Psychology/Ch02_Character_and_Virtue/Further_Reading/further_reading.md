# Further Reading: Character and Virtue

## Primary Sources

**Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* (c. 350 BCE, trans. Irwin)** — The foundational text of virtue ethics, arguing that the good life consists in the exercise of virtues—stable character traits that constitute human excellence—and that virtues are acquired through habituation rather than instruction. Aristotle's account of practical wisdom (phronesis) as the master virtue that guides the exercise of all others, and his doctrine of the mean, remain central to virtue ethics and to discussions of moral psychology.

**John Doris, *Lack of Character: Personality and Moral Behavior* (2002)** — Doris develops the situationist challenge to virtue ethics, arguing that social psychological research (especially Milgram's obedience experiments and the Good Samaritan study) shows that behavior is more strongly predicted by situational factors than by character traits. His challenge forces virtue ethicists to either revise the concept of virtue or provide better evidence that character exists.

**Gilbert Harman, "Moral Philosophy Meets Social Psychology: Virtue Ethics and the Fundamental Attribution Error" (1999), *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*** — Harman's situationist challenge argues that lay attributions of character are systematic errors—fundamental attribution errors—and that moral psychology should abandon character-based explanations of behavior.

**Lawrence Kohlberg, "The Claim to Moral Adequacy of a Highest Stage of Moral Judgment" (1973), *Journal of Philosophy*** — Kohlberg's stage theory of moral development, arguing that moral reasoning develops through pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional stages culminating in universal principled reasoning. His rationalist developmental account remains influential despite significant empirical and theoretical challenges.

**Carol Gilligan, *In a Different Voice: Psychological Theory and Women's Development* (1982)** — Gilligan's challenge to Kohlberg, arguing that his stage theory reflects a male bias toward justice and abstract principles at the expense of an ethics of care grounded in relationships and particularity. Her account opened debates about gender and moral development that continue today.

## Secondary Literature

**Julia Annas, *Intelligent Virtue* (2011)** — A sophisticated defense of virtue ethics against situationist challenges, arguing that genuine virtue is a skill developed through practice—like musical or athletic skill—and that situationist research targets pseudo-virtues rather than the real thing.

**Maria Merritt, John Doris, and Gilbert Harman, "Character" (2010)** — A careful examination of the situationism debate, assessing the evidence and its implications for virtue ethics. The authors defend a "situationist" conclusion while acknowledging that the best response may be to revise rather than abandon virtue ethics.

**Owen Flanagan, *Varieties of Moral Personality: Ethics and Psychological Realism* (1991)** — Argues that moral philosophy must be psychologically realistic—constrained by what we know about human psychology—and examines what kinds of moral character are psychologically achievable.

**James Rest, *Development in Judging Moral Issues* (1979)** — A development of Kohlberg's framework that examines how moral judgment develops and how it relates to moral motivation and moral behavior. Provides empirical evidence for the complexity of moral development.

**Flanagan and Rorty (eds.), *Identity, Character and Morality: Essays in Moral Psychology* (1990)** — An important anthology collecting philosophical and psychological approaches to character, identity, and moral development, bridging analytic moral philosophy and moral psychology.
