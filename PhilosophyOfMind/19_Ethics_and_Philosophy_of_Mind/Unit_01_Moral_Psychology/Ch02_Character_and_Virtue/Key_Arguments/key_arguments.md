# Key Arguments, Concepts, and Thought Experiments: Character and Virtue

## Key Arguments

**The Situationist Challenge to Character**
John Doris and Gilbert Harman argue, drawing on Milgram's obedience studies and Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment, that behavior is primarily determined by situational factors (authority, social roles, trivial contextual cues) rather than stable character traits. If character traits are the stable, cross-situational dispositions to behave in characteristic ways, then the situationist evidence — showing that most people's behavior is highly variable across situations — undermines the existence of character traits in the philosophically robust sense. The argument targets Aristotelian virtue ethics, which holds that virtuous people reliably act well across diverse situations.

**The Aristotelian Response to Situationism**
Defenders of virtue ethics (Annas, Zagzebski, Kamtekar) argue that situationist evidence does not refute Aristotelian virtue theory because: (1) Aristotle himself acknowledged that virtue requires habituation and that most people are not virtuous; (2) the experimental situations tested were novel and extreme, not everyday situations for which virtues are cultivated; and (3) genuine virtues (like justice and practical wisdom) involve sensitivity to situational features rather than context-blind behavioral consistency. The response argues that situationism refutes a behaviorist caricature of virtue rather than the Aristotelian conception.

**The Relationship Between Neuroscience and Virtue**
Some researchers (Damasio, Aristotle-inspired cognitive scientists) argue that virtues can be understood as stable affective-cognitive dispositions implemented in neural circuits, particularly those involving the prefrontal cortex and limbic system. Damasio's somatic marker account suggests that the virtuous person has well-calibrated emotional responses that guide practical judgment. Critics argue that reducing virtue to neural dispositions risks biologizing what is fundamentally a normative category and losing the evaluative dimension of virtue.

**The Empirical Case Against Strong Character (Milgram)**
Milgram's obedience studies — in which 65% of participants administered apparently lethal electric shocks to innocent victims when instructed to by an authority figure — are the primary empirical evidence for situationism. The result challenged folk-psychological assumptions about character: people who seemed kind, normal, and decent in everyday settings participated in apparent atrocity when situation-specific pressures were applied. The study suggests that situational forces are far more powerful determinants of behavior than character traits, with implications for both moral psychology and practical ethics.

## Core Concepts

**Virtue**
In Aristotelian virtue ethics, a virtue (aretē) is a stable, reliable disposition (hexis) to feel, desire, and act appropriately across a wide range of situations — the result of habituation that trains both the rational and the affective parts of the soul to respond well. A virtuous person not only acts rightly but acts with the right emotional engagement, from the right motive, and without the internal conflict that characterizes the merely continent (self-controlled) person. The virtuous person has practical wisdom (phronesis) — the ability to perceive the morally salient features of situations and deliberate correctly about how to respond.

**Situationism**
Situationism in moral psychology is the view, associated with Doris and Harman, that behavior is primarily determined by situational features (authority, social roles, minor contextual variations) rather than by stable, cross-situational character traits. Situationism is defended empirically by pointing to the dramatic situational variability in helping behavior (Good Samaritan studies), obedience (Milgram), and anti-social behavior (Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment). The philosophical implications concern the viability of virtue theory and the conditions for moral responsibility.

**Practical Wisdom (Phronesis)**
Practical wisdom is Aristotle's term for the master virtue: the ability to perceive what is morally relevant in a situation, to deliberate well about the means to good ends, and to integrate emotional and rational responses appropriately. Phronesis is not merely intelligence (techne) or theoretical wisdom (sophia) but the specifically practical capacity for sound deliberation about what to do and how to live. It is central to Aristotelian virtue ethics because virtues without phronesis are blind, and phronesis without virtues is impotent.

**Trait Theory vs. Situationism**
The debate between trait theory (in personality psychology: Allport, Cattell, Big Five theorists) and situationism concerns whether stable, cross-situational personality traits reliably predict behavior. Situationists (Mischel's classic critique) argue that situational factors explain more variance in behavior than traits; trait theorists respond by noting that: (1) traits predict behavior better when measured as aggregates across situations; (2) trait-situation interactions are important; and (3) traits predict behavior reasonably well in appropriate conditions. The debate is empirically ongoing.

**Character Fragmentation (Doris)**
Doris argues that even if people have some character traits, they tend to be highly local and situation-specific rather than robust and global: someone who is generous with friends may be stingy with strangers; someone honest in low-stakes contexts may be dishonest when it matters. This "character fragmentation" means that the robust, global character traits required by virtue ethics are empirically rare, even if local traits exist. The practical upshot is that we should focus on improving the situations people face rather than trying to improve their characters.

**Habituation**
Aristotle's account of how virtues are acquired: just as we become builders by building and musicians by playing music, we become courageous by doing courageous acts, just by doing just acts, and so on. Virtues are not innate but must be cultivated through repeated practice until the appropriate responses become second nature — part of one's character in the full dispositional sense. Habituation involves training both action and emotion: the virtuous person not only acts rightly but feels the right emotions at the right times.

## Thought Experiments

**The Milgram Obedience Study**
Milgram's famous study has the structure of a thought experiment made real: participants believed they were administering increasing electric shocks to a "learner" who cried out in pain and eventually became silent. Sixty-five percent of participants continued to the maximum shock level when instructed by an authority figure. The thought experiment asks: if you were in this situation, what would you do? Most people before the experiment predict they would refuse early; most participants in the experiment did not. The gap reveals the limits of moral self-knowledge and the power of situational pressures.

**The Good Samaritan Study (Darley and Batson)**
Seminary students on their way to give a sermon about the Good Samaritan were more likely to help a person slumped in a doorway if they were not in a hurry than if they were late. The content of their intended sermon (helping behavior) was irrelevant to their actual helping behavior; situational time pressure determined their behavior. The thought experiment is used by Doris to argue that even people with relevant character dispositions (religious commitment to helping) have their behavior dominated by situational factors.

**Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment**
Zimbardo randomly assigned psychology students to roles as "guards" or "prisoners" in a simulated prison. Within days, the "guards" were exhibiting systematic abusive and dehumanizing behavior toward the "prisoners," who showed signs of psychological breakdown. The experiment was terminated early due to the severity of the effects. The case is taken as evidence for situationism: ordinary, psychologically healthy students became abusive when placed in a role that situationally legitimized domination. It raises profound questions about the relationship between character, role, and institutional context.
