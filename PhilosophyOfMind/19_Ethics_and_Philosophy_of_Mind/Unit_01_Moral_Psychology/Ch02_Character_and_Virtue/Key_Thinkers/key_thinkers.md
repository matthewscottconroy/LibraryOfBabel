# Key Thinkers: Character and Virtue

---

**Aristotle (384–322 BCE)**
Ancient Greek philosopher, student of Plato. Aristotle's virtue ethics holds that moral character consists in stable dispositions—virtues—to feel, perceive, and act appropriately, and that these are acquired through practice and habituation (we become just by doing just actions). His concept of practical wisdom (phronesis) as the intellectual virtue that enables good judgment about particular cases, and his "doctrine of the mean" defining virtues as means between excess and deficiency, provide a rich psychological account of moral character. His account of how virtues are stable, situation-transcending traits has been directly challenged by contemporary situationism.
*Key works:* *Nicomachean Ethics* (c. 350 BCE), *Politics* (c. 350 BCE), *De Anima* (c. 350 BCE)

---

**John Doris (1963–)**
American philosopher at Washington University in St. Louis. Doris's *Lack of Character* (2002) developed the most philosophically rigorous version of the situationist challenge to virtue ethics, arguing that social psychological evidence—particularly Milgram's obedience studies and situational manipulations like the Good Samaritan experiment—shows that behavior is more strongly determined by situational factors than by character traits. He argues for a "fragmented" account of character that acknowledges the power of situations while preserving a role for thin, context-specific psychological traits.
*Key works:* *Lack of Character* (2002), *Talking to Our Selves: Reflection, Ignorance, and Agency* (2015)

---

**Lawrence Kohlberg (1927–1987)**
American developmental psychologist at Harvard University. Kohlberg's six-stage theory of moral development—from pre-conventional (self-interest), through conventional (social norms), to post-conventional (universal principles) stages—dominated developmental moral psychology for decades. His rationalist framework held that moral development consists in the acquisition of increasingly abstract and universal moral reasoning. His methodology (hypothetical moral dilemmas requiring reasoned justification) has been criticized for privileging a particular kind of reasoning and for cultural and gender bias.
*Key works:* "The Development of Children's Orientations toward a Moral Order" (1963), *The Psychology of Moral Development* (1984)

---

**Carol Gilligan (1936–)**
American psychologist and ethicist at Harvard University. Gilligan's challenge to Kohlberg's stage theory—based on her research showing that women's moral reasoning was systematic and sophisticated despite scoring "lower" on Kohlberg's scale—introduced the ethics of care as a major moral framework. She argued that Kohlberg's scale measured abstract justice reasoning and thereby systematically downgraded relational, care-based moral thinking. Her work opened debates about gender and moral development and launched the care ethics tradition.
*Key works:* *In a Different Voice* (1982), *Mapping the Moral Domain* (1988, ed.)

---

**Julia Annas (1946–)**
British-American philosopher at the University of Arizona. Annas has developed a neo-Aristotelian virtue ethics that engages rigorously with both ancient and contemporary challenges. Her account of virtue as a skill—developed through practice, with an intelligent rather than automatic character—responds to situationist challenges by arguing that situationism targets pseudo-virtues while genuine virtue exhibits the flexibility and judgment that skills develop. Her work connects ancient virtue ethics to contemporary psychology and philosophy of mind.
*Key works:* *The Morality of Happiness* (1993), *Intelligent Virtue* (2011), *Platonic Ethics, Old and New* (1999)
