# Further Reading: Consciousness and Moral Obligation

## Primary Sources

**Peter Singer, *Animal Liberation* (1975)** — Singer's foundational argument for the moral consideration of sentient beings holds that the capacity to suffer—not rationality, language, or species membership—is the basis for moral status. His preference utilitarianism requires that we give equal consideration to the equal interests of all sentient creatures, making phenomenal consciousness directly relevant to moral obligation.

**Peter Singer, *Practical Ethics* (3rd ed., 2011)** — Singer's comprehensive treatment of applied ethics, covering infanticide, disability, euthanasia, and animal ethics. His account of personhood and moral status draws explicitly on consciousness and sentience as the criteria that ground moral consideration.

**Jeff McMahan, *The Ethics of Killing: Problems at the Margins of Life* (2002)** — McMahan develops a sophisticated account of moral status that combines psychological continuity with the deprivation account of death's badness: moral status tracks the capacity for a biographical life, which requires a higher level of psychological integration than mere sentience. His framework generates graduated moral status tied to cognitive and experiential complexity.

**Mary Anne Warren, "Moral and Legal Status of Abortion" (1973), *The Monist*** — Warren's influential account of personhood identifies consciousness as one of several features—including self-awareness, rationality, self-motivated activity, and the capacity to communicate—that together constitute full personhood. Her account has been applied to questions about fetal status, animal status, and AI.

**Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky, "The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence" (2011), in *Cambridge Handbook of Artificial Intelligence*** — Examines the moral status of AI systems, arguing that consciousness is the key variable and that we should take seriously the possibility of morally considerable AI before we create it.

## Secondary Literature

**David DeGrazia, *Taking Animals Seriously* (1996)** — A rigorous philosophical examination of animal mental life and its moral implications, arguing that animals with rich enough inner lives have moral status that demands serious consideration.

**Allen Buchanan, *Beyond Humanity: The Ethics of Biomedical Enhancement* (2011)** — Examines how cognitive and psychological enhancement technologies might change the basis of moral status and what implications this has for how enhanced persons should be treated.

**Eric Olson, "Personal Identity and the Grounds of Moral Consideration" (various)** — Examines the relationship between different accounts of personal identity and the grounds of moral consideration, showing how metaphysical and ethical questions are intertwined.

**Christine Korsgaard, *Fellow Creatures: Our Obligations to the Other Animals* (2018)** — Develops a Kantian argument for animal moral status grounded in the claim that animals have their own kind of good—a perspective on what matters—which grounds obligations toward them even without full rational autonomy.

**David Chalmers, "The Singularity: A Philosophical Analysis" (2010)** — Examines the moral status of potential posthuman and AI entities, arguing that consciousness is the key variable for moral status and that we should think carefully about obligations to radically altered or artificial minds.
