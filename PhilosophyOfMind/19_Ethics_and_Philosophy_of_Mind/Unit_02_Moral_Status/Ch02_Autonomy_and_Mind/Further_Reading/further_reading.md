# Further Reading: Autonomy and Mind

## Primary Sources

**Immanuel Kant, *Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785)** — Kant's account of moral autonomy as self-legislation through pure practical reason is the foundational account of autonomy. For Kant, the capacity for autonomous self-determination is what gives persons their incomparable dignity, and violations of autonomy are the deepest wrongs. Understanding Kantian autonomy is essential for grasping both its appeal and its limitations in the context of mental illness.

**Harry Frankfurt, "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971), *Journal of Philosophy*** — Frankfurt's hierarchical account of autonomy distinguishes first-order desires (desires to do things) from second-order desires (desires about what desires to have) and second-order volitions (desires about what desires to be effective). A free person is one whose will is in accord with her second-order volitions—she identifies with her effective desires.

**Gerald Dworkin, *The Theory and Practice of Autonomy* (1988)** — Dworkin's account of personal autonomy as authenticity and self-identification—the capacity to form second-order preferences and to identify with one's own motivations—provides the framework for thinking about how mental illness can compromise autonomy without entirely eliminating it.

**John Christman, *The Politics of Persons: Individual Autonomy and Socio-Historical Selves* (2009)** — A comprehensive treatment of autonomy that addresses both procedural and substantive conceptions, examining the relationship between autonomy and social context, history, and self-narrative. Particularly relevant for understanding autonomy in the context of oppression and illness.

**Joel Feinberg, *Harm to Self: The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law*, Vol. 3 (1986)** — Feinberg's detailed treatment of paternalism—the justification for limiting someone's freedom for their own good—examines the conditions under which interference with autonomous choices is justified, which is directly relevant to debates about paternalism in mental health care.

## Secondary Literature

**Natalie Stoljar, "Feminist Perspectives on Autonomy" (2018), *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*** — Reviews feminist challenges to standard accounts of autonomy, arguing that socially oppressive conditions can undermine autonomy even when formal conditions of self-legislation are met. Relevant for thinking about autonomy in populations whose choices are constrained by systemic disadvantage.

**Louis Charland, "Mental Competence and Value: The Problem of Normativity in the Assessment of Decision-Making Capacity" (2011)** — Examines how decisions about mental competence in healthcare inevitably involve normative assessments that go beyond purely cognitive criteria, raising questions about the relationship between autonomy, rationality, and mental illness.

**Peter Breggin, *Brain-Disabling Treatments in Psychiatry* (2nd ed., 2008)** — A critical psychiatrist's examination of how psychiatric treatments—medications, ECT, and psychosurgery—can affect autonomy and authentic self-expression, raising questions about the relationship between treatment, coercion, and the authentic self.

**Jennifer Radden (ed.), *The Philosophy of Psychiatry: A Companion* (2004)** — A comprehensive handbook covering philosophical issues in psychiatry including autonomy, informed consent, and the concept of mental competence. Essential reference for philosophy of psychiatry.

**Scott Kim, *Evaluation of Capacity to Consent to Treatment and Research* (2010)** — A clinical and philosophical guide to assessing decision-making capacity in patients with psychiatric illness, examining how autonomy considerations bear on clinical practice.
