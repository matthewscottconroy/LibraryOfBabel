# Key Thinkers: Autonomy and Mind

---

**Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)**
German philosopher of the Enlightenment. Kant's account of autonomy as self-legislation through pure practical reason grounds both his moral philosophy and his account of human dignity. For Kant, autonomy—the capacity to give oneself the moral law through reason—is what distinguishes persons from things and grounds their incomparable worth. Mental illness, on Kantian grounds, is morally significant primarily because it compromises the capacity for rational self-determination; and paternalistic intervention is justified only insofar as it restores rather than overrides autonomous capacity. This framework remains the dominant philosophical background for discussions of mental competence.
*Key works:* *Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), *The Metaphysics of Morals* (1797)

---

**Harry Frankfurt (1929–)**
American philosopher at Princeton University. Frankfurt's hierarchical account of freedom and autonomy has been enormously influential in philosophy of mind and moral psychology. His distinction between first- and second-order desires, and his concept of second-order volitions (caring about which of one's desires are effective), provides a framework for understanding how addiction, compulsion, and mental illness can compromise autonomy without eliminating it. Conditions in which a person cannot identify with their effective desires—where their will is alien to their second-order self—represent paradigm cases of unfreedom.
*Key works:* "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971), "Identification and Wholeheartedness" (1987), *The Importance of What We Care About* (1988)

---

**Gerald Dworkin (1937–)**
American philosopher at UC Davis. Dworkin's account of personal autonomy as the capacity for critical reflection on and identification with one's own motivations provides a procedural framework that can distinguish genuine autonomous choices from compelled or manipulated ones. His analysis of paternalism—when it is and is not justified to override someone's choices for their own good—is a careful treatment that respects autonomy while acknowledging its limits in cases of diminished capacity.
*Key works:* "Paternalism" (1972), *The Theory and Practice of Autonomy* (1988)

---

**John Christman (1957–)**
American philosopher at Penn State University. Christman's procedural account of autonomy focuses on the process by which values and preferences are formed rather than their content, arguing that an autonomous agent is one who can reflectively endorse her own history of preference formation. His approach is particularly relevant for mental illness cases, where the question is often whether a history of psychological distress has compromised the capacity for authentic self-determination.
*Key works:* *The Inner Citadel: Essays on Individual Autonomy* (ed., 1989), *The Politics of Persons* (2009)

---

**John Stuart Mill (1806–1873)**
British utilitarian philosopher. Mill's harm principle—that the only legitimate justification for interfering with someone's liberty is to prevent harm to others—is the classical statement of anti-paternalism. His arguments for individual liberty are grounded in the importance of autonomous self-development to human flourishing rather than purely in rights. In *On Liberty*, he distinguished between competent and incompetent choices, acknowledging that children and those not "in the maturity of their faculties" may legitimately be protected by paternalistic intervention.
*Key works:* *On Liberty* (1859), *Utilitarianism* (1863), *A System of Logic* (1843)
