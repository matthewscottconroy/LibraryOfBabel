# Further Reading: Brain Interventions

## Primary Sources

**Julian Savulescu and Nick Bostrom (eds.), *Human Enhancement* (2009)** — A comprehensive collection covering the ethics of cognitive enhancement, including pharmacological, genetic, and technological means. Key essays cover the debate between transhumanists who favor unlimited enhancement and critics who worry about equality, authenticity, and human nature.

**Michael Sandel, *The Case Against Perfection: Ethics in the Age of Genetic Engineering* (2007)** — Sandel's critique of enhancement argues that the drive to perfect and enhance human capacities reflects an excessive striving for mastery that threatens the virtues of humility, giftedness, and solidarity. His concerns about pharmacological enhancement connect to broader questions about what we value in human achievement.

**Adam Kolber, "Therapeutic Forgetting: The Legal and Ethical Implications of Memory Dampening" (2006), *Vanderbilt Law Review*** — An examination of the ethical and legal implications of using drugs like propranolol to dampen traumatic memories. Kolber argues that there are good reasons to allow memory modification while addressing concerns about authenticity, identity, and evidence.

**Paul Appelbaum, "Law and Psychiatry: The New Lies of Brain Science" (2005)** — Examines the uses and misuses of neuroscience in legal contexts, including as evidence in criminal trials, and raises questions about the appropriate role of brain science in attributions of responsibility and punishment.

**President's Council on Bioethics (chair Leon Kass), *Beyond Therapy: Biotechnology and the Pursuit of Happiness* (2003)** — The influential report examining the ethics of using biotechnology for enhancement rather than therapy, with chapters on memory modification, mood control, and performance enhancement. Represents a broadly conservative bioethical position.

## Secondary Literature

**Nick Bostrom and Anders Sandberg, "Cognitive Enhancement: Methods, Ethics, Regulatory Challenges" (2009), *Science and Engineering Ethics*** — A comprehensive treatment of the landscape of cognitive enhancement methods and the ethical issues they raise, covering both pharmacological and technological approaches.

**Walter Glannon, *Brain, Body, and Mind: Neuroethics with a Human Face* (2011)** — A rigorous treatment of neuroethical issues including brain interventions, memory modification, and deep brain stimulation, addressing questions of authenticity, identity, and moral responsibility.

**David DeGrazia, "Prozac, Enhancement, and Self-Creation" (2000), *Hastings Center Report*** — Examines whether pharmacological modification of personality and mood compromises authentic self-expression, arguing against the view that pharmacology-enabled changes are necessarily inauthentic.

**Martha Farah et al., "Neurocognitive Enhancement: What Can We Do and What Should We Do?" (2004), *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*** — An influential overview of pharmacological cognitive enhancement, covering the empirical evidence for enhancement effects and the ethical questions they raise.

**Jens Clausen and Neil Levy (eds.), *Handbook of Neuroethics* (2015)** — A comprehensive reference covering all major topics in neuroethics, including cognitive enhancement, brain reading, deep brain stimulation, and the ethics of psychiatric treatment.
