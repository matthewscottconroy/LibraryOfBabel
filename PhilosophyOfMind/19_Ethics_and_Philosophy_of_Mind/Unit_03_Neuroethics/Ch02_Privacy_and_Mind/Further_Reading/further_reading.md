# Further Reading: Privacy and Mind

## Primary Sources

**Nita Farahany, *The Battle for Your Brain: Defending the Right to Think Freely in the Age of Neurotechnology* (2023)** — Farahany's comprehensive and timely treatment of cognitive liberty and neural privacy, arguing that advances in brain-computer interfaces and neural monitoring technology require new legal and ethical frameworks protecting the right to mental self-determination. She develops the concept of "cognitive liberty" as the right to think freely and to control access to one's own mental states.

**Rafael Yuste and Sara Goering et al., "Four Ethical Priorities for Neurotechnologies and AI" (2017), *Nature*** — An influential statement by neuroscientists and ethicists of four priorities for governing neurotechnologies: privacy and consent, agency and identity, augmentation, and bias. This paper helped establish the agenda for neurorights as a legal and ethical concern.

**Walter Glannon, *Brain, Body, and Mind: Neuroethics with a Human Face* (2011)** — Contains important chapters on the ethics of brain reading and mental privacy, examining what access to neural states would mean for personal autonomy and the integrity of the mental sphere.

**Paul Bernal, *Internet Privacy Rights: Rights to Protect Autonomy* (2014)** — While focused on internet privacy, Bernal's framework for understanding informational privacy as grounded in autonomy provides the conceptual tools for extending privacy rights to neural data.

**Francis Fukuyama, *Our Posthuman Future: Consequences of the Biotechnology Revolution* (2002)** — Examines how biotechnological changes—including neural monitoring—might alter the nature of human experience and identity, raising questions about the privacy and integrity of the self.

## Secondary Literature

**Marcello Ienca and Roberto Andorno, "Towards New Human Rights in the Neurotechnology Era" (2017), *Life Sciences, Society and Policy*** — Argues for new human rights specifically protecting mental privacy and cognitive liberty, including the right to mental integrity and the right to psychological continuity. This paper has been influential in neurorights advocacy.

**Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis, "The Right to Privacy" (1890), *Harvard Law Review*** — The classic legal statement of the right to privacy as the right to be left alone. While not about neural privacy, this foundational paper established the conceptual basis for privacy law that has been extended to newer domains.

**Miriam Boeri, "The Neuroscience of Privacy: Neuroimaging, Mental States, and the Right to Cognitive Liberty" (2017)** — Examines the implications of neuroimaging for privacy law, arguing that mental states accessed through brain scanning require stronger privacy protections than ordinary health information.

**Brian Chen, "Neural Interfaces, Autonomy, and Mental Privacy" (2020)** — Examines the philosophical foundations of neural privacy, arguing that the special intimacy of mental states grounds a distinctive right to mental privacy that extends beyond standard informational privacy.

**José-Luis Pérez Triviño, "Neuroethics and Sports" (2019)** — Examines the ethics of neural monitoring and enhancement in sports contexts, illustrating the range of contexts in which questions of neural privacy and cognitive liberty arise.
