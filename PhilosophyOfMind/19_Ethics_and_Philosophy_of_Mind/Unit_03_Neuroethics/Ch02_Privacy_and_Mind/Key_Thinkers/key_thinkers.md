# Key Thinkers: Privacy and Mind

---

**Nita Farahany (1975–)**
American legal scholar, philosopher, and bioethicist at Duke University. Farahany is the most prominent advocate for cognitive liberty and neural privacy rights, arguing in *The Battle for Your Brain* (2023) that advances in consumer neurotechnology—including brain-computer interfaces, neural monitoring headsets, and brain decoding algorithms—require new legal protections for mental self-determination. She argues that existing privacy law is inadequate to protect against neural surveillance and that cognitive liberty—the right to think freely and to control access to one's mental states—must be recognized as a fundamental right.
*Key works:* *The Battle for Your Brain* (2023), "The Duty to Self-Report Brain Abnormalities" (2012)

---

**Rafael Yuste (1963–)**
Spanish-American neuroscientist at Columbia University, co-inventor of two-photon microscopy and a leader in the BRAIN Initiative. Yuste has been a prominent advocate for "neurorights"—legal and ethical protections for mental states accessed through neurotechnology. His 2017 *Nature* paper with colleagues articulating four ethical priorities for neurotechnologies helped establish neurorights as a global policy agenda, and his advocacy contributed to Chile becoming the first country to incorporate neurorights into its constitution in 2021.
*Key works:* "Four Ethical Priorities for Neurotechnologies and AI" (2017), *Neurorights Foundation* (organization)

---

**Marcello Ienca (1987–)**
Swiss-Italian bioethicist at the Technical University of Munich. Ienca has been a leading academic voice for neurorights and cognitive liberty, arguing for new human rights specifically tailored to the neurotechnology era. His proposed rights include the right to mental privacy (protection of neural data from unauthorized access), the right to mental integrity (protection from unwanted neural manipulation), and the right to psychological continuity (protection of one's sense of self from external disruption). His work bridges bioethics, human rights law, and neuroscience.
*Key works:* "Towards New Human Rights in the Neurotechnology Era" (2017, with Andorno), "Big Data, Small Hands" (2017)

---

**Walter Glannon (1957–)**
Canadian philosopher at the University of Calgary. Glannon's neuroethics work includes careful philosophical analysis of the ethics of brain reading, examining what access to neural states would mean for personal autonomy, the integrity of the private self, and the limits of legitimate third-party knowledge. He connects questions about neural privacy to broader philosophical accounts of personhood, self-knowledge, and the importance of mental interiority for human dignity.
*Key works:* *Brain, Body, and Mind* (2011), *Bioethics and the Brain* (2006)

---

**Francis Fukuyama (1952–)**
American political scientist at Johns Hopkins University. Fukuyama's *Our Posthuman Future* (2002) raises concerns about biotechnological changes—including neural monitoring and enhancement—that might alter the nature of human experience and identity in ways that threaten what he calls "Factor X"—the essentially human dignity that underlies human rights. While writing from a political science rather than philosophy of mind perspective, his concerns about the integrity of the self provide a conservative bioethical perspective on neural privacy.
*Key works:* *Our Posthuman Future* (2002), *The End of History and the Last Man* (1992)
