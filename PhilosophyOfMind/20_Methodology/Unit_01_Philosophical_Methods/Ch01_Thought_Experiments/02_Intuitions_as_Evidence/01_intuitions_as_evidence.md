# Intuitions as Evidence in Philosophy of Mind

The epistemic status of philosophical intuitions is one of the most debated methodological questions in contemporary philosophy. Philosophy of mind relies heavily on intuitive judgments — about what is conceivable, what concepts apply, what follows from what — and the legitimacy of this reliance is contested. Understanding what intuitions are, why they might carry evidential weight, and when they should be trusted or revised is essential for philosophical methodology.

**What Philosophical Intuitions Are**

A philosophical intuition is a felt sense that something is true or false, right or wrong, possible or impossible, without the intuition being the conclusion of an explicit inference. When we consider whether Mary learns something new when she first sees red, we don't calculate an answer; we just find ourselves with the view that yes, she does, and this view has the character of seeming obvious or evident rather than merely probable.

Several things can count as intuitions: conceptual intuitions (it seems conceptually necessary that anything colored red is colored), modal intuitions (it seems conceivable that a zombie world is possible), moral intuitions (it seems obviously wrong to push the innocent person off the bridge), and phenomenological intuitions (it seems that there is something it is like to see red).

These are phenomenologically distinct from both ordinary perceptual judgments (which are caused by perception of the external world) and from the conclusions of explicit arguments (which are arrived at through reasoning). They have a distinctive "seeming evident" quality — they present themselves as obvious without being derived from evidence or argument.

**Why Intuitions Might Have Evidential Value**

Several accounts of why intuitions carry evidential weight have been developed. The conceptual competence account holds that intuitions about philosophical cases reflect implicit knowledge of the concepts being applied. When we intuit that Mary learns something new, we are drawing on our implicit knowledge of what "knowledge" and "learning" mean — knowledge that is built into our competence with these concepts. Intuitions are evidence about what our concepts mean and what follows from them.

The faculty rationalism account (à la George Bealer) holds that intuitions are the deliverances of a rational faculty that tracks modal and conceptual truths — a priori knowledge of necessary connections. Just as perception is evidence about the external world, rational intuition is evidence about modal and conceptual facts.

The reflective equilibrium method (Rawls) holds that intuitions are the initial data that philosophical theorizing must explain and systematize. We construct theories to make sense of our intuitions, revise intuitions when theory requires it, and aim at a state of reflective equilibrium where theory and intuitions cohere. Intuitions are not incorrigible, but they provide the initial data that any adequate theory must largely respect.

**Challenges from Experimental Philosophy**

Experimental philosophy has challenged the epistemic status of intuitions by showing that they vary in ways that undermine their claim to evidential reliability. If philosophy aims to uncover necessary truths or universal conceptual facts, it should generate universal intuitions. But experimental philosophy studies have found that intuitions about Gettier cases, intuitions about intentionality attribution, and intuitions about moral responsibility vary across cultures, educational levels, and social circumstances.

This variation can be interpreted in different ways. The debunking interpretation: intuitive variation shows that intuitions are tracking culturally variable factors rather than universal conceptual or modal facts — which undermines their evidential weight. The expertise interpretation: the intuitions of philosophical experts who have thought carefully about the relevant cases are more reliable than lay intuitions, just as the judgments of trained scientists are more reliable than lay judgments about empirical matters. The theoretical interpretation: what looks like intuitive disagreement often reflects disagreement about background theoretical commitments — resolving the theoretical disagreement would bring intuitions into alignment.

**The Proper Use of Intuitions**

The most defensible position acknowledges both the indispensability and the fallibility of philosophical intuitions. Intuitions are indispensable because they are the primary starting point for philosophical inquiry — we cannot get philosophical arguments started without some initial judgments about cases. But they are fallible because they can reflect cognitive biases, limited imagination, cultural assumptions, and other distorting factors.

The appropriate response is not to abandon intuitions but to subject them to critical scrutiny: to investigate why they are held, to check whether they survive reflection and theoretical challenge, to compare them across different framings and different populations, and to look for convergence between different intuitive approaches. Intuitions that are wide-spread, stable under reflection, explicable by the best theories, and consistent across populations and framings have stronger evidential weight than those that are parochial, unstable, or theoretically unmotivated.
