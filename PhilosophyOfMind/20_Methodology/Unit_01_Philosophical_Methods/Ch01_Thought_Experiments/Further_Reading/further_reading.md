# Further Reading: Thought Experiments

## Primary Sources

**Daniel Dennett, *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking* (2013)** — Dennett's collection and analysis of "intuition pumps"—thought experiments designed to generate intuitive responses. He examines both his own thought experiments (the Cartesian theater, zombie thought experiments, the Chinese Room) and those of others, arguing that many famous philosophical thought experiments are misleading pumps that generate unreliable intuitions. A valuable guide to thinking critically about thought experiments.

**Frank Jackson, "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982), *Philosophical Quarterly*** — Mary the color scientist who knows everything physical about color but has never seen red—the "knowledge argument"—is one of the most discussed thought experiments in philosophy of mind. Jackson's argument that Mary learns something new when she finally sees red is intended to show that physicalism cannot account for phenomenal consciousness. The paper exemplifies thought experiments at their most influential.

**Kathleen Wilkes, *Real People: Personal Identity Without Thought Experiments* (1988)** — Wilkes's systematic critique of thought experiments in personal identity argues that they trade in fictions so distant from actual human psychology and metaphysics that the intuitions they generate are unreliable. She argues for grounding philosophy of personal identity in actual cases from psychology, medicine, and anthropology instead.

**Roy Sorensen, *Thought Experiments* (1992)** — A comprehensive philosophical treatment of the nature and methodology of thought experiments, examining how they work, what they establish, and how they should be evaluated. Sorensen defends thought experiments as genuine a priori instruments while acknowledging their limitations.

**John Norton, "Are Thought Experiments Just What You Thought?" (1996), *Canadian Journal of Philosophy*** — Norton's influential argument that thought experiments are nothing but arguments—they establish their conclusions through explicit or implicit reasoning, not through some special faculty of philosophical insight. If true, this undermines the view that thought experiments provide a priori access to necessary truths.

## Secondary Literature

**Timothy Williamson, *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007)** — Williamson's defense of armchair philosophy, arguing that philosophical thought experiments are legitimate instruments for modal knowledge. His account connects intuitions about thought experiments to our ability to evaluate counterfactual conditionals.

**George Bealer, "Intuitions and the Autonomy of Philosophy" (1998)** — Bealer's rational intuitionism holds that intuitions are a priori evidence for philosophical conclusions, grounding a defense of thought experiments as epistemically valid.

**Jonathan Ichikawa and Matthias Steup, "The Analysis of Knowledge" (2018), *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*** — Covers the Gettier problem—the decisive thought experiment against the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief—and subsequent philosophical responses. Essential background for understanding the role of thought experiments in analytic epistemology.

**James Robert Brown, *The Laboratory of the Mind: Thought Experiments in the Natural Sciences* (1991)** — Examines thought experiments in physics and other sciences, arguing that they provide genuine a priori knowledge through Platonic insight into natural laws. Provides context for understanding philosophical thought experiments.

**Eric Schwitzgebel and Fiery Cushman, "Philosophers Are No Better Than Non-Philosophers at Avoiding Reasoning Biases" (2012), *Philosophical Psychology*** — An experimental philosophy study showing that philosophers are subject to the same order effects and framing biases in responding to moral dilemmas as non-philosophers, raising questions about whether philosophical expertise actually improves intuitive reliability.
