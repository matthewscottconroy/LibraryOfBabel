# Key Thinkers: Thought Experiments

---

**Daniel Dennett (1942–)**
American philosopher at Tufts University. Dennett has been the most prolific and self-reflective deployer of thought experiments in philosophy of mind, as well as the most systematic critic of misleading intuition pumps. His *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking* (2013) examines dozens of thought experiments—Searle's Chinese Room, Nagel's bat, his own "Where Am I?" brain-in-a-vat story—and explains what makes some philosophically productive and others misleading. His criterion: a good thought experiment should clarify rather than beg questions, and should generate reliable intuitions rather than exploiting our tendency toward confusion.
*Key works:* *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking* (2013), *Consciousness Explained* (1991), *Brainstorms* (1978)

---

**Frank Jackson (1943–)**
Australian philosopher at the Australian National University and Princeton. Jackson's "Mary's Room" thought experiment—the color scientist who knows all the physical facts about color perception but learns something new when she first sees red—is among the most discussed in contemporary philosophy of mind. The argument is intended to show that physicalism cannot account for the subjective, phenomenal character of experience. Jackson himself later retracted the anti-physicalist conclusion, arguing that the intuition pump misleads, making his case a valuable example of self-critical thought experiment methodology.
*Key works:* "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982), *From Metaphysics to Ethics* (1998)

---

**Kathleen Wilkes (1946–2003)**
British philosopher at Oxford University. Wilkes's *Real People* (1988) mounted the most systematic critique of thought experiments in personal identity, arguing that the fanciful scenarios involved (fission, teletransportation, brain transplants) are too distant from actual human psychology to generate philosophically reliable intuitions. She advocated replacing fictional cases with real cases from neuropsychology and medical ethics as a more epistemically solid basis for philosophical conclusions about personal identity.
*Key works:* *Real People: Personal Identity Without Thought Experiments* (1988)

---

**Roy Sorensen (1952–)**
American philosopher at Washington University in St. Louis. Sorensen has provided the most comprehensive philosophical treatment of thought experiments as a methodological tool, arguing that they are genuine a priori instruments for evaluating modal claims and for testing conceptual frameworks. His work distinguishes different types of thought experiments—destructive, constructive, and rhetorical—and examines the conditions under which they yield reliable philosophical insight.
*Key works:* *Thought Experiments* (1992), *Blindspots* (1988)

---

**Timothy Williamson (1955–)**
British philosopher at Oxford University. Williamson's defense of armchair philosophy in *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007) provides the most rigorous contemporary justification for thought experiments as legitimate philosophical method. He argues that our competence with ordinary modal concepts—what could and could not be the case—gives us non-trivial a priori access to necessary truths, and that philosophical thought experiments deploy this competence in a systematic way. His account grounds philosophical methodology in a theory of counterfactual knowledge.
*Key works:* *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007), *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000), *Modal Logic as Metaphysics* (2013)
