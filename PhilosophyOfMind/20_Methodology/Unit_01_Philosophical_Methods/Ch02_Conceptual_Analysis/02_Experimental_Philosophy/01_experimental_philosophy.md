# Experimental Philosophy

Experimental philosophy — the use of empirical, survey-based methods to investigate philosophical questions — has emerged as a significant and controversial research program since the early 2000s. By systematically studying the intuitions of ordinary people (rather than relying on the philosopher's own intuitions), experimental philosophy aims both to provide better data about intuitions and to challenge the authority of philosophical intuitions as evidence about conceptual or modal truths.

**The Emergence of Experimental Philosophy**

The experimental philosophy movement was launched by Jonathan Weinberg, Shaun Nichols, and Stephen Stich's (2001) paper showing that South Asian and East Asian subjects gave different responses than North American subjects to Gettier-style cases. Their interpretation: if intuitions about knowledge vary cross-culturally, they cannot be reliable evidence about universal conceptual truths. The philosophical practice of appealing to intuitions as evidence is undermined by their cultural variability.

Subsequent experimental philosophy work has examined intuitions in many areas: about intentional action (Knobe effect — the effect of moral valence on intentionality attributments), about causation, about personal identity, about free will and moral responsibility. These studies have found patterns of variance and convergence that challenge and complicate the traditional reliance on philosophical intuitions.

**The Knobe Effect**

Joshua Knobe's experimental philosophy research on intentional action is among the most influential. The Knobe effect: people judge that a side-effect of an action is brought about "intentionally" when the side-effect is harmful (and thereby bad) but "not intentionally" when it is beneficial (and thereby good). In the original study, subjects judged that a chairman who knowingly harmed the environment in pursuit of profit acted "intentionally" with respect to the harm, while a chairman who knowingly helped the environment in pursuit of profit did not act "intentionally" with respect to the benefit.

This finding is surprising from a purely descriptive standpoint: the logical structure of the two cases is symmetric — in both, the chairman knew the side-effect would occur and proceeded anyway. But the moral valence of the outcome affects the attribution of intentionality. This suggests that our concept of intentional action is not a purely descriptive concept but has built-in evaluative dimensions.

The Knobe effect has generated extensive philosophical discussion. Some interpret it as showing that folk psychology is inconsistent and that philosophical accounts of intentional action need to either explain away or accommodate the moral valence effect. Others interpret it as revealing something philosophically significant about how intention is understood — as involving not just knowledge and foreseeability but something about the relationship between the agent's attitudes and the outcome.

**The Scope and Limits of Experimental Philosophy**

Experimental philosophy raises important methodological questions about how to interpret survey results and what they establish. Finding that intuitions vary does not straightforwardly establish that no intuition is reliable: variance might reflect noise, comprehension failures, or the influence of task-irrelevant factors. Finding that intuitions converge does not straightforwardly establish that the convergent intuition is correct: universal intuitions can be systematically biased (as our shared evolutionary heritage might produce).

Philosophers have responded to experimental philosophy in various ways. The "restrictionist" response (Timothy Williamson, Sosa) holds that the relevant intuitions for philosophy are those of experts who have reflected carefully on the cases — just as scientists' experimental evidence is more reliable than lay observation. Lay intuitions are noisy data that may not accurately reflect the underlying conceptual facts. The relevant intuitions are those of trained philosophers considering well-specified cases.

The "revisionist" response holds that experimental philosophy should prompt revision of philosophical practice: philosophers should work harder to control for task-irrelevant factors in thought experiments, should take cross-cultural variation seriously as potential evidence of culturally variable concepts, and should be more cautious about generalizing from parochial intuitions.

The "positive" experimental philosophy program goes beyond critique: rather than just questioning the reliability of intuitions, it uses empirical methods to make positive contributions to philosophical questions — studying the structure of folk psychological concepts, the development of moral intuitions in children, the cross-cultural variation in notions of self and agency, and the neural basis of the intuitions themselves.

**Significance for Philosophy of Mind**

Experimental philosophy's significance for philosophy of mind is considerable. Many key thought experiments in philosophy of mind — zombie scenarios, inverted spectrum, Chinese Room, Mary's Room — depend on intuitions whose reliability and universality has not been systematically tested. Applying experimental philosophical methods to these cases could reveal whether the intuitions are robust or parochial, and could identify the factors that drive intuitive responses.

More broadly, experimental philosophy represents a productive blurring of the line between philosophy and empirical psychology — a recognition that philosophical questions about mind, self, and action are not purely a priori but connect to how people actually think, how concepts are actually deployed, and how psychological processes actually work. This methodological shift is consistent with the naturalistic turn in philosophy of mind more broadly.
