# Further Reading: Conceptual Analysis

## Primary Sources

**Edmund Gettier, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" (1963), *Analysis*** — Gettier's three-page paper offered two counterexamples to the traditional analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, inaugurating decades of epistemological work and demonstrating that even long-accepted conceptual analyses can be decisively overturned by carefully constructed cases. This paper is the paradigmatic example of how thought experiments can refute conceptual analyses.

**Frank Jackson, *From Metaphysics to Ethics: A Defence of Conceptual Analysis* (1998)** — Jackson's ambitious defense of conceptual analysis as the core method of metaphysics and ethics, arguing that philosophy's task is to analyze our folk concepts and then determine what, if anything, in the world those concepts apply to. His "two-dimensional semantics" provides a framework for conceptual analysis that is both systematic and revisable.

**W.V.O. Quine, "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), *Philosophical Review*** — Quine's critique of the analytic-synthetic distinction challenges the basis of traditional conceptual analysis, arguing that there is no clear distinction between analytic truths (true by meaning) and synthetic truths (true by fact). If there is no analytic-synthetic distinction, conceptual analysis cannot be the foundation for a priori philosophical knowledge.

**Jonathan Weinberg, Shaun Nichols, and Stephen Stich, "Normativity and Epistemic Intuitions" (2001), *Philosophical Topics*** — The foundational experimental philosophy paper, showing that epistemic intuitions (about Gettier cases and other scenarios) vary systematically across cultural groups, challenging the universality assumptions of traditional conceptual analysis.

**Herman Cappelen, *Philosophy Without Intuitions* (2012)** — Cappelen argues that the claim that philosophy essentially relies on intuitions is exaggerated—most philosophical argumentation does not appeal to pre-theoretical intuitions but to considered judgments, and experimental challenges to philosophical methodology have been overstated.

## Secondary Literature

**David Chalmers and Frank Jackson, "Conceptual Analysis and Reductive Explanation" (2001), *Philosophical Review*** — A defense of the viability of conceptual analysis within a two-dimensional semantic framework, arguing that conceptual analysis can proceed even if there are no analytic truths in Quine's sense.

**Timothy Williamson, *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007)** — Williamson's defense of philosophical methodology includes a careful account of how conceptual analysis, properly understood, can proceed without relying on contentious claims about analyticity. He argues that philosophical knowledge is knowledge of modal facts rather than meanings.

**Joshua Knobe and Shaun Nichols (eds.), *Experimental Philosophy* (2008)** — The leading anthology collecting experimental philosophy papers, covering folk psychology, moral judgment, agency, and metaphysics. Essential for understanding what experimental philosophy has established and what it claims.

**Brian Weatherson, "What Good Are Counterexamples?" (2003), *Philosophical Studies*** — Examines the methodology of using counterexamples in conceptual analysis, arguing that they should be treated as fallible evidence rather than decisive refutations, since our intuitions about cases can be as suspect as our theories.

**Edouard Machery, *Philosophy Within Its Proper Bounds* (2017)** — Argues that philosophy cannot make progress on many traditional questions because the ordinary concepts it analyzes are unreliable epistemic guides, and that philosophy should focus on questions it can actually address—a radical challenge to the conceptual analysis enterprise.
