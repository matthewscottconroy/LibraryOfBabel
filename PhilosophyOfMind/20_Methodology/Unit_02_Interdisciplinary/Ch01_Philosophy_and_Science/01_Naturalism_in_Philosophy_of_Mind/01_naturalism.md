# Naturalism in Philosophy of Mind

Naturalism — broadly, the view that philosophy should be continuous with and constrained by natural science — has been the dominant methodological orientation in philosophy of mind since the cognitive revolution of the 1960s. But "naturalism" covers a wide range of positions, from modest methodological recommendations to strong metaphysical claims, and understanding the varieties of naturalism and their implications is essential for understanding contemporary philosophy of mind.

**The Core Naturalist Commitment**

The core of naturalism is the rejection of a priori methods as the exclusive or primary approach to philosophical questions. Traditional rationalist philosophy held that the deepest philosophical truths — about the nature of reality, knowledge, and mind — could be established by pure reason, independently of empirical investigation. Naturalism rejects this: philosophical claims are not immune to empirical revision, and where philosophical questions overlap with scientific ones, scientific methods and findings are directly relevant.

In philosophy of mind, naturalism means that questions about consciousness, intentionality, mental representation, and cognition cannot be answered by conceptual analysis alone — they require engagement with the empirical findings of cognitive science, neuroscience, evolutionary biology, and psychology. The nature of perception, memory, emotion, and mental representation are partly empirical questions, not just conceptual ones.

This commitment to empirical engagement does not make philosophy of mind identical to empirical science. Philosophy of mind addresses normative and conceptual questions — about what consciousness is, what mental representation requires, how mental causation is possible — that are not purely empirical. But these questions cannot be answered in isolation from empirical findings.

**Quine's Naturalized Epistemology**

W.V.O. Quine's "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969) is the canonical statement of naturalist methodology. Quine argued that traditional epistemology — the project of justifying our knowledge claims from the ground up — has failed and should be replaced by an empirical study of how humans actually form beliefs. Epistemology should become "a chapter of psychology": the normative questions of justification should yield to empirical questions about how knowledge is actually acquired.

This radical naturalism has been widely criticized for eliminating the normative dimension of epistemology — for turning the question "how should we form beliefs?" into the merely descriptive question "how do we form beliefs?". But even philosophers who resist Quine's strongest claims typically accept the weaker methodological point: philosophy cannot proceed in ignorance of relevant empirical findings.

**Varieties of Naturalism**

Several distinct positions shelter under the "naturalist" umbrella.

Ontological naturalism holds that everything that exists is part of the natural order — that there are no non-natural substances (souls, non-physical minds) or non-natural properties. This is a metaphysical claim, but it is broadly accepted across the philosophical spectrum in analytic philosophy of mind.

Methodological naturalism holds that philosophy should use methods continuous with those of natural science — empirical, defeasible, revisable in light of evidence — rather than purely a priori methods. This is widely but not universally accepted.

Scientific naturalism holds that the natural sciences, as currently practiced, set the standards for what is real and knowable. This stronger claim is contested: some philosophers argue that science itself rests on normative and conceptual foundations that cannot be given a purely scientific account, and that philosophy cannot simply defer to science without begging foundational questions.

Normative naturalism holds that normative concepts (good, right, rational) can be analyzed in naturalistic terms — that ethics, epistemology, and the theory of rationality can be fully accommodated within a naturalistic framework. This is among the most contested positions: the naturalistic fallacy argument (Moore) and the autonomy of the normative (Kant) represent major challenges.

**Naturalism and the Hard Problem**

The hard problem of consciousness is the most significant internal challenge for naturalism in philosophy of mind. Naturalism is committed to explaining consciousness in natural terms — in terms of physical processes, functional organization, or neural mechanisms. But the hard problem argues that there is an explanatory gap between any such account and the phenomenal character of experience: even a complete neural or functional account leaves open the question of why these processes are accompanied by experience.

If the hard problem is genuine, it threatens the naturalist program: it suggests that phenomenal consciousness cannot be fully explained in natural terms, that something is left out by any account that works only with objective, third-person descriptions of physical processes. Naturalists respond by challenging the hard problem's premise (denying there is a genuine explanatory gap), arguing that the explanatory gap will close as neuroscience matures, or developing forms of naturalism (panpsychism, property dualism) that make room for phenomenal properties within a broadly naturalistic framework.
