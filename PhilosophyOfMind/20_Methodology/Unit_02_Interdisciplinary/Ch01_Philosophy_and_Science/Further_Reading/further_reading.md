# Further Reading: Philosophy and Science

## Primary Sources

**W.V.O. Quine, "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969), in *Ontological Relativity and Other Essays*** — Quine's classic argument that epistemology should be "naturalized"—replaced by or continuous with empirical science, specifically cognitive psychology. He argues that the normative project of justifying knowledge from the ground up has failed and that epistemology should instead study empirically how belief is actually formed and revised.

**David Papineau, *Philosophical Naturalism* (1993)** — A systematic defense of philosophical naturalism, arguing that philosophical questions about mind, meaning, and knowledge should be answered in terms that are continuous with natural science. Papineau argues for physicalism about mind on the basis of the causal closure of the physical world.

**Patricia Churchland, *Neurophilosophy: Toward a Unified Science of the Mind-Brain* (1986)** — The foundational argument for co-evolutionary development of philosophy and neuroscience, treating philosophical questions about mind as continuous with scientific questions about the brain. Churchland's approach exemplifies a thoroughgoing naturalism about mind.

**Alvin Goldman, *Epistemology and Cognition* (1986)** — Goldman's naturalistic epistemology argues that what makes a belief-forming process epistemically justified is its tendency to produce true beliefs—a reliabilist account that grounds epistemology in empirical psychology of cognition.

**Jerry Fodor, "Special Sciences (Or: The Disunity of Science as a Working Hypothesis)" (1974), *Synthese*** — Fodor's argument for the irreducibility of "special sciences" (psychology, economics, etc.) to fundamental physics, based on the multiple realizability of higher-level kinds. This paper provides the basis for methodological autonomy of cognitive science from neuroscience and physics.

## Secondary Literature

**Thomas Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962)** — Kuhn's account of scientific development through normal science and revolutionary paradigm shifts provides essential context for understanding how philosophy of science relates to scientific practice and how scientific revolutions change the philosophical questions scientists take seriously.

**Philip Kitcher, *The Advancement of Science* (1993)** — A sophisticated account of scientific progress and rationality that steers between naive cumulative realism and relativist anti-realism. Relevant for understanding how philosophy can illuminate and be informed by scientific practice.

**Paul Thagard, *Conceptual Revolutions* (1992)** — Examines how major conceptual changes in science occur and what cognitive and social processes are involved, providing a cognitive science account of scientific methodology that connects to philosophy of mind.

**Alvin Goldman, *Pathways to Knowledge: Private and Public* (2002)** — Goldman's social epistemology extends naturalistic epistemology to cover group knowledge, testimony, and the social processes that produce collective knowledge, connecting individual cognitive psychology to social scientific methodology.

**Jesse Prinz, *Beyond Human Nature: How Culture and Experience Shape Human Minds* (2012)** — Argues against nativist accounts of mind and for a more empiricist, culturally situated account, exemplifying how philosophy of mind can be informed by anthropology, developmental psychology, and cognitive science.
