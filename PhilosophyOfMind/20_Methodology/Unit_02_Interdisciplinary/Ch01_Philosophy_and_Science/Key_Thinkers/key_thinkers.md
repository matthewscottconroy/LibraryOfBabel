# Key Thinkers: Philosophy and Science

---

**W.V.O. Quine (1908–2000)**
American analytic philosopher at Harvard University. Quine's naturalism in philosophy of mind holds that there is no fundamental distinction between philosophical and empirical inquiry: both are parts of the ongoing project of understanding the world. His "epistemology naturalized" proposal—that epistemology should be replaced by or continuous with cognitive psychology—and his rejection of the analytic-synthetic distinction have been foundational for naturalistic approaches to philosophy of mind. While many philosophers resist his most radical conclusions, his challenge to the a priori status of philosophy has shaped the terms of methodology debates.
*Key works:* "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), *Word and Object* (1960), "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969)

---

**David Papineau (1947–)**
British philosopher at King's College London. Papineau is one of the clearest and most systematic defenders of philosophical naturalism—the view that philosophy of mind should be answerable to and continuous with natural science. His argument for physicalism rests on the "completeness of physics" (the causal closure of the physical world), and he has argued that the "hard problem" of consciousness is a product of conceptual confusion rather than a genuine metaphysical difficulty. His naturalism extends to epistemology, arguing that our epistemic concepts and norms can be studied empirically.
*Key works:* *Philosophical Naturalism* (1993), *Thinking About Consciousness* (2002), *Knowing the Score* (2017)

---

**Patricia Churchland (1943–)**
Canadian-American philosopher at UC San Diego. Churchland's neurophilosophy exemplifies the co-evolutionary approach: philosophical questions about the nature of mind are addressed in dialogue with neuroscience, and scientific findings revise philosophical frameworks as much as philosophical analysis guides scientific research. Her approach has been criticized for being too deflationary about philosophical autonomy, but it represents the most serious and sustained attempt to integrate philosophy and neuroscience as genuine partners.
*Key works:* *Neurophilosophy* (1986), *Brain-Wise* (2002), *Touching a Nerve* (2013)

---

**Alvin Goldman (1938–)**
American philosopher at Rutgers University. Goldman's reliabilist epistemology grounds justified belief in the reliability of belief-forming processes, connecting epistemological norms to empirical psychology. His "social epistemology" extends this naturalistic approach to cover testimony, expert knowledge, and the social institutions that produce knowledge. His work on simulation theory connects the psychology of social cognition to epistemological questions about our knowledge of other minds.
*Key works:* *Epistemology and Cognition* (1986), *Liaisons: Philosophy Meets the Cognitive and Social Sciences* (1992), *Simulating Minds* (2006)

---

**Jerry Fodor (1935–2017)**
American philosopher and cognitive scientist at MIT and CUNY. Fodor's career represents a distinctive form of naturalism that is also resistant to eliminativism: he argued vigorously for the reality of intentional states and the autonomy of psychology from neuroscience, while insisting that intentionality should be naturalistically grounded in causal-informational relations. His modularity thesis and language of thought hypothesis show how cognitive scientific hypotheses can be philosophically motivated and evaluated. His work demonstrates that naturalism about mind need not collapse into reductionism.
*Key works:* *The Language of Thought* (1975), *The Modularity of Mind* (1983), *Psychosemantics* (1987)
