# Further Reading: First-Person Methods

## Primary Sources

**Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch, *The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience* (1991)** — The foundational text for neurophenomenology and enactive cognition, arguing that cognitive science and phenomenology must be developed together rather than in isolation. Varela, Thompson, and Rosch propose "mindful reflection" as a disciplined first-person method that can generate data relevant to cognitive science.

**Francisco Varela and Jonathan Shear (eds.), *The View from Within: First-Person Approaches to the Study of Consciousness* (1999)** — A collection examining different first-person methods for consciousness research, including phenomenological analysis, introspection, psychophysics, and meditation-based observation. Covers both the methods themselves and the epistemological questions about their reliability.

**Daniel Dennett, *Consciousness Explained* (1991)** — Dennett's heterophenomenological method holds that studying consciousness requires taking first-person reports seriously as data about behavioral dispositions without assuming that these reports directly describe inner phenomenal states. His approach is a rigorously third-person interpretation of first-person reports—the "heterophenomenological stance."

**Eric Schwitzgebel, *Perplexities of Consciousness* (2011)** — A systematic examination of how unreliable introspection is as a method for studying consciousness. Schwitzgebel argues through a series of case studies that we are systematically wrong about our own conscious experiences in ways that suggest deep problems for first-person methods.

**Francisco Varela, "Neurophenomenology: A Methodological Remedy for the Hard Problem" (1996), *Journal of Consciousness Studies*** — Varela's programmatic paper proposing neurophenomenology as a research strategy: using phenomenological investigation to guide neuroscientific research design and interpret neuroscientific data, creating a "mutual constraints" relationship between first-person and third-person methods.

## Secondary Literature

**Evan Thompson, *Waking, Dreaming, Being: Self and Consciousness in Neuroscience, Meditation, and Philosophy* (2015)** — Thompson develops the neurophenomenological project through examination of sleep, dreaming, and meditative states, using both contemplative first-person data and neuroscience to illuminate consciousness. Shows what the neurophenomenological project looks like in practice.

**Natalie Depraz, Francisco Varela, and Pierre Vermersch, *On Becoming Aware: A Pragmatics of Experiencing* (2003)** — Develops methods for investigating first-person experience, including the "epoché as a practice" and elicitation interviews. Provides the methodological framework for neurophenomenological research.

**Pierre Vermersch, "Introspection as Practice" (1999), *Journal of Consciousness Studies*** — Vermersch's "explicitation interview" technique for eliciting detailed first-person reports of cognitive processes, providing a more rigorous and reliable approach to first-person data than naive introspection.

**Shaun Gallagher and Dan Zahavi, *The Phenomenological Mind* (2nd ed., 2012)** — Chapters on phenomenological methodology and its relationship to cognitive science, providing a clear account of how first-person phenomenological analysis can be used as scientific data.

**Max Velmans, *Understanding Consciousness* (2nd ed., 2009)** — Develops a "reflexive monism" that takes both first-person and third-person perspectives seriously as complementary rather than competing views on the same events. Addresses the methodological question of how first-person and third-person methods relate in consciousness research.
