# Key Thinkers: First-Person Methods

---

**Francisco Varela (1946–2001)**
Chilean biologist and philosopher, co-founder with Humberto Maturana of autopoiesis theory and developer of neurophenomenology. Varela's neurophenomenology proposed using phenomenological investigation as a first-person method to constrain neuroscientific research: careful description of the structure of experience can identify phenomena that neuroscience should then look for neural correlates of, and vice versa. His *The Embodied Mind* (with Thompson and Rosch) argued for an enactive approach to cognition that takes first-person phenomenal experience seriously as irreducible to third-person description. His work bridges phenomenology, cognitive science, and Buddhist contemplative tradition.
*Key works:* *The Embodied Mind* (1991, with Thompson and Rosch), "Neurophenomenology" (1996), *The View from Within* (1999, ed.)

---

**Daniel Dennett (1942–)**
American philosopher at Tufts University. Dennett's heterophenomenology is a rigorously third-person interpretation of first-person reports about consciousness. Rather than taking introspective reports at face value, Dennett proposes treating them as data about what a subject believes about their experience—data that can be used in scientific investigation without committing to the existence of phenomenal properties that reports seem to describe. His method is designed to take first-person reports seriously while avoiding what he sees as the illegitimate hypostatization of qualia.
*Key works:* *Consciousness Explained* (1991), *Sweet Dreams: Philosophical Obstacles to a Science of Consciousness* (2005), *Intuition Pumps* (2013)

---

**Eric Schwitzgebel (1968–)**
American philosopher at UC Riverside. Schwitzgebel has conducted the most rigorous empirical and philosophical examination of introspection's reliability, arguing through a series of case studies that we are systematically wrong about our own conscious experiences. His work on "color imagery," "peripheral vision," and "cognitive phenomenology" shows that what people report about their experiences is often inconsistent and contradictory in ways that suggest deep limitations of introspection as a method. His work does not eliminate first-person methods but insists on their limitations.
*Key works:* *Perplexities of Consciousness* (2011), "The Unreliability of Naive Introspection" (2008)

---

**Evan Thompson (1962–)**
Canadian philosopher at the University of British Columbia. Thompson has been the most prolific developer of the neurophenomenological and enactive program after Varela, examining sleep, dreaming, meditative states, and the relationship between consciousness and life from a perspective that integrates phenomenology, cognitive science, and Buddhist philosophy. His *Waking, Dreaming, Being* (2015) is the most sustained example of neurophenomenology in practice, showing how contemplative first-person data and neuroscience can illuminate consciousness.
*Key works:* *The Embodied Mind* (1991, with Varela and Rosch), *Mind in Life* (2007), *Waking, Dreaming, Being* (2015)

---

**Rupert Hurlburt (1942–)**
American psychologist at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas. Hurlburt developed Descriptive Experience Sampling (DES), a first-person method using randomly triggered beepers to interrupt subjects and prompt immediate reports of their ongoing conscious experience. His method has generated important empirical data about the diversity of conscious experience—including the frequency and character of inner speech, sensory imagery, and emotional feeling. His dialogue with Schwitzgebel about the reliability of DES represents the most direct engagement between a defender and critic of first-person methods.
*Key works:* *Sampling Inner Experience in Disturbed Affect* (1993), *Describing Inner Experience?: Proponent Meets Skeptic* (2007, with Schwitzgebel)
