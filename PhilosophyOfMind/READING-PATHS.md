# Reading Paths Through Philosophy of Mind

This guide recommends three routes through the twenty units of this textbook. Each path is designed for a different reader with different prior background and different goals. The paths are not the only possible sequences; they are designed to build concepts in an order that minimizes unexplained dependencies.

A note on depth: each path specifies whether to read unit introductions only (for orientation), selected chapters (for focused engagement), or the full unit. On a first reading, breadth matters more than depth; return to units that reward closer study once you have a map of the field.

---

## Path 1: Philosophy of Consciousness

**For:** Philosophers and philosophically sophisticated readers whose central interest is the nature of conscious experience — the hard problem, phenomenal consciousness, qualia, and the debates about whether physicalism can explain them.

**Prerequisite background:** Basic familiarity with the mind-body problem as an undergraduate philosophy course would cover it. Some exposure to analytic philosophy is helpful.

**Core sequence:** Units 1 → 2 → 3 → 6 → 15 → 16

**Extended sequence (for deeper engagement):** 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 6 → 11 → 15 → 16 → 20

---

### Step 1: Unit 01 — Foundations and History

**What to read:** Unit introduction (00-unit-introduction.md), then the Descartes and Hume chapters from Unit 02 (Medieval and Early Modern).

**Why start here:** The hard problem of consciousness is Descartes' problem. Chalmers' 1994 paper is brilliant, but it is essentially a sharpened restatement of the question Descartes raised when he noticed that *thinking substance* — even thinking about thinking itself — could not be derived from the *extended* material world. If you understand why Descartes' dualism was compelling and what exactly the interaction problem is, you will understand why every subsequent physicalist position has an explanatory burden to discharge.

The Humean background matters for a different reason: Hume's bundle theory of the self — the denial that there is any impression of a persisting substantial self behind the stream of experiences — anticipates contemporary debates about whether the unified character of consciousness requires a special kind of unified substrate. Understanding the bundle theory will also prepare you for Parfit on personal identity and for Metzinger on the no-self hypothesis, both of which you will encounter in the extended path.

**What to skip on first read:** The ancient Greek material (Unit 01) and the nineteenth-century psychology chapters are historically interesting but not load-bearing for the consciousness path. Read the unit introduction for orientation and return for depth later.

**Most important single reading for this step:** Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, Meditations I-III (45 pages). If you can read only one primary source before beginning, make it this.

---

### Step 2: Unit 02 — The Mind-Body Problem

**What to read:** Full unit (all three sub-units: Dualism, Physicalism, Alternatives). This is the theoretical scaffolding for the rest of the path.

**Why here:** The consciousness debates cannot be understood without the categories the mind-body problem establishes: property dualism, type identity, non-reductive physicalism, eliminativism, panpsychism. These are not merely historical positions; they are the live options in consciousness research. Understanding the multiple realizability argument, the exclusion problem, and the case for property dualism will equip you to evaluate Chalmers' position (Unit 03) without naively accepting or dismissing it.

The most important conceptual distinction to secure before continuing: the difference between *type* identity (mental state types are identical to brain state types — C-fibers firing = pain) and *token* identity (particular mental events are identical to particular physical events). Davidson's anomalous monism is a token identity theory; it is the most important attempt to be a thoroughgoing physicalist while preserving the autonomy of psychological explanation.

**What to skip on first read:** The deeper material on alternative positions (panpsychism, neutral monism) can be deferred until Unit 03 is complete. Return to it when you need to understand what drives philosophers like Galen Strawson and Philip Goff toward panpsychism.

**Most important single reading for this step:** Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind* (1996), Chapters 1-2 — "Two Concepts of Mind" and "Supervenience and Explanation." These chapters are the bridge from the mind-body problem to the hard problem.

---

### Step 3: Unit 03 — Consciousness (Core of the Path)

**What to read:** The full unit, in the following order: Chapter 1 (What is Consciousness), Chapter 2 (The Hard Problem), then the Theories of Consciousness chapters.

**Why here:** This is the center of the consciousness path and deserves the most time. Begin with Block's distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness (Ch01-02) before reading any of the theoretical chapters. The distinction is load-bearing: a theory that explains access consciousness (why information is available for report and reasoning) is not thereby a theory of phenomenal consciousness (why experience *feels like anything*). Without this distinction, the Global Workspace Theory can look like a complete theory of consciousness when it is not.

Work through the hard problem chapters (Ch02) with care. Chalmers' argument is: (1) consciousness has phenomenal properties that are not logically entailed by any physical facts; (2) if zombies are conceivable and conceivability tracks metaphysical possibility, then phenomenal consciousness is not identical to any physical property. The two premises are independent — you can reject either. Most physicalists reject the inference from conceivability to possibility; Chalmers' defense of the inference is in *The Conscious Mind* Chapters 3-4.

Once you have the hard problem clearly in hand, work through the theoretical alternatives in this order: (a) Global Workspace Theory as the leading access-consciousness theory; (b) IIT as the leading phenomenal-consciousness theory; (c) Higher-Order Theories as the leading representationalist alternative; (d) Illusionism as the most radical deflationary response.

**What to skip on first read:** The unit on Altered States (Unit 04 of consciousness) is fascinating but peripheral for the philosophical consciousness path. Return to it if you want to explore what psychedelics and disorders of consciousness tell us about the default mode network and the conditions for consciousness.

**Most important single reading for this step:** Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974) — 8 pages — followed immediately by Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1994) — 22 pages. These two papers are the clearest statement of the problem.

---

### Step 4: Unit 06 — Functionalism

**What to read:** Both sub-units (Core Theories and Challenges). Focus on Block's absent and inverted qualia arguments, and on the Chinese Room as a challenge to functionalism's account of intentionality.

**Why here (after Unit 03):** Functionalism is the dominant physicalist theory that consciousness debaters are targeting. Reading it after grappling with the hard problem means you can evaluate Block's absent qualia argument from the perspective of someone who already understands why phenomenal consciousness is supposed to be something over and above functional organization. The Chinese Room connects to Searle's biological naturalism, which is a more radical anti-functionalist position than property dualism.

The key insight to secure from this unit: functionalism is not just one theory but a family of theories. The multiple realizability argument establishes that mental states are not neural states; it does not establish that they are functional states. The further step — from "not neural" to "functional" — requires an additional argument, and identifying that argument is what makes it possible to assess whether functionalism is the right response to the type identity theory.

**Most important single reading for this step:** Block, "Troubles with Functionalism" (1978) — specifically the absent qualia (China brain) argument, which is in sections 1-2 of the paper. Once you have this clearly in hand, the subsequent debates about phenomenal consciousness become much easier to follow.

---

### Step 5: Unit 15 — Phenomenology

**What to read:** Unit introduction, then Husserl's intentional analysis (Unit 01) and Merleau-Ponty's lived body (Unit 03). Heidegger and Sartre can be engaged more deeply on second reading.

**Why here:** Phenomenology provides the *first-person* description of consciousness that the previous units have been analyzing from the *outside*. After working through the hard problem and the functionalist debates, it is worth asking: what exactly is the phenomenon that these debates are about? Husserl's account of intentional structure — how consciousness is directed toward objects through the noematic content that presents them — is the most detailed first-person account of the structure of experience available. Merleau-Ponty's analysis of the lived body gives the clearest account of how perception is not a matter of building an internal representation of the world but a mode of skilled bodily engagement.

The connection to the hard problem is this: phenomenologists have a rich vocabulary for describing what consciousness is like from the inside. This vocabulary can function as a constraint on theories of consciousness: a theory of consciousness must account for the phenomena Husserl describes (the temporal structure of experience, the directedness of intentional acts, the first-person character of consciousness). Whether any physical or functional theory can do so is a question phenomenological analysis can help answer.

**Most important single reading for this step:** Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945), Introduction and Chapter 1 of Part I ("The Body as Object and Mechanistic Physiology"). Accessible and makes the point about the lived body with vivid examples.

---

### Step 6: Unit 16 — Philosophy of Neuroscience

**What to read:** Unit introduction, then Levels of Explanation (for Marr) and Neural Correlates (for the NCC program and IIT).

**Why here (at the end of the path):** By this point you have the philosophical framework needed to assess what neuroscientific findings mean. The question the hard problem raises — why does *this* neural activity give rise to *this* experience? — is precisely what the NCC program cannot answer. Coming to neuroscience of consciousness after the philosophy means you can see why identifying the neural correlate of consciousness does not solve the hard problem, why the methodology of NCC research faces the phenomenal/access confusion Block identifies, and why IIT is an attempt to go further than correlation to explanation.

Marr's three-levels framework is particularly important for evaluation of cognitive neuroscience claims: distinguishing the computational level (what the brain computes) from the implementational level (how it does so in neurons) is essential for understanding why findings at one level cannot straightforwardly be extrapolated to conclusions at another.

**Most important single reading for this step:** Block, "Consciousness, Accessibility, and the Mesh Between Psychology and Neuroscience" (2007). This paper crystallizes the methodological issues in consciousness neuroscience and is directly relevant to evaluating empirical claims about consciousness.

---

### What to add for the extended path

For readers who want a deeper engagement, add these units after the core sequence:

- **Unit 04 (Intentionality):** Consciousness and intentionality are deeply connected; understanding externalism about content (Putnam, Burge) is necessary for fully evaluating phenomenal concepts strategies.
- **Unit 11 (Personal Identity):** Parfit's work connects philosophy of mind to questions about the self, selflessness, and what matters in survival — questions that have direct bearing on the practical significance of consciousness.
- **Unit 20 (Methodology):** Especially the sections on heterophenomenology and neurophenomenology, which address the question of how to conduct philosophical investigation of consciousness.

## Path 2: Cognitive Science

**For:** Scientists — cognitive scientists, neuroscientists, psychologists, computer scientists — who want philosophical depth on the foundations of their discipline. This path emphasizes the theoretical frameworks underlying empirical cognitive science and the philosophical questions those frameworks raise.

**Prerequisite background:** Familiarity with basic cognitive science concepts (attention, memory, representation, computation). No prior philosophy is required, though curiosity about foundational questions is.

**Core sequence:** Units 5 → 6 → 9 → 7 → 10 → 16 → 14

---

### Step 1: Unit 05 — Mental Representation

**Why start here:** Mental representation is the central theoretical concept of cognitive science. Every cognitive science theory commits to some account of how mental states carry information about the world. Starting with representation grounds the subsequent discussions of cognitive architecture, perception, and emotion in a common theoretical framework.

Focus on the Language of Thought hypothesis (Fodor), the connectionist challenge, and predictive coding. The LOT/connectionist debate is not resolved, but understanding its terms — systematicity, compositionality, distributed representation — is necessary for following contemporary debates about what neural networks are actually doing.

**What to skip on first read:** The detailed material on the imagery debate (Kosslyn vs. Pylyshyn) can be deferred. Its main value is as a case study in how philosophical analysis and experimental methods can be combined to address a question about representation format.

**Most important single reading:** Fodor and Pylyshyn, "Connectionism and Cognitive Architecture" (1988) — even if you ultimately disagree with the argument, the paper crystallizes the theoretical commitments of classical cognitive science with unusual clarity.

---

### Step 2: Unit 06 — Functionalism

**Why here:** Functionalism is the philosophical foundation of cognitive science. The idea that mental states are defined by their functional roles — that the medium of implementation doesn't matter, only the causal-computational organization — is what makes it possible to build a science of cognition that abstracts from specific neural implementation. Understanding functionalism makes it possible to ask the right questions about neural network models: what is the right level of functional description? Does performance on a task demonstrate that the system has the relevant cognitive capacity?

Focus on the core functionalist theories (Unit 01) and on the qualia objections (Unit 02 Ch01). The qualia objections are directly relevant to cognitive science: they raise the question of whether functional organization is sufficient for consciousness, and this question has practical implications for AI development and for neuroscientific theories of consciousness.

**Most important single reading:** Putnam, "The Nature of Mental States" (1968). This paper makes the multiple realizability argument and the case for functionalism in three pages; it is the most efficient entry point.

---

### Step 3: Unit 09 — Cognitive Architecture

**Why here:** This is the empirical core of the path. Work through the full unit, but focus on the debate between classical and connectionist architectures (Units 01-02), the extended mind thesis (Unit 04, Chapter 1), and the predictive processing framework (Units 03-04). The 4E cognition material (embodied, embedded, extended, enacted) provides the most radical challenge to the traditional cognitive science picture and is directly relevant to debates about the scope and limits of cognitive science.

For cognitive scientists, the most practically important part of this unit is the predictive processing framework. Friston's free energy principle and Clark's philosophical elaboration suggest that virtually all perception, action, and learning can be understood as minimizing prediction error. Whether this is a genuine unifying framework or a mathematical language for redescribing existing findings is a live question.

**What to skip on first read:** The detailed philosophical material on the metaphysics of the extended mind can be deferred. The key argument — that cognitive processes can extend into external artifacts when the external elements play the right functional role — is the important claim; the detailed metaphysics can wait.

**Most important single reading:** Clark and Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (1998) — 15 pages. The argument is clear, the implications are large, and it sets up the debates about the boundaries of cognition that are increasingly important for cognitive science.

---

### Step 4: Unit 07 — Perception

**Why here (after cognitive architecture):** The debate about perceptual representation — whether perception is a process of building an internal model of the world or a form of direct contact with environmental affordances — connects directly to the cognitive architecture debates. Predictive processing transforms the perception question: rather than asking how the brain builds up a representation from incoming signals, we ask how the brain generates predictions and updates them in response to prediction errors. This reframes many traditional perception debates.

Focus on the representationalism chapter and the predictive processing chapter. The sense-data tradition and direct realism debates are historically important but secondary for a cognitive science audience.

**Most important single reading:** Clark, *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016), Chapter 2 ("The Predictive Mind"). This chapter gives the clearest account of how predictive processing reframes perception.

---

### Step 5: Unit 10 — Emotions and Affect

**Why here:** Emotions have moved from the periphery to the center of cognitive science, driven by Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis, affective neuroscience (LeDoux, Panksepp), and the applied significance of emotion regulation. For the cognitive science path, the most important debates are: (a) basic emotion theory vs. constructionist theory (what are the natural kinds of emotion?); (b) the role of emotion in decision-making and rationality (Damasio); and (c) the neural basis of emotional processing (the amygdala, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, the insula).

The philosophical debates about the intentionality of emotion and its relationship to rationality are important background for understanding what the empirical work is trying to explain.

**Most important single reading:** Damasio, *Descartes' Error* (1994), Chapters 1-4. These chapters describe the case of Elliot (pseudonym) in memorable clinical detail and develop the somatic marker hypothesis. Essential reading for anyone interested in the relationship between emotion and cognition.

---

### Step 6: Unit 16 — Philosophy of Neuroscience

**Why here:** Having worked through the theoretical frameworks (representation, functionalism, architecture, perception, emotion), you are positioned to ask the foundational questions about neuroscientific explanation. Marr's three-levels framework is the essential starting point: the distinction between computational, algorithmic, and implementational levels of explanation is often confused in neuroscience, and the confusion generates spurious debates (e.g., about whether perception is "really" top-down or bottom-up: it is both, at different levels).

Focus on the levels of explanation material and on the NCC research program. The philosophy of psychiatry chapter is also relevant for cognitive scientists interested in psychiatric disorders.

**Most important single reading:** Craver, *Explaining the Brain* (2007), Chapter 1 ("Levels of Explanation"). This provides the mechanistic explanation framework that is the most philosophically sophisticated account of how neuroscientific explanation works.

---

### Step 7: Unit 14 — Other Minds and Social Cognition

**Why end here:** Social cognition — the ability to understand, predict, and respond to other minds — is among the most important and distinctively human cognitive capacities. The theory-theory vs. simulation-theory debate has direct implications for cognitive science: if we understand other minds by applying a tacit theory, we should look for the neural implementation of that theory; if we understand them by simulation, we should look for neural systems that implement perspective-taking and emotional resonance (which is where mirror neurons enter the picture).

The mirror neuron discovery and the theory of mind developmental literature provide some of the most directly relevant empirical material for this debate.

**Most important single reading:** Wimmer and Perner, "Beliefs about Beliefs" (1983). The false-belief paradigm is still generating debates, and understanding the original findings — and what exactly they show — is essential for following the literature.

---

## Path 3: AI and Mind

**For:** AI researchers, AI safety researchers, and technologists who want to engage seriously with the philosophical questions raised by increasingly capable AI systems. This path addresses the nature of understanding, intentionality, consciousness, and moral status as they bear on artificial minds.

**Prerequisite background:** Familiarity with basic concepts in machine learning and large language models. No prior philosophy required.

**Core sequence:** Units 5 → 6 → 18 → 9 → 19 → 3 → 4

---

### Step 1: Unit 05 — Mental Representation

**Why start here:** To evaluate whether an AI system has mental states, you need an account of what mental states *are* at the representational level. The LOT hypothesis and connectionism debate is directly relevant: are LLMs implementing something like a symbolic language of thought (with genuine compositional structure) or something sub-symbolic and associative? Predictive coding provides a third alternative that may be particularly relevant to transformer architectures.

Focus on the LOT hypothesis and the connectionist debate. The core question: does the remarkable performance of LLMs support the claim that they have *representations* in the sense relevant for mental states, or only that they implement statistical patterns that produce appropriate outputs?

**What to skip on first read:** The mental imagery debate. Return if you want to understand debates about spatial and visual representation in neural networks.

**Most important single reading:** Fodor, *The Language of Thought* (1975), Chapter 1. Even if LOT is wrong, understanding what it requires — compositional structure, discrete symbols, systematicity — gives you the benchmark against which LLM representations can be assessed.

---

### Step 2: Unit 06 — Functionalism

**Why here:** Functionalism is the philosophical framework that makes AI minds possible in principle. If mental states are defined by functional roles, then any system that implements the right functional organization has mental states — regardless of its substrate. This is the core premise of most arguments for the possibility of artificial consciousness.

Work through the core theories (Unit 01) and the Chinese Room challenge (Unit 02, Ch02). The Chinese Room is the most important philosophical challenge to AI cognition: Searle's argument that syntactic symbol manipulation is insufficient for semantic understanding bears directly on the question of whether LLMs understand language. Whether the systems reply succeeds is not a settled question.

**Most important single reading:** Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980). Read the original paper, then the commentaries (particularly Dennett's and Hofstadter's). The debate between Searle and his critics is a model of philosophical argumentation about AI.

---

### Step 3: Unit 18 — Artificial Minds (Core of the Path)

**What to read:** Full unit.

**Why here:** This is the central unit for the AI and Mind path. By this point you have the theoretical framework (functionalism, mental representation) needed to evaluate the specific debates about artificial minds.

Work through the units in order: Turing (the imitation game, the conceivability of machine thought, the precursor arguments); AI Consciousness (IIT and its implications for AI, GWT and its implications, biological naturalism); LLMs and Cognition (what LLMs actually do, and whether it constitutes understanding); AGI and Superintelligence (the alignment problem, the orthogonality thesis, the philosophical dimensions of the singularity hypothesis).

The most important conceptual point to secure: the distinction between *behavioral* criteria for mindedness (does the system behave as if it understands?) and *constitutive* criteria (does the system have the right kind of internal organization?). The Turing test is a behavioral criterion; IIT and biological naturalism are constitutive criteria. Whether behavioral evidence is sufficient to settle questions about consciousness — or whether the hard problem means behavioral evidence is in principle insufficient — is the key methodological question for AI and mind.

**Most important single reading:** Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950). This is the founding text for the field and contains an extraordinary anticipation of nearly all subsequent debates. Essential reading.

---

### Step 4: Unit 09 — Cognitive Architecture

**Why here:** Having engaged with the specific question of artificial minds, it is worth stepping back to ask about cognitive architecture. The 4E cognition material raises questions directly relevant to AI: if cognition is essentially embodied and enacted — if it requires a body, sensorimotor engagement with the world, and a history of physical interaction — then purely linguistic systems (LLMs) may be missing something essential.

The extended mind thesis is particularly relevant: if cognitive processes can extend into notebooks and smartphones, then AI systems might constitute (part of) an extended cognitive system rather than a cognitive system in their own right. This reframes the question of AI cognition: rather than asking whether a given AI system is cognitive, we might ask whether a human-AI system is cognitive in ways that neither component is alone.

**Most important single reading:** Clark and Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (1998). The implications for AI — that AI tools might constitute cognitive extensions of human minds — are philosophically important for anyone working with AI systems.

---

### Step 5: Unit 19 — Ethics and Philosophy of Mind

**Why here:** The practical urgency of AI development makes the ethical questions about AI moral status among the most important philosophical questions of our time. This unit directly addresses: what features of mental life generate moral status, whether AI systems could have those features, and how to make moral decisions under uncertainty about AI consciousness.

Focus on the Consciousness and Ethics sub-unit (moral patiency and the conditions for moral status) and the Moral Status of AI chapter. The neuroethics material is secondary for this path, though the discussion of moral enhancement is relevant to AI safety debates.

**Most important single reading:** Shoemaker, "Moral Address, Moral Responsibility, and the Boundaries of the Moral Community" (2007). Shoemaker's careful analysis of what is required for membership in the moral community is directly applicable to AI systems.

---

### Step 6: Unit 03 — Consciousness

**Why here (after ethics):** You have already encountered consciousness through IIT and GWT in Unit 18. Now is the time to engage with the full philosophical analysis. The sequence is important: having already encountered the practical stakes (from Unit 19), reading the hard problem and the theories of consciousness with those stakes in mind brings out the urgency of the philosophical questions.

Focus on the hard problem (Ch02), Block's phenomenal/access distinction (Ch01), and the major theories (GWT, IIT, illusionism). The illusionism chapter is particularly important for AI researchers: Frankish's argument that phenomenal consciousness is an illusion — that there are functional states but no phenomenal ones — would, if correct, deflate many concerns about AI consciousness.

**Most important single reading:** Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1994). Even if you think the hard problem is less hard than Chalmers suggests, understanding the argument precisely is essential for evaluating claims about AI consciousness.

---

### Step 7: Unit 04 — Intentionality and Mental Content

**Why end here:** Intentionality — the mind's power to be *about* things — is the capacity that most clearly separates human cognitive systems from any system that merely processes information without representing it. Searle's distinction between original intentionality (genuine aboutness, present in mental states) and derived intentionality (aboutness by convention, present in symbols and artifacts) is the most important conceptual distinction for evaluating claims about AI systems.

Whether LLMs have original intentionality — whether their internal states are genuinely about things in the world — is a live philosophical question. Externalist accounts of content (Putnam, Burge) suggest that content is determined partly by environmental relationships that LLMs, trained on text corpora rather than on embodied interaction with the world, may lack. The question of whether language model outputs are genuinely meaningful or are sophisticated statistical patterns that lack original intentionality is not settled.

**Most important single reading:** Searle, *Intentionality: An Essay in the Philosophy of Mind* (1983), Chapter 1 ("The Nature of Intentional States"). Searle's clear analysis of the structure of intentional states and his distinction between original and derived intentionality are the essential background for thinking about whether AI systems are genuinely intentional.

## Cross-Path Notes

**Papers that appear in multiple paths (read once, referenced widely):**

- Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1994) — essential for Paths 1 and 3
- Clark and Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (1998) — essential for Paths 2 and 3
- Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980) — essential for Paths 2 and 3
- Block, "On a Confusion About a Function of Consciousness" (1995) — essential for Paths 1 and 3
- Putnam, "The Nature of Mental States" (1968) — essential for Paths 1 and 2

**Units valuable for all three paths:**

- **Unit 03 (Consciousness):** The theoretical heart of the book
- **Unit 20 (Methodology):** The meta-level self-understanding every serious reader needs
- **Unit 15 (Phenomenology):** The first-person perspective that all three paths eventually need to engage with

**On reading primary sources vs. secondary literature:**

For Paths 1 and 3, primary sources are generally accessible and should be read directly (Nagel, Chalmers, Block, Searle). For Path 2, secondary literature is often more practically useful for getting up to speed (Chalmers' *The Conscious Mind* is long; Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" is short and can be read in 20 minutes). For phenomenology (Unit 15 in Path 1), secondary literature — particularly Zahavi's *Husserl's Phenomenology* and Dreyfus's *Being-in-the-World* — is nearly indispensable for a reader without prior background.

**Estimated time commitments:**

- Path 1 (core sequence, primary sources): 8-10 weeks at 4-5 hours/week
- Path 2 (core sequence, mix of primary and secondary): 6-8 weeks at 4-5 hours/week
- Path 3 (core sequence, focused on contemporary debates): 5-7 weeks at 4-5 hours/week

Extended versions of each path add approximately 3-4 weeks. The full twenty-unit book, read carefully, is a year's work.
