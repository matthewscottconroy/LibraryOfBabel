# Chapter 1: Exercises, Questions, and Labs

---

## Part I — Comprehension and Analysis

**1. The Augustine Problem**
Augustine writes: "If no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to one that asks, I do not know." He is talking about time. Pick a different phenomenon — consciousness, color, personal identity, beauty — and write a paragraph showing why the same paradox applies or does not apply to it. What distinguishes cases where the gap between knowing-how and knowing-what is wide from cases where it is narrow?

**2. The Two Models**
We distinguished *descriptive metaphysics* (systematizing the concepts implicit in ordinary thought) from *revisionary metaphysics* (arriving at the true account even if it conflicts with ordinary thought). Identify a case where you think descriptive metaphysics is clearly the right approach, and a case where revisionary metaphysics is clearly the right approach. What features of the domain seem to determine which approach is appropriate?

**3. Reflective Equilibrium Exercise**
You are given the following set of commitments about time. Work through the tensions between them using the method of reflective equilibrium. For each conflict you identify, propose which commitment should be revised and why.

- (a) Only what is present is real.
- (b) You are the same person you were ten years ago.
- (c) Statements about the past can be true or false.
- (d) Special relativity entails that simultaneity is frame-relative.
- (e) The past is fixed and nothing can change it.
- (f) The arrow of time is mind-independent.

**4. Underdetermination**
Explain in your own words why metaphysical questions about time are underdetermined by physical theory. Give a specific example: describe a case where two incompatible metaphysical views are equally compatible with a specific physical result. What would a philosopher need to do to argue for one view over the other in such a case?

**5. Method Evaluation**
Of the four methods discussed in Section 1.2 — conceptual analysis, thought experiments, reflective equilibrium, naturalism/revisionism — which do you find most compelling for the philosophy of time, and which least compelling? Write a 300-word argument defending your ranking.

## Part II — Thought Experiments

**6. The Slow Freeze**
Imagine that every process in the universe — every physical, biological, mental, and chemical process — suddenly slows down uniformly to one-millionth of its previous rate for one hour (as measured by an external observer outside the universe, if one were possible), then returns to normal. During the slow period, would time itself slow down, or would only the processes slow? What does your answer reveal about whether time depends on physical processes?

**7. The Empty Universe**
Suppose a universe exists that contains no matter, no energy, no events of any kind — but that it nevertheless endures for (let us say) a billion years before matter spontaneously appears. Does this scenario make sense? What would it mean for time to pass in a universe with no events? Consider both the relational view (time is constituted by events) and the absolutist view (time flows independently of events).

**8. The Reversed Film**
You are shown a short film — ten seconds of footage — with no identifying information. The film shows: (a) broken glass assembling itself into a complete glass; (b) a candle flame growing taller and brighter over time; (c) a bouncing ball losing height on each bounce. Which of these would you identify as the film run backward? What does your pattern of identification reveal about which physical processes you take to be intrinsically asymmetric, and which symmetric?

## Part III — Original Inquiries

**9. The Philosopher's Stone of Temporal Vocabulary**
Make a list of twenty temporal words or phrases from ordinary English (examples: "meanwhile," "eventually," "since," "until," "beforehand," "no longer," "not yet"). For each, write a tenseless paraphrase — a reformulation that replaces tensed vocabulary with relations of "earlier than," "later than," or "simultaneous with." For which words does the paraphrase feel adequate? For which does it feel like something is lost? What is lost, and why might that matter philosophically?

**10. The Interdisciplinary Survey**
Choose one of the four regions of the philosophy of time identified in Section 1.4 (metaphysics, philosophy of physics, phenomenology/psychology, ethics/identity). Find one recent paper (published after 2015) from a *scientific* journal that addresses a question relevant to your chosen region. Write a 400-word analysis of how the scientific findings bear on the philosophical questions — what they settle, what they constrain, and what they leave open.

## Part IV — Labs and Observational Exercises

**Lab 1: Personal Time vs. Clock Time**
For three days, keep a log in which you record (a) the time shown on a clock every hour, and (b) your subjective estimate of how much time has elapsed since the previous hour. Do not look at the clock before estimating. At the end, compare the two records. When did time seem to move faster than the clock? Slower? What were you doing during each period? What does this reveal about the relationship between experiential time and physical time?

**Lab 2: The Specious Present**
This exercise requires a partner. Person A counts aloud steadily at one count per second ("one... two... three...") while Person B concentrates on their experience of "the present moment" — on trying to identify what feels like "now" as opposed to "just past" or "about to come." After the count reaches 30, Person B reports: how many counts felt like part of the "present moment" simultaneously? Not just consciously registered, but felt as *present*? Discuss the results in light of the philosophical concept of the specious present (Section 1.4.3).

**Lab 3: Temporal Direction in Nature**
Spend thirty minutes in a natural setting (a park, a garden, near water, anywhere with observable processes). Identify five processes that have a clear temporal direction — that you would immediately recognize as wrong if filmed and played in reverse. Then identify five processes that seem temporally symmetric — that you could not distinguish from their reversal. Record your observations. What distinguishes the asymmetric from the symmetric processes? Can you find any pattern?

**Lab 4: Linguistic Time-Bending**
Collect ten sentences from a newspaper, novel, or online text that use complex temporal constructions (past perfect, future perfect, conditional past, etc.). For example: "She had been planning the trip before she learned he would have arrived by then." Diagram the temporal structure of each sentence — identify all the temporal relationships it encodes. What does the complexity of natural-language temporal constructions suggest about the complexity of human temporal cognition?

---

*Note to instructors: Labs 1–4 are designed to be completed before the subsequent chapter on pre-theoretical intuitions, so that students bring fresh observational data to the theoretical discussions.*
