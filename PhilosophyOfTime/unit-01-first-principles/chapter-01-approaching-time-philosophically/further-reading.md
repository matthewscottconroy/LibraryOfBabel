# Chapter 1: Further Reading and References

## Primary Sources Referenced in This Chapter

- Aristotle. *Physics*. Translated by Robin Waterfield. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1996. [Books III–IV are the most relevant.]
- Augustine of Hippo. *Confessions*. Translated by Henry Chadwick. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1991. [Book XI on time is essential; it can be read independently.]
- Broad, C.D. *Scientific Thought*. London: Kegan Paul, 1923. [Chapter 2 for the growing block theory.]
- Einstein, Albert. "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies." *Annalen der Physik* 17 (1905): 891–921. [Available in English translation in *The Principle of Relativity*. New York: Dover, 1952.]
- Hume, David. *A Treatise of Human Nature*. Edited by L.A. Selby-Bigge, revised by P.H. Nidditch. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1978. [Book I, Part II, Sections 3–5 on time.]
- Husserl, Edmund. *On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time (1893–1917)*. Translated by John B. Brough. Dordrecht: Kluwer, 1991.
- Kant, Immanuel. *Critique of Pure Reason*. Translated by Paul Guyer and Allen Wood. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1998. [The "Transcendental Aesthetic" and the First Antinomy.]
- Leibniz, G.W., and Samuel Clarke. *The Leibniz-Clarke Correspondence*. Edited by H.G. Alexander. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1956.
- McTaggart, J.M.E. "The Unreality of Time." *Mind* 17, no. 68 (1908): 457–474.
- Newton, Isaac. *The Principia: Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*. Translated by I. Bernard Cohen and Anne Whitman. Berkeley: University of California Press, 1999.
- Putnam, Hilary. "Time and Physical Geometry." *Journal of Philosophy* 64, no. 8 (1967): 240–247.
- Quine, W.V.O. "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." *Philosophical Review* 60, no. 1 (1951): 20–43.
- Rawls, John. *A Theory of Justice*. Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1971.
- Smart, J.J.C. "The River of Time." *Mind* 58, no. 232 (1949): 483–494.
- Williams, Donald C. "The Myth of Passage." *Journal of Philosophy* 48, no. 15 (1951): 457–472.
- Wittgenstein, Ludwig. *Philosophical Investigations*. Translated by G.E.M. Anscombe, P.M.S. Hacker, and Joachim Schulte. Oxford: Wiley-Blackwell, 2009.

## Recommended Further Reading

**Accessible Introductions**

1. Le Poidevin, Robin. *Travels in Four Dimensions: The Enigmas of Space and Time*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2003.
   *The best single-volume introduction to the philosophy of time for newcomers. Le Poidevin covers all the main topics — metaphysics, physics, phenomenology — in prose that is both rigorous and readable. Start here if you want breadth.*

2. Callender, Craig. *What Makes Time Special?* Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2017.
   *A remarkably clear-eyed tour of why time seems so different from the way physics represents it, and what to make of this difference. Callender writes with unusual directness and empirical sensitivity.*

3. Rovelli, Carlo. *The Order of Time*. Translated by Simon Carnell and Erica Segre. New York: Riverhead, 2018.
   *A physicist's meditation on what modern physics says about time. Beautifully written, philosophically engaged, and a genuinely original contribution. Not a textbook but an argument — read it critically.*

**Essential Collections and Handbooks**

4. Callender, Craig, ed. *The Oxford Handbook of Philosophy of Time*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2011.
   *The definitive reference work. Contains authoritative survey articles on every major topic by leading specialists. The introductory essay and the essays by Callender, Maudlin, Mellor, and Sider are especially recommended.*

5. van Inwagen, Peter, and Dean Zimmerman, eds. *Metaphysics: The Big Questions*. 2nd ed. Oxford: Blackwell, 2008.
   *Part IV (on time) contains several of the most important papers in the field, including extracts from McTaggart, Smart, Mellor, Prior, and Lewis.*

6. Sider, Ted, John Hawthorne, and Dean Zimmerman, eds. *Contemporary Debates in Metaphysics*. Oxford: Blackwell, 2008.
   *Contains paired essays by defenders and critics of presentism, four-dimensionalism, and related positions. Ideal for seeing debates in action.*

**On Method in Philosophy of Time**

7. Maudlin, Tim. *The Metaphysics Within Physics*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007.
   *A forceful argument that metaphysics should be guided by physics rather than armchair intuition. The opening chapters on the methodology of the physics-metaphysics interface are essential.*

8. Price, Huw. *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point: New Directions for the Physics of Time*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1996.
   *The most argued-over book in the philosophy of time of the last thirty years. Price's "Archimedean" methodology — trying to adopt a view from outside time — is controversial but philosophically stimulating even if you ultimately reject it.*

9. Ladyman, James, and Don Ross. *Every Thing Must Go: Metaphysics Naturalized*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007.
   *The most systematic recent defense of a naturalist approach to metaphysics, with implications for the philosophy of time. Chapter 1 on the "principle of naturalistic closure" is directly relevant to Section 1.3.*

**On the History of the Philosophy of Time**

10. Sorabji, Richard. *Time, Creation and the Continuum: Theories in Antiquity and the Early Middle Ages*. London: Duckworth, 1983.
    *The definitive scholarly treatment of ancient and medieval philosophy of time. Indispensable for anyone who wants to understand the Aristotelian and Augustinian traditions with precision.*

11. Gale, Richard M., ed. *The Philosophy of Time: A Collection of Essays*. London: Macmillan, 1968.
    *An older collection, but contains classic papers by Russell, Prior, Broad, Smart, and others that remain essential reading. The editor's introductions are helpful.*

**On Phenomenology of Time**

12. Zahavi, Dan. *Husserl's Phenomenology*. Stanford: Stanford University Press, 2003.
    *The clearest secondary account of Husserl's phenomenology of time-consciousness. Chapter 5 on internal time-consciousness is directly relevant.*

13. Heidegger, Martin. *Being and Time*. Translated by John Macquarrie and Edward Robinson. New York: Harper and Row, 1962.
    *Division II, §65 ("Temporality as the Ontological Meaning of Care") is the central text. It is difficult; read with Blattner (1999) or Dreyfus (1991) as guides.*

**On Thought Experiments**

14. Sorensen, Roy A. *Thought Experiments*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1992.
    *A systematic philosophical analysis of what thought experiments are and how they work. Relevant to the methodological discussions in Section 1.2.*

15. Brown, James Robert. *The Laboratory of the Mind: Thought Experiments in the Natural Sciences*. 2nd ed. London: Routledge, 2011.
    *Argues that thought experiments can generate a priori knowledge about the natural world — a controversial but important view relevant to the discussion of method.*

## A Note on Reading Strategy

The primary sources listed above vary dramatically in difficulty. Augustine's *Confessions* Book XI can be read in an afternoon and yields rich philosophical material immediately. Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* requires weeks of sustained engagement, preferably with a reliable guide (the introductory chapters of Strawson's *The Bounds of Sense* remain the best). The physics papers (Einstein 1905, Putnam 1967) are short but require careful attention to argument structure.

The recommended secondary literature is listed in rough order of accessibility. Le Poidevin (2003) and Callender (2017) are genuinely accessible to undergraduates with no background in physics. Maudlin (2012) and Price (1996) require more persistence but reward it handsomely.
