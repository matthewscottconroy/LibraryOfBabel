# Chapter 1: Important Concepts

---

**Conceptual Analysis**
The philosophical method of articulating the necessary and sufficient conditions for the correct application of a concept. Applied to time, it attempts to specify what conditions must hold for something to count as temporal, as present, as earlier than something else, etc. Its limits include the Quinean challenge to the analytic-synthetic distinction and the possibility that our temporal concepts are shaped by cognitive architecture rather than temporal reality.

**Underdetermination**
The thesis that a given body of empirical evidence is logically compatible with more than one theoretical account. In the philosophy of time, metaphysical views (such as presentism vs. eternalism) are underdetermined by physical theories: the same physics is compatible with incompatible metaphysical interpretations. This is a structural feature of the physics-metaphysics relationship, not a temporary deficit.

**Descriptive Metaphysics**
The project, associated with P.F. Strawson, of systematizing and articulating the conceptual scheme implicit in ordinary thought and language — making explicit what our practice already implicitly commits us to. Contrasted with revisionary metaphysics, which aims to arrive at the true account of reality even if it departs from ordinary intuitions.

**Revisionary Metaphysics**
The philosophical approach that aims to determine the true structure of reality, revising ordinary concepts when they are found to be confused or inconsistent with well-established theory. In the philosophy of time, the view that the experienced flow of time is an illusion, or that the present has no objective metaphysical significance, exemplifies revisionary conclusions.

**Reflective Equilibrium**
A method of philosophical inquiry, introduced by John Rawls, involving the iterative adjustment of particular intuitions and general principles in light of one another until a stable, coherent system is reached. Applied to time, it requires balancing our strong intuitions about the present, the past, and temporal flow against theoretical pressures from physics and metaphysics.

**Intuition Pump**
A term from Daniel Dennett for a thought experiment designed to elicit intuitions about how a concept applies. Intuition pumps can be illuminating (the twin paradox, Zeno's Arrow) or misleading (if they smuggle in contested background assumptions). Their evidential weight depends on the firmness of the intuition they elicit and the neutrality of the background assumptions they require.

**The Block Universe**
The metaphysical view (also called "eternalism" or "four-dimensionalism") that all times — past, present, and future — are equally real, forming a static four-dimensional spacetime manifold. The present is like "here": a perspectival indexical, not a metaphysically privileged location. Most naturally suggested by the spacetime structure of special relativity.

**Presentism**
The metaphysical view that only the present moment is real. Past things no longer exist; future things do not yet exist; only what is currently happening has genuine ontological status. This view captures strong ordinary intuitions but faces challenges from special relativity's denial of observer-independent simultaneity.

**The Growing Block**
The metaphysical view, introduced by C.D. Broad, that the past and present are real (they have grown into existence), but the future is not yet real. Time is genuinely asymmetric: reality grows from a fixed past toward an open future. This view captures the intuition that the past is fixed while the future is not, but raises the epistemological puzzle of how we know we are at the edge of the growing block rather than embedded within a completed one.

**Temporal Passage**
The phenomenological impression that time flows — that the present moves forward, leaving the past behind and approaching the future. Whether this passage is a real, objective feature of time or a systematically misleading feature of conscious experience is one of the most contested questions in the field. The "rate of passage" problem (at what rate does time flow?) is a well-known challenge to passage-realism.

**The Specious Present**
The psychologically extended present moment — roughly 2–3 seconds — within which experience is unified and temporally integrated, as opposed to the mathematical instant (durationless point) that classical physics would assign as "the present." Introduced by philosopher William James. The specious present is important for the phenomenology of time and raises the question of how a durationless mathematical present can correspond to an experientially extended one.

**Naturalism (about time)**
The methodological view that philosophical theories of time should be continuous with and constrained by the best available scientific theories. Naturalism does not require that philosophy simply read metaphysics off physics, but it does require that philosophical theories which conflict with well-established physics are ipso facto suspect.

**Temporal Indexicals**
Words whose reference shifts with the time of utterance: "now," "today," "yesterday," "soon," "then," etc. "Now" refers to the time of the utterance; "yesterday" refers to the day before the utterance. Whether "now" is merely an indexical (like "here") or whether it picks out an objectively special time (the present) is a central question dividing A-theory from B-theory approaches to time.

**A-Theory / A-Series**
The family of positions, introduced by McTaggart (1908), that hold the A-series (the ordering of events as past, present, or future) to be fundamental to the nature of time. A-theories typically hold that there is an objective present and that time genuinely passes. Presentism, the growing block, and the moving spotlight are all A-theories.

**B-Theory / B-Series**
The family of positions that hold the B-series (the ordering of events by the permanent, tenseless relations of "earlier than," "later than," and "simultaneous with") to be sufficient for the nature of time. B-theories typically deny that the present is objectively special and that time objectively passes. The block universe is a B-theory.

**The Phenomenology of Time**
The philosophical study of the structures of temporal experience from the first-person perspective. Initiated by Franz Brentano's problem of how we can hear a melody as sequential, and developed by Edmund Husserl's analysis of retention, primal impression, and protention, Martin Heidegger's account of authentic temporality, and Maurice Merleau-Ponty's analysis of embodied time.

**Temporal Topology**
The mathematical and metaphysical study of the global structural features of time: whether it is linear, circular, or branching; whether it is discrete, dense, or continuous; whether it is bounded or unbounded. Temporal topology affects what kinds of events and processes are possible and how causal structures can be arranged.
