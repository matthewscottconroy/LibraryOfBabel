# Chapter 1: Important Researchers

---

**Augustine of Hippo (354–430 CE)**
North African bishop and theologian whose *Confessions* (c. 397–400 CE) contains the most quoted and philosophically influential passage in the history of the philosophy of time. Augustine combined Platonic metaphysics with Christian theology to produce an original phenomenological account of temporal experience, locating time in the "distension of the soul" rather than in external reality. His analysis of the three-fold present — memory, attention, expectation — anticipates twentieth-century phenomenology and remains philosophically vital today. His treatment of creation and time (the question of what God was doing "before" creation) is the first rigorous formulation of the puzzle of temporal origins.

**Aristotle (384–322 BCE)**
Greek philosopher whose *Physics* (IV.10–14) contains the first systematic philosophical treatment of time in the Western tradition. Aristotle defined time as "the number of motion with respect to before and after" — a definition that has been debated, refined, and contested for two and a half millennia. His analysis of the Now (*to nun*), his argument that time requires a soul to count it, and his case for the eternity of the world established the terms of debate for medieval and early modern philosophy. Aristotle's treatment is remarkable for its combination of conceptual precision and genuine puzzlement.

**J.M.E. McTaggart (1866–1925)**
British idealist philosopher whose 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time" introduced the distinction between the A-series (past/present/future properties) and the B-series (earlier/later/simultaneous relations) that has organized analytical philosophy of time ever since. McTaggart argued that the A-series is essential to time as we understand it, that the A-series involves a vicious regress, and therefore that time is unreal. Whether or not this conclusion is accepted, the conceptual framework McTaggart provided is indispensable. His two-volume *The Nature of Existence* (1921–27) develops the argument in its full systematic context.

**Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)**
German philosopher of the critical period whose *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781/1787) proposed the most radical modern theory of time before the twentieth century. Kant argued that time is the form of inner sense — a structure contributed by the mind to experience, not a feature of things as they are in themselves. Time is "transcendentally ideal" but "empirically real": it governs all our experience but does not describe mind-independent reality. The First Antinomy of the Critique — that reason generates equally valid arguments both for and against time having a beginning — remains a landmark in the debate about temporal origins.

**David Hume (1711–1776)**
Scottish philosopher whose *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40) gave the classical empiricist account of our temporal concepts. Hume argued that our idea of time comes from the succession of impressions in the mind — not from any separate impression of time per se — and that we have no impression of the self that persists through time. His analysis of causation, with its claim that causes necessarily precede their effects, anchors the connection between temporal order and causal order that subsequent philosophy has widely accepted.

**W.V.O. Quine (1908–2000)**
American philosopher whose essay "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) challenged the analytic-synthetic distinction on which much traditional conceptual analysis rests. Quine's naturalistic philosophy — treating philosophy as continuous with science, subject to empirical revision — has been enormously influential on the methodology of the philosophy of time, pushing philosophers to take physical theories seriously as constraints on temporal metaphysics rather than as mere background.

**Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)**
American philosopher whose 1967 paper "Time and Physical Geometry" gave one of the most influential arguments that special relativity requires the block universe. Putnam argued that if real = present, and if the present is relative to a reference frame, then we can construct scenarios in which different reference frames disagree about whether a distant event is real — an unacceptable result. The only coherent response, Putnam argued, is to hold that all events are equally real. This "Rietdijk-Putnam argument" is a touchstone for subsequent debate.

**Tim Maudlin (1958–)**
American philosopher of physics whose work, including *The Metaphysics Within Physics* (2007) and *Philosophy of Physics: Space and Time* (2012), has done more than any other contemporary philosopher to clarify the philosophical implications of relativistic physics for the metaphysics of time. Maudlin defends a form of the view that there is a primitive temporal direction — a "passage" — that is metaphysically fundamental and not reducible to physical asymmetry. His rigorous engagement with the physics while defending robustly realist philosophical conclusions models the dialogue between physics and philosophy advocated in this chapter.

**Huw Price (1953–)**
Australian philosopher of physics whose *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point* (1996) is one of the most penetrating examinations of temporal asymmetry. Price argues for a thoroughgoing perspectivalism about temporal direction: the "arrow of time" that we observe is a feature of our perspective as agents embedded in time, not an objective feature of the physics. His "Archimedean" recommendation — to try to adopt a perspective outside time — is methodologically controversial but philosophically illuminating. Price's work exemplifies the revisionary approach described in Section 1.2.

**C.D. Broad (1887–1971)**
British philosopher whose *Scientific Thought* (1923) introduced the growing block theory of time and gave one of the most careful early analyses of the ontological options in the metaphysics of time. Broad distinguished sharply between (a) the claim that all times are equally real, (b) the claim that only the present is real, and (c) the claim that past and present are real but the future is not. His clear presentation of these options, and his defense of (c), have been the starting point for subsequent debate about temporal ontology.
