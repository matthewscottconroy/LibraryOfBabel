# 1.1.1 — Augustine's Confession: The Problem Stated

---

The most famous sentence in the entire history of the philosophy of time belongs not to a scientist or to a professional philosopher but to a North African bishop writing in the late fourth century. In Book XI of the *Confessions*, Augustine of Hippo asks, with a directness that no subsequent writer has improved upon:

> *"What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to one that asks, I do not know."*
> (Augustine, *Confessions*, XI.14.17, trans. Chadwick 1991: 230)

The sentence is worth pausing over. It is not a confession of ignorance in the ordinary sense — Augustine is not saying he has never thought about time or that he lacks the relevant vocabulary. He is saying something more precise and more disturbing: that the pre-theoretical, lived familiarity we all have with time does not translate into theoretical understanding. There is a gap between *knowing how* to use something and *knowing what* it is. We know how to use temporal concepts fluently; we deploy tenses every time we speak; we remember the past and anticipate the future without any sense of theoretical difficulty. But as soon as we try to formulate what we mean — as soon as we try to say what time *is* — the concepts that seemed solid become elusive.

This is not a problem peculiar to the fourth century. It has recurred in every subsequent period of philosophical reflection on time. Aristotle, who gave the first systematic philosophical treatment of time, began by cataloguing a set of *aporiai* — puzzles or difficulties — that show why time is hard to place within ordinary ontological categories (Aristotle, *Physics*, IV.10, 217b29–218a30). He asked: Does time exist at all, given that its two apparent parts — the past and future — do not exist, and the present is a dimensionless instant that vanishes before it can be grasped? And yet time seems clearly to exist in some sense — we measure it, order events by it, plan around it. Something that does not exist at all could hardly be so practically indispensable.

The same puzzle appears in a different guise in the philosophy of physics. When physicists write down the equations of mechanics, relativity, or quantum theory, they include a variable *t* that represents time. But what does that variable stand for? One way of putting the problem is this: in physics, time appears as a *parameter* against which other quantities are measured, but the nature of that parameter — what kind of thing it is, whether it flows, whether the past and future are equally real, whether there is an objective "now" — is not settled by the equations themselves. The equations work whatever the answer to those questions turns out to be (Maudlin 2012: 5–12). Physics uses time without explaining it.

## The Gap Between Use and Understanding

There is a philosophical tradition that treats this gap — between competent use and theoretical understanding — as the central method-revealing feature of philosophical problems. Wittgenstein, who was preoccupied throughout his career with the difference between using a word correctly and understanding it, put the general point like this: *"We are under the illusion that what is special, profound, essential in our investigation resides in its trying to grasp the incomparable essence of language."* (*Philosophical Investigations* §110, trans. Anscombe et al. 2009). His suggestion — not always helpful, but illuminating here — is that philosophical puzzlement often arises when we take words out of their ordinary working environments and ask them to do something they were not built for.

This diagnosis partly applies to time. We use temporal words — "before," "after," "now," "past," "future," "duration," "earlier than," "later than" — with perfect fluency in ordinary life. The puzzlement arises when we try to step back and ask what these words *pick out* in the world. Do they pick out properties of events? Relations between events? Features of our own mental representations? Something else entirely? The very fluency of ordinary temporal talk can disguise the fact that these are not settled questions.

But Wittgenstein's therapeutic approach — which would dissolve rather than answer the question — is ultimately insufficient here, for a reason that will become a recurring theme in this book. Time is not only a feature of ordinary discourse; it is a feature of the *physical world* that our discourse is trying to describe. The question "what is time?" is partly a question about language, but it is principally a question about nature. When physicists tell us that time passes at different rates in different reference frames, or that time may have had a beginning, or that the equations of quantum gravity seem to eliminate time as a fundamental variable, they are making claims that go beyond the analysis of ordinary usage. The philosophical question of what time is cannot be dissolved by attending more carefully to how we talk; it must be answered, at least in part, by attending to what the world is like.

## Why the Puzzle Is Distinctive

Not all philosophical questions have this character. Many philosophical questions — about the nature of justice, or the conditions for knowledge, or the analysis of causation — are primarily questions about concepts, where careful attention to how we use terms will do most of the explanatory work. The philosophy of time has a component of this kind, but it also has a robustly metaphysical and empirical component that makes it distinctive.

The puzzle of time is distinctive in at least three ways. First, time is *existentially unavoidable* in a way that many other philosophical topics are not. You can bracket the question of the existence of numbers or the nature of universals and live your life without feeling the gap. You cannot bracket time. Whatever answer we give, it has to coexist with the fact that you experience yourself as moving through time, that you remember a past and anticipate a future, and that this structure of experience is not optional. Any theory of time that cannot accommodate the phenomenology of temporal experience — or that requires us to dismiss it as wholesale illusion — owes us an account of why we should accept such a radical conclusion.

Second, time is *scientifically fundamental* in a way that creates genuine constraints on philosophical theorizing. Unlike questions about justice or knowledge, the question of what time is has answers that are partly constrained by the best physics we have. A theory of time must be compatible with the physical facts about time — about its relationship to space, its role in causal structure, its topology, its apparent asymmetry. Philosophy cannot simply ignore what physics tells us.

Third, time involves what we might call the *coincidence of the subjective and the objective*. Time is not only something in the world that we study from the outside; it is the medium of our own experience. We are in time in a way we are not in space — we can step sideways but we cannot step temporally. This creates a peculiar reflexivity: any account of time must account not only for time as a feature of the world but for our experience of being in it. The phenomenology of time and the physics of time cannot be kept in entirely separate compartments.

## Augustine's Own Answer

It is worth noting that Augustine's confession of ignorance is followed by a sustained and brilliant attempt to answer the question he has so memorably posed. His answer — that time is a *distentio animi*, a stretching or extension of the soul — is the first great phenomenological account of time in Western philosophy, and it anticipates, in striking ways, some of the most sophisticated treatments of temporal experience in the twentieth century. We will examine it in detail in Chapter 8. For now, the important point is simply that Augustine's puzzlement is not the last word; it is the right first word. It names the problem correctly. The philosophy of time begins in honest acknowledgment of what we do not know.
