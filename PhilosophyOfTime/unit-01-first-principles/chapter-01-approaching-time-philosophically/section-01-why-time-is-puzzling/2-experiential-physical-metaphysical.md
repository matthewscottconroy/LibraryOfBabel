# 1.1.2 — Time as Simultaneously Experiential, Physical, and Metaphysical

---

Part of what makes time so difficult to pin down philosophically is that the concept does not belong to any single domain of inquiry. It is not purely a matter for physics, nor purely a matter for phenomenology, nor purely a matter for metaphysics. It sits at the intersection of all three, and the theories that emerge from each domain place different — and sometimes conflicting — constraints on what a satisfactory account must look like.

Understanding why this is so is essential preparation for the investigations that follow. This subsection traces each of the three dimensions of the problem and begins to map out how they relate to one another.

## Time as Experienced

Consider first what it is like to live in time. Right now, as you read these words, you are not merely processing a static array of information. You are doing something irreducibly temporal: reading is a sequential activity in which earlier words inform the understanding of later ones, in which sentences accumulate meaning over their duration, in which anticipation of what comes next shapes the comprehension of what is happening now. Language itself, and therefore thought itself insofar as it is linguistically structured, is temporal through and through.

But the temporal character of experience goes deeper than that. You experience yourself as continuous — as the same person who read the previous paragraph, who had breakfast this morning, who was a child two decades ago. This sense of continuity, of being a self that extends through time, is not a belief you hold as a result of inference; it is a basic feature of experience, or at least of what philosophers call *the structure of the given*. You experience yourself as coming from a past and moving toward a future.

This experiential dimension of time has attracted sustained attention from phenomenologists — philosophers who take the structures of experience as philosophically primary. Edmund Husserl, in his *Lectures on the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* (1928/1991), argued that our experience of time is not produced by passively receiving a sequence of atomic moments and then mentally arranging them into an order. Rather, we experience time through what he called *retention* and *protention*: a just-passed impression remains phenomenally present (retained) as the next impression arrives, while a not-yet-arrived impression is anticipated (protended). Consciousness is always already temporally extended, even in what seems like an instant of present experience. The notes of a melody are not heard one by one and then assembled into a sequence; they are heard as flowing, each note retaining a trace of the one before and anticipating the one to come (Husserl 1991: 27–42).

This phenomenological account is rich and important, and we will examine it carefully in Chapter 26. For now, what matters is the observation that experiential time has its own distinctive features — features of flow, of the structured present, of memory and anticipation — that any adequate philosophy of time must somehow accommodate.

## Time as Physical

Meanwhile, physics has developed its own account of time, and it is dramatically different from the picture suggested by experience. In Newtonian mechanics, time is absolute and universal: there is a single, uniform temporal flow against which all events in the universe are coordinated, and all clocks that are not interfered with measure the same thing (Newton 1687/1999: 408–415). This fits relatively well with common experience.

Einstein's special theory of relativity shattered this picture. According to special relativity, simultaneity is relative to a reference frame: two events that are simultaneous as measured in one inertial frame may not be simultaneous as measured in another. There is no observer-independent fact about which events are happening "now" across different locations (Einstein 1905; Minkowski 1908/1952). Time dilation is real: a moving clock runs slower than a stationary one, not as an illusion or a measurement artifact but as a physical fact. Two twins who age at different rates due to differential motion will return from a journey genuinely different ages (Maudlin 2012: 47–76).

General relativity goes further still: the rate of time's passage is affected by gravity. A clock deeper in a gravitational well runs slower than one higher up. This is not a theoretical prediction with no practical consequences; GPS satellites must account for this effect or their location calculations drift by kilometers per day (Ashby 2002).

And the most radical challenge comes from attempts to unify quantum mechanics with general relativity. In the Wheeler-DeWitt equation — one of the central equations of canonical quantum gravity — time does not appear as a fundamental variable at all. The wavefunction of the universe is timeless; time, on some interpretations, must emerge from something more basic (DeWitt 1967; Barbour 1999: 229–271). Whether this is the last word on the physics of time remains deeply contested, but it reveals that even the physicist's conception of time is far less settled than it might appear.

## Time as Metaphysical

Between the phenomenological and the physical sits a set of distinctively metaphysical questions — questions about what kinds of things exist, what properties they have, and how they relate to one another — that neither experience nor physics alone can settle.

The most fundamental of these concerns *what times there are*. Are only present things real, with past and future things having no genuine ontological status? This is the view called *presentism*. Or do past, present, and future things all exist equally, the "present" being merely the time we happen to be at — just as "here" names the place you happen to be, with no implication that other places are less real? This is *eternalism* or the *block universe* view. Or is there a growing ontological totality — the past and present are real, the future is not yet, and time genuinely grows as new events occur? This is the *growing block* view (Broad 1923: 66–84).

These options are not distinguished by physical predictions — the physics is, to a significant degree, neutral between them — and they are not resolved by the phenomenology of experience, since experience is consistent with several of the views. They are purely metaphysical questions about the fundamental ontological structure of the temporal world.

Other metaphysical questions about time include: Does time have a direction, and if so, what grounds it? What is the relationship between time and causation? Does change require time, or does time require change? Can there be time without events? Is the universe's temporal structure discrete or continuous? Could time have a topology different from a simple linear order — circular, branching, or closed?

## The Intersection and Its Tensions

What is important to grasp at the outset is that these three domains — experiential, physical, and metaphysical — are not sealed off from one another. They interact and constrain each other in complex ways, and a recurring challenge throughout this book will be to keep the interactions clear.

Consider one example of the tension. Our experience of time presents it as *flowing* — as having a direction, a "moving now," a genuine passage from past through present to future. This is one of the most vivid and pervasive features of temporal experience. But many philosophers and physicists hold that physical time is static: the block universe, if correct, contains no objective flow, no moving now, no genuine becoming. If this is right, then the experienced flow of time is in some sense an illusion generated by the structure of consciousness — it is not a feature of time itself (Price 1996: 12–14; Callender 2000).

Whether this conclusion should be accepted — whether physics really does mandate a static conception of time and whether the phenomenology of flow must therefore be debunked — is one of the deepest and most contested questions in the entire field. It will occupy us in Unit V, Unit VII, and Unit VIII. For now, the point is simply that the question cannot be answered by attending to one domain alone. You cannot settle it by phenomenology, because the phenomenology might be misleading about metaphysics. You cannot settle it by physics, because the physics underdetermines the metaphysics. And you cannot settle it by pure conceptual analysis, because the question has empirical components. You need all three tools, and you need to be careful about how they interact.
