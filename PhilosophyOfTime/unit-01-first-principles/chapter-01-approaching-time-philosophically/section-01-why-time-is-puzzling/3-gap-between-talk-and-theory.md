# 1.1.3 — The Gap Between Ordinary Talk About Time and Theoretical Accounts

---

When you say "I'll meet you in an hour," nobody is confused. When you say "that was a long time ago," your listener understands immediately. When you say "things were better then," or "the future is uncertain," or "she died before he was born," you are communicating temporal information with perfect efficiency. Ordinary temporal language is rich, flexible, and in constant practical use.

But look more closely at some of the things we say — really look at them — and they begin to seem stranger.

## The Vocabulary of Temporal Experience

We speak of time as something that *passes* or *flows*. We say "time flies," "how quickly this afternoon has gone," "the years seem to slip by faster as you get older." These idioms treat time as something that moves — presumably relative to some fixed reference point. But relative to what? In ordinary spatial motion, when we say a train moves, we mean it moves relative to the track or the earth. When we say time passes, what is time moving relative to? If everything is in time — every event, every process, every observer — then there is nothing *outside* time relative to which it could be measured as moving. The notion of time's flow threatens to be incoherent, or at least deeply obscure (Williams 1951: 464–465; Smart 1949: 483–484).

We speak of the present as a kind of *location* — where we are in time. "I'm stuck in the present," someone might joke. "If only I could see the future." This spatial metaphor — the present as a place, the future as somewhere we haven't yet arrived — is so natural and so pervasive that it is difficult even to notice that it *is* a metaphor. And like all metaphors, it can illuminate some aspects of a phenomenon while obscuring others. In space, you can move in any direction you choose. In time, there seems to be only one direction available to you. In space, other places are simultaneous with where you are — they exist right now, even though you are not there. In time, the future does not exist yet (at least according to some views), and the past no longer exists. The spatial metaphor breaks down at exactly the points where temporal experience is most distinctive.

We speak of the past as *fixed* and the future as *open*. "What's done is done." "Anything could happen." This asymmetry — the immutability of the past versus the indeterminacy of the future — is one of the deepest features of ordinary temporal thinking. And yet from the standpoint of physics, it is puzzling. The fundamental laws of physics (with minor exceptions we will discuss in Chapter 18) are time-symmetric: they work equally well run forwards or backwards. Nothing in the laws singles out a direction. The asymmetry between past and future — the sense in which the past is fixed and the future open — must therefore come from somewhere else: from the initial conditions of the universe, from thermodynamics, from the structure of causation, from the nature of agency. Where exactly it comes from is, as we will see, one of the deepest questions in the entire field.

## The Problem of Tense

Perhaps the deepest divergence between ordinary temporal talk and theoretical accounts concerns *tense* — the grammatical feature of language that marks events as past, present, or future. When I say "the meeting *is* happening," I mean something different from "the meeting *was* happening" and different again from "the meeting *will* happening." Tense is not merely a grammatical convention; it is how we express something about the temporal position of events relative to the moment of utterance.

But many philosophers of time — those who favor the B-theory, which we will examine closely in Chapter 14 — argue that tense is in a significant sense a *misleading* feature of language. Their claim is that the real temporal structure of the world consists entirely of *tenseless* relations: the fact that event A is earlier than event B, or that B is later than A, or that A and B are simultaneous. On this view, there is no fact of the matter about which events are *now* happening, independent of a perspective. "Now" is like "here" — it picks out a position relative to the utterer, not an objective feature of the world. The proper way to describe temporal reality, they argue, is in terms of these tenseless before-and-after relations, with no irreducible use of past, present, or future (Mellor 1998: 1–20; Russell 1903: 469–471).

This view is philosophically coherent, and there are good reasons to take it seriously. But it is deeply revisionary. It implies that ordinary temporal talk — all that rich, practical, phenomenologically saturated talk of the past and future and present — involves a form of systematic distortion when taken at face value. It implies that the "nowness" of the present is not an objective feature of time but a perspectival feature of the mind. That conclusion may be correct, but it should not be adopted lightly, and it certainly should not be adopted before we have examined the alternatives.

## Two Models of the Problem

There is a useful way of thinking about the gap between ordinary talk and theoretical account that will be helpful throughout this book. We can distinguish between two models of what philosophy of time is trying to do.

On the first model — call it *descriptive metaphysics* (a phrase borrowed from Strawson 1959) — the goal is to articulate and systematize the conceptual scheme implicit in ordinary temporal talk and thought. What does it presuppose about the structure of time? What commitments does it carry that might be made explicit? On this model, the intuitive conviction that the present is special, that the past is fixed, that time flows in a particular direction — these are *data* that a theory of time must accommodate or explain away.

On the second model — call it *revisionary metaphysics* — the goal is to arrive at the *true* account of temporal reality, even if this requires departing significantly from ordinary intuitions. On this model, ordinary temporal talk may embed systematic errors: perhaps time does not really flow, perhaps the present is not objectively special, perhaps tense is not a real feature of the world. Science and rigorous argument, not intuition, are the guides.

Both models have their defenders, and both have their dangers. The descriptive model risks making intuition too authoritative, ignoring the possibility that our pre-theoretical picture of time might be seriously wrong. The revisionary model risks an overconfidence in its ability to derive metaphysical conclusions from physics, ignoring the genuine evidential weight of how time appears to us from the inside.

The best approach — and the one this book will attempt to model — holds both commitments in productive tension. Ordinary temporal talk is taken seriously as data; departures from ordinary intuitions require genuine justification; and the goal is an account that does justice to both what time is like in experience and what the best available physics and metaphysics tells us it is.
