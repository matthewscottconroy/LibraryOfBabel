# Section 1.1: Why Time Is Philosophically Puzzling

---

## Section Introduction

The first step in any philosophical investigation is to resist the temptation to already know the answer. Time is so deeply woven into ordinary experience — we talk about it constantly, plan around it, fear it, mourn its passage — that it can seem self-evident. But self-evidence is a philosophical trap. The most obvious things are often the hardest to understand, because we have learned to use them without examining them.

This section works to make time strange. That is not an end in itself, but it is a necessary beginning. We need to feel the puzzlement before we can properly formulate the question, and we need to formulate the question before we can make progress toward an answer.

Three distinct sources of puzzlement are identified in the subsections that follow: the gap between experience and articulation that Augustine identified; the multi-dimensional nature of time as simultaneously experiential, physical, and metaphysical; and the curious gap between our ordinary temporal talk and what any systematic theory will have to say. Together these set the stage for the methodological discussions that follow in the rest of the chapter.
