# 1.2.1 — Conceptual Analysis

---

The classical project of conceptual analysis is as old as Socrates: to take a concept we use fluently in practice — courage, knowledge, justice — and articulate the necessary and sufficient conditions for its application. What must be true for something to be an instance of knowledge? Plato's *Theaetetus* suggests: justified true belief. What must be true for someone to count as courageous? The analysis proceeds by proposing a definition and then testing it against cases — including, especially, counterexamples that expose where the proposed definition goes wrong.

Applied to time, conceptual analysis asks: what does it mean for something to be temporal? What distinguishes a temporal sequence from a non-temporal one? What must be true for two events to stand in the relation "earlier than"? What are the conditions under which we would say a particular moment is *present*?

## What Conceptual Analysis Can Accomplish

At its best, conceptual analysis achieves two things. First, it makes *implicit commitments explicit*. When we say "the meeting was before the lecture," we are committed to a relation between two events that is asymmetric (if A is before B, then B is not before A), irreflexive (nothing is before itself), and transitive (if A is before B and B is before C, then A is before C). These are logical properties of the relation of temporal precedence, and they constrain what a theory of time must look like. They are commitments we had all along; conceptual analysis just surfaces them.

Second, conceptual analysis can identify *tensions and inconsistencies* in our ordinary thinking. Consider the following cluster of ordinary temporal beliefs: (a) only the present is real; (b) the past was once present; (c) the past is gone. These three seem individually compelling, but they pull against one another in subtle ways. If only the present is real, and the past is no longer present, then the past is not real — which seems to conflict with (b), which attributes reality to the past "when it was present." Conceptual analysis makes this tension visible, so we can decide whether to resolve it by revision, by contextual disambiguation, or by adopting one of the metaphysical theories we will examine in Unit V.

## The Limits of Conceptual Analysis

The method has two important limitations in the philosophy of time.

First, there is the problem of *concept empiricism*: our temporal concepts may be shaped by features of our specific perceptual and cognitive architecture in ways that do not reflect the metaphysical structure of time itself. The concept of a "present moment," for instance, may be shaped by the width of our specious present (approximately 2–3 seconds) rather than by anything metaphysically fundamental. If so, analyzing the concept of the present may tell us more about human temporal cognition than about temporal reality. We will return to this point in Chapter 27.

Second, there is what Quine (1951) called the challenge to the *analytic-synthetic distinction*: the sharp line between claims that are true in virtue of meaning (analytic) and claims that are true in virtue of the world (synthetic) may not be as clear as the method of conceptual analysis assumes. If there is no sharp line, then "conceptual truths" about time may be partly empirical — revisable in light of new evidence. This does not destroy the method, but it does chastening. Conceptual analysis is not a way of arriving at necessary truths that are immune to empirical revision; it is a way of making our current commitments explicit so that they can be held up to scrutiny.

## Conceptual Analysis in Practice: The Case of "Earlier Than"

Consider a concrete example. The concept *earlier than* seems to have the following features: it is a two-place relation between events; it is asymmetric, irreflexive, and transitive; it is total or at least dense; and it is connected to causation (causes are earlier than their effects) and to memory (we remember earlier events, not later ones).

Now, special relativity tells us that the relation *earlier than* is not, in general, frame-independent. Whether A is earlier than B depends on the reference frame of the observer. Does this mean our concept of "earlier than" is confused or incoherent? Or does it mean that what we ordinarily call "earlier than" is really a composite of different relations (proper-time ordering, causal precedence, simultaneity-relative ordering) that come apart in the relativistic regime?

The answer shapes everything. If our ordinary concept of "earlier than" is irrevocably committed to frame-independence, then special relativity shows that concept to be inapplicable to physical time — and the correct concept of temporal ordering must be the frame-relative one. If instead our ordinary concept is neutral between frame-dependent and frame-independent versions, then we can accommodate relativity without concluding that our pre-theoretic temporal concepts are mistaken.

This is not a merely verbal question. Which answer we give affects what we think special relativity shows about the *nature* of time — about whether the present is objective, whether presentism is coherent, whether the passage of time is real. The methods of conceptual analysis and of physics must cooperate to answer it.
