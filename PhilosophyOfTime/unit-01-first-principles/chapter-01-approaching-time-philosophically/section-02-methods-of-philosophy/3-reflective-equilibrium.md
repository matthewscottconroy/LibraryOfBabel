# 1.2.3 — Reflective Equilibrium

---

Imagine you are trying to work out your ethical commitments. You have some considered judgments about particular cases — it is wrong to torture innocents for entertainment; it is admirable to help strangers in distress. You also have some general principles — perhaps "maximize welfare" or "respect persons as ends." The method of *reflective equilibrium*, introduced by John Rawls in the context of moral theory (Rawls 1971: 46–53) and extended to epistemology and metaphysics, describes the process of moving back and forth between particular judgments and general principles, adjusting each in light of the other, until you arrive at a coherent and stable system.

The starting point is always this: when the principle conflicts with a firm particular judgment, you have a choice. You can revise the judgment (concluding that your initial intuition was mistaken) or revise the principle (concluding that the principle is too strong). The method provides no algorithm for choosing; it requires judgment about which commitments are firmest. The goal is a state of coherence — what Rawls called "wide reflective equilibrium" — in which principles and particular judgments are mutually supporting, neither flatly inconsistent nor merely accidentally related.

## Applying Reflective Equilibrium to the Philosophy of Time

The method of reflective equilibrium applies directly to temporal inquiry, and it disciplines the use of thought experiments. Consider the following constellation of commitments:

**Intuitions about particular cases:**
1. The present moment is special — uniquely real — in a way that past and future moments are not.
2. Events in the past happened; they are fixed and nothing can change them.
3. Events in the future are open; whether they happen depends on what we and others do.
4. The temporal distance between two events — how long ago, how far in the future — is an objective fact.

**General principles candidates:**
P1. All times are ontologically equal: the past, present, and future are equally real.  
P2. Only present things exist.  
P3. The past and present exist; the future does not yet.  
P4. There is no observer-independent fact about simultaneity.

Intuition 1 conflicts with P1. If all times are equally real, why does the present feel uniquely special? Perhaps, P1's defenders say, because we are *at* the present — just as a place feels special when you are there, not because it is objectively special. Intuition 2 supports both P1 (the past is real, so fixed) and P2 (the past was real when it was present — now it is gone, which is why it is fixed). Intuition 3 conflicts with P1: if the future is equally real, it is "already there" and its being open seems illusory. Principle P4 conflicts with intuition 4: if temporal distance is objective but simultaneity is not, how do we measure distance from one event to another?

Reflective equilibrium asks: given all these conflicts, what should be revised? The B-theorist (who adopts something like P1 and P4) concludes that intuitions 1 and 3 are systematically misleading — they reflect our perspectival position in time, not its objective structure. The presentist (who adopts P2) accepts intuition 1 at face value but faces serious challenges from P4. The growing block theorist (who adopts something like P3) splits the difference but faces the epistemological challenge of knowing which block we are at the edge of.

This is genuine philosophical work, and it has no algorithmic resolution. Different philosophers weight their commitments differently, and the debate is live.

## The Discipline the Method Provides

What reflective equilibrium provides, importantly, is a *discipline*: it prevents both the pure rationalist move of simply deducing conclusions from axioms (ignoring the weight of intuitions) and the pure intuitionist move of simply insisting on intuitions (ignoring theoretical pressure). In the philosophy of time, both moves are tempting and both are mistakes.

The pure rationalist move is tempting because the physics is elegant and the metaphysics is amenable to formal treatment. It is easy to feel that the block universe — with its clean four-dimensional geometry — is simply *obviously* correct, and that anyone who insists on an objective present is just confused. But this is to give too much weight to theoretical elegance and too little to phenomenological data. The experience of the present as uniquely real is not a trivial or negligible datum; it is an extremely pervasive, detailed, and compelling feature of the human situation.

The pure intuitionist move is tempting because our temporal experience is so vivid and immediate. It is easy to feel that the block universe must be wrong because we *know* time flows — we feel it. But this is to give too much weight to phenomenology and too little to physical theory, which has repeatedly surprised us by showing that things are structured very differently from how they appear.

Reflective equilibrium keeps both pressures in play. It is the method of theoretical humility: willing to revise either theories or intuitions, but only under genuine pressure, and with eyes open about what is being abandoned.
