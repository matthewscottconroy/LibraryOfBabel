# 1.2.4 — Naturalistic and Revisionary Approaches

---

The methods discussed so far — conceptual analysis, thought experiments, reflective equilibrium — operate primarily within the space of concepts and intuitions. They start from what we ordinarily think and work to make it coherent. But there is a long tradition in philosophy that is skeptical of this starting point: perhaps ordinary concepts and intuitions are too anthropocentric, too parochial, too limited by our evolutionary and cultural inheritance to serve as reliable guides to the deep structure of reality. On this view, philosophy should be *naturalistic* — taking its cue from science — or even *revisionary* — willing to abandon ordinary concepts wholesale if they conflict with our best theoretical understanding.

In the philosophy of time, naturalistic and revisionary pressures are especially acute, because physics has so dramatically revised what we thought we knew about time. This subsection examines what these approaches consist in and where they lead.

## Naturalism in the Philosophy of Time

The naturalistic approach to time, most prominently associated with Willard Van Orman Quine and developed in different ways by philosophers like David Lewis, David Albert, and Tim Maudlin, holds that philosophical theories of time must be *continuous* with — and ultimately constrained by — our best scientific theories. Philosophy and science are not two separate enterprises with separate subject matters; they are part of a single ongoing inquiry into the nature of reality, differing in degree of generality rather than in kind (Quine 1969: 126–127).

For the naturalist, what Newton's mechanics tells us about time, what Einstein's relativity tells us about time, and what quantum gravity (when we have a coherent version) tells us about time, are all data that any adequate philosophical theory of time must accommodate. Philosophy cannot arrive at truths about time by *a priori* reasoning alone; it cannot simply deduce the structure of time from the armchair. The armchair is useful for conceptual work, but the empirical content of our temporal concepts must ultimately be answerable to physics.

This does not mean that philosophy simply reads off metaphysics from physics, which would be naive. As we saw in subsection 1.1.2, physics underdetermines metaphysics: the same physical theories are compatible with multiple metaphysical interpretations. What naturalism requires is not that philosophy take physics' dictates as final, but that it take physics seriously as evidence — that philosophical theories which conflict with well-established physics are suspect, and that philosophical theories which are best motivated by physics are, other things equal, preferable.

## The Revisionary Impulse

The *revisionary* approach goes further than naturalism. The revisionary metaphysician holds that ordinary concepts of time — the flow of time, the objective present, the fixity of the past — are not merely incomplete but positively mistaken, and that a proper philosophical and scientific account will require replacing them.

J.J.C. Smart, writing in the 1950s, articulated perhaps the most direct version of this view: the concept of time as flowing, as having a "river of time" that carries us from past to future, is incoherent — there is no sense that can be made of it; it should be abandoned, not analyzed (Smart 1949; 1980). D.H. Mellor, in a series of important papers and books culminating in *Real Time II* (1998), argued that tensed facts — facts about what is past, present, or future — are not real features of the world but are systematically reducible to tenseless facts about temporal relations. Nothing is "really" present; "present" means nothing more than "simultaneous with this utterance."

These revisionary views have important implications. If they are correct, then a large portion of what ordinary people think about time is wrong. The "nowness" of the present, the "passage" of time, the "openness" of the future — all of these are illusions, projections of our cognitive structure onto a static four-dimensional manifold. Whether this conclusion is correct is one of the central disputes of the philosophy of time and will occupy much of this book.

## The Case for Taking Ordinary Intuitions Seriously

One should not capitulate to revisionary pressure without strong reasons. Ordinary temporal intuitions are not merely psychological curiosities; they are the data that any theory of time must ultimately answer to. The world appears to us as temporal; the appearance of temporality is the most pervasive structural feature of experience. A theory that explains away this appearance must offer an account of why it appears — and that account must be both empirically and philosophically convincing.

Moreover, revisionary arguments often conceal dependencies on the very intuitions they claim to revise. When Smart argues that the concept of temporal flow is incoherent because flow requires a second temporal dimension against which the first can be measured, he is relying on an intuition about what flow consists in (involving a rate) to argue that temporal flow is incoherent. If the ordinary person's concept of temporal flow does not involve a rate — if it is a different kind of phenomenon from spatial flow — then Smart's argument misses its target. Identifying these hidden dependencies is a large part of what careful philosophical work in this area consists in.

The lesson is methodological: revisionary arguments are worth taking seriously, but they must be scrutinized as carefully as the ordinary intuitions they challenge. Revision must be earned.
