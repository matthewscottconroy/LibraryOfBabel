# 1.3.1 — When Physics Settles Philosophical Questions

---

There is a temptation, especially among scientifically minded philosophers, to think that philosophy is merely "pre-science" — the confused early stage of an inquiry that, once properly formalized and subjected to empirical test, becomes science and leaves philosophy behind. On this view, philosophical questions about time are provisional questions that were asked before physics had the tools to answer them, and now that those tools exist, the questions are answered.

There is something to this view, and it would be intellectually dishonest to deny it. Physics has settled some philosophical questions about time. Let us be specific about which ones.

## Genuine Philosophical Progress from Physics

**The question of absolute versus relational time.** From Aristotle through Newton, a central question was whether time is an independent entity (a kind of container in which events occur) or whether it consists entirely of relations among events. Newton defended the first view; Leibniz attacked it; the debate seemed philosophically intractable (see Chapter 10). Einstein's special relativity did not *definitively* settle this question, but it decisively changed its terms. Without a preferred frame of reference, there is no fact of the matter about what is happening "at the same time" at distant locations — a result that removes one of the most compelling motivations for absolute Newtonian time. The relational character of temporal facts has been substantially vindicated (Maudlin 2012: 60–68).

**The eternity of the world.** For much of the Western philosophical and theological tradition, the question of whether the world (and time) had a beginning was both philosophically and theologically live. Kant thought it was antinomial — neither answer could be established by reason alone. Modern cosmology has transformed this debate: we have compelling empirical evidence that the universe had a beginning roughly 13.8 billion years ago, and the best current models describe what that beginning was like (at least down to a Planck time of ~10⁻⁴³ seconds). This does not close every philosophical question about temporal origins — quantum gravity may revise the picture, and the question of *why* there was a Big Bang remains open — but it rules out the eternalist answer in its simple classical form.

**The universality of temporal flow.** Newton's assumption of a single universal time — the same for all observers, everywhere — has been decisively refuted. Different observers, moving at different velocities or occupying different gravitational fields, genuinely experience different amounts of time passing. This is not a theoretical nicety; it is measured every day by GPS satellites (Ashby 2002). Any philosophy of time must accommodate this fact.

## What This Means for Method

The lesson is not that physics is sufficient for philosophy of time. It is that philosophical theorizing about time cannot proceed in empirical isolation. A philosophical theory of time that requires universal Newtonian simultaneity, or that requires the world to be eternal, or that requires all observers to share a single temporal experience, is not merely philosophically inadequate — it is *false*, ruled out by the evidence.

Philosophers of time must therefore be scientifically literate. They do not need to derive tensor equations, but they need to understand what the equations say and what they imply for temporal structure. A philosopher who argues confidently for presentism without engaging seriously with special relativity is like a philosopher who argues about species without knowing what Darwin established. The engagement is not optional.
