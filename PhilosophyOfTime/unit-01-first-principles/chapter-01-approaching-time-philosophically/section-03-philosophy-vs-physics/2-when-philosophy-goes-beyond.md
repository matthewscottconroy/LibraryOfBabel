# 1.3.2 — When Philosophy Must Go Beyond Physics

---

If physics settles some philosophical questions about time, it equally fails to settle others — and these are often the ones that matter most for the philosophy of time. The incompleteness of physics as a guide to temporal metaphysics is not a temporary deficiency that better physics will remedy; it is a structural feature of the relationship between empirical science and metaphysical interpretation.

## The Underdetermination Problem

The core issue is *underdetermination*: multiple, incompatible metaphysical interpretations are consistent with the same physical theory. This is not a new observation; it was articulated clearly by Pierre Duhem in the context of classical physics (Duhem 1906/1954) and has been extensively studied in contemporary philosophy of science. Applied to time, it means that the same physical equations — the same predictions, the same empirical tests, the same observational consequences — are compatible with metaphysical theories that disagree profoundly about what is *real*.

The block universe interpretation and the presentist interpretation, for instance, give identical physical predictions. Both are consistent with special relativity, with the measured rate of clocks, with the entropy gradient, with every physical experiment ever performed. They disagree about whether the past and future *exist*, about whether there is an objective "now," about whether the universe is fundamentally static or dynamic. These are metaphysical disagreements that physics, by itself, cannot settle.

This is not because physics is insufficiently developed; it is because the question "which times are real?" is not a question about observable phenomena. Observational equivalence holds between the block universe and its alternatives in principle, not merely in practice.

## Questions Physics Does Not Address

Several major questions in the philosophy of time are, in this sense, beyond physics:

**The ontological status of times.** Are past, present, and future events all equally real? Or does only the present exist? Physics uses a four-dimensional formalism, but the formalism does not *assert* that all four dimensions are equally real; it simply uses them as coordinates. The metaphysical question of what is real is a further question that the formalism leaves open.

**The phenomenology of temporal experience.** Physics has nothing to say about what it is *like* to be in time — about the peculiar character of the experienced present, the phenomenal difference between the lived now and the remembered then, the feeling that time flows. These are questions about consciousness and experience. Even a complete physics of temporal structure would leave these questions untouched, just as a complete physics of light and surface reflection leaves untouched the question of why red *looks* the way it does to a conscious observer.

**The direction of time as normative vs. descriptive.** Physics describes the thermodynamic arrow of time — entropy tends to increase in one direction. But the question of whether this makes that direction the "true" direction of time, whether there is something wrong with the other direction, whether the future is "really" open in a way the past is not — these are normative or interpretive questions that no amount of thermodynamic data settles (Price 1996: 1–16).

**Personal identity through time.** What makes you the same person who was here yesterday? This question about persistence through time — which we examine in Unit IX — depends on philosophical considerations about what constitutes a person, what makes a person the same person, how memory and psychological continuity contribute to identity. Physics has no account of persons; it deals with physical configurations, not persons. The philosophy of personal identity through time therefore requires distinctively philosophical tools.

## The Danger of Physics-Imperialism

There is a form of intellectual overreach that might be called *physics imperialism* in the philosophy of time: the assumption that any philosophical claim that cannot be settled by physics is thereby confused or meaningless. This assumption has been influential — it is a descendant of logical positivism — but it is wrong.

The most direct argument against it: physicists themselves must interpret their equations, and interpretation is a philosophical activity. When Niels Bohr said the wavefunction should not be taken to represent a real physical state, and when Hugh Everett said it should, they were engaged in philosophical interpretation of a physical theory. When Barbour says quantum gravity shows time is unreal, and when Smolin says it shows time is the most real thing, they are engaged in philosophical argument about the implications of physical results. Physics does not automatically interpret itself. Philosophy of physics is genuinely necessary — not merely decorative.
