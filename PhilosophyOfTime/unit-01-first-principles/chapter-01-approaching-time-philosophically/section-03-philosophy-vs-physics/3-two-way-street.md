# 1.3.3 — The Two-Way Street: Philosophy and Physics in Dialogue

---

The relationship between philosophy and physics in the study of time is best described not as subordination in either direction but as a genuine *dialogue* — a two-way exchange in which each discipline contributes to and is constrained by the other. This dialogue has a long history. It produced Leibniz's critique of Newton, Mach's critique of absolute space and time, the neo-Kantian interpretation of special relativity, and the contemporary debate over what quantum gravity implies about temporal ontology. Understanding its structure helps us navigate the material that follows.

## Philosophy Informing Physics: The Leibniz-Mach Tradition

The first direction of the exchange is philosophy constraining physics. Philosophers and philosophically minded scientists have repeatedly identified conceptual problems with physical theories that motivated theoretical revision.

Leibniz's critique of Newton is the paradigm case. Leibniz argued, on philosophical grounds (the Principle of Sufficient Reason and the Identity of Indiscernibles), that Newton's absolute time was metaphysically unjustified: if the world had been created one hour earlier, occupying the same absolute times plus one hour, there would be no difference in the relations between events — hence no reason to prefer one hypothesis over the other, hence no fact of the matter about absolute temporal position (Leibniz, Fifth Letter to Clarke, 1716). This philosophical critique was mathematically vindicated by Einstein two centuries later: special relativity eliminates the preferred temporal frame that Newton required.

Ernst Mach pressed an analogous critique against Newton's bucket argument. Newton claimed that the rotation of a bucket (relative to absolute space) was revealed by the concavity of the water's surface — proving that absolute space is real. Mach argued (on philosophical grounds of empirical meaningfulness) that the result could equally be interpreted as rotation relative to the fixed stars — the totality of matter in the universe (Mach 1883/1960: 271–284). Mach's Principle directly influenced Einstein's development of general relativity, in which (in a partial sense) the distribution of matter determines the inertial structure of spacetime.

In both cases, philosophical argument identified a tension or unjustified assumption in a physical theory, motivating theoretical development.

## Physics Informing Philosophy: The Relativity Tradition

The second direction of the exchange is physics constraining philosophy. When physical theories are well-confirmed, they place genuine constraints on what philosophical theories can coherently maintain.

The relativity of simultaneity in special relativity is the clearest case. If there is no observer-independent fact about whether two spatially separated events are simultaneous, then any philosophical theory that requires an objective global present — a set of events that are all "happening now" — is in tension with the physics. Hilary Putnam drew the most direct conclusion: special relativity, together with plausible assumptions about the relationship between reality and the present, implies that all events in spacetime are equally real — the block universe. This argument (Putnam 1967) has been contested (Stein 1991; Zimmerman 2008), but it demonstrates the direct relevance of physical results to metaphysical conclusions.

Similarly, the discovery that entropy increases in one temporal direction — and the subsequent development of the statistical mechanical explanation of this increase — places constraints on philosophical accounts of the arrow of time. Any account of temporal direction that makes the thermodynamic arrow a mystery, or that denies that thermodynamic processes have an asymmetric temporal structure, is in tension with the physics.

## The Indispensability of Interpretation

There is a further, deeper sense in which the relationship is two-way: physics cannot be understood without philosophical interpretation, and philosophical interpretation is constrained by physical theory. This is not a defect of physics; it is a structural feature of any theoretical enterprise.

When we say that special relativity "implies" the relativity of simultaneity, we are already interpreting: the equations of the Lorentz transformation relate coordinates, not necessarily facts about what exists. The inference from the coordinate transformation to the metaphysical conclusion that there is no objective simultaneity requires a philosophical premise about the relationship between coordinates and facts. Different philosophers make different choices about that premise and reach different metaphysical conclusions.

When we say that quantum gravity "suggests" that time is not fundamental, we are again interpreting: the Wheeler-DeWitt equation has no explicit time variable, but this may mean that time is not fundamental, or it may mean that the equation is expressed in a convenient formalism, or it may mean that our classical concept of time fails to apply at the quantum gravity scale while time as such remains real. Choosing between these interpretations is philosophical work.

This is the two-way street in its fullest form: physics and philosophy are neither independent nor in a relationship of simple authority, but are engaged in a continuous, mutually correcting dialogue. Philosophers of time who ignore physics are working with one hand tied behind their back. Physicists who think their equations automatically resolve philosophical questions are misreading what their equations say. The productive zone is the interface — and much of this book lives there.
