# Section 1.3: Philosophy and Physics — Complements or Competitors?

---

## Section Introduction

Perhaps the most practically urgent methodological question in the philosophy of time is this: what is the relationship between what philosophers say about time and what physicists say about it? Are these two independent inquiries that can proceed in isolation? Are they competitive claims about the same subject, so that one must win and the other lose? Or are they genuinely complementary — addressing different aspects of the same phenomenon, with each depending on and informing the other?

The answer matters enormously for how you read this book. If physics has already answered the questions of the philosophy of time, then much of what follows is merely historical. If physics is irrelevant to those questions, then much of what follows in the physics chapters (Unit VII) is mere background. Neither extreme is right. The relationship is complex, productive, and irreducibly tense — and understanding its character is itself a significant philosophical achievement.

This section examines three aspects of this relationship: when physics settles philosophical questions; when philosophical questions go beyond anything physics can settle; and the "two-way street" by which philosophical analysis informs how we interpret physical theories, and physical results constrain and motivate philosophical hypotheses.
