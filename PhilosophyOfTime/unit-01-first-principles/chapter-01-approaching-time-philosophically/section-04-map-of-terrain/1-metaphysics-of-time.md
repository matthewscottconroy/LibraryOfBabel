# 1.4.1 — Metaphysics of Time: Ontology, Topology, Direction

---

The metaphysics of time is the oldest and most central region of the philosophy of time. It asks questions about what time *is* at the most fundamental level — questions that are partly conceptual and partly ontological, and that connect to the broadest issues in general metaphysics.

## The Ontology of Time: What Exists?

The most fundamental metaphysical question about time concerns which times exist. Three main positions have dominated the debate:

*Presentism* holds that only the present moment is real. The past has ceased to exist and the future does not yet exist; only what is happening now is real in the full, unqualified sense. This view has intuitive appeal: when you reach for an object, the past position of the object is gone and its future position does not yet exist; only its current position is there to be reached for.

*Eternalism* (the "block universe" view) holds that past, present, and future are all equally real — they are all "there" in the four-dimensional spacetime manifold, distinguished only by their temporal positions, not by any difference in ontological status. The present is to time what "here" is to space: the position you happen to occupy, not a privileged location in the metaphysical order of things. This view is strongly suggested by the spacetime structure of special relativity.

*The growing block theory*, proposed by C.D. Broad (1923), splits the difference: the past and present are real (they have "grown" into existence), but the future is not yet real. Time grows from a fixed beginning toward an open future.

Each of these positions generates its own philosophical difficulties, which we examine in detail in Unit V. The ontological question connects directly to questions of change, persistence, and identity — for what it means to persist through time depends on what times there are to persist through.

## The Topology of Time: What Structure Does It Have?

Beyond the question of which times exist, there are structural questions about how times relate to one another — questions about the *topology* of time. These include:

*Linearity vs. circularity.* Is time ordered like a line (with a definite before-and-after everywhere) or like a circle (so that events eventually recur)? If time is circular, then every past event is also a future event, which has radical implications for causation and identity.

*Discreteness vs. continuity.* Are there ultimate, indivisible temporal atoms, or can time always be further divided? Quantum gravity suggests that time may be quantized at extremely small scales, but no definitive answer is available.

*Boundedness.* Does time have a beginning, an end, or both? The Big Bang cosmology suggests a beginning; physics makes no decisive prediction about an end. Kant thought the question was antinomial — unanswerable by pure reason.

*Branching.* Can the future branch into multiple distinct trajectories? Some interpretations of quantum mechanics suggest it can. This has implications for both the metaphysics of possibility and for the analysis of free will.

## The Direction of Time: What Grounds the Arrow?

The laws of fundamental physics are (with minor exceptions) time-symmetric: they work equally well run forward or backward. Yet time as we experience it has a clear direction: the past is fixed, the future is open; entropy increases, not decreases; causes precede effects. The question of *what grounds this asymmetry* — what makes one direction of time special — is one of the deepest problems in the field, sitting at the intersection of physics and metaphysics. It is examined in Unit VI.
