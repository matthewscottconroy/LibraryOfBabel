# 1.4.2 — Philosophy of Physics of Time

---

The philosophy of physics of time is, in a sense, the newest region of the field — not because the questions are new, but because the physical theories that frame them are. This region asks how the discoveries and formalism of modern physics bear on the metaphysics, ontology, and phenomenology of time. It requires philosophers to engage seriously with the content of physical theories, and physicists to engage seriously with the conceptual and interpretive issues their theories raise.

## Special Relativity and the Geometry of Time

Special relativity (1905) replaced the Newtonian picture of absolute, universal time with a four-dimensional spacetime in which time and space are interwoven. The philosophical implications are substantial: temporal relations between distant events become frame-relative; the "present moment" loses its claim to objective universality; and the notion of a "block universe" — all events equally real in a static four-dimensional manifold — becomes the most natural interpretation of the theory's formalism.

The central philosophical question raised by special relativity is whether it *requires* the block universe interpretation, or merely *permits* it. Different answers yield different positions on the metaphysics of time. This debate, examined in Chapter 21, is the deepest single intersection of physics and metaphysics in the philosophy of time.

## General Relativity and Dynamical Time

General relativity (1915) makes time dynamical: the rate of time's passage varies with the gravitational field, and the global temporal structure of spacetime depends on the distribution of matter. This raises philosophical questions that go beyond special relativity: what does it mean for time to be "curved"? Can time have exotic topological features — loops, branches? Does general relativity permit the physical possibility of time travel? What does the Big Bang singularity say about whether time had a beginning?

## Thermodynamics and the Arrow

The second law of thermodynamics — entropy increases over time — is the most direct physical expression of time's asymmetry. Yet the microscopic laws are symmetric. Explaining this asymmetry is partly a physical problem (which David Albert 2000 addresses by positing a "Past Hypothesis" about the universe's initial conditions) and partly a philosophical one (whether the Past Hypothesis is explanatory, what explanatory success requires here, how probability enters the picture). This discussion spans Chapters 18 and 23.

## Quantum Mechanics and Quantum Gravity

Quantum mechanics raises distinctive questions about time's role in physics: time is not an observable in standard QM; the measurement problem involves questions about when collapse occurs; some interpretations suggest that temporal structure at the fundamental level is very different from what classical physics assumed. And the search for a quantum theory of gravity — which must reconcile the dynamical time of GR with the parametric time of QM — has led to suggestions that time might not be fundamental at all, but emergent from a more basic non-temporal structure. These are among the most exciting and unsettled questions in contemporary philosophy of physics, examined in Chapters 24 and 25.
