# Chapter 2 Exercises

---

## Comprehension and Analysis

**Exercise 1.** Explain, in your own words, the distinction between the *container view* of time and the *relational view* of time. For each view, give one reason in its favor and one difficulty it faces. Then explain how Newton and Leibniz would differ on the following question: "If all matter in the universe were destroyed, would time continue to pass?"

**Exercise 2.** The folk conception of time includes the claim that the past is fixed and the future is open. Critically assess this claim. What does it mean for the future to be "genuinely open"? Could the openness of the future be merely a feature of our ignorance (we don't know what will happen) rather than a feature of reality (what will happen is not yet settled)? What would be at stake in choosing between these interpretations?

**Exercise 3.** McTaggart distinguished the A-series from the B-series. Consider the sentence "The lecture *was* interesting." Is this an A-series statement or a B-series statement? Justify your answer. Now consider: could this statement be *translated* into a tenseless B-series statement without loss of meaning? What does the token-reflexive analysis say about this? Do you find that analysis convincing?

**Exercise 4.** Summarize the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis in both its strong and weak forms. Explain what Malotki's research on Hopi showed, and what it failed to show. Does the cross-linguistic variation in grammatical tense tell us anything about the *metaphysics* of time, or does it only tell us about the *grammar* of particular languages? Argue for a position.

**Exercise 5.** Kaplan distinguishes the *character* of an expression from its *content*. Explain these two concepts using temporal indexicals as your examples. Why does Kaplan say that "I am here now" is necessarily true? Is this the same kind of necessity as "Water is H₂O" or "2 + 2 = 4"? Explain the difference.

**Exercise 6.** Compare and contrast two of the following temporal frameworks: (a) Hindu cyclical cosmology (kalpas and yugas), (b) Abrahamic linear eschatological time, (c) Aboriginal Australian Dreamtime. For each framework you discuss, identify at least one philosophical question about time that the framework either raises or resolves differently from the default Western linear model.

**Exercise 7.** What is the philosophical significance of the distinction between sidereal time and solar time? How does the existence of these two natural time measures bear on the debate between Newton's absolute time and Leibniz's relational time?

**Exercise 8.** Discuss Lewis Mumford's claim that the mechanical clock — not the steam engine — is the key machine of modernity. Do you agree that the clock has changed not just how we measure time but how we conceptualize it? What philosophical issues does this raise about the relationship between technology and temporal experience?

## Critical Thinking

**Exercise 9.** The folk conception of time includes the belief in an "objective moving present" — a present moment that is objectively distinguished from past and future, and that *moves* through time. Critically assess this belief. What would it mean for the present to be *objectively* distinguished? Is there a coherent version of this view? What is the main challenge it faces (consider the "how fast?" problem)?

**Exercise 10.** A critic argues: "The discussion of non-Western temporal frameworks in this chapter is culturally relative — it implies that there is no fact of the matter about whether time is really linear or really cyclical; it's just a matter of cultural perspective." How would you respond to this objection? Is it possible to take indigenous and non-Western frameworks seriously without collapsing into cultural relativism about time? Write a structured argument for a position.

## Lab Exercises

**Lab 1: Timing Without a Clock.** Go to an outdoor location (a park, a backyard, or a window with a clear view). Without using any clock or phone, estimate: (a) what time it is now (time of day), (b) how much time passes between two chosen events (e.g., the time it takes you to walk a certain distance, or the time between two observations). Then check against a clock. Record your estimates and the actual values. Reflect: what information did you use to make your estimates? What does this reveal about the pre-clockwork resources human beings have for temporal orientation? Write a brief (one page) reflection connecting your observations to the philosophical discussion in Section 4.

**Lab 2: Temporal Indexical Observation.** For one full day, keep a log of every temporal indexical expression you encounter (in conversation, on your phone, in written text): "now," "today," "soon," "yesterday," "later," "recently," "in a moment," etc. Count how many you encounter of each type. Then: (a) Identify three cases where the reference of the indexical would have been ambiguous to someone hearing it out of context. (b) Identify one case where the indexical was used to express something that a tenseless B-series statement could capture fully, and one case where you think it could not. Write a one-page reflection on what the pervasiveness of temporal indexicals in everyday language suggests about human temporal cognition.

**Lab 3: Circadian Rhythm and Temporal Orientation.** For three consecutive days, at the same time each day (e.g., on waking), before checking any clock, write down: (a) your best estimate of what time it is, (b) how rested or tired you feel, (c) how much time you feel has passed since you fell asleep. Then compare your estimates to actual clock times. Are you closer on some days than others? What factors seem to affect your accuracy? This exercise explores the relationship between *felt* time — the temporal experience of consciousness — and *clock* time. Reflect: does your biological sense of time constitute evidence that time is "really" something beyond mere clockwork? Connect your observations to the distinction between lived time and clock time discussed in Section 4.
