# Chapter 2: Further Reading and Bibliography

---

## Annotated Further Reading

**Adam, Barbara. 1990. *Time and Social Theory*. Cambridge: Polity Press.**
Adam provides a wide-ranging sociological and philosophical analysis of time, examining how temporal concepts are embedded in social structures, economic systems, and natural rhythms. Particularly good on the difference between clock-time and the multiple, overlapping temporalities of lived experience. An important corrective to overly physics-focused approaches to time.

**Augustine of Hippo. *Confessions*, Book XI. Translated by Henry Chadwick. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1991.**
Book XI of the *Confessions* is the foundational text for phenomenological approaches to time. Augustine's discussion of the "distension of the mind," of what God was doing before creation, and of the psychological modes of past, present, and future is philosophically original and inexhaustibly rich. Read it slowly.

**Boroditsky, Lera. 2001. "Does Language Shape Thought? Mandarin and English Speakers' Conceptions of Time." *Cognitive Psychology* 43: 1–22.**
One of the best empirical studies of the weak Sapir-Whorf hypothesis. Boroditsky compared how English speakers (who tend to use horizontal spatial metaphors for time: ahead/behind) and Mandarin speakers (who also use vertical metaphors: up/down) conceptualize temporal relations. Her findings suggest that language use can prime and activate particular ways of thinking about time, even if it does not determine them.

**Dahl, Östen. 1985. *Tense and Aspect Systems*. Oxford: Blackwell.**
A systematic typological study of how languages encode tense and aspect. Essential for understanding the cross-linguistic evidence about temporal grammaticalization. Dahl's data show both the considerable variation and the underlying universals in how human languages handle temporal distinctions.

**Eliade, Mircea. 1954. *The Myth of the Eternal Return: Cosmos and History*. Translated by Willard Trask. Princeton: Princeton University Press.**
Eliade's argument that traditional and archaic cultures understand sacred time as periodic return to the founding creative time, not as linear historical succession, remains the most important scholarly treatment of cyclical time. Read critically but read it: the account of "strong" versus "profane" time raises genuine philosophical questions.

**Kaplan, David. 1989. "Demonstratives." In *Themes from Kaplan*, edited by Joseph Almog, John Perry, and Howard Wettstein, 481–563. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
Kaplan's foundational paper on indexicality. Dense but rewarding. The character/content distinction is introduced here, along with the formal semantics of indexical expressions. Chapter 17 of this paper ("Logic of Indexicals") is particularly relevant to temporal indexicals.

**Landes, David S. 1983. *Revolution in Time: Clocks and the Making of the Modern World*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press.**
The definitive scholarly history of timekeeping. Landes traces the mechanical clock from its origins in medieval Europe through the marine chronometer to the quartz and atomic clocks of the twentieth century, and argues throughout that changes in timekeeping technology are inseparable from changes in how people think about time.

**Leibniz, G. W., and Samuel Clarke. 1956. *The Leibniz-Clarke Correspondence*. Edited and translated by H. G. Alexander. Manchester: Manchester University Press.**
The primary source for the Newton-Leibniz debate about absolute versus relational time. The correspondence is accessible, philosophically rigorous, and historically important. It is also a model of philosophical debate: both sides argue carefully, and neither simply talks past the other. Read all five letters.

**Malotki, Ekkehart. 1983. *Hopi Time: A Linguistic Analysis of the Temporal Concepts in the Hopi Language*. Berlin: Mouton.**
The meticulous refutation of Whorf's Hopi claims. An extraordinary piece of sustained linguistic scholarship, though not light reading. Even if you only read the introduction and conclusion, you will come away with a clear sense of why Whorf was wrong and what linguistic evidence about temporal cognition can and cannot establish.

**Mbiti, John S. 1969. *African Religions and Philosophy*. London: Heinemann.**
Mbiti's influential account of traditional African time concepts, organized around the claim that time in many African cultures is primarily event-constituted and oriented toward the past. The work has been contested but remains essential reading for anyone interested in non-Western temporal frameworks. Read alongside Wiredu (below) for critical context.

**Mellor, D. H. 1998. *Real Time II*. London: Routledge.**
The most systematic and rigorous contemporary defense of the B-theory of time. Mellor argues that all tensed facts can be accounted for in terms of tenseless "earlier than" relations, and that the appearance of an objective present is a feature of how temporal experience represents reality, not a feature of reality itself. Demanding but essential.

**Prior, Arthur N. 1967. *Past, Present and Future*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.**
Prior's foundational exposition of tense logic. The first chapter, "Time and Tense," is relatively accessible and provides the best introduction to Prior's reasons for thinking that tensed discourse cannot be eliminated in favor of tenseless discourse.

**Whorf, Benjamin Lee. 1956. *Language, Thought, and Reality: Selected Writings of Benjamin Lee Whorf*. Edited by John B. Carroll. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.**
Whorf's own writings, including the essays on Hopi and on the relation between language and thought that sparked the Sapir-Whorf debate. Essential for understanding what Whorf actually claimed, rather than the sometimes caricatured version in secondary sources.

**Wiredu, Kwasi. 1996. *Cultural Universals and Particulars: An African Perspective*. Bloomington: Indiana University Press.**
A critical and philosophically sophisticated perspective on African philosophy, including a critical reassessment of Mbiti's temporal thesis. Wiredu argues that many claims about distinctively "African" time are overstated and may encode colonial assumptions about African thought as pre-rational. An important corrective that does not abandon the genuine philosophical interest of non-Western temporal frameworks.
