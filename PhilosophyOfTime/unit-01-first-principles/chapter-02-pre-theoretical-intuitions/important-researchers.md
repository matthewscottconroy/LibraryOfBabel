# Chapter 2: Important Researchers and Thinkers

---

**Isaac Newton (1642–1727)**
English mathematician, physicist, and natural philosopher. Newton formulated the doctrine of absolute time in the *Scholium* to the *Principia Mathematica* (1687), arguing that time "flows equably without relation to anything external." His absolute time was theoretically productive for classical mechanics, providing a unique, universal standard against which all motion could be measured. The doctrine was challenged in the eighteenth century by Leibniz and eventually overturned — or at least severely qualified — by Einstein's special theory of relativity in 1905.

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)**
German philosopher and mathematician, co-inventor (with Newton) of calculus. Leibniz articulated the relational theory of time in correspondence with Samuel Clarke (1715–1716), arguing that time consists entirely of relations among events and has no existence independent of them. His arguments turned on the Principle of Sufficient Reason and the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles. Leibniz also contributed fundamental work on the logic of relations and on the nature of substance, both of which bear on the philosophy of time.

**J. M. E. McTaggart (1866–1925)**
British idealist philosopher at Cambridge. His 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time" introduced the distinction between the A-series and the B-series — one of the most influential conceptual contributions to analytic philosophy of time. McTaggart argued that the A-series is essential to genuine time (because only it captures temporal passage) but self-contradictory, and therefore that time is unreal. Although his conclusion is widely rejected, the A/B distinction he introduced remains fundamental.

**Arthur Prior (1914–1969)**
New Zealand logician and philosopher. Prior invented modern tense logic, developing a formal semantic framework for tensed temporal discourse modeled on modal logic. His work, especially *Time and Modality* (1957) and *Past, Present and Future* (1967), gave rigorous formal content to A-theoretic commitments and demonstrated that the dispute between tensed and tenseless temporal discourse could be investigated with precision. Prior was a passionate defender of the philosophical significance of the open future.

**David Kaplan (1933–)**
American philosopher of language at UCLA. Kaplan's work on indexicality, especially his paper "Demonstratives" (1989), provided the standard semantic framework for context-sensitive expressions including temporal indexicals. His distinction between character (the rule for reference relative to context) and content (what is contributed to a proposition in a specific context) is essential for understanding how "now," "then," and other temporal indexicals function, and why they raise special metaphysical issues that purely descriptive terms do not.

**Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897–1941)**
American linguist and insurance investigator (his day job). Whorf's studies of the Hopi language led him to formulate the strong version of linguistic relativity — the claim that language determines thought. His claims about Hopi temporal concepts were enormously influential in mid-twentieth-century linguistics and anthropology. Subsequent research, particularly Malotki's detailed study, showed that Whorf had misanalyzed the Hopi language, but his work stimulated productive debate about the relationship between language and temporal cognition that continues today.

**Ekkehart Malotki (1938–)**
German-born American linguist and Arizona State University professor, specialist in Hopi language and culture. His monumental *Hopi Time: A Linguistic Analysis of the Temporal Concepts in the Hopi Language* (1983) systematically refuted Whorf's claims about Hopi temporality, documenting extensive temporal vocabulary and grammatical resources in Hopi that Whorf had overlooked. Malotki's work stands as a cautionary tale about inferring metaphysical conclusions from inadequate linguistic data.

**David Landes (1924–2013)**
American economic historian at Harvard. His *Revolution in Time: Clocks and the Making of the Modern World* (1983) is the standard history of timekeeping from antiquity to the twentieth century. Landes argued that the mechanical clock was among the most transformative technologies in human history, not simply because it measured time more accurately but because it produced a new conception of time as a homogeneous, quantitative medium susceptible to rational management. Essential reading for understanding the relationship between technology and temporal experience.

**Augustine of Hippo (354–430 CE)**
North African bishop and theologian, author of the *Confessions* and *The City of God*. Augustine's treatment of time in *Confessions* XI is among the most philosophically profound ever written. He argued that time is a "distension of the mind" (*distentio animi*), with past, present, and future understood as modes of psychological orientation rather than external realities. He also developed the most sophisticated early response to the question "what was God doing before creation?" — arguing that the question is malformed, since time itself was created with the universe.

**Mircea Eliade (1907–1986)**
Romanian historian of religions at the University of Chicago. His *The Myth of the Eternal Return* (1954) remains the foundational study of cyclical and primordial time in archaic and traditional cultures. Eliade argued that sacred time in traditional societies is fundamentally different from profane historical time — it involves a periodic return to the founding creative time, not merely a repetition of similar events. His work is essential context for understanding the range of temporal frameworks across human cultures.

**Lewis Mumford (1895–1990)**
American cultural critic and historian of technology. Mumford's *Technics and Civilization* (1934) included an influential analysis of the mechanical clock as the key machine of modernity — not for what it measured but for the cultural transformation it produced, making time into a commodity subject to rational allocation and exchange. His critique of clock-time as an impoverished abstraction from the richness of lived experience anticipates later phenomenological analyses.

**John Mbiti (1931–2019)**
Kenyan philosopher and theologian. His *African Religions and Philosophy* (1969) proposed a distinctive analysis of traditional African time sense, arguing that time in many African cultures is primarily event-constituted rather than abstractly linear, and that the future (as a domain of not-yet-existing events) has little substance. While his specific claims have been contested, Mbiti's work was enormously important in establishing African philosophy of time as a serious academic field and challenging the assumption that Western linear temporality is universal.
