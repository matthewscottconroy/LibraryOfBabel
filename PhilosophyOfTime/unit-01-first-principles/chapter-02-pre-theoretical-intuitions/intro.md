# Chapter 2: Pre-Theoretical Intuitions About Time

---

## Chapter Introduction

Before philosophy gets to work on time, time has already been at work on us. By the time you sit down to think carefully about what time is, you have spent your entire life navigating it — planning, remembering, anticipating, regretting, celebrating anniversaries, missing deadlines, wondering where the afternoon went. You are not a blank slate approaching the question of time from nowhere. You bring with you an enormous, largely tacit, largely unexamined body of temporal knowledge. Call it the folk conception of time, or temporal common sense, or the pre-theoretical picture. Whatever you call it, it is the starting point for philosophy, and philosophy ignores it at its peril.

This chapter takes the pre-theoretical picture seriously — not as something to be immediately discarded in favor of sophisticated theory, but as evidence. Ordinary temporal experience and ordinary temporal talk encode real constraints on any adequate theory of time. If a philosophical theory of time implies that you never actually remember anything, or that your plans never genuinely refer to a future, or that the word "now" is systematically misleading, those implications need to be faced squarely. Sometimes the theory will survive the confrontation. Sometimes the confrontation will reveal that the theory has quietly changed the subject.

We proceed in four stages. Section 1 examines what the folk conception of time actually contains — the common-sense picture of time as linear, one-directional, with a moving present that separates the fixed past from the open future. This is not a simple picture, and pulling it apart reveals tensions that drive much of the philosophical debate in later chapters. Section 2 turns to language. The way human languages encode time is philosophically revealing: tense and aspect, tensed versus tenseless discourse, and the logic of indexical expressions like "now" and "yesterday" all raise questions that intersect with deep metaphysical disputes. Section 3 broadens the picture by examining temporal frameworks in myth, religion, and culture. The assumption that time is linear and unrepeatable turns out to be a historically and culturally specific assumption, not a universal human given. Section 4 considers pre-scientific natural philosophy: the origins of time measurement in astronomy and agriculture, and the transformation of temporal experience wrought by the mechanical clock.

The through-line of the chapter is a single methodological point: understanding what we already think about time is philosophically productive, not just therapeutically interesting. Our pre-theoretical picture is data. It does not determine the outcome of philosophical inquiry, but it does constrain it. A theory that cannot account for our temporal data — that cannot explain why ordinary time-talk is at least approximately right — is a theory that owes us an explanation.
