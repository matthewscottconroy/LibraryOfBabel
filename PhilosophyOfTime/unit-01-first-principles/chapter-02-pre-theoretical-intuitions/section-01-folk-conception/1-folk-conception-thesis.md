# 1.1 The Folk Conception of Time

---

## What Do We Pre-Theoretically Believe About Time?

You have almost certainly never sat down to construct a theory of time from scratch. And yet you have a theory, of sorts. It is implicit, unargued, and largely unconscious — but it guides every temporal judgment you make, every plan you form, every memory you recall. Philosophers call it the *folk conception* of time, and understanding it is the right starting place for serious inquiry.

The folk conception is not a set of propositions someone taught you. It is closer to what Ludwig Wittgenstein called a *form of life* (Wittgenstein 1953: §241) — a background of practices, assumptions, and responses that constitutes ordinary competence in a domain. Just as you do not need a theory of grammar to speak your native language, you do not need a theory of time to navigate temporal reality. The folk conception is what temporal navigation presupposes.

### The Main Features

Let us try to make the implicit explicit. What does the folk conception say about time?

**Time is linear.** Ordinary temporal experience presents time as a single dimension, like a line extending from past through present to future. Events are temporally ordered — some are earlier, some later, some simultaneous — and this ordering is like a total linear order: for any two distinct events, one comes before the other or they occur at the same time. The folk picture knows nothing of branching time or circular time (at the ordinary scale of experience, at least). It takes for granted that there is a single, unified temporal order.

**Time is one-directional.** The line runs one way. You can reverse your direction of travel through space, but not through time. You move inexorably from past to future, not from future to past. This asymmetry is deeply felt: you remember the past but anticipate the future; you regret actions already taken but not actions not-yet-taken; you can affect tomorrow but not yesterday. Common sense treats temporal asymmetry as an obvious, brute feature of reality.

**There is an objective moving present.** Perhaps the most distinctive feature of the folk picture is the *now* — the present moment, felt as uniquely real, as the locus of experience and agency. Moreover, the present does not stay fixed: it *moves*. What was future becomes present, and what was present becomes past. This movement of the now through time is something folk common sense feels as immediately given. It is the "flow" of time that poets describe and that everyone recognizes from the inside (Skow 2015: 1–3).

**The past is fixed; the future is open.** What has happened cannot be changed. This is a deep intuition, not merely an inference. The fixity of the past underwrites our practices of regret, historical record, and moral accountability. By contrast, the future is felt to be genuinely open: not already settled, available for deliberation and choice. Folk temporal thought embeds something like a picture of branching possibilities that narrow to actuality as time advances.

**Time is continuous and divisible.** There is no smallest moment, no "atom" of time below which further division is impossible. Between any two moments there are others; between noon and 12:01 pm lies a dense continuum of moments. This intuition is less vivid than the others — it takes reflection to notice — but it is widely shared, and it rules out naively discrete pictures of time without argument.

### Knowledge-How and Knowledge-That

Possessing the folk conception does not mean being able to articulate it. This is the crucial point. Most people who navigate time perfectly well cannot state, for example, that temporal order is a total linear order, or that the topology of time is isomorphic to the real line. They do not need to know this to live temporally competent lives.

Philosophers of mind distinguish *knowledge-how* — practical skill, the ability to do something — from *knowledge-that* — propositional knowledge, the ability to state that something is the case. Gilbert Ryle, who gave the distinction its canonical philosophical form, argued that knowledge-how is irreducible to knowledge-that (Ryle 1949: 25–61). Riding a bicycle is not the same as knowing propositions about bicycle mechanics. The expert cyclist may know less propositional physics than the physics professor who has never ridden one.

Temporal competence is pre-eminently a matter of knowledge-how. The infant who anticipates feeding, the teenager who coordinates a rendezvous, the surgeon who sequences the steps of a procedure — all exhibit extraordinary temporal knowledge-how that outstrips their ability to articulate what they know. Indeed, when pressed to articulate it, temporal knowledge-how generates the very puzzle that Augustine identified: "If no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to one who asks, I do not know" (Augustine, *Confessions*, XI.14.17).

This gap between knowledge-how and knowledge-that is philosophically important for two reasons. First, it explains why the folk conception is not a theory. It is a practical competence, not a set of doctrines. Second, it explains why unpacking the folk conception requires genuine philosophical work rather than simple introspection. The folk conception does not wear its commitments on its sleeve. You have to excavate them.

### The Methodological Stakes

Why spend time on the folk conception before turning to serious philosophical theories? Because the folk conception serves as a constraint on theory construction. If a philosophical theory of time implies that ordinary temporal language is radically and systematically mistaken — that there is, for example, no genuine distinction between past, present, and future, or that temporal passage is an illusion — then that theory owes us an explanation of why the folk picture is so pervasive, so practically successful, and so phenomenologically compelling.

This is not to say that the folk picture is always right. It may be that some features of it need to be revised in light of careful argument or scientific discovery. Special relativity, for example, gives us strong reason to revise the folk picture's commitment to a *single*, *absolute* present moment shared by all observers (Putnam 1967: 240). But even here, the revisionary theory must engage the folk picture, must explain why the folk picture is a good approximation under ordinary conditions, and must earn the right to depart from it.

Think of the folk conception as a kind of data set: not infallible, not sacrosanct, but genuinely evidential. A theory of time that could not account for the data — that could not explain why we talk, think, plan, and remember as we do — would be a defective theory, not a bold one.

The rest of this section begins the work of examining what the folk conception contains and where, under pressure, it begins to come apart.
