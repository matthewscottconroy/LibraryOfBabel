# 1.2 Time as Container vs. Time as Relation

---

## Two Ways of Picturing Time

Suppose you are standing in an empty room. You know the room exists — it has walls, floor, ceiling — even before any furniture is moved in. The room is a container. It would still be there if it were empty. Now suppose instead that what we call "the room" is just a way of talking about the spatial relations among the walls, floor, and ceiling. On this view, there is no room over and above the surfaces that define it: the room just *is* those relations. No walls, no room.

These two pictures of space correspond to two deep and opposing pictures of time that have been at the center of philosophical debate since antiquity and that find their most precise formulation in the famous dispute between Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz at the opening of the eighteenth century. Understanding the dispute — and, more importantly, feeling the intuitive pull of both sides — is essential preparation for almost everything else in this book.

### The Container View

On the container view, time is a kind of structure — a medium, a vessel — that exists independently of the events that occur within it. Time is the stage on which the drama of the universe is enacted, and the stage would still be there even if the drama never happened. Events have their temporal positions — their dates, their distances from one another — not because they stand in relations to each other but because they each occupy particular *locations in time*, positions in the temporal medium.

Isaac Newton gave this view its most explicit and influential formulation. In the *Scholium* to his *Principia Mathematica*, Newton distinguished absolute time from relative, apparent, or common time. Absolute time, he wrote, "of itself, and from its own nature, flows equably without relation to anything external" (Newton 1729: 6). Relative time, by contrast, is our ordinary measurement of time by reference to clocks and natural processes — an approximation of the real thing. Real time is absolute: it flows uniformly, it is the same everywhere, and it is entirely independent of whether anything exists to measure it or occur within it.

The container view has considerable intuitive appeal. When we ask "how long ago did the dinosaurs die?", we seem to be asking about a real temporal fact: the actual distance between their extinction and the present moment. This question seems to have a determinate answer whether or not anyone measures it. And when two events are simultaneous, they occupy the same moment — not merely the same position in a relational ordering but the same *point in time*. The container view captures the sense that time is out there in the world, as real and as mind-independent as the space the room occupies.

### The Relational View

Leibniz thought Newton's absolute time was philosophical nonsense — not merely empirically unverifiable but conceptually incoherent. His argument, developed in correspondence with Newton's spokesman Samuel Clarke, turned on two great principles: the Principle of Sufficient Reason and the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles (Leibniz 1956: 25–29).

If absolute time exists, Leibniz argued, then God could have created the universe one hour earlier or one hour later than He did while keeping everything else identical. But there could be no reason to prefer one over the other — the two universes would be absolutely indistinguishable. The Principle of Sufficient Reason rules out any difference without a difference-making reason. Therefore there is no fact of the matter about whether the universe began at one absolute moment or another. Therefore absolute time does not exist.

On the relational view, time consists entirely of temporal relations among events: simultaneity, succession, duration measured in terms of intervals between events. There is no temporal container over and above the events themselves. To say that event A occurred "before" event B is not to say anything about their positions in an absolute temporal manifold — it is simply to say that A stands in the relation of *earlier than* to B. Time, on this view, is a way of talking about how events relate to each other, not a thing they are all in.

The relational view also has considerable intuitive appeal. When you ask what time it is, you are asking where you are relative to a network of events — relative to noon, to yesterday's departure, to tomorrow's meeting. You never ask about your absolute position in the temporal container; that would be a strange question. All practical temporal reasoning is relational: what you want to know is how things are positioned relative to each other, not where they sit in some mind-independent medium.

### The Leibniz-Clarke Correspondence and Its Legacy

The Leibniz-Clarke correspondence of 1715–1716 is one of the great documents of the philosophy of time and philosophy of science (Leibniz and Clarke 1956). Clarke defended Newton's absolutism; Leibniz pressed the relational view with remarkable sophistication. Neither side definitively won. Newton had the advantage of physics: absolute time (and space) were theoretically productive in classical mechanics in a way that relational alternatives struggled to match. Leibniz had the advantage of parsimony: he was refusing to posit entities that do no explanatory work.

The debate was transformed, but not resolved, by Einstein's special theory of relativity in 1905. Einstein's theory eliminated absolute simultaneity — the claim that there is a fact of the matter, independent of reference frame, about whether two spatially separated events occur at the same time. This was a decisive victory for a certain kind of anti-absolutism. But general relativity introduced a spacetime that has structural properties of its own — curvature, topology — and this led to neo-Newtonian views arguing that spacetime, though not the classical absolute container, is still a substantive entity rather than a mere logical construction from relational facts (Earman 1989: 12–15).

So the debate continues. In the modern literature, it takes the form of a dispute between *substantivalists* (who hold that spacetime is a genuine entity with its own existence and properties) and *relationalists* (who deny this) (Sklar 1974; Pooley 2013).

### The Folk Picture and the Two Views

Here is an interesting observation: the folk conception of time seems to encode both views simultaneously, in different aspects of temporal thought. When you think of time as "passing" — when you picture the present moment moving through time — you are implicitly using a container metaphor: you are picturing yourself as located in time, moving through it as through a medium. But when you coordinate with others — "meet me at the same time as yesterday's appointment" — you are using relational temporal reasoning, anchored in a network of events and their relations.

This suggests that the folk conception is not a fully coherent theory but a family of intuitions that pull in different directions. The container picture is more natural for thinking about temporal *location* and *passage*; the relational picture is more natural for thinking about temporal *measurement* and *coordination*. A complete theory of time needs to adjudicate between them — or to find a more sophisticated position that can accommodate the legitimate insights of each.
