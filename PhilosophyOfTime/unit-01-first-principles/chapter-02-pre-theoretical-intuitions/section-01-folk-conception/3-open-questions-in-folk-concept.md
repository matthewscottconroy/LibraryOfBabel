# 1.3 Open Questions in the Folk Conception

---

## Where Common Sense Runs Out

The folk conception of time is remarkably good at what it does. It allows us to plan, to remember, to coordinate, to narrate, and to live. But when you press it — when you ask not just *what* it says but *why* what it says is true — it stops giving answers. This is not a defect unique to ordinary people. It is where philosophy begins.

Consider three features of the folk picture that seem obvious until you try to think about them carefully: the fixity of the past, the openness of the future, and the specialness of *now*. Each of these generates a genuine philosophical puzzle that ordinary intuition identifies but cannot resolve.

### The Fixity of the Past

There is something you cannot do, no matter how powerful you are, no matter how advanced your technology, no matter what capabilities some future civilization develops: you cannot change the past. The past is *fixed*, *settled*, *done*. Julius Caesar was assassinated on the Ides of March, 44 BCE. That is not going to change. If you could somehow travel back to Rome on that day, you could perhaps prevent the assassination — but if you did, you would have done so all along, and the historical record would reflect it. You cannot change what *has happened*; you can only change what *will happen*.

This intuition is so deep that it might seem obvious — a mere truism. But when you press it, questions multiply. Why exactly is the past fixed? What makes it different from the future? The obvious answer is temporal direction: the past is earlier, and we cannot go back. But that just relocates the question: why does the direction of time make a difference to fixity? Can the future be genuinely open while the past is settled, if time is a single extended whole? And if the future is genuinely open — if there are real branching possibilities that have not yet resolved — what is the ontological status of those branches? Do they exist in some attenuated sense? Or is "future openness" just a psychological state — our ignorance of what will happen — rather than a feature of time itself?

These questions lead directly into the metaphysics of time's arrow, the debate about fatalism and determinism, and the logic of future contingents — all topics for later in this book. For now, the point is that the folk picture firmly asserts the asymmetry (past: fixed; future: open) without explaining it.

### The Openness of the Future

The folk picture treats the future as genuinely undecided — not merely unknown to us, but not yet settled in reality. This underwrites our practices of deliberation, choice, and planning. When you deliberate about what to do tomorrow, you are not merely calculating what you *will* do (as if it were already determined); you are *deciding* what you *shall* do, as one of several genuinely available options.

But maintaining the openness of the future is philosophically difficult. Consider the principle, traced to Aristotle's *De Interpretatione* (9.18b9–16), that every proposition is either true or false. If it is true or false *now* that you will eat breakfast tomorrow, then it seems like a settled fact. And if it is a settled fact, in what sense is the future open? One response is to deny that future-tensed propositions have a determinate truth value right now — to say "it will be the case that p" is neither true nor false until the relevant time arrives. But this threatens our ordinary reasoning about the future (we do make confident predictions), and it requires a significant departure from classical logic.

The openness of the future is not just a puzzle for specialists. It is embedded in the way all of us think about choice, responsibility, and regret — and working out how to think about it carefully is one of the genuinely important tasks of the philosophy of time.

### The Nowness of Now

Here is the strangest feature of the folk picture: the present moment feels *special*. Not just different from the past and future, but *real* in a way that they are not. You can remember the past; you can anticipate the future. But you cannot *experience* the past or the future — you can only experience the *now*. The present seems to be the locus of reality itself.

Moreover, the present *moves*. As time passes, the present moment changes: what was future becomes present, what was present becomes past. This movement of the now — the *flow* of time — is one of the most vivid features of temporal experience. We feel time passing. Afternoons drag; years disappear. The sense of temporal passage — of *being in* a moving present — is among the most fundamental features of conscious experience (Husserl 1964: 21–35; Merleau-Ponty 1962: 410–418).

But the flow of time is philosophically puzzling in at least two ways. First, if the present moment is *objectively* distinguished — if the world really has a now that stands apart from all other moments — then the temporal ordering of events must include not just "earlier than" and "later than" but also "is present." And this "is present" property seems to change over time: events acquire presentness and then lose it. This is the core of the debate between A-theories and B-theories of time, introduced in the next unit and examined in depth throughout this book (McTaggart 1908: 457–461).

Second, the flow of time raises the question: how fast does time flow? If it flows, it presumably flows at some rate — so many moments per moment? But that seems to be a rate of one moment per moment, which says nothing at all. Or perhaps it flows at a rate relative to some other measure of time — but then we seem to need a second time against which the first is measured, and we are off on a regress (Smart 1949: 484–485; van Inwagen 2015: 77–78).

The folk picture does not resolve this puzzle. It just presents the datum: time seems to flow. What that means, and whether it can be theoretically vindicated, is among the hardest questions in the philosophy of time.

### Where Common Sense Ends

What the folk conception offers, then, is a set of structural features (linearity, directionality, moving present) together with a set of contrasts (past/future, fixed/open, real/unreal). What it does not offer is any explanation of *why* time has these features, or any way of adjudicating between competing theoretical accounts of them.

Consider the question of the present. Common sense is very clear that there *is* a present — that "now" refers to something real. But it has nothing to say about whether the present is a global, objective feature of reality (as pre-relativistic physics assumed) or a perspectival feature of each observer's experience (as relativity suggests). Common sense was formed under circumstances — ordinary human experience, ordinary spatial and temporal scales — where this question never pressed. It is the right starting point, but not the ending point.

Philosophy begins where common sense runs out. The questions opened here — about the fixity of the past, the openness of the future, and the ontological status of now — are the questions that structure the rest of this book. They are genuinely hard. But they are also genuinely important, because they bear on everything from the nature of causation and free will to the meaning of memory and the possibility of hope.
