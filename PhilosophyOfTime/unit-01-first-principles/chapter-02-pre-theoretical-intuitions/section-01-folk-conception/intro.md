# Section 1: The Folk Conception of Time

---

## Section Introduction

Start with what you already know. You wake in the morning knowing that yesterday is over and today is beginning. You remember that the coffee you made an hour ago is cooling, that the meeting you have been dreading is in forty-five minutes, that a year from now you hope to be somewhere different. All of this temporal cognition proceeds without the slightest philosophical effort. It is as natural and automatic as breathing.

But what picture of time does this ordinary competence presuppose? That is the question of this section. The "folk conception" of time is not a theory anyone consciously adopted — it is the background framework that makes ordinary temporal thought and talk possible. Articulating it carefully is harder than it sounds, because the framework operates precisely by not being in the foreground. We see through it, not at it.

Philosophers of mind distinguish between *knowledge-how* — practical, embodied competence at navigating a domain — and *knowledge-that* — propositional knowledge about how things stand. You have extraordinary knowledge-how with respect to time: you coordinate your actions across time with extraordinary precision, you calibrate your expectations to different temporal scales, you sequence your memories and plans. But much of this practical mastery outruns your knowledge-that. You can tell that your friend arrived "just before" you without knowing much about what the phrase "just before" requires metaphysically. This section explores what the folk picture says, where it is indeterminate, and where it points toward genuine philosophical puzzles.

Three subsections carry the analysis. The first examines what the folk conception actually contains: the main structural features of common-sense time and what theoretical commitments they may encode. The second contrasts two fundamental ways of thinking about time — as a container versus as a set of relations — and shows how these map onto the classical Newton-Leibniz dispute. The third locates the open questions: places where ordinary intuition runs out and genuine philosophical work must begin.
