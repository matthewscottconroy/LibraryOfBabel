# 2.1 Grammatical Tense

---

## How Languages Talk About Time

Every time you say "she *went* to the store" rather than "she *goes* to the store," you are encoding temporal information directly into the verb. The difference between *went* and *goes* is not merely one of meaning layered on top of a neutral form — it is baked into the grammar itself. This encoding of temporal information into the morphological structure of verbs is what linguists call *grammatical tense*: the systematic, obligatory marking of the temporal location of an event relative to some reference point, typically the moment of utterance.

Tense is so natural to speakers of English that it feels inevitable. But it is not. Human languages differ dramatically in how — and whether — they grammaticalize time, and attending to this variation tells us something philosophically important about the relationship between language, cognition, and time.

### Tense and Aspect

Before examining the cross-linguistic evidence, we need to distinguish two grammatical categories that are often conflated: *tense* and *aspect*.

*Tense* locates an event in time relative to a reference point. In a simple tense system, past tense places the event before the time of utterance; future tense places it after; present tense places it at or overlapping the time of utterance. This is, at root, a deictic function — it orients the event in a temporal space anchored to the speaker.

*Aspect* does something different. Aspect characterizes the internal temporal structure of an event: whether it is viewed as completed (perfective aspect: "she walked to the store") or as ongoing (imperfective aspect: "she was walking to the store"), whether it is habitual ("she walks to the store every morning") or inceptive ("she began to walk"). Aspect is about how the event is *shaped* in time; tense is about where it *is* in time (Comrie 1985: 1–10; Klein 1994: 3–20).

The distinction matters philosophically because aspect encodes temporal structure in a way that tense does not. An event described in the imperfective is presented as temporally extended, its boundaries not in view; an event described in the perfective is presented as bounded, as a completed whole viewed from the outside. This difference corresponds, at least roughly, to the philosophical distinction between events viewed as processes and events viewed as completed episodes — a distinction that bears on questions about the persistence of objects through time and the individuation of events.

### Cross-Linguistic Variation

Many of the world's languages are tensed in roughly the way English is: verbs obligatorily mark past, present, or future. But the details vary enormously. Many languages distinguish more than three tenses: Hopi (on some analyses) and several African languages distinguish recent past, remote past, and evidential ("I have evidence that") subcategories (Dahl 1985: 101–125). Mandarin Chinese encodes temporal information primarily through aspect markers and temporal adverbs rather than tense morphology — the verb form itself is not obligatorily tensed (Li and Thompson 1981: 184–210). Pirahã, a language of the Brazilian Amazon, has been reported to lack grammatical tense entirely, though this claim is disputed (Everett 2005: 625–626).

What should we make of this variation? Three positions are worth considering.

First, one might argue that tense is a *language universal* — present in all languages, even if encoded differently. On this view, the apparent absence of tense in some languages reflects inadequate description or an unusual mode of encoding, not genuine absence. This is close to the position of universal grammar approaches in the tradition of Chomsky (1995: 170–174), which posit abstract syntactic features of tense even when they are not overtly morphologically realized.

Second, one might argue that tense is *culturally contingent* — a way of organizing temporal experience that some cultures have developed and others have not. On this view, a language without tense would encode temporal information through other means (aspect, adverbs, context), and speakers of such languages might conceptualize time differently.

Third, one might adopt a middle position: grammatical tense is a *preferred* but not *necessary* way of encoding temporal information. All languages can express temporal distinctions, but not all languages obligatorily encode them in verb morphology. The cognitive and metaphysical significance of the choice to grammaticalize tense (versus leaving it to aspect and context) is a matter for empirical research.

### The Hopi Controversy

No discussion of time and language can avoid the case of Hopi. In a series of publications in the 1930s and 1950s, Benjamin Lee Whorf argued that the Hopi language of the American Southwest lacked grammatical tense and that Hopi speakers consequently conceived of time in a fundamentally different, non-linear way (Whorf 1956: 57–64, 216–219). Where English (and Indo-European languages generally) spatialize time — treating it as a line along which events are located — Hopi, Whorf argued, treated temporal distinctions in terms of psychological states and intensities rather than positions along a temporal axis.

Whorf's claims were enormously influential. They became a cornerstone of the *Sapir-Whorf hypothesis* — the strong version of linguistic relativity, the claim that the language you speak shapes or determines the thoughts you can have (Sapir 1929: 209–210; Whorf 1956: 212–214).

But Whorf's Hopi data were challenged forcefully by Ekkehart Malotki's meticulous study of the language (Malotki 1983). Malotki, a trained linguist who had spent years with Hopi speakers, documented extensive temporal vocabulary and grammatical resources in Hopi — temporal adverbs, phrases for yesterday and tomorrow, morphological markers encoding temporal relationships. "Hopi is not a timeless language," Malotki concluded; Whorf had misanalyzed the grammar and overstated his case (Malotki 1983: 2–6, 530–532).

The consensus among linguists today is that Whorf was substantially wrong about Hopi: the language does have resources for encoding temporal distinctions, even if they differ from English (Pinker 1994: 59–67; Gumperz and Levinson 1996: 22–27). The strong Sapir-Whorf hypothesis — the claim that language determines thought — has very few defenders among linguists. The weaker hypothesis — that language can *influence* or *facilitate* certain ways of thinking — has more support, and remains an active area of research in cognitive linguistics (Boroditsky 2001: 1–22; Levinson 2003: 97–121).

### Philosophical Implications

What does the cross-linguistic evidence tell us philosophically? At minimum, it tells us that the *grammaticalization* of tense — its encoding in verb morphology — is not a universal feature of human language. Languages can convey temporal information through a variety of means, and the particular strategy a language adopts is, at least partly, a historical and cultural contingency.

This does not establish that temporal concepts themselves are culturally contingent. Even a language without grammatical tense will have ways of talking about temporal relations and temporal location. The Whorfian dream of a language whose speakers cannot distinguish before and after, earlier and later, past and future, has not been vindicated. The temporal distinctions that seem most fundamental — sequence, duration, the contrast between now and not-now — appear to be cross-linguistically universal in the weak sense that every language has resources for expressing them.

But this leaves open a more subtle question: does the *way* a language encodes time influence the *way* its speakers experience or conceptualize time? Not in the strong Whorfian sense, perhaps. But the weaker hypothesis — that obligatory grammatical tense encourages a particular way of orienting experience toward a present moment, while aspect-dominant languages might encourage a different emphasis — is not obviously false, and it is being actively investigated (Boroditsky 2001; Winawer et al. 2007).

For the philosophy of time, the key point is this: the virtually universal human capacity to distinguish past from present from future, to sequence events in time, and to orient oneself temporally suggests that temporal cognition is a deep feature of human minds, not merely a product of linguistic convention. But the variation in *how* languages encode this capacity is a reminder that the particular temporal concepts of English-speaking, Western-educated philosophers are not the only options available.
