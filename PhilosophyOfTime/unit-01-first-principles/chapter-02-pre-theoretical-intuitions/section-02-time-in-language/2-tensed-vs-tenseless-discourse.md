# 2.2 Tensed vs. Tenseless Discourse

---

## Two Kinds of Temporal Statement

Here are two temporal statements. Consider them carefully before reading on:

(A) "The Battle of Thermopylae is occurring now."
(B) "The Battle of Thermopylae is earlier than the Battle of Actium."

Both sentences contain the present tense verb "is." But they are doing very different things. Statement (A) was true for a few days in August 480 BCE and has been false ever since. If I say it today, I am saying something false. Statement (B), by contrast, is true whenever it is said — in 480 BCE, in 31 BCE, today, ten thousand years from now. It does not locate anything relative to a moving present. It merely asserts a fixed temporal relation between two events.

This distinction — between tensed statements (whose truth value changes over time) and tenseless statements (whose truth value is permanent) — is one of the most philosophically important distinctions in the philosophy of time. It maps onto, and partly constitutes, the great debate between A-theories and B-theories of time.

### The A-Series and the B-Series

The distinction between tensed and tenseless temporal facts was given its canonical philosophical formulation by J. M. E. McTaggart in a 1908 paper that remains essential reading in the field (McTaggart 1908). McTaggart observed that there are two ways of ordering the events in time.

The *B-series* orders events by the fixed, permanent relations of *earlier than*, *later than*, and *simultaneous with*. These relations hold permanently: if the Battle of Marathon is earlier than the Battle of Thermopylae, it is *always* earlier than it. The B-series is the series of events ordered by these permanent, two-place, tenseless relations.

The *A-series* orders events by the *dynamic*, *one-place* properties of being *past*, being *present*, and being *future*. These properties are not permanent: what is now future will become present, and what is now present will become past. The A-series is the series of events ordered by these flowing, tensed properties.

McTaggart argued — somewhat contentiously — that the A-series is *more fundamental* than the B-series, because temporal *passage* requires the A-series. A world in which events are merely ordered by permanent "earlier than" relations has no genuine *flow* of time: it is a static ordering, like a line of people standing in a queue. For time to genuinely *pass*, the A-series must be real. But McTaggart also argued that the A-series is self-contradictory, and therefore concluded that time itself is ultimately unreal (McTaggart 1908: 457–474). We will assess that argument in detail later in the book (Unit V).

For present purposes, what matters is the distinction itself. *A-theorists* hold that the A-series is real: events genuinely are past, present, or future, and the present moment is objectively distinguished from all others. *B-theorists* hold that only the B-series is real: the fundamental temporal facts are the permanent "earlier than" relations, and the appearance of a moving present is, in some sense, a perspectival or psychological phenomenon rather than a feature of objective reality (Smart 1963; Mellor 1998).

### Tensed Discourse and Its Semantics

Tensed statements — "the battle is happening," "the battle happened," "the battle will happen" — are paradigmatically A-series statements. They are true relative to a moment (the moment of utterance) and change their truth value as that moment changes. This makes their semantics interesting.

Arthur Prior, the founder of modern tense logic, developed a formal semantic framework for tensed discourse in the 1950s and 1960s (Prior 1957; 1967). Prior treated temporal operators as analogous to modal operators. Just as in modal logic we have operators for "necessarily" (□) and "possibly" (◇), Prior introduced operators for "it was the case that" (*P*) and "it will be the case that" (*F*). On this approach, "P(φ)" is true at time *t* if and only if φ is true at some time earlier than *t*; "F(φ)" is true at *t* if and only if φ is true at some time later than *t*.

Prior's tense logic was not merely a formal toy. It represented a substantive philosophical commitment: that the semantics of temporal discourse requires reference to an *objective present* — a privileged moment relative to which "was" and "will be" are evaluated. Prior was, in this sense, a committed A-theorist, and he argued passionately that B-theorists who tried to eliminate tensed discourse in favor of tenseless representations were falsifying the genuine temporal structure of reality (Prior 1967: 1–15; Prior 1972).

### Tenseless Discourse and the B-Theory Response

B-theorists have offered a different semantic treatment of tensed statements. The key move is the *token-reflexive analysis*, developed in various forms by Bertrand Russell, Hans Reichenbach, and D. H. Mellor (Russell 1940: 102–108; Reichenbach 1947: 284–286; Mellor 1998: 17–32).

On the token-reflexive analysis, a tensed statement like "the meeting is happening now" does not refer to an objective present moment. Instead, it means something like: "the meeting is occurring at the same time as this utterance." The statement is not made true by the objective presentness of the meeting; it is made true by the relation between the meeting and the particular act of utterance — the *token* — in which the statement is produced.

This analysis eliminates the need for objective A-series properties. What seemed like a claim about objective presentness turns out to be a claim about the temporal relation between an event and an utterance — and that relation is a perfectly ordinary B-series relation (simultaneity). The token-reflexive analysis thus offers B-theorists a way of accounting for the phenomenon of tensed discourse without conceding the A-theorist's claim that there is an objective, moving present.

The debate between these two approaches is one of the central debates in analytic philosophy of time, and we will return to it repeatedly. At stake is not just a question of semantics but a question about the fundamental nature of temporal reality: is "now" a perspectival feature of how conscious beings relate to a temporally extended world, or is it an objective feature of the world itself?

### The Significance of the Distinction

Notice what is at stake in the tensed/tenseless distinction. If tenseless discourse is fundamental — if all temporal facts can ultimately be expressed in terms of permanent "earlier than" relations without any essential reference to a present moment — then the world, as described by physics, is a four-dimensional block of events, none of which is objectively present, past, or future. The sense of a moving now would be a kind of cognitive illusion, a perspectival artifact of conscious experience rather than a feature of objective reality.

If tensed discourse is fundamental — if there is no way to eliminate the essentially perspectival, present-oriented character of temporal talk — then the world has a genuine "now" built into it, and the A-series is as real as the B-series. The flow of time is not an illusion but a feature of the world's own structure.

This is not a merely verbal question. It matters to how we understand agency, consciousness, and the asymmetry between past and future. It matters to whether our practices of hoping, regretting, and planning are tracking something real about the difference between the future and the past. Getting this right is one of the central tasks of the philosophy of time.
