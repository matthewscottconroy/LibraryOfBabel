# 2.3 Temporal Indexicals: "Now," "Then," "Yesterday," "Soon"

---

## The Peculiar Logic of Context-Dependent Terms

"I am here now." This sentence, David Kaplan famously observed, is necessarily true: any utterance of it will be true (Kaplan 1989: 509). The speaker of the sentence is, by definition, wherever they are speaking from, at the moment they are speaking. And yet the sentence tells us nothing specific. It does not identify any particular person, place, or time. Its necessity is somehow empty — a formal property of how the words refer, not a deep metaphysical truth.

*Indexical expressions* are words and phrases whose reference shifts systematically with features of the context in which they are used. "I" refers to whoever is speaking; "here" refers to wherever the speaker is located; "this" refers to whatever object the speaker is indicating. These are *context-sensitive* terms: their semantic value is not fixed by meaning alone but by meaning together with context.

Temporal indexicals work in the same way. "Now" refers to the time of utterance; "yesterday" refers to the day preceding the day of utterance; "soon" picks out a time in the near future relative to the utterance time; "then" refers to some contextually salient time that is not the time of utterance. Understanding how these terms work — and why they raise special puzzles that purely spatial indexicals do not — is philosophically illuminating.

### Kaplan's Framework

The most influential theoretical treatment of indexicals is David Kaplan's (1989). Kaplan introduced a systematic distinction between two kinds of meaning: *character* and *content*.

The *character* of an expression is a rule that, given a context, determines what the expression refers to in that context. The character of "I" is the rule: "refers to the agent of the context." The character of "now" is: "refers to the time of the context." Characters are constant across contexts — the rule never changes.

The *content* of an expression, by contrast, is what it contributes to the proposition expressed by a sentence in a particular context. When I say "now" at noon on May 20, 2026, the content of "now" is the time 12:00 PM, May 20, 2026. Content varies across contexts.

This two-level semantics allows Kaplan to explain several interesting features of indexicals. It explains why "I am here now" is true in every context (its character guarantees this), yet not necessarily true in the sense that might be metaphysically significant (the proposition it expresses is a contingent one). It also explains why indexical sentences cannot simply be replaced by their non-indexical equivalents without loss. "The lecture is happening now" and "The lecture is happening at 2:00 PM on May 20, 2026" express different propositions even if they are simultaneously uttered at 2:00 PM on May 20, 2026. The first is context-sensitive; the second is not (Kaplan 1989: 493–501).

### "Now" vs. "Here": An Asymmetry

Spatial and temporal indexicals behave similarly in many ways. But there is a philosophical asymmetry between "now" and "here" that has attracted significant attention (Perry 1979; Sider 2001: 11–13).

Consider the spatial indexical "here." My current location — wherever I happen to be — is not metaphysically special. There is nothing philosophically distinctive about the place I happen to occupy: every place is on an ontological par. The specialness of "here" is purely perspectival: it is special to me, in my situation, but there is no sense in which my current location is *objectively* special or privileged.

Now consider "now." According to the folk conception — and, as we saw in the previous subsection, according to A-theorists — the present moment *is* objectively special. It is not merely the moment I happen to occupy; it is the moment that *is real*, the locus of existence, the place where the becoming is happening. On the A-theory, "now" picks out a genuinely privileged moment in the history of the universe in a way that "here" does not pick out a privileged place.

If the A-theory is right, "now" is not merely an indexical like "here." It refers to a property — *presentness* — that is distributed unevenly across time, present at some times and absent at others. The reference of "now" tracks this distribution. But "here" does not pick out anything analogous: there is no spatial analogue of presentness that distinguishes my current location objectively from all others.

On the B-theory, by contrast, the temporal case collapses back to the spatial case: "now" is just like "here," an indexical that picks out a time perspectivally, without any objective privilege. The asymmetry between temporal and spatial indexicals that the folk picture encodes is, on the B-theory, merely psychological — a feature of how temporally bound consciousness relates to a symmetrical temporal reality (Mellor 1998: 34–38).

This is a philosophically live dispute, and the linguistics of temporal indexicals is directly implicated in it.

### The Semantics of "Yesterday" and Temporal Adverbs

While "now" raises the deepest metaphysical questions, the broader family of temporal indexicals is philosophically interesting for other reasons.

"Yesterday" does not merely pick out a time relative to the utterance; it picks out a *day* — a structured period, not an instant. This temporal granularity is philosophically interesting: temporal indexicals do not simply refer to points on a continuum but to temporally structured units (days, weeks, seasons) that are culturally and pragmatically significant. The structure of temporal indexical reference reflects the structure of human temporal experience, which is organized by the rhythms of natural cycles (light and dark, seasonal change) and social institutions (work weeks, fiscal years).

"Soon" and "recently" are *vague* temporal indexicals: they pick out a range of times without sharp boundaries, and the range shifts with context. "I'll be there soon" means something different in the context of a five-minute walk than in the context of an interstellar journey. This vagueness is not a defect; it is a practical resource. Human temporal planning and communication operate at many scales, and context-sensitive temporal vocabulary allows speakers to calibrate their claims appropriately without specifying exact quantities.

John Perry's work on indexicals and self-locating beliefs adds another dimension to this picture (Perry 1979). Perry argued that indexical beliefs — beliefs expressed using "I," "here," "now" — are irreducibly first-personal: they cannot be captured by any description that does not essentially refer to the agent's current context. A person who knows all the objective (B-series) facts about the world may still wonder, "Is *now* the time at which I have an appointment?" — and this question is not answered by any B-series fact. The temporal case generalizes this to "When is *now*?" — a question that presupposes a kind of self-location in time that no purely objective temporal description captures (Perry 1979: 3–21; also see Lewis 1979 on centered worlds).

### What Temporal Indexicals Reveal

The linguistic study of temporal indexicals reveals two things of philosophical importance.

First, temporal language is essentially perspectival. Even if the world, in itself, is a B-series — a four-dimensional array of events ordered by permanent "earlier than" relations — temporal *discourse* is oriented toward a present. We speak from a now, looking backward to a past and forward to a future. This orientation is not merely optional or conventional; it is built into the grammar and semantics of natural language.

Second, the question of whether this orientation is a *cognitive fact* about speakers or a *metaphysical fact* about time itself is not settled by the linguistic data alone. The data are compatible with the A-theory (the orientation tracks objective presentness) and with the B-theory (the orientation reflects the perspectival character of context-sensitive reference). Linguistics sets the puzzle; philosophy must work to solve it.
