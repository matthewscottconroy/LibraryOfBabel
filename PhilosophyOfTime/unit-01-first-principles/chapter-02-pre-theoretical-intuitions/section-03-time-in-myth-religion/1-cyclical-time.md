# 3.1 Cyclical Time

---

## Time That Comes Back Around

Imagine that the year 2026 is not merely similar to 2025, not merely the same type of year with the same kind of seasons and rhythms, but *literally the same year* — that history is not a line extending forward from a past that recedes and a future that approaches, but a great wheel, and that the events of this year are destined to recur, exactly as they have occurred, through an infinity of revolutions. This is the idea of cyclical time: not merely that nature is periodic, but that history itself returns.

The idea strikes most Western readers today as strange, even absurd. But it was held, in various forms, by many of the most sophisticated thinkers of the ancient world. Understanding why it was taken seriously — and what philosophical questions it forces into the open — is an important part of understanding the philosophy of time.

### Hindu Cosmological Time

The most elaborately developed cyclical temporal cosmology is found in Hindu philosophical thought, particularly in the Puranas and in the speculative cosmology of the Mahabharata. The basic unit of cosmic time is the *kalpa*, a day of Brahma lasting 4.32 billion years. Each kalpa is divided into fourteen *manvantaras* (periods of Manu), each of which is itself divided into seventy-one *mahayugas*, great cycles of four ages (Lochtefeld 2002: 343–344).

Each *mahayuga* consists of four *yugas* of declining virtue and increasing suffering: the Satya Yuga (the golden age of perfection), the Treta Yuga, the Dvapara Yuga, and the Kali Yuga (the current age, characterized by conflict and spiritual decline). At the end of the Kali Yuga, the cosmos is destroyed and the cycle begins again. One kalpa is a day of Brahma; the god's night is an equal period of dissolution. The complete lifespan of Brahma — 100 divine years — is itself followed by a period of total dissolution before a new Brahma appears (Eliade 1954: 113–115).

The scale of Hindu cosmological time is staggering, and it was philosophically productive: it allowed Hindu thinkers to take the immensity of geological and cosmic time seriously long before Western science had the tools to do so. But the metaphysical significance of the cyclical structure goes beyond scale.

In a cyclical cosmology, the question of ultimate origins does not arise in the way it does in a linear cosmology. There is no first moment, no initial creation ex nihilo. Every beginning is a re-beginning, every creation a re-creation. The problem of what existed "before" time started — which is a genuine puzzle for linear cosmologies with a first moment — simply does not arise.

### Ancient Greek Eternal Return

The idea of temporal cycles also appears in ancient Greek thought, though in a more philosophical and less mythological guise. The Stoics held that the universe passes through a series of great cycles, each beginning with the emergence of the cosmos from fire (ekpyrosis), passing through the familiar history of the world, and ending with the conflagration (ekpyrosis again) in which all things are dissolved back into fire, only to re-emerge in exactly the same form (Long and Sedley 1987: 274–276).

The Stoic eternal recurrence is not merely that similar events will occur again; it is that the *very same* events will occur, because the cosmic cycle is governed by the same rational principle (the *logos*) throughout. Socrates will drink hemlock again, in exactly the same circumstances, in the next cycle. The cycle is a literal repetition, not merely an analogy.

Friedrich Nietzsche revived and radicalized this idea in the nineteenth century under the name of the *Eternal Return of the Same* (Nietzsche 1974: §341). For Nietzsche, the thought that everything will recur eternally is a test of one's relationship to life: can you live in such a way that you would will the eternal recurrence of all your choices? The idea is less a cosmological claim than a psychological and ethical one — but it draws on the ancient philosophical picture.

### Mesoamerican Calendar Systems

The Aztec calendar system illustrates a different form of cyclical time. The Aztec calendar interleaved two cycles: a 260-day ritual calendar (*tonalpohualli*) and a 365-day solar calendar (*xiuhpohualli*). Every 52 years, the two cycles aligned, producing what is sometimes called the Calendar Round — a period after which the same combination of day-name and year-name recurred (Clendinnen 1991: 21–24).

The end of each 52-year cycle was a time of collective anxiety among the Aztecs: would the sun rise again? Elaborate ritual (the New Fire ceremony) was performed to ensure the continuation of the cosmic order into the next cycle. Time was not a neutral background medium; it was a charged, dangerous, and potentially finite resource. The cycles of the calendar were the joints of a temporal structure that could fail.

The Maya possessed an even more sophisticated calendrical system, including the Long Count that measures time in units extending to millions of years — a system sufficiently precise to predict astronomical events with remarkable accuracy. The famous "Maya 2012 apocalypse" misrepresented a genuine feature of the Maya temporal framework: the completion of a 5,125-year cycle (*b'ak'tun*), not a predicted end of the world, but a transition between cosmic periods (Coe 1994: 63–67).

### Philosophical Implications of Cyclical Time

What philosophical questions does cyclical time raise?

**Personal identity across recurrence.** If history literally repeats, in what sense is the Socrates of the next cycle *the same person* as Socrates in this one? He has the same properties, the same experiences, the same history — but no continuous causal connection to his "previous" instantiation. This is a hard question for the standard accounts of personal identity. Psychological continuity requires memory, and there is no memory across cycle-boundaries. Physical continuity requires an unbroken causal chain, and the conflagration breaks all such chains. Perhaps the cycles produce type-identical but numerically distinct individuals — but then Nietzsche's "eternal return of the *same*" becomes the eternal return of the *similar*, which is far less philosophically provocative.

**Freedom and fatalism.** If every event that occurs in this cycle has occurred in exactly the same form in infinitely many previous cycles and will occur in infinitely many future cycles, then the notion of genuine individual choice seems strained. What was there to choose, if your "choice" has been fixed through all the cycles? Linear time, by contrast, makes room for novelty, genuine alternatives, and historical uniqueness. The connection between linear time and meaningful freedom is not coincidental.

**The problem of temporal origin.** One powerful argument for cyclical time is that it avoids the cosmological regress: what happened before the first moment? In a linear time with a genuine beginning, this question has no answer (or requires appeal to a timeless cause). Cyclical time dissolves the question by eliminating the first moment.

**The measure of cosmic significance.** In a cyclical cosmos, no event is historically unique. What happens, happens again and again. In a linear cosmos with a beginning and an end, events can be singular, unrepeatable, and therefore (in a sense) supremely significant. The monotheistic sense that history is *going somewhere* — toward redemption, toward consummation — depends on the linear picture. Cyclical time, by contrast, embeds events in a structure that minimizes the significance of any particular moment.

These implications are genuine and deep. The choice between cyclical and linear temporal frameworks is not merely a question of cultural preference; it is a question about the structure of reality that has consequences for identity, freedom, and meaning.
