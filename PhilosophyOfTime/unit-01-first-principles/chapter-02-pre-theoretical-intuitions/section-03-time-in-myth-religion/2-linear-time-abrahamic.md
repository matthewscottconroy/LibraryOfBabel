# 3.2 Linear Time and the Abrahamic Traditions

---

## Creation, Redemption, and the End of History

The idea that time has a unique beginning, a directional middle, and a final end — that history is an irreversible narrative with a plot and a terminus — is not a neutral or universal assumption. It is a deeply specific theological and cultural inheritance that Western philosophy has absorbed, often without acknowledgment, from its Jewish, Christian, and Islamic background. Understanding this inheritance is essential for understanding why the Western philosophy of time has the shape it does: its preoccupation with creation ex nihilo, its treatment of temporal passage as morally and metaphysically significant, and its assumption that the question of time's ultimate structure has a single determinate answer.

### Creation Ex Nihilo

The opening words of Genesis — *Bereshit bara Elohim* ("In the beginning, God created") — are among the most philosophically charged words ever written. They assert that there was a beginning: a first moment before which nothing existed except God. The universe was created at a specific time, by a divine act, and has been running ever since.

This doctrine of *creatio ex nihilo* — creation out of nothing — poses immediate philosophical challenges. What was God doing before creation? Augustine's answer — that there was no "before," because time itself was created with the universe — is philosophically sophisticated and, as we shall see, anticipates aspects of modern cosmology (Augustine, *Confessions*, XI.13.16; XI.30.40). Time is not a container in which God existed waiting to create; time is itself part of the created order.

The philosophical implications are significant. If time has a genuine beginning — a first moment with no prior moment — then temporal experience has a directionality that is not merely conventional. Time runs from creation forward. Events are historically unique: they occur once, not as part of an infinite cycle. And the universe as a whole has a narrative shape: it is going somewhere, not merely cycling.

### Eschatology and Directed History

Jewish, Christian, and Islamic accounts of time are not merely cosmological (about how time began) but eschatological (about how time will end and what it all means). The Hebrew prophetic tradition conceived history as a series of divine interventions — covenant, exile, return — oriented toward a messianic future. Christian theology added the belief that the decisive event of history — the Incarnation and Resurrection — had already occurred, dividing time into a pre-messianic before and a post-messianic after (Cullmann 1962: 17–23, 63–66). Islamic thought placed the Quran and the career of the Prophet at the culminating moment of prophetic history, with the Day of Judgment as its end point (Izutsu 1964: 129–137).

What all three traditions share is the sense that time is *directed* — it has a telos, an end toward which it moves. Events in history are not mere occurrences but *meaningful episodes* in a narrative that is moving toward a goal. This conception of meaningful, directed time is one of the most powerful ideas in Western culture. It shapes the way Western historians write history (as progress, or decline, or redemption), the way Western ethics understands moral responsibility (as tied to a temporally extended narrative of a life), and even the way Western science frames the question of cosmological evolution.

### The Philosophical Legacy

Augustine's treatment of time in *Confessions* XI is the most philosophically profound confrontation between the Abrahamic conception of linear created time and the Greek philosophical tradition (Augustine, *Confessions*, XI.4–31). Augustine is acutely aware of the problem: if God exists outside time, and created time with the universe, then questions about "what came before" or "why did God wait so long" are malformed — they presuppose a temporal framework that applies to the created order but not to God.

This leads Augustine to a genuinely original philosophical position: time is not a feature of the external world but a "distension of the mind" (*distentio animi*). The past exists as memory, the future as anticipation, and the present as attention — all three as psychological modes of a consciousness that is stretched, distended, across the span of its experience (Augustine, *Confessions*, XI.26.33–XI.28.38). This is one of the earliest and most sophisticated phenomenological accounts of time, anticipating Husserl by fifteen centuries.

The Abrahamic linear-temporal framework also bequeathed to Western philosophy a distinctive set of *problems* that Greek philosophy had not posed in the same way:

- The problem of the first moment: Can time have a genuine beginning without an infinite temporal regress?
- The problem of eternity: Is God's existence simply very long (sempiternally), or is it genuinely outside time (timelessly)?
- The problem of divine foreknowledge: If God knows the future, is the future fixed? Does this threaten free will?
- The problem of eschatological time: What happens to time at the Last Day? Does time end, or does it transform into eternity?

These problems — all generated by the intersection of Abrahamic theology with Greek philosophy — set the agenda for much of medieval philosophy and continue to reverberate in contemporary discussions. Boethius's treatment of divine eternity in the *Consolation of Philosophy* (Boethius, *Consolatio* V.6, trans. Relihan), Aquinas's discussion of God's sempiternity versus timelessness (*Summa Theologica* I.10.1–4), and Plantinga's and Stump and Kretzmann's modern treatments all trace back to this fundamental collision (Stump and Kretzmann 1981; Leftow 1991).

### The Legacy for Secular Philosophy

Even in the post-theological context of modern secular philosophy, the linear Abrahamic framework has left deep traces. The idea of historical *progress* — that events in human history form a narrative moving toward a better condition — is a secularized form of eschatological thinking, visible in Kant's philosophy of history, Hegel's dialectic, Marx's historical materialism, and the liberal theory of democratic development (Löwith 1949: 1–20). The very idea of philosophical *modernity* — a decisive break with the past — is a secularized application of the theological idea of a turning point in sacred history.

Understanding that Western philosophy's dominant temporal framework is rooted in specific theological commitments is not to say that the framework is wrong. Linear time may well be closer to the truth than cyclical time. But it does mean that the framework is not self-evidently universal — it needs to be argued for, not assumed — and that confronting genuinely different temporal frameworks, as the next subsection does, is not merely an exercise in multicultural awareness but genuine philosophical work.
