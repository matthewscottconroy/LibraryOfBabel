# 3.3 Indigenous and Non-Western Temporal Frameworks

---

## Beyond the Western Default

Philosophy of time, as it is taught in Western universities, proceeds largely within a Greek-Christian framework. Time is linear (or is argued to be). It has a direction (or is argued to have one). The present moment is unique (or is argued to be). These are taken to be the default positions from which inquiry begins. Alternative views are considered as departures from the default — exotic positions that need special motivation.

But this framing is culturally specific, not universal. Different cultures and intellectual traditions have developed temporal frameworks that are genuinely distinct from the Western linear model — not because they are primitive approximations to a truth the West has discovered, but because they are exploring different aspects of temporal experience, different features of time that are genuinely present but tend to be suppressed or ignored within Western conceptual frameworks. Surveying several such frameworks briefly is philosophically important, even if our treatment here must necessarily be introductory.

A word of caution is in order. These are living traditions, and summarizing them briefly risks distortion, reduction, and appropriation. The goal here is philosophical alertness — an expansion of the range of options under consideration — not ethnographic comprehensiveness. Readers are encouraged to follow the further reading to encounter these frameworks in their full complexity.

### Aboriginal Australian Dreamtime

Among Aboriginal Australian peoples, the temporal framework most fundamental to cultural and spiritual life is often translated into English as the "Dreamtime" or the "Dreaming" — though these English terms are acknowledged to be inadequate approximations (Swain 1993: 17–22). The Aranda term *Altyerre* (and equivalent terms in other languages) refers to a founding creative period — a time of immense formative power — that is not simply past, not simply a historical epoch that has concluded and receded.

This is the crucial philosophical point: the Dreaming is not experienced as past in the Western sense. It is not over; it is *continuously present*. The ancestral beings who shaped the landscape, created species, and established the patterns of human life are not merely historical figures who existed in a remote past. They are ongoing presences whose formative power is still active in the land, in ritual, in social structure, and in the relationship between human beings and country (Strehlow 1947: 15–17; Rose 1992: 38–42).

This gives rise to a temporal ontology that is fundamentally different from the Western linear model. In Western linear time, past, present, and future are mutually exclusive: what is past is no longer present. But in the Aboriginal framework, the founding time is simultaneously original and contemporary — it *underlies* the present rather than preceding it in a linear sequence. The landscape itself is a temporal document, not a record of past events but an ongoing expression of the Dreaming's continuous creative power.

Philosophers have noted interesting parallels between this framework and certain strands of Western philosophy of time. Mircea Eliade argued that many archaic cultures treat sacred time as a periodic recovery of the founding time — a return to the beginning that does not abolish but regenerates the present (Eliade 1954: 34–37). David Bohm's distinction between the *explicate order* (the unfolded, sequential temporal world of everyday experience) and the *implicate order* (the enfolded, timeless ground from which explicate events unfold) has been noted as bearing structural similarities to the Aboriginal framework, though such comparisons require care (Bohm 1980: 172–177).

### Lakota Temporal Concepts

Among the Lakota peoples of North America, time is understood in close relation to the structure of community, kinship, and the natural world. The Lakota word *wicasa* (man, person) implies a relational identity — a person is constituted by their relations to family, to land, to the community of the living and the dead — and this relational constitution extends across time (Deloria 1999: 45–52; Vine Deloria Jr., *Spirit and Reason*, 1999).

Past and future are not simply absent in Lakota thought, but their significance is not primarily governed by linear succession. Ancestral figures are experienced as present in ceremony, in the landscape, in dreams and visions; they are not merely remembered but encountered (Powers 1977: 57–62). The ceremonial cycle embeds temporal experience in natural rhythm — the Lakota calendar is organized by the cycles of the moon, the migrations of animals, and the passage of the seasons — rather than in a linear historical narrative with a beginning and end.

What is philosophically interesting about this framework is its emphasis on *relational* time. Time is not an abstract container in which individuals are located; it is a medium of relationship — between the living and the dead, between human beings and the rest of the natural world, between individuals and the community across generations. This relational ontology of time resonates with aspects of process philosophy (Whitehead 1929), where temporal reality is fundamentally constituted by relations among events rather than by substances persisting through time.

### Ubuntu and Relational Time in African Philosophy

The concept of *Ubuntu*, widely developed in Southern African philosophical traditions, is often glossed with the phrase "I am because we are" (Ramose 1999: 49–54; Shutte 1993: 46–50). Ubuntu is, at root, an ontology of personhood: one is not a self-contained individual who happens to enter into relations with others, but a relational being whose very existence is constituted by community and by what one has received from others.

Temporally, Ubuntu implies a conception of time as fundamentally *communal*. The past is not merely over; it persists in the community as a living inheritance of values, practices, and relationships. The ancestors are not simply dead; they are active participants in the community's life, addressed in ritual, consulted in decision-making, honored in ceremony (Mbiti 1969: 20–25). And the future is not something toward which individuals project themselves autonomously; it is a horizon of communal becoming in which individual destinies are bound up with communal ones.

John Mbiti's distinction between what he calls the "African time sense" and the Western linear model has been influential, though also contested. Mbiti argued that traditional African time is primarily constituted by *events* rather than abstract temporal units — that time is "created" or "made" by events, and that the future in the Western sense (the domain of not-yet-existing events) has little substance in African temporal thought (Mbiti 1969: 16–28). Critics have argued that Mbiti overstates the case and may have been unconsciously imposing a colonial framework that renders African thought as temporally deficient rather than as expressing a different set of priorities (Deng 1971; Wiredu 1996: 39–41).

The philosophical point, stripped of the contested ethnographic claims, is this: a conception of time organized primarily around events, community, and relationship, rather than around abstract linear succession, is not incoherent. It reflects a set of emphases — on the persistence of the past in community, on the relational constitution of persons, on time as saturated with meaning rather than neutral and homogeneous — that deserve serious philosophical engagement.

### The Philosophical Import of This Survey

This survey has necessarily been brief and selective. The aim has not been comprehensive ethnographic coverage but a demonstration that the folk conception familiar to Western-educated philosophy students is not the universal human starting point. Cyclical time, Dreamtime, relational time — these are not naive approximations to the Western linear model but genuine philosophical alternatives.

Three lessons for the philosophy of time follow:

First, the assumption that time is linear and homogeneous is an assumption that needs to be argued for, not merely assumed. The Greek-Christian-Newtonian framework has enormous explanatory resources, but so have the alternatives, in their own domains.

Second, features of temporal experience that the Western framework tends to suppress or marginalize — the persistence of the past in the present, the relational constitution of temporal identity, the non-linear character of sacred or foundational time — are features that a complete philosophy of time should be able to accommodate or explain away.

Third, the philosophy of time, like all philosophy, benefits from comparative breadth. If our only touchstone is a single cultural tradition, we are unlikely to notice the assumptions that tradition builds into its very starting points.
