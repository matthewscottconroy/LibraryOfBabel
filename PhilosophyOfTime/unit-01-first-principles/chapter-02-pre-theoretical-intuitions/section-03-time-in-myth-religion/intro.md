# Section 3: Time in Myth, Religion, and Culture

---

## Section Introduction

Philosophy did not invent the problem of time. Long before anyone wrote the word "metaphysics," human beings were constructing elaborate accounts of time's structure, origin, and meaning — accounts embedded in myth, ritual, cosmology, and theology. These accounts are not merely historical curiosities or pre-scientific approximations to the truth. They are repositories of genuine wisdom about time's phenomenological, social, and cosmological dimensions, and they reveal the range of ways in which the question of time's nature has been — and can be — approached.

This section surveys three areas of cultural time-thought. The first concerns cyclical time: the conception, found in Hindu cosmology, ancient Greek philosophy, and Mesoamerican calendar systems, that time does not run from a unique beginning to a unique end but rather repeats, loops, or revolves. Understanding cyclical time is philosophically important both because it represents a genuinely different temporal metaphysics and because it poses sharp challenges to intuitions about identity, freedom, and historical meaning that Western linear temporality takes for granted.

The second concerns the linear temporal frameworks embedded in the Abrahamic religious traditions. Creation, redemption, and eschatology — the beginning, middle, and end of sacred history — construct a framework in which time has a direction, a purpose, and a terminal point. This theological conception of linear, directed time had an enormous influence on Western philosophy's assumptions about temporal structure, and understanding it is essential for understanding why Western philosophy of time has the shape it does.

The third offers a brief and necessarily selective survey of non-Western temporal frameworks: Aboriginal Australian Dreamtime, Lakota temporal concepts, and Ubuntu's relational conception of time. The aim is not comprehensive ethnographic coverage but philosophical alertness: a reminder that the assumptions of Western academic philosophy about time are not universal human assumptions, and that other frameworks may illuminate aspects of temporal experience that the Western tradition overlooks or suppresses.

Throughout, the emphasis is philosophical rather than historical or anthropological. We are not primarily concerned with whether these accounts are historically accurate or scientifically defensible. We are concerned with what philosophical options they represent — what they take for granted, what they deny, and what they notice that other frameworks miss.
