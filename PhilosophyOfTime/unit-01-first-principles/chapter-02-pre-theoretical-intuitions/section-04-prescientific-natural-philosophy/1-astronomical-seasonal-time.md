# 4.1 Astronomical and Seasonal Time

---

## Time Before Clocks

Go outside on a clear night, away from city lights, and look up. The sky is moving — or rather, the Earth is rotating, but the effect, from your perspective on the ground, is that the stars wheel across the sky in great arcs, rising in the east and setting in the west. If you watch the same star on successive nights, you will find it rising approximately four minutes earlier each evening — a consequence of the Earth's orbital motion around the sun. Over the course of a year, the sky cycles through its entire celestial cast.

This is free, publicly available information, legible to anyone patient enough to observe. And for most of human history, this was the only clock available. Time was not something you read off a device; it was something you read off the sky, and off the landscape, and off your own body.

### The Origins of Time Measurement

Archaeological evidence of systematic astronomical observation extends at least to the Neolithic period. Stonehenge (built in phases from ca. 3000–1500 BCE) was oriented to the midsummer sunrise and the midwinter sunset — evidence of precise solar observation used, almost certainly, for agricultural and ceremonial timing (Hawkins 1965: 112–125; Ruggles 1999: 70–76). The Antikythera mechanism, a remarkable ancient Greek computational device (ca. 150–100 BCE), could predict the positions of the sun and moon, calculate eclipse cycles, and track the dates of athletic festivals (Freeth et al. 2006: 587–591). In Mesopotamia, Babylonian astronomers from at least the seventh century BCE maintained detailed records of planetary positions and used mathematical models to predict astronomical events (Neugebauer 1969: 97–122).

What all these astronomical practices shared was the use of *periodic natural processes* — the daily rotation of the Earth, the monthly cycle of the moon, the annual orbit of the Earth around the sun — as temporal measures. Time was reckoned by the repetition of celestial events.

### Sidereal vs. Solar Time

The distinction between *sidereal* and *solar* time is philosophically instructive, because it reveals that even at the most practical level of time measurement, there is more than one "natural" measure of time, and these measures do not perfectly agree.

*Sidereal time* is measured relative to the fixed stars — specifically, relative to a reference direction pointing toward the distant stellar background. A sidereal day is the time it takes the Earth to rotate 360° relative to the stars: approximately 23 hours, 56 minutes, and 4 seconds.

*Solar time* is measured relative to the sun. A solar day is the time from one solar noon to the next: approximately 24 hours. Solar time differs from sidereal time because the Earth is also orbiting the sun: by the time the Earth has completed one rotation relative to the stars, it has moved slightly along its orbit, so the sun appears to have shifted position relative to the stars, and the Earth must rotate a little more before the sun returns to the meridian.

This four-minute discrepancy accumulates: over a year, sidereal time "gains" one full day relative to solar time. For practical purposes — agriculture, daily life — solar time is more natural, because it tracks the cycle of daylight and darkness that governs human activity. For astronomy — tracking the fixed stars, determining the sidereal positions of celestial objects — sidereal time is more natural.

The existence of these two natural measures of time raises a philosophical question: which is the "real" measure of time? Is there a fact of the matter about how long a day "really" is, independent of which natural process we use to measure it? Newton's absolute time provides one answer: real time flows uniformly, and both sidereal and solar time are imperfect approximations to it. Leibniz's relational view provides a different answer: there are multiple legitimate temporal measures, none more "real" than the others, because time just is the set of temporal relations among events, and there is no unique natural measure that all others must match.

### Time, Periodicity, and Aristotle

Aristotle's definition of time as "the number of motion with respect to before and after" (Aristotle, *Physics* 219b1–2) was, in part, a philosophical articulation of the practical consensus that time is measured by motion. For Aristotle, the most natural and regular motion — the rotation of the celestial spheres — provided the standard against which all other temporal measures were calibrated (Aristotle, *Physics* 223b12–20).

But Aristotle was not merely endorsing the practical astronomical tradition. He was raising the philosophical question: what is the relationship between time and the processes used to measure it? Is time *identical* with the celestial motion, or is it something independent that the celestial motion reveals?

Aristotle's own answer is subtle. He holds that time is the *number* of motion — not the motion itself, but the measurable feature of motion that makes it possible to say "how long" a motion took. The celestial rotation is the preferred measure, but it is not the only possible measure; it is preferred because it is the most uniform, the most continuous, and the most universally observable. We will examine this in detail in Chapter 5.

### Implications for Philosophy of Time

Two philosophical implications of the astronomical tradition deserve emphasis.

First, the dependence of time measurement on periodic physical processes raises the question of whether time could exist without such processes. If all motion stopped — if the universe were instantaneously frozen — would time still pass? Aristotle's answer was no: time without motion is impossible (*Physics* 218b21–219a2). Newton's answer was yes: time would continue to flow even if the universe were entirely static, because absolute time is independent of all physical processes.

This debate about the relationship between time and its physical measures is not merely academic. It bears directly on questions in modern physics about whether time is a fundamental feature of reality or an emergent property of more basic physical processes — a question that remains unresolved in quantum gravity (Barbour 1999: 1–10).

Second, the natural origins of time measurement remind us that time, as initially encountered by human beings, was not a neutral, homogeneous, mathematically precise medium. It was saturated with qualitative significance: the season for planting and the season for harvest, the time of festival and the time of mourning, the dangerous days and the propitious ones. The quantitative, homogeneous conception of time that dominates modern thought is a historical achievement — as we shall see in the next subsection — not a discovery of what time always already was.
