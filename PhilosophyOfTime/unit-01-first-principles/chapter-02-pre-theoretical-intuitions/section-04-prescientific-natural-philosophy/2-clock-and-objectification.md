# 4.2 The Mechanical Clock and the Objectification of Time

---

## A Device That Changed Everything

In the last decade of the thirteenth century, large mechanical clocks began to appear on the towers of European churches and civic buildings. The earliest recorded mechanical clock in Western Europe was installed at Milan in 1335; by the mid-fourteenth century, such clocks were common in major Italian, French, and English towns (Landes 1983: 58–60). These were not merely convenient devices for telling the time. They were — gradually, subtly, and then with increasing speed — engines of a transformation in how human beings experienced, conceptualized, and organized temporal reality.

The historian David Landes, in his magisterial study *Revolution in Time*, argued that the mechanical clock was among the most consequential technological inventions in human history, not because of what it did to time but because of what it did to *thought about time* (Landes 1983: 1–7). The clock made time into something that could be divided, counted, possessed, bought, and sold. It made time *scarce*. And in so doing, it made possible a particular conception of time — as a homogeneous, quantitative, fungible medium — that had not previously been available.

### From Qualitative to Quantitative Time

Before the mechanical clock, time was primarily *qualitative*. Time was the time for something — the time for planting, for praying, for sleeping, for working. It was structured by natural rhythms (light and dark, seasons) and social institutions (liturgical hours, market days, feast days). The length of an "hour" was not fixed: the canonical hours of medieval monastic life divided daylight and darkness into twelve hours each, so that summer hours were longer and winter hours shorter. Time was embedded in activity and context; it was not an abstract container waiting to be filled.

The mechanical clock changed this. Its escapement mechanism — the gear-and-detent system that converts continuous mechanical force into discrete, equal time intervals — produced a new kind of temporal object: the equal, regular, context-independent unit. An hour was now always the same duration, regardless of season, location, or activity. Time became *homogeneous*: one hour was intrinsically the same as every other hour, differing only numerically.

This transformation had far-reaching consequences. Equal-unit time made possible the precise scheduling of labor — and therefore the wage economy, in which workers are paid by the hour rather than by the task (Thompson 1967: 56–97). It made possible navigation by chronometer, since determining longitude requires knowing exact time (Sobel 1995: 3–15). It made possible the coordination of the industrial revolution, which depended on the precise synchronization of machines, workers, and transportation networks. In short, the quantitative, homogeneous, clock-measured time was not discovered; it was *produced* — by a particular technology, in a particular cultural context, serving particular social and economic interests.

### Does the Clock Discover Time or Construct It?

Here is the philosophical question the history of clocks raises in its sharpest form: is the equal-unit time produced by the mechanical clock a discovery of something that was always there — the genuine structure of time, previously approximated only crudely — or is it a *construction*, a particular way of carving up reality that serves human purposes but does not simply read off objective temporal structure?

The Newtonian absolutist says: discovery. Absolute time flows equably and uniformly; the mechanical clock, by producing equal intervals, is approximating the genuine structure of absolute time better than the irregular rhythms of natural processes do. The clock is more accurate, not merely more convenient.

The relationist says: the question "is the clock accurate?" only makes sense relative to some standard, and standards are human choices. There is no clock-independent fact about whether two intervals are "really" equal. The mechanical clock imposes an equal-interval measure on temporal experience; it does not reveal a pre-existing equal-interval structure. The homogeneous time of the clock is a social and technological artifact, not a metaphysical discovery.

Lewis Mumford, in his influential cultural history of technology, put the point memorably: "The clock, not the steam-engine, is the key-machine of the modern industrial age" — not because of what it measures, but because of what it creates: the concept of time as a neutral, homogeneous medium that can be allocated, optimized, and exchanged (Mumford 1934: 14).

A more nuanced view is available. The mechanical clock is both discovery and construction. It discovers something real — the approximate uniformity of many natural processes — and it constructs something new — the cultural practice of treating time as a fungible commodity. These two aspects are not mutually exclusive. The clock reveals temporal structure that was genuinely there (the approximate regularity of mechanical oscillation), while also imposing a social and conceptual framework that was not simply "given" in pre-clockwork temporal experience.

### Philosophical Implications: Time as Measure

The history of clocks illuminates several philosophical issues that recur throughout this book.

**The relationship between time and its measures.** If the equal unit of the mechanical clock is a construction rather than a discovery, this raises the question: what makes one time measurement system better than another? The answer, presumably, is that a better system is one that coheres better with the rest of our physics — one that gives simpler, more unified laws of nature. Philosophers of physics call this the criterion of *simplicity* or *theoretical coherence*, and it is the criterion that physicists actually use when they define the second in terms of atomic oscillations. The "correct" measure of time is the one that simplifies physics (Poincaré 1898: 1–13; Dummett 2000: 497–499).

**Time and power.** The sociological observation that the mechanical clock served the interests of capital — giving employers control over workers' time — raises a more radical philosophical question: are our temporal concepts, including the apparently neutral concept of equal, homogeneous time, embedded in structures of social power? This question connects the philosophy of time with the sociology of knowledge and critical theory (Thompson 1967; Adam 1990: 1–25; Fabian 1983). The answer is almost certainly yes, in part — but this does not mean that temporal concepts are *merely* ideological. The capacity to identify the ideological dimensions of a concept and the capacity to identify its genuine cognitive content are not mutually exclusive.

**The phenomenology of clock time.** Edmund Husserl and Martin Heidegger both argued, in different ways, that the clock-time of the modern world — abstract, homogeneous, quantitative — is a derivative and in some sense impoverished mode of temporal experience, a far cry from the rich, qualitative, lived time of immediate temporal consciousness (Husserl 1964: 21–35; Heidegger 1962: 456–472). This phenomenological critique of clock time is an important counterweight to the Newtonian tendency to treat clock-time as simply the *real* time that other measures approximate.

These implications will recur throughout this book. For now, the key point is that the folk conception of time — including its implicit picture of a homogeneous, measurable, linear medium — is not a neutral given. It is historically shaped, technologically mediated, and philosophically loaded. Recognizing this is not a reason to abandon it. It is a reason to examine it carefully.
