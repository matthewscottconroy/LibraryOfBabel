## The Peculiarity of the Question

There is something paradoxical about asking what time is. The paradox is not merely rhetorical — it is not just that the question is "deeper than it looks" or "harder than we thought." The paradox has a precise structure, identified first by Augustine and given rigorous form by several subsequent philosophers.

The paradox is this: we use temporal concepts to answer almost every question we ask. We ask when things happened, how long they lasted, what the order of events was, whether one thing caused another. All of these questions presuppose a framework of temporal reference. But when we ask what *time itself* is, we cannot reach for temporal concepts in our answer without circularity. Time cannot be defined as "the thing in which events occur" without presupposing what it means for something to "occur." It cannot be defined as "the measure of duration" without presupposing what "duration" means.

This is not a problem unique to time. We face similar difficulties with other fundamental concepts: truth, existence, identity, causation. But with time there is an additional twist: we are not merely conceptually inside time, the way we are inside the concept of truth. We are *physically* inside time. We cannot step outside it to examine it from a neutral vantage point. Every attempt to understand time must be made from within time, by creatures whose very experience is temporal through and through.

This is what Augustine meant when he said he knows what time is until asked. The knowledge he has before being asked is not conceptual knowledge — it is not the kind of knowledge that can be articulated or explained. It is the knowledge of one who is immersed in something and knows it from the inside. Philosophical inquiry into time is, among other things, an attempt to make that immersive knowledge explicit — and that attempt will always be incomplete.

## Three Kinds of Questions About Time

The difficulty of the question is compounded by the fact that "what is time?" is not one question but several, with different methods appropriate to each.

**The metaphysical question**: What is the nature of time? Does it exist independently of minds and physical processes, or is it dependent on them? Is the past real? Is the future real? Is the present objectively distinguished from other times? These questions concern the ontological structure of time — what there is, when it comes to time.

**The epistemological question**: How do we know about time? What is the source of our temporal concepts? Can we have a priori knowledge of time's structure, or is all temporal knowledge empirical? How reliable is our experience of time as a guide to time's real nature? These questions concern how we come to know what we know about time.

**The phenomenological question**: What is the experience of time like? How do we experience duration, succession, simultaneity? What is the structure of our temporal awareness — the living present, the retention of the just-past, the anticipation of the about-to-happen? These questions concern the first-person character of temporal experience.

**The physical question**: What does science tell us about time? Is there an objective direction of time? Is time fundamental or derived? Can time begin or end? These questions concern the scientific account of time and its relationship to the other kinds of questions.

These four kinds of questions interact in complex ways. The physical question has implications for the metaphysical question (special relativity seems to undermine presentism). The phenomenological question has implications for the epistemological question (our experience of temporal flow seems to provide evidence for A-theoretic claims about the present). The metaphysical question constrains what answers to the other questions are available.

Unit I is primarily concerned with the metaphysical and phenomenological questions. The physical questions are deferred to Unit VII. The epistemological questions are addressed throughout.

## The A/B Distinction as a Structural Key

McTaggart's distinction between the A-series and the B-series, introduced in Chapter 1, is the structural key to the contemporary debate about time, and it is worth pausing on it here.

The **B-series** orders events by the relations *earlier than*, *simultaneous with*, and *later than*. These relations are permanent: if the Battle of Hastings is earlier than the French Revolution, this is true at all times. The B-series is the static, relational order of events.

The **A-series** characterises events as *past*, *present*, or *future*. These characterisations are not permanent: what is now future will become present and then past. The A-series is the dynamic, "flowing" order of events.

The philosophical debate, cast in these terms, is: **Is the A-series reducible to the B-series, or does it add something irreducible?**

**B-theorists** say the A-series reduces to the B-series: an event is "present" iff it is simultaneous with the utterance of the word "present"; "past" iff it is earlier than the utterance; "future" iff it is later. The appearance of temporal flow is a perspective-effect, not a metaphysical reality.

**A-theorists** say the A-series is irreducible: the "moving now" captures something real about time that the B-series misses. Different A-theorists differ on what, exactly, the A-series adds: presentists say only the present exists; growing block theorists say the past and present exist but the future does not; moving spotlight theorists say all times exist but the present is objectively distinguished.

This debate is not merely terminological. It has consequences for the following questions:

- **Does time pass?** B-theorists say no (or: "pass" is a misleading metaphor). A-theorists say yes (in some sense that goes beyond the B-series).
- **Is free will possible?** If the future is as real as the past (eternalism), it seems fixed — and this raises worries about determinism and freedom.
- **Does the past exist?** Presentists say no. The consequent difficulty of explaining how we know about the past, and how claims about the past have truth conditions, is a central challenge for presentism.
- **Is the present objectively real?** Special relativity seems to undermine any objective, extended present. How A-theorists respond to this is one of the most discussed topics in philosophy of physics.

## Temporal Experience and Its Philosophical Significance

Chapter 2 of Unit I examines what we might call the "folk metaphysics" of time — the pre-theoretical temporal concepts that ordinary experience and language embody. This is philosophically important for two reasons.

First, the folk conception is our starting point for inquiry. We cannot examine temporal concepts from outside our own experience; we must begin where we are. The folk conception provides the data that philosophical accounts of time must explain or explain away.

Second, the folk conception may encode genuine metaphysical information. Consider the asymmetry between past and future: in ordinary experience, the past is fixed and the future is open. This asymmetry is encoded in our language (we "remember" the past and "anticipate" the future, not vice versa), in our emotional responses (regret is about the past; anxiety is about the future), and in our agency (we act to bring about future states, not past ones). This asymmetry may reflect a real metaphysical difference between past and future — or it may be an artifact of our thermodynamic situation (we live in a low-entropy universe with increasing entropy, and this produces the asymmetric folk conception even if the fundamental laws are time-symmetric).

The relationship between folk temporal concepts and the fundamental physical account of time is one of the central problems of the philosophy of time. Husserl and the phenomenological tradition hold that phenomenological time — the time of experience — cannot be straightforwardly identified with physical time and requires its own analysis. Other philosophers (especially those in the analytic tradition inspired by Russell and Quine) are more optimistic about the prospects for reducing folk concepts to physical ones.

## What a Philosophical Account Must Achieve

Summarising the work of Unit I, we can identify the desiderata for a satisfactory philosophical account of time. Such an account must:

1. **Explain temporal becoming**: The sense in which events come to be and pass away, and in which the future is open in a way that the past is not.

2. **Account for temporal experience**: The phenomenological character of our temporal awareness — the specious present, retention, protention, the feeling of passage — must be explained (or explained away).

3. **Cohere with physics**: The account must be compatible with the physical facts about time — including special relativity, the thermodynamic arrow, and eventually quantum gravity.

4. **Handle the A/B distinction**: The account must take a principled stand on the reducibility of the A-series to the B-series, and explain how the "moving now" relates to the objective temporal order.

5. **Address temporal existence**: The account must say what it means for past and future events to be real (or not), and explain how singular terms referring to past and future events have truth conditions.

6. **Solve the paradoxes**: The classical paradoxes of time — Augustine's paradox of the present, Zeno's paradoxes of continuous time, McTaggart's paradox of the A-series — must be dissolved or resolved.

No existing account fully satisfies all six desiderata. The history of the philosophy of time is, in large part, the history of attempts to satisfy some while managing the failures on others.

## Methodology: How to Philosophise About Time

A final note on method. Philosophy of time uses several distinctive methodological tools that are worth identifying explicitly, since they differ from the methods of both physics and ordinary historical inquiry.

**Conceptual analysis** is the attempt to give necessary and sufficient conditions for the application of a concept. Applied to time, conceptual analysis tries to spell out what must be true of something for it to count as a temporal relation, a temporal instant, a duration, and so on.

**Intuition pumps** are thought experiments designed to elicit specific intuitive responses that then constrain theoretical options. The Grandfather Paradox (if I travel back in time and kill my grandfather, I was never born, so I could not have travelled back) is a classic intuition pump about time travel.

**Reflective equilibrium** is the process of adjusting both particular intuitive judgements and general principles until they cohere. In philosophy of time, this means adjusting between intuitions about specific cases (the past is fixed; the future is open; the present is real) and general theoretical commitments (eternalism; presentism; growing block theory).

**Naturalistic approaches** hold that philosophical questions should be answered by attending to the best science. On a naturalistic view, the physics of time (special relativity, thermodynamics, quantum gravity) is directly relevant to the metaphysics of time, and philosophical accounts that conflict with physics are simply wrong.

**Revisionary approaches** hold that ordinary temporal concepts may be systematically misleading about the nature of time, and that a sophisticated theory may legitimately revise these concepts. On this view, the feeling that time passes may be an illusion; the present may not be objectively special; the asymmetry between past and future may be merely perspectival.

These methodological options are not mutually exclusive, and most working philosophers of time deploy several. But knowing which ones are being used — and why — is essential for evaluating the arguments encountered in the chapters that follow.
