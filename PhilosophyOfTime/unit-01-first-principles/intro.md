# Unit I: First Principles — What Is Time?

---

## Unit Introduction

There is a peculiar kind of knowing that defeats itself the moment you try to articulate it. You can ride a bicycle, but explaining the precise neural and muscular sequence that keeps you upright is another matter entirely. You can recognize a face in a crowd, but you cannot describe the algorithm you use to do it. Time is like this — only more so. You have never, not for a single moment of your conscious life, been outside of it. You know it from the inside with an intimacy nothing else approaches. And yet, if someone asks you what it *is*, you will find yourself reaching for words that slip away.

This is not a sign of intellectual failure. It is the first indication that time is philosophically deep.

Augustine of Hippo, writing in the fourth century, gave the most honest formulation of the problem that anyone has ever managed: *"What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to one that asks, I do not know."* (Augustine, *Confessions*, XI.14.17) The sentence is funny, paradoxical, and exactly right. It locates the problem with surgical precision. The difficulty with time is not that we lack experience of it — we are saturated with experience of it — but that our experience resists conceptual capture. Time keeps sliding between our fingers the moment we try to hold it still long enough to examine.

This unit is about getting the problem clearly in front of us before we attempt to solve it. That sounds modest, but it is genuinely difficult work. Philosophy moves badly when it rushes past the question toward an answer. The history of the philosophy of time is littered with theories that are ingenious but answer a slightly different question from the one they were supposed to answer — or that dissolve the question rather than answer it, which is sometimes useful and sometimes merely evasive.

We will begin, in Chapter 1, by asking what kind of question "What is time?" actually is. Is it a scientific question, a conceptual question, or something else? We will examine the methods available to us and think carefully about the relationship between philosophical and physical inquiry into time — a relationship that is cooperative but irreducibly tense. Chapter 2 then turns to the ground floor of human experience: the pre-theoretical intuitions, linguistic structures, and cultural frameworks through which time first becomes visible to us before philosophy gets to work on it.

The goal of this unit is not to answer the question of what time is. That would be premature. The goal is to equip you to ask it well. Asking a philosophical question well is more than half the battle — and it is a battle that most people lose by charging in too quickly.

**A note on what to expect.** The chapters that follow are demanding but not technical. They require no background in physics or formal logic, only the willingness to think slowly and carefully about things you ordinarily take for granted. That kind of thinking is harder than it sounds. We are accustomed to passing over the obvious because it is obvious. Philosophy asks you to stop and look at it. Prepare to find it strange.
