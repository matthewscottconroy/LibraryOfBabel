# Chapter 3 Exercises

---

## Comprehension and Analysis

**Exercise 1.** Heraclitus says (DK 49a): "Into the same rivers we step and we do not step; we are and we are not." Explain both the minimal and the stronger philosophical reading of this fragment. Which reading do you find more philosophically significant? How does the river case illuminate the general problem of identity through change?

**Exercise 2.** Reconstruct Parmenides' argument from the Way of Truth as precisely as you can, identifying each step. Which step do you think is most vulnerable to criticism? Reconstruct the best possible Aristotelian response to that step, drawing on Aristotle's distinction between substance, form, and privation (*Physics* 191a3–b34). Does Aristotle's response fully resolve the difficulty?

**Exercise 3.** The Dichotomy and the Achilles are often treated as essentially equivalent paradoxes. Explain both carefully. Are there any philosophically significant differences between them? Does the mathematical response (convergence of infinite series) dissolve both paradoxes equally, or does it leave different residues in each case?

**Exercise 4.** The Arrow paradox argues that a moving arrow is, at every instant, at rest. Aristotle's response is that this involves a false assumption: that time is composed of instants. Evaluate this response. What is the difference between instants as *limits* of intervals and instants as *parts* of time? Does this distinction adequately address the paradox, or does it merely relocate it?

**Exercise 5.** Explain the Stadium paradox as clearly as you can. What specific picture of space and time is it attacking? Aristotle says the paradox confuses passing a body at rest with passing a body in motion. Does this response fully address the paradox, or does the deeper puzzle about discrete motion survive?

**Exercise 6.** Compare the Atomist response to the Eleatics with the Platonic response (as you understand it from the Unit II introduction and any relevant background reading). Both are attempts to "save" the appearances of change and plurality against the Eleatic challenge. Which do you find more philosophically satisfying, and why?

**Exercise 7.** The Pythagoreans held that the cosmos has a mathematical structure expressed in numerical ratios and that temporal cycles (including the Great Year) reflect this structure. Evaluate this claim philosophically. What does it mean for mathematical structure to be "fundamental" to temporal reality? Is there any sense in which modern physics supports this Pythagorean intuition?

**Exercise 8.** Heraclitean flux and Parmenidean stasis represent two extreme positions on the reality of change. One says everything changes; the other says nothing changes. Design a middle position that allows for genuine change without running into the Parmenidean problem. What philosophical resources does this middle position need?

## Critical Thinking

**Exercise 9.** Many philosophers have argued that Zeno's paradoxes are fully dissolved by the theory of convergent infinite series (as developed in calculus). Consider the following objection: "The mathematical response shows that the sum of the infinite series is finite, but it does not show that infinitely many acts can actually be performed. Therefore the paradoxes remain." Critically assess this objection. Is there a principled response to it, or is the objection pointing to a genuine philosophical residue?

**Exercise 10.** Parmenides argues that "thought and being are the same: you can only think what is." This rules out thoughts about non-existent things. But we do seem to have thoughts about non-existent things — fictional characters, the present king of France, the golden mountain. How would a modern philosopher of language (drawing on the tools of Unit I, Chapter 2) respond to this Parmenidean claim? Does the modern response fully engage the original argument?

## Lab Exercises

**Lab 1: The Dichotomy in Practice.** Choose a physical task that involves approaching something: for example, walking across a room and reaching a wall, or extending your arm to touch a target. Before you begin, think through the Dichotomy: you must first reach the halfway point, then the three-quarter point, then the seven-eighths point, and so on. Now perform the task. Write a short reflection (one page) on what the experience reveals. Did the paradox affect your performance? Did the task feel different after thinking through the argument? What does this experiment suggest about the relationship between philosophical argument and ordinary temporal experience?

**Lab 2: The Arrow and Instantaneous States.** Find a video of a ball in flight (or take your own, using a phone's slow-motion feature). Pause the video at a single frame. The ball appears stationary in the frame — it is, in that instant, "at rest" in the sense that it occupies a definite region of space. Now consider: how do you know, from a single frame, that the ball is actually moving? What information in a single frame (if any) carries information about velocity? Write a one-page reflection on what this reveals about the relationship between instantaneous states and motion, connecting your observations to the Arrow paradox and the modern concept of instantaneous velocity.

**Lab 3: Natural Cycles and Cosmic Time.** For one week, observe and record at least one natural periodic process every day: the rise and set times of the sun (note the time each day), the phase of the moon (observe and sketch it), the position of Venus or Jupiter in the evening sky (if visible). At the end of the week, reflect on the following: What temporal information did you gather from natural cycles that you could not have gathered from a clock? How does observing natural cycles change your sense of temporal scale? Connect your observations to the Pythagorean account of cosmic time as mathematical harmony and to the philosophical question of whether natural cycles "measure" time or "constitute" it.
