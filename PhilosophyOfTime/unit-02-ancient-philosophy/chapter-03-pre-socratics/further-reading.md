# Chapter 3: Further Reading and Bibliography

---

## Annotated Further Reading

**Barnes, Jonathan. 1979. *The Presocratic Philosophers*. 2 vols. London: Routledge and Kegan Paul.**
The most comprehensive and philosophically rigorous study of Pre-Socratic thought in English. Barnes is demanding but fair; he takes the arguments seriously and evaluates them on their merits. His chapters on Parmenides and Zeno are especially valuable. Volume 1 covers Thales through Parmenides; Volume 2 covers Zeno through the Sophists.

**Gallop, David. 1984. *Parmenides of Elea: Fragments*. Toronto: University of Toronto Press.**
The best English-language translation and commentary on Parmenides. Gallop's translation is precise and his philosophical notes are illuminating. Essential for close reading of the Way of Truth.

**Grünbaum, Adolf. 1967. *Modern Science and Zeno's Paradoxes*. Middletown, CT: Wesleyan University Press.**
Grünbaum argues that modern mathematics (measure theory and the theory of real numbers) provides a complete dissolution of Zeno's paradoxes. This is the strongest version of the "mathematical dissolution" position. Engaging with it is essential for forming a considered view on whether anything philosophically substantive survives the mathematical response.

**Huggett, Nick. 2019. "Zeno's Paradoxes." In *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, edited by Edward N. Zalta. Spring 2019 ed.**
The best freely accessible overview of Zeno's paradoxes and the literature on them. Huggett covers all four paradoxes clearly, discusses the mathematical responses, and identifies the philosophical residues that survive them.

**Kirk, G. S., J. E. Raven, and M. Schofield. 1983. *The Presocratic Philosophers*. 2nd ed. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.**
The standard scholarly reference for Pre-Socratic texts and commentary. Contains Greek text, translation, and discussion of all major figures from Thales to the Sophists. The chapters on Heraclitus, Parmenides, Zeno, Democritus, and the Pythagoreans are essential.

**Long, A. A. 1999. "The Scope of Early Greek Philosophy." In *The Cambridge Companion to Early Greek Philosophy*, edited by A. A. Long, 1–21. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.**
An excellent orienting essay that situates the Pre-Socratics within the broader intellectual context of the Greek world and explains what kinds of problems they were trying to solve. Essential background for anyone new to the subject.

**McKirahan, Richard D. 2010. *Philosophy Before Socrates: An Introduction with Texts and Commentary*. 2nd ed. Indianapolis: Hackett.**
A user-friendly introduction to Pre-Socratic philosophy, with generous selections of primary texts in translation and accessible philosophical commentary. Particularly good for students encountering the Pre-Socratics for the first time.

**Owen, G. E. L. 1960. "Eleatic Questions." *Classical Quarterly* 10: 84–102.**
A landmark paper in the scholarly literature on Parmenides that introduced several influential interpretive proposals, including the analysis of Parmenides' key move as depending on a "no-reference" principle. Owen's interpretation has been widely discussed and debated.

**Salmon, Wesley. 1980. *Space, Time, and Motion: A Philosophical Introduction*. 2nd ed. Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press.**
An accessible and philosophically careful introduction to the philosophy of space, time, and motion. Salmon's treatment of Zeno's paradoxes is among the best in the introductory literature; his analysis engages both the mathematical responses and the philosophical residues.

**Taylor, C. C. W. 1999. *The Atomists: Leucippus and Democritus — Fragments*. Toronto: University of Toronto Press.**
A careful translation and scholarly commentary on the surviving fragments of the Atomists. Essential for anyone who wants to study Democritus's views in detail rather than through Aristotle's frequently hostile reports.

**Vlastos, Gregory. 1967. "Zeno of Elea." In *Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, edited by Paul Edwards, vol. 8, 369–379. New York: Macmillan.**
The classic philosophical essay on Zeno. Vlastos reconstructs the paradoxes with great care, discusses their intended targets (continuous versus discrete), and evaluates the classical responses. Though now dated in some details, it remains the best single treatment of the paradoxes at an introductory-to-intermediate level.
