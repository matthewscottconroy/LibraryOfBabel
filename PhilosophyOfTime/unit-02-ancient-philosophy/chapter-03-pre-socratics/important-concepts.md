# Chapter 3: Important Concepts

---

**Flux Doctrine (Heraclitus)**
The view that everything is in a constant state of change — that no thing remains the same from one moment to the next. Not a claim that nothing can be identified or known, but that identity persists through change at the level of structure and proportion (the Logos) rather than at the level of material composition. The river is the same river but never the same water.

**Logos (Heraclitus)**
The rational principle, measure, or account that governs the universal flux. Not a separate divine being outside the world, but the immanent rational structure of reality itself — the law of measure and proportion that determines how opposites cycle and how change is regulated. Everything occurs "according to the Logos," though most people fail to understand it.

**Unity of Opposites**
A central Heraclitean theme: opposites (hot and cold, wet and dry, day and night, life and death) are not simply exclusive of each other but constitutively related — each requires and passes into the other. The cosmos is constituted by the tension between opposites held in unity by the Logos, like the opposing forces in a bow or a lyre.

**Way of Truth / Way of Seeming (Parmenides)**
The two paths of inquiry in Parmenides' poem *On Nature*. The Way of Truth follows the argument that Being is one, unchanging, and without plurality or temporal structure. The Way of Seeming describes the world of ordinary experience — change, plurality, temporal succession — which is characterized as mere appearance, not genuine knowledge.

**Being (Parmenidean)**
For Parmenides, Being (*to eon*) is the only genuine reality: one, continuous, undivided, unchanging, without beginning or end, "like a well-rounded sphere." Being cannot not-be, cannot be divided, and cannot change. The argument depends on the principle that non-being is strictly impossible — it cannot exist, be thought, or be spoken.

**Non-Being and the Parmenidean Prohibition**
Parmenides' claim that non-being (*to mê eon*) is strictly impossible: it cannot exist, be thought, or be talked about. This prohibition on non-being underlies the entire Parmenidean argument, because change, plurality, and temporal structure all seem to require reference to what is not (what was but no longer is; what is not here but is there). The prohibition generates the Eleatic denial of time and change.

**Eleatics**
The school of philosophy associated with Parmenides and his followers (including Zeno) in the Greek city of Elea (southern Italy). Characteristic Eleatic doctrines: Being is one and unchanging; plurality and change are illusory; the senses are unreliable guides to reality; reason alone reveals the truth. The Eleatics set the agenda for subsequent Greek philosophy of nature.

**Dichotomy (Zeno)**
The first of Zeno's paradoxes of motion: to move from A to B, you must first reach the midpoint; before that, the quarter-point; before that, the eighth-point; and so on without limit. The journey consists of infinitely many sub-journeys, and infinitely many sub-journeys cannot be completed. Therefore motion is impossible. The paradox targets continuous conceptions of space and time.

**Achilles Paradox (Zeno)**
Achilles (fast) races a tortoise (slow) with a head start. At each stage, by the time Achilles reaches where the tortoise was, the tortoise has moved on. The tortoise always maintains a positive lead. Therefore Achilles can never catch the tortoise. Mathematical response: the infinite series of "catching up" intervals converges to a finite total time.

**Arrow Paradox (Zeno)**
At any instant, the arrow occupies a region of space exactly equal to its own size — which is what it means to be at rest. At every instant the arrow is at rest. If time is composed of instants and the arrow is at rest at every instant, the arrow never moves. Targets the composition of time out of durationless instants.

**Stadium Paradox (Zeno)**
Three rows of bodies moving relative to each other in a discrete spacetime. The relative velocity of two rows moving in opposite directions is twice the absolute velocity of each; but in a discrete spacetime, twice the absolute velocity might require half a spatial or temporal atom, which is impossible. Targets discrete conceptions of space and time.

**Instantaneous Velocity**
The concept, introduced with calculus by Newton and Leibniz, of the velocity of an object at a single durationless instant: defined as the limit of the ratio of distance to time as the time interval shrinks toward zero. Allows us to say that a moving object has a definite velocity at each instant without requiring that any displacement occur at that instant. The standard modern response to the Arrow paradox.

**Atomism (Leucippus, Democritus)**
The cosmological theory that reality consists of indivisible, eternal, homogeneous particles (atoms) moving through void. Atoms differ only in shape, arrangement, and position; macroscopic change is produced by the combination and separation of atomic configurations. Atomism affirms the reality of void (contra Parmenides) and explains temporal change through the eternal motions of unchanging atoms.

**Void (kenon)**
For the Atomists, the empty space through which atoms move. Void is "nothing" in the sense of being without material content, but it is real in the sense of being causally efficacious: it is what makes motion possible. The Atomists' insistence on the reality of void was a direct response to the Parmenidean prohibition on non-being.

**Principle of Sufficient Reason (Leucippus)**
The principle that nothing happens without a reason or cause: "Nothing happens for no reason; all things come from reason and necessity." Applied to temporal structure, this implies causal determinism: the sequence of events is causally necessitated by prior states. An early formulation of the deterministic picture of temporal causation.

**Great Year (Pythagorean)**
The period of cosmic time after which all celestial bodies return to their original positions. The Great Year represents the complete cycle of the cosmos — the fullest temporal expression of the mathematical harmony governing the heavenly spheres. It connects Pythagorean mathematics to cyclical temporal cosmology.

**Harmony of the Spheres**
The Pythagorean doctrine that the motions of the planets and stars produce a celestial music, with speeds in mathematical ratios corresponding to musical intervals. The cosmos is a mathematical harmony expressed in temporal cycles; the temporal structure of the heavens is an expression of the same numerical ratios that govern musical concords.

**Metempsychosis (Pythagorean)**
The doctrine of soul-transmigration: the soul is immortal and passes through many bodies across many lifetimes, gradually purifying itself until it achieves release from the cycle of rebirths. Implies a temporal structure for the soul that is much longer and more complex than a single human lifespan — a multi-lifetime temporal trajectory toward eventual transcendence of temporal existence.
