# Section 1: Heraclitus of Ephesus

---

## Section Introduction

Heraclitus of Ephesus (active ca. 500 BCE) is the first philosopher of whom we can say with any confidence that he took time and change as central philosophical concerns rather than as side notes to a cosmogony. He left behind a book — or more likely a collection of aphorisms — that was already famous in antiquity for its difficulty and obscurity. Ancient readers called him "the Obscure" (*ho Skoteinos*), and the nickname was deserved. His sentences are dense, paradoxical, and compressed. They reward slow reading and resist paraphrase.

What we have of Heraclitus survives only in fragments — short passages quoted by later ancient writers. The scholarly convention is to identify these fragments by the numbering in Diels and Kranz's *Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker* (1951), abbreviated DK. The most philosophically important fragments for the philosophy of time are the so-called "river" fragments (DK 12, 49a, 91), which have been endlessly interpreted, disputed, and repurposed.

Heraclitus did not write a philosophy of time. He wrote about reality as a whole. But his central claims — about the universal flux of things, about the unity of opposites, and about the rational principle (the Logos) that governs all change — are deeply and inseparably temporal. For Heraclitus, to be is to be in time; to exist is to change; and the most fundamental truths about reality are truths about how opposites are united through temporal process.

This section examines the flux doctrine and the Logos in detail. The two subsections are complementary: the first focuses on what the flux doctrine claims and what it does not claim (for Heraclitus has been seriously misread by many ancient and modern interpreters); the second examines the Logos as the rational structure that makes the flux intelligible rather than chaotic.
