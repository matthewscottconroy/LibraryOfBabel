# 2.1 The Way of Truth

---

## An Argument That Won't Go Away

Imagine that someone hands you a piece of paper with the following argument written on it and says: "This is a valid argument. At least one of the premises is true. Tell me which premise is false." This is the situation that Parmenides puts his readers in. The argument is bad in some way — it must be, since the conclusion is incredible — but identifying exactly where it goes wrong is genuinely difficult.

Here is the core of the Parmenidean argument, reconstructed from DK B2, B3, B6, and B8 (Gallop 1984: 56–72; Kirk, Raven, and Schofield 1983: 245–252):

1. Whatever is, is (*to eon estin*). (Principle of being)
2. Whatever is not, is not. (Principle of non-being)
3. Thought and being are the same: you can only think what is; what is not cannot be thought or spoken of. (DK B3, B8.34–38)
4. Therefore, you cannot think or speak of not-being.
5. Change requires something to go from being to not-being, or from not-being to being. (That is what it is to come to be or pass away.)
6. From (4): coming to be (from not-being to being) and passing away (from being to not-being) are impossible — they require what cannot be thought or spoken.
7. Therefore there is no change, no coming to be, no passing away.
8. For the same reason, there is no plurality: what would divide one thing from another would have to be something, or nothing. If something, it is, and it is part of being, not a division of being. If nothing, it cannot divide. Therefore there is no plurality.
9. The One that Is is: continuous, unchanging, without beginning or end, perfect, and like a well-rounded sphere. (DK B8)

The famous lines from DK B8 (Gallop trans.): "It is ungenerated and imperishable, whole, of one kind and immovable and perfect; nor was it once, nor will it be, since it is now, all together, one, continuous" (Gallop 1984: 66).

### Reconstructing the Argument

Let us look at the key moves more carefully.

**Step 3 (the identity of thought and being)** is the most philosophically consequential. Parmenides seems to be arguing that the only coherent objects of thought are things that are. You cannot think of nothing — not because nothing is hard to conceive, but because thought necessarily latches onto something, and if it latches onto nothing, it fails to be a thought at all. This is sometimes called the *no-reference* principle: a term that fails to refer cannot be used in meaningful speech (Owen 1960: 48–55).

**Step 5 (what change requires)** assumes that genuine change is a matter of something coming to be that was not previously, or ceasing to be what it previously was. This assumption is not obviously wrong; it is close to the folk conception. But it assumes a particular model of change — *replacement* — on which the old thing goes out of existence and the new thing comes in. Aristotle will later contest this model, arguing for a different account of change that does not require anything to come from pure non-being (Aristotle, *Physics* 191a23–b34).

**Step 8 (no plurality)** follows by a parallel argument about spatial division. What would make the One not-one (would divide it into many) would have to be something other than the One — but there is nothing other than Being. Therefore Being is undivided.

### Why the Argument is More Serious Than It Looks

It is easy to dismiss Parmenides as obviously wrong — we see change all around us — but this misses the point. Parmenides is not confused about ordinary experience. He is making a claim about reason: that when you follow the argument wherever it leads, it leads to the denial of change, and therefore the world of ordinary experience cannot be what reason says is real. The dichotomy he establishes is between *knowledge* (episteme), which is of Being, and *opinion* (doxa), which concerns the world of appearances.

This dichotomy has an impressive philosophical pedigree. Plato's distinction between the intelligible Forms (objects of genuine knowledge) and the sensible world (object of mere opinion or belief) is directly descended from Parmenides. Kant's distinction between things-in-themselves (noumena) and things as they appear (phenomena) has structural parallels. The impulse to locate genuine reality in something beyond or beneath the changing world of experience is one of the most persistent in the history of philosophy.

The argument also anticipates the modern distinction between the *manifest image* (the world as it appears to ordinary perception and experience) and the *scientific image* (the world as it is described by fundamental physics). If the fundamental ontology of physics does not include a present moment, then in some sense the manifest image's picture of time is not a direct reading of physical reality. Parmenides was the first to drive a wedge between appearance and reality in a principled, argumentative way.

### Difficulties with the Argument

Three lines of response have been most influential:

**The Aristotelian response**: Change does not require coming from pure non-being. When a bronze statue is made, the bronze doesn't come from nothing; it comes from what was bronze-but-not-yet-statue. Change always involves a *substrate* (the matter, the bronze) and the acquisition of a new *form* (the shape of the statue) from a *privation* (the not-yet-statue condition). The terminus of change is not non-being but privation — a determinate, qualified absence (Aristotle, *Physics* 191a3–b34).

**The Platonic response**: Parmenides is right that change cannot involve pure non-being, but "not being" in the sense of "not being this" or "being different" is not nothing — it is merely a different being. In the *Sophist* (256e–258e), Plato introduces the concept of *otherness* or *difference* as a Form that explains how something can "not be" in a sense that does not violate the Parmenidean prohibition on non-being. "Not-F" does not mean pure non-being; it means "being other than F."

**The logical response**: Modern logicians diagnose the argument as committing a *use-mention confusion*. "Not-being" as a grammatical subject in a sentence does not require that there be an entity (non-being) that the subject refers to. The sentence "Nothingness is terrifying" does not entail the existence of Nothingness as a thing. The Parmenidean argument conflates the use of the word "nothing" with a reference to an entity called Nothing.

None of these responses is fully satisfying, and each has generated its own philosophical controversy. The enduring importance of Parmenides is that he forced philosophy to become precise about the concepts of being, non-being, change, and existence — concepts that anyone doing philosophy of time needs to handle carefully.
