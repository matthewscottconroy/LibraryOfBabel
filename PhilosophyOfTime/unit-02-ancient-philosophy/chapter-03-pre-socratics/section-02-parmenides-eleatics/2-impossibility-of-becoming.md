# 2.2 The Impossibility of Becoming

---

## What It Means to Say Time Is Appearance

Parmenides' argument culminates in a striking claim: "Nor was it once, nor will it be, since it is now, all together, one, continuous" (DK B8.5–6, Gallop trans.). The past tense ("was") and the future tense ("will be") are denied. What is, is *now*, in an eternal present that admits no before or after. The Eleatic One is not a thing that has existed for a very long time; it is a thing that exists outside time entirely.

This is the Parmenidean denial of time, and understanding it requires distinguishing two possible claims that are easy to conflate.

### Two Different Denials

The first claim is that change is impossible. On Parmenides' view, nothing undergoes genuine change — nothing comes to be or passes away — because change requires movement between being and non-being, and non-being is strictly impossible. The objects of ordinary experience that appear to change, to come to be, and to pass away are mere appearances. They are not real in the strict sense.

The second claim is that the temporal distinctions of *was*, *is*, and *will be* do not apply to genuine being. "Was" refers to a past that no longer is; "will be" refers to a future that is not yet. Both involve a reference to non-being — to what was but is no longer, or to what will be but is not yet. Since non-being is impossible, these temporal distinctions fail to apply to Being. Real being is in an eternal present — not a present *moment* that will pass, but a present that simply *is*, without ever having been otherwise and without ever becoming otherwise.

These two denials are related but distinct. The first is about change; the second is about temporal structure. The second goes further: it is not merely that things don't change, but that the very temporal structure of past-present-future — the A-series, in McTaggart's terminology — is inapplicable to genuine being.

### Parmenides and the B-Theory

It is tempting, and not entirely misleading, to see Parmenides as an ancestor of the B-theory of time. The B-theory holds that the fundamental temporal facts are the permanent "earlier than" relations among events, and that A-series properties (past, present, future) are perspectival rather than objective features of reality. Parmenides goes further: he denies even the B-series relations of earlier-than and later-than, because these too involve something being at a different time — and different times imply that some of them are no longer, which brings in non-being.

But the parallel illuminates something. Both Parmenides and the B-theorist are skeptical about the ontological significance of the distinction between past, present, and future. Both think that the apparent "movement" of time — the sense that the now is shifting through events — is a misleading appearance. And both hold that what is philosophically or physically real is a timeless or temporally undifferentiated structure.

The difference is that Parmenides denies multiplicity altogether, while the B-theorist is happy with a four-dimensional manifold of events ordered by "earlier than" — a manifold that is temporally differentiated even if it has no privileged present. Parmenides is more radical: for him, even the B-series is illusory.

### Knowledge and Opinion

DK B1 introduces the distinction between two "ways" of inquiry: the Way of Truth, on which Being is and is only one, and the Way of Seeming, on which mortals wander in confusion, thinking that coming-to-be and passing-away, being and not-being, are "the same and not the same." The Way of Seeming corresponds to ordinary experience — the world of time, change, and plurality — which is characterized epistemologically as *doxa* (opinion, belief) rather than *episteme* (knowledge, understanding).

This epistemological distinction reinforces the metaphysical one. The temporal world is not merely ontologically inferior to the One Being; it is epistemically inaccessible to genuine knowledge. Knowledge, for Parmenides, can only be of what is stable, necessary, and unchanging. The senses, which present the world of change to us, are unreliable guides; reason alone, following the argument wherever it leads, can reach the truth.

This distinction between the objects of genuine knowledge (stable, unchanging) and the objects of mere opinion (changing, temporal) is the seed from which Plato's theory of Forms will grow. Plato will be less radical than Parmenides — he will allow the temporal world to have a secondary reality as a "likeness" of the Forms — but the structure of the Platonic epistemology is Parmenidean at its core.

### The Philosophical Challenge

Parmenides poses a challenge to anyone who wants to take temporal reality seriously. If you believe that time is real — that the temporal world is genuine, not illusory — you need to explain how the temporal distinctions of past, present, and future are possible given the apparently iron logic of Parmenides' argument.

Aristotle's response — the most systematic in antiquity — is to attack step 5 of the argument: the claim that change requires something to come from pure non-being. Aristotle will argue that change is always change *of* something — a substance that persists through the change — and that the terminus of change is not non-being but privation, a determinate qualified absence. This response reframes the problem without simply dismissing Parmenides: it engages the argument on its own terms and attempts to show that the concept of change, properly analyzed, does not violate the Parmenidean prohibition on non-being.

But whether Aristotle fully succeeds is a question that remains open. Parmenides' challenge continues to be one of the most important starting points for philosophy of time precisely because it forces clarity about the concepts of being, change, and temporal existence that are easy to take for granted.
