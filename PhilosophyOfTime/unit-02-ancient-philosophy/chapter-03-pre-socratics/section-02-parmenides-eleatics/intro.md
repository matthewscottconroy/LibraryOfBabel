# Section 2: Parmenides and the Eleatics

---

## Section Introduction

Parmenides of Elea (active ca. 480 BCE) is perhaps the most philosophically audacious thinker of antiquity. Where Heraclitus had pushed the fact of change to its limit, Parmenides pushed in precisely the opposite direction. Starting from what looks like a simple logical principle — "It is, and it is not possible that it is not" — Parmenides derived the conclusion that reality is a single, undivided, unchanging, eternal whole, and that the world of change, plurality, and time that we experience through our senses is sheer illusion.

This is not a comfortable conclusion. It contradicts everything we think we know about the world. But Parmenides' argument is not obviously wrong, and understanding why it is serious — and what would be required to refute it — is essential preparation for everything that follows in the history of philosophy. Plato's entire project of the Forms can be understood as a response to Parmenides. Aristotle's *Physics* is in large part a sustained attempt to show that change and time are philosophically coherent concepts. Modern philosophy of time is still, in a deep sense, wrestling with Parmenides' challenge.

The poem *On Nature* (or *Peri Physeos*), of which we have significant fragments, is divided into a proem (prologue), a Way of Truth (*aletheia*), and a Way of Seeming (*doxa*). The Way of Truth contains the philosophical argument; the Way of Seeming offers a cosmological account of the world of appearances (which Parmenides does not take to be literally true). We focus here on the Way of Truth.

This section examines the argument of the Way of Truth (subsection 1) and Parmenides' implications for the impossibility of becoming and the status of time (subsection 2).
