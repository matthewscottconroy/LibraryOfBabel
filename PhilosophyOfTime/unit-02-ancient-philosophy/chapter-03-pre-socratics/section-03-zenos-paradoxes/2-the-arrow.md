# 3.2 The Arrow Paradox

---

## Motion in the Instant

The Dichotomy and the Achilles exploit the infinite divisibility of continuous time to generate puzzles. The Arrow paradox exploits the opposite direction: the properties of individual instants. It asks not "how do you complete infinitely many sub-tasks?" but "how does anything move at an instant?" The Arrow targets the concept of the instant itself — the durationless point in time — and argues that if time is composed of such points, then motion is impossible.

### The Paradox

Here is Aristotle's formulation (*Physics* 239b5–9):

"Zeno's argument claims that if everything when it occupies an equal space is at rest, and if what moves is always in the present moment occupying such a space, then the flying arrow is motionless."

More explicitly, the argument runs as follows:

1. At any instant of time, the arrow occupies a definite position in space — a region of space exactly equal to its own size.
2. To occupy a region of space exactly equal to one's own size is to be at rest (there is no room to be anywhere else).
3. Therefore, at every instant, the arrow is at rest.
4. If the arrow is at rest at every instant, and time is composed of instants, then the arrow is at rest throughout its flight.
5. Therefore the arrow never moves. But it does move. Contradiction.

The premise doing the most work is (4): the claim that if something is at rest at every instant, it is at rest throughout its motion. This presupposes that motion is composed of its instantaneous states in the way that, say, a pile of sand is composed of sand grains. But is that right?

### Aristotle's Response

Aristotle's response (*Physics* 239b31–240a1) is quick and pointed: the Arrow paradox, he says, depends on the false assumption that time is composed of instants. If time is not composed of instants — if instants are limits of intervals rather than the basic components of time — then you cannot infer from the arrow's being at rest at every instant that it is at rest during any interval. The inference from "at-rest-at-every-instant" to "at-rest-throughout" requires that every period of time is simply an accumulation of instants, which Aristotle denies.

For Aristotle, the instant (*to nun*, the now) is not a part of time but a *boundary* of time — the limit of a temporal interval, analogous to a geometric point on a line. Just as a geometric line is not composed of points (points are limits, not parts), time is not composed of instants. Motion occurs during intervals of time; instants are limits of those intervals, not smallest temporal units.

This response has considerable force. The question it raises is whether Aristotle's conception of time is coherent — whether the idea of instants as limits rather than parts can be made fully precise. As we will see in Chapter 5, Aristotle's account of the relationship between the Now and temporal continuity is philosophically subtle.

### The Modern Response: Instantaneous Velocity

The most important development in the modern approach to the Arrow paradox is the concept of *instantaneous velocity*, introduced mathematically by Newton and Leibniz in the development of calculus. Instantaneous velocity is not motion occurring at an instant; it is the *limit* of the ratio of distance to time as the time interval shrinks toward zero. The velocity of the arrow at an instant is defined as:

v(t) = lim[Δt→0] (x(t + Δt) - x(t)) / Δt

This definition allows us to say that the arrow is *moving* at an instant in the sense of having a non-zero instantaneous velocity, even though no displacement occurs at that instant. Motion, on this account, is a property of the arrow's trajectory *through* time, not of its instantaneous state.

Does this response dissolve Zeno's worry? It shows that there is a coherent mathematical concept of instantaneous motion that does not require displacement within the instant. But some philosophers argue that instantaneous velocity is a mathematical tool for describing trajectories rather than a physically real property of the arrow at an instant (Arntzenius 2000: 189–208; Tooley 1988: 225–282). If so, the question returns: what is the physical basis for saying the arrow is moving at an instant?

### The Deeper Puzzle

The Arrow paradox points to a deep puzzle about how temporal dynamics — how things change from one instant to the next — is possible at all if instants are durationless and independent. Consider the following question: what "connects" the successive instants of the arrow's flight? What makes it the case that the arrow at t+ε is located where it is, given its location at t?

In Newtonian mechanics, the answer is given by the equations of motion: the future state is determined by the present position and velocity. But position and velocity at an instant are already abstractions from the trajectory — they say nothing about a single isolated instant. The temporal evolution of a system is governed by differential equations that essentially involve how quantities change over time. The "state" of the arrow at an instant is not complete unless it includes velocity (and acceleration, etc.), and these are relational — they describe the arrow's trajectory through time, not any one instant.

This suggests a philosophically interesting conclusion: instants are not genuinely independent. You cannot understand any instant in isolation from the intervals around it. This is consistent with Aristotle's view that instants are limits rather than parts, and it suggests that any adequate philosophy of time must treat temporal continuity as primitive rather than derived from a composition of independent instants.

The Arrow paradox, in sum, is not fully dissolved by either calculus or Aristotle's response. It points to a genuine philosophical puzzle about the relationship between instantaneous states and temporal dynamics that remains at the frontier of philosophy of physics.
