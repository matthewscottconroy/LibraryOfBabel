# 3.3 The Stadium Paradox

---

## The Problem with Discrete Motion

If the Arrow paradox targeted the continuous view of time, the Stadium paradox targets the discrete view: the hypothesis that time (and space) consists of indivisible atoms, smallest units below which no further division is possible. Zeno wants to show that this view also generates absurdities. Either way you analyze motion — continuous or discrete — you get a contradiction. Therefore motion is impossible.

The Stadium paradox (also called the Moving Rows paradox) is the most contested and most obscure of Zeno's four paradoxes, in part because the ancient descriptions are incomplete and in part because the argument only works if a specific assumption is made that might not have seemed obvious even to Zeno's ancient readers.

### The Paradox Reconstructed

Aristotle describes the Stadium paradox in *Physics* 239b33–240a18, but his description is brief and his refutation confident — perhaps too confident, as if he thinks the paradox is easy, when it may not be.

Here is a reconstruction (following Vlastos 1967: 369–372; Huggett 2019):

Suppose space and time consist of discrete, indivisible units — call them *spatial atoms* (the smallest possible distance) and *temporal atoms* (the smallest possible time).

Now imagine three rows of bodies:
- Row A: four bodies at rest, aligned at the center: A1, A2, A3, A4
- Row B: four bodies moving right at one spatial atom per temporal atom: B1, B2, B3, B4
- Row C: four bodies moving left at one spatial atom per temporal atom: C1, C2, C3, C4

At time t, suppose:
- B1 is aligned with A1
- C4 is aligned with A4

After one temporal atom (at t+1):
- B1 has moved one spatial atom to the right, now aligned with A2
- C4 has moved one spatial atom to the left, now aligned with A3

So far, no problem. But now consider: relative to the B row, the C row has moved not one but *two* spatial atoms in one temporal atom. B1 started aligned with C4 and now is aligned with C2 (or similar — the exact alignment depends on the setup). The C row has moved two spatial atoms relative to the B row in the space of one temporal atom.

But *one temporal atom* is the *smallest possible time* — no motion can be faster. And *one spatial atom* is the *smallest possible distance*. Yet the C row has moved the equivalent of two spatial atoms relative to B in one temporal atom. If we define "speed" in terms of spatial atoms per temporal atom, the C row is moving at "two" relative to B. But the minimum speed is "one spatial atom per temporal atom." To move *past* something at one spatial atom per temporal atom should take two temporal atoms — yet it seems to happen in one.

In other words: on the discrete hypothesis, the relative velocity of B and C (moving in opposite directions) should be twice the absolute velocity of each — but doubling the absolute velocity would mean moving *half* a spatial atom per temporal atom, and there is no half of an indivisible spatial atom. The discrete hypothesis generates a contradiction in the arithmetic of motion.

### Aristotle's Dismissal

Aristotle (*Physics* 240a1–18) quickly dismisses the paradox on the grounds that it involves a confusion: Zeno is comparing the time it takes for B to pass A (a body at rest) with the time it takes for B to pass C (a body in motion in the opposite direction). These are different comparisons, and there is no obligation that they take the same amount of time. You can pass a stationary object more slowly than you pass a moving object without contradiction.

Aristotle is right that this is one version of the puzzle. But this response does not fully engage the point about discrete motion. The deeper issue is that on the discrete hypothesis, relative velocities cannot always be calculated as simple multiples of absolute velocities, because the multiples might require fractions of indivisible units. This is a genuine problem for any discrete theory of space and time.

### What Zeno May Have Been Targeting

There is scholarly debate about what exactly Zeno was targeting with the Stadium. Two interpretations are defensible.

The first (following Vlastos 1967) is that the paradox targets *discrete* theories of space and time specifically, showing that they cannot give a coherent account of relative motion. On this reading, the Stadium is the companion to the Arrow: the Arrow shows that continuous time leads to problems with motion, and the Stadium shows that discrete time does too.

The second interpretation (Salmon 1980: 31–37) holds that the paradox is less focused — that it is more of a general puzzle about relative motion and the measurement of time, without specifically targeting a discrete theory.

On either reading, the Stadium makes a contribution to the philosophy of time: it shows that the problem of motion and time is not simply solved by choosing between continuous and discrete models. Each model generates difficulties of its own.

### Discrete Time in Modern Physics

The Stadium paradox has acquired new relevance in contemporary physics of space and time. There are serious theoretical proposals — particularly in loop quantum gravity and causal set theory — that spacetime is discrete at the Planck scale: that there is a minimum meaningful length (the Planck length, approximately 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ meters) and a minimum meaningful time (the Planck time, approximately 5.4 × 10⁻⁴⁴ seconds), below which the usual concepts of length and duration do not apply (Penrose 2004: 961–966; Sorkin 2003: 1–27).

If such theories are correct, then Zeno's Stadium paradox is not merely a curiosity from ancient philosophy. It is a real constraint on the physics of discrete spacetime: any theory of discrete motion must be able to handle the arithmetic of relative velocities without generating the contradiction Zeno described. Loop quantum gravity and causal set theory have technical resources that Zeno and Aristotle did not, but the conceptual problem he identified — how to relate absolute and relative motion in a discrete framework — remains a live challenge.

Zeno was wrong, almost certainly, in his target conclusion (that motion is impossible). But he was right that the conceptual foundations of motion, continuity, and discrete structure are more complicated than they appear. The Stadium paradox is a lasting reminder that even apparently simple physical facts about motion depend on philosophical assumptions about the structure of space and time that are not obviously true.
