# Section 3: Zeno's Paradoxes

---

## Section Introduction

Zeno of Elea (ca. 490–430 BCE) was a student or associate of Parmenides, and his philosophical contribution was, in essence, a sustained campaign of indirect support for his teacher. If Parmenides had argued that motion and change are impossible in principle, Zeno showed — or tried to show — that our ordinary concepts of motion, continuity, and spatial and temporal extension are internally incoherent. You don't need to accept Parmenidean metaphysics on faith; just try to think clearly about what motion actually is, and you will find that the concept self-destructs.

Aristotle, our main source for Zeno's arguments, describes four paradoxes of motion: the Dichotomy, the Achilles, the Arrow, and the Stadium (*Physics* 239b5–240a18). These four arguments were designed to target the two main pictures of space and time available in antiquity: the continuous view (on which space and time are indefinitely divisible, with no smallest unit) and the discrete view (on which space and time consist of indivisible atoms). Zeno's genius was to argue that *both* views lead to absurdities. Whatever you say about the structure of motion, he has an objection.

The paradoxes have fascinated and frustrated philosophers and mathematicians for twenty-five centuries. Mathematical responses — particularly the development of the theory of convergent infinite series in calculus — address some versions of some paradoxes, but there is a persistent feeling among careful philosophers that the mathematical responses, while technically correct, leave a philosophical residue. This section examines each paradox in turn.

The first subsection covers the Dichotomy and Achilles, the two "infinite division" paradoxes. The second covers the Arrow, the paradox about instantaneous motion. The third covers the Stadium, the paradox about discrete space and time.
