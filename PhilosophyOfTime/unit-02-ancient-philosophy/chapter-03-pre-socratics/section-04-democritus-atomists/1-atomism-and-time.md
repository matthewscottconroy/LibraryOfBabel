# 4.1 Atomism and the Conception of Time

---

## Atoms, Void, and the Problem of Change

The atomist response to the Eleatic challenge is philosophically clever. Parmenides had argued: being is one, full, and unchanging, because any alternative requires the existence of not-being (void, plurality, change) which is impossible. Leucippus and Democritus accepted the Parmenidean account of individual atoms — each atom is uncreated, indestructible, homogeneous, unchanging — and then simply *insisted* on the existence of void. "Nothing" (*ouden*, the void) is, as much as "something" (*den*, atoms). As Democritus reportedly put it: "*ouden* is not less real than *den*" (DK B156; Luria 2017: fr. 68). The void is not literally nothing — it is the empty space in which atoms move — but it is the "not-being" that Parmenides had denied.

This move is philosophically audacious. The Atomists simply denied the Parmenidean principle that non-being cannot exist. For them, void is a genuine kind of being — "real" in the sense of being causally efficacious (it is what makes motion possible) — even if it lacks the positive solidity of atoms.

### Atoms and Their Properties

For Democritus, atoms differ from each other in three ways: shape (schêma), arrangement (taxis), and position (thesis) (Aristotle, *Metaphysics* 985b13–19). They do not differ in quality — they are not hot or cold, bright or dark — these are secondary properties that arise from the interactions of atoms with perceivers. Atoms are eternal and uncreated; they were always in motion, moving through the infinite void; they collide, combine, and separate to produce the macroscopic world of apparently changing things.

This is a bold and clean picture. Macroscopic change — the coming-to-be and passing-away of ordinary objects — is explained in terms of the combination and separation of unchanging atomic components. The metabolic change of a living body, the burning of wood, the rusting of metal — all are atomic rearrangements. Nothing at the level of atoms changes; everything at the macroscopic level does. The temporal character of macroscopic reality is, in a sense, real but derivative: it is the temporal character of atomic motion and rearrangement, not of anything fundamental.

### The Atomist Conception of Time

What conception of time does atomism imply? The ancient sources are somewhat indirect on this question, but several points are clear.

**Time and motion.** For the Atomists, time is the medium of atomic motion. Atoms are "always in motion" (Aristotle, *De Caelo* 300b8–10), and the motions of atoms constitute the temporal order of events. Time, for the Atomists, seems to be relational in character: it is not a separate container over and above the atoms and the void, but a feature of how atomic motions are ordered. This makes atomist time closer to Leibniz's relational view than to Newton's absolute time (Taylor 1998: 222–224).

**The Principle of Sufficient Reason.** Leucippus, Democritus's predecessor and co-founder, is credited with a remarkable methodological principle: "Nothing happens for no reason; all things come from reason and necessity" (DK A1, cited in Aëtius; Taylor 1999: 158). This is an early formulation of the Principle of Sufficient Reason — the claim that every event has a sufficient cause or reason. Applied to temporal structure, this principle means that the temporal order of events is not arbitrary or random but determined by causal necessity. Atomic collisions follow deterministic laws; the temporal sequence of events is fixed by the prior configuration of atoms and their motions.

This is a thoroughgoing *causal determinism* about temporal order. Everything that happens at any time is causally necessitated by what preceded it. The future, for the Atomists, is as fixed as the past, since both are determined by the prior configuration of atoms. This implication — that the future is already settled — is in tension with the folk conception's intuition about future openness, and it anticipates the debate about determinism and free will that runs through the rest of the history of philosophy.

**The Eternity of the Cosmos.** Democritus, like most Pre-Socratics, held that the cosmos is eternal — without beginning or end. Atoms have always existed and will always exist; the void has always existed; and atomic motion has been occurring forever. There was no first moment, no creation, no point at which everything began. This implies that time has no temporal beginning or end: it is infinite in both directions.

This commitment to an infinite, beginningless time is in direct conflict with the Abrahamic doctrine of creation ex nihilo and with the Platonic doctrine of a created cosmos. It will be challenged by Plato and Aristotle (who hold different positions on the question) and will recur in modern cosmology.

### Atoms of Time?

There is an interesting question about whether the Atomists held that time itself — like space and matter — is composed of indivisible atoms: smallest units of duration below which no further division is possible. If so, this would make atomist time an early version of discrete time, and the Stadium paradox would be directly relevant.

The evidence is equivocal. Some scholars have argued that Democritus's atomism, applied consistently, should yield atomic time as well as atomic space (Furley 1967: 111–130). If space is composed of spatial atoms, the consistency of the atomist program might seem to require temporal atoms as well. But the ancient sources do not explicitly attribute the doctrine of temporal atoms to Democritus, and Aristotle does not discuss it in connection with atomism.

What we can say is that the atomist framework is at least *compatible* with discrete time and that atomist arguments about the discreteness of matter can be adapted to temporal discreteness. The philosophical questions raised by discrete time — particularly the Stadium paradox — would apply.

### The Legacy of Atomism

Democritean atomism is one of the most important ideas in the history of science and philosophy. Its intellectual progeny include Epicurean atomism, the Stoic theory of *pneuma*, and — most importantly — the scientific revolution's conception of matter as particulate. Descartes, Newton, Locke, and Boyle all drew on ancient atomist ideas in developing early modern natural philosophy.

For the philosophy of time specifically, atomism's legacy is the materialist programme: the attempt to explain all temporal phenomena — change, causation, temporal order — in terms of the motions and interactions of elementary material constituents. This programme has been extraordinarily successful in physics and chemistry. Whether it provides a complete account of temporal reality — whether the phenomenological and experiential dimensions of time can be fully explained in atomistic terms — remains one of the central questions of the philosophy of mind and the philosophy of time.
