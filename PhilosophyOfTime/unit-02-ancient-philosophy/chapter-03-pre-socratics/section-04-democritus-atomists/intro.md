# Section 4: Democritus and the Atomists

---

## Section Introduction

Leucippus (active ca. 450 BCE) and Democritus of Abdera (ca. 460–370 BCE) developed the most ambitious and systematic materialist cosmology of ancient Greek philosophy: *atomism*. Against the Eleatic denial of plurality and change, the Atomists argued that reality consists of an infinite number of indivisible particles — *atoma* (uncuttables) — moving through void (*kenon*, the empty). The appearances of change, generation, and destruction are produced by the combination and separation of atoms; the atoms themselves are eternal, indestructible, and unchanging.

Atomism is important for the philosophy of time for several reasons. Most obviously, it represents a systematic alternative to both Heraclitean flux and Parmenidean stasis: a world in which things genuinely change (at the macroscopic level of atomic combinations) while what is ultimately real (the atoms themselves) is unchanging. But atomism also implies a particular conception of time that is worth examining: time as the medium through which atoms move, relational in character, and dependent on the existence of motion.

Our sources for atomist doctrine are fragmentary and often indirect. Democritus left no complete text that survives; we depend on reports in Aristotle, Theophrastus, and later doxographers. Nonetheless, enough is preserved to reconstruct the main lines of atomist temporal metaphysics.
