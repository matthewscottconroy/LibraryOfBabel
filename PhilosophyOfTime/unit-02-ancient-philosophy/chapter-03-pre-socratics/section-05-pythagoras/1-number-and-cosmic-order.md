# 5.1 Number and Cosmic Order

---

## Mathematics as the Structure of Time

Aristotle reports that the Pythagoreans "suppose that the elements of numbers are the elements of all things, and that the whole heaven is a harmony (*harmonia*) and a number" (*Metaphysics* 985b23–986a3). This is a startling claim. Not that mathematics is useful for describing nature — everyone agrees to that — but that mathematics is *constitutive* of nature: that what things *are* is a matter of mathematical structure, number, and proportion.

The specific claim about "harmony" connects mathematical structure to temporal structure in a direct way. For the Pythagoreans, harmonia is not merely an aesthetic property of music but a structural principle of the cosmos. The ratios that govern musical concords — 2:1 for the octave, 3:2 for the fifth, 4:3 for the fourth — are the same ratios that govern the motions of the heavenly bodies and the structure of the soul. Mathematical proportion is the deep reality underlying all apparently diverse phenomena.

### The Harmony of the Spheres

The most famous expression of Pythagorean cosmological mathematics is the doctrine of the *harmonia tôn sphaerôn* — the harmony of the spheres. According to this doctrine, the planets and the fixed stars are embedded in spherical shells that rotate at different speeds. The speeds of these rotations stand in the same ratios as the intervals of the musical scale. Therefore, the motions of the heavens produce a celestial music — a vast, silent (or inaudible to ordinary human ears) harmony (Aristotle, *De Caelo* 290b12–291a28).

This idea is not merely poetic. It implies a specific claim about the mathematical structure of cosmic time: the temporal rhythms of the heavenly bodies — their periods of revolution — stand in ratios of musical proportion. The cosmos is a harmony in time; its temporal structure is an expression of the same mathematical ratios that structure musical intervals.

The philosophical significance is that mathematical necessity and temporal order are directly connected. The temporal cycles of the cosmos are not arbitrary; they are governed by the same necessity that governs the ratios of musical concords. Understanding time at the deepest level means understanding the mathematical structure that governs its cycles.

### The Great Year

The Pythagoreans, along with other ancient cosmologists, posited a *Great Year* (*megas eniautos* or *magnus annus*) — a cycle of time after which all the celestial bodies return to exactly the same positions they occupied at the beginning (Plato, *Timaeus* 39d; Aristotle, *Meteorologica* 339b27). The length of the Great Year was a matter of debate; estimates varied from 3,600 to 36,000 ordinary years, depending on which astronomical parameters were used.

The Great Year has several philosophical implications worth noting.

**Cosmic periodicity.** The Great Year implies that cosmic time is fundamentally periodic. History repeats, at least at the level of celestial configuration. This connects Pythagorean thought to the broader ancient tendency toward cyclical temporal frameworks, and it raises the same questions about identity and recurrence that we examined in the context of Hindu and Stoic cyclical time.

**The measure of time.** The Great Year is a natural, mathematically necessary unit of cosmic time — a unit determined by the mathematical structure of the celestial system rather than by arbitrary human convention. This fits the Pythagorean conviction that mathematical structure is fundamental: the true measure of time is not the human day or year but the period determined by the cosmic harmonia.

**The relationship between time and eternity.** For the Pythagoreans, the Great Year is the closest the temporal cosmos can come to the eternal perfection of number. Each cycle is a complete expression of the cosmos's mathematical nature; the periodicity approximates, in a temporal medium, the timeless necessity of mathematical truth. This idea will be developed, in a very different key, by Plato in the *Timaeus*, where time is created as a "moving image of eternity."

### Pythagorean Mathematics and Modern Physics

The Pythagorean conviction that mathematical structure is fundamental to physical reality has proven extraordinarily productive. Modern physics is, in many respects, thoroughly Pythagorean: it explains the behavior of matter in terms of mathematical equations, it discovers mathematical symmetries as the fundamental principles of nature, and it increasingly treats mathematical structure as more fundamental than any particular physical realization of that structure.

For the philosophy of time, the Pythagorean legacy is the idea that temporal structure is mathematical structure — that the periodicity of time, the rhythms of natural processes, and the order of cosmic cycles are not contingent features of the world but expressions of necessary mathematical relationships. This idea connects to the modern notion that the laws of physics — which are mathematical — govern temporal evolution, and that understanding time requires understanding the mathematics of dynamical laws.

Whether mathematical structure is genuinely fundamental — whether the world is mathematical "all the way down," as the physicist Max Tegmark has recently argued (Tegmark 2008: 101–114) — or whether mathematics is a highly successful descriptive tool remains a live philosophical question. Pythagoras raised it first.

### Pythagorean Ethics and Temporal Purification

A final Pythagorean theme worth noting is the doctrine of metempsychosis — the transmigration of souls. According to Pythagorean belief, the soul is immortal and passes through many bodies over many lifetimes, gradually being purified of its material entanglements until it achieves release from the cycle of rebirths (Burnet 1892: 84–86; Huffman 2010: 219–222).

This doctrine implies a distinctive temporal structure for the soul: not a single temporal span from birth to death, but a very long, cyclically structured temporal trajectory across multiple lifetimes. The individual's temporal existence is embedded in a much larger temporal framework — the cycle of metempsychosis — that is itself governed by rational and moral necessity.

The philosophical connection to time is that the soul's journey is a temporal purification — a progress through time toward a condition that transcends time. This idea, mediated through Plato, will become central to the philosophical treatment of eternity and temporal existence in subsequent centuries. The soul's goal, for the Pythagoreans, is ultimately to escape the temporal cycle and achieve the permanent, unchanging condition of pure mathematical ratio — the condition of the eternal.
