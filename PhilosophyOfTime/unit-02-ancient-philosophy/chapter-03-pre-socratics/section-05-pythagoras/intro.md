# Section 5: The Pythagoreans

---

## Section Introduction

The Pythagoreans occupy a distinctive and somewhat paradoxical place in the history of ancient philosophy. As a school, they were secretive, quasi-religious, and devoted to a founder — Pythagoras of Samos (ca. 570–495 BCE) — whose own writings (if any existed) have been lost entirely. Yet their intellectual legacy is enormous: they bequeathed to subsequent Greek philosophy a conviction that mathematical structure is fundamental to the order of reality, and that the cosmos can be understood in terms of number, ratio, and proportion.

This mathematical conviction had direct implications for the philosophy of time. If the deep structure of reality is numerical, then the deep structure of temporal order is also numerical: time is governed by mathematical ratios, expressed in the harmonies of the spheres and the cycles of the Great Year. Pythagorean temporal thinking is thus a cosmological mathematics of time — an attempt to understand temporal order as an expression of mathematical necessity.

Our sources for Pythagorean philosophy are difficult: Pythagoras himself left no writings, and the early Pythagorean tradition is known primarily through later reports (Aristotle in the *Metaphysics* is a primary source) and through documents of disputed authenticity. What can be reconstructed with reasonable confidence is enough to trace the main lines of Pythagorean temporal cosmology.
