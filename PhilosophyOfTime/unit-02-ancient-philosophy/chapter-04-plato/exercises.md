# Chapter 4 Exercises

---

## Comprehension and Analysis

**Exercise 1.** Plato describes the Demiurge as acting from goodness "without envy." Explain what this means philosophically and why it is significant for Plato's account of creation. How does the Demiurge's motivation connect to the claim that the created cosmos is genuinely good, even though it is ontologically inferior to the eternal model? Compare this to the Abrahamic doctrine of creation discussed in Chapter 2, Section 3.2.

**Exercise 2.** Analyze Plato's definition of time as "a moving image of eternity" (*Timaeus* 37d). Identify (a) the respects in which time is like eternity (the "image" aspect) and (b) the respects in which time differs from eternity (the "moving" and "temporal" aspects). What would it mean for an image to be a good image of eternity? What would it mean for it to be a poor one?

**Exercise 3.** Explain the distinction between *aion* (eternity) and *chronos* (time) as Plato uses these terms. Why does Plato say that "was" and "will be" should not be predicated of eternal being? How does Boethius's later formulation (*tota simul*) capture and extend this Platonic insight?

**Exercise 4.** Describe the Receptacle (*chora*) and explain its role in Plato's cosmogony. Why does Plato say we know it "by a kind of bastard reasoning"? What philosophical problem does the Receptacle solve, and what new philosophical problems does it introduce?

**Exercise 5.** In the *Republic*'s Divided Line, temporal things (perceptible objects) are assigned to the level of *pistis* (perception-based belief), not *episteme* (knowledge). Does this mean that natural science — physics, chemistry, biology — is merely opinion for Plato? Develop a careful response, drawing on Plato's distinction between knowledge and opinion and on the *Timaeus*'s account of a "likely story."

**Exercise 6.** Explain the Meno Paradox and Plato's response to it via the doctrine of recollection (*anamnesis*). How does the slave boy episode (*Meno* 82b–85e) illustrate recollection? Is the evidence it provides convincing? Reconstruct Aristotle's objection to the doctrine (from *Prior Analytics* 67a21–26) and evaluate it.

**Exercise 7.** Plato's *Timaeus* presents a creation myth — a "likely story" about the origin of the cosmos. The debate in scholarship is whether this is meant literally (there was an actual creation event) or metaphorically (the creation narrative describes structural features of the cosmos, not an historical event). What philosophical considerations bear on this question? Which interpretation do you find more defensible, and why?

**Exercise 8.** Compare Plato's account of the relationship between eternity and time with the Heraclitean account of the relationship between the Logos and temporal flux. Both accounts include a "rational principle" underlying the temporal world. How do they differ in their conception of this principle's relationship to time?

## Critical Thinking

**Exercise 9.** Consider the following objection to Plato's ontological hierarchy: "If eternal, unchanging Forms are more real than temporal things, then mathematical truth is more real than the existence of conscious persons. But surely the existence of a conscious person is at least as real as the truth of a mathematical theorem — indeed, it seems to be the precondition of any knowledge of such truths." Construct the strongest possible Platonic response to this objection. Then assess whether the response succeeds.

**Exercise 10.** Plato's account implies that temporal passage — the movement of time — reflects a kind of incompleteness or deficiency: time spreads out sequentially what eternity holds all at once. Is this a plausible picture of temporal passage? Compare it to A-theorist accounts that treat temporal passage as an objective feature of reality (not a deficiency) and B-theorist accounts that deny that time "passes" at all. Which picture is closest to Plato's, and why?

## Lab Exercises

**Lab 1: The Still Point and the Moving Image.** Find a clock with a circular face and a second hand. Observe the motion of the second hand for two minutes. Now: (a) Consider the circle that the second hand describes — a perfect, complete circle, "all at once" in a single geometrical figure. Now consider the second hand's motion, which takes 60 seconds to trace that circle. In what sense does the motion "image" the circle? In what sense is the circle "eternal" by comparison with the motion? (b) Write a one-page philosophical reflection on what this observation reveals about Plato's definition of time as a moving image of eternity. Try to identify both where the analogy is apt and where it breaks down.

**Lab 2: Recollection and Recognition.** This lab explores the difference between temporal memory (remembering past temporal events) and what Plato might call recollection (recognizing eternal truths). Take a mathematical proof that you have not seen before — a simple geometry proof from Euclid's *Elements* Book I (available freely online), such as Proposition 1 (constructing an equilateral triangle) or Proposition 5 (the base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal). Work through it carefully. Then reflect: when you follow the proof and see that each step must be so — does this feel like *learning something new from experience*, or like *recognizing something you could not have not known*? Write a one-page reflection on whether your experience of mathematical understanding provides any evidence for the Platonic doctrine that mathematical knowledge is "recollection."

**Lab 3: Becoming and Instability.** Choose an object in your environment — a plant, a piece of fruit, a candle, a glass of water with ice. Observe it closely at one-hour intervals over the course of a day and record your observations in as much detail as possible: color, shape, texture, temperature (estimated), smell, apparent state. At the end of the day, reflect: has the object changed? In what ways is it "the same object"? In what ways is it different? Now consider: is there any respect in which the object's properties are *necessary* and *unchanging* — any respect in which the object genuinely exemplifies a stable Form — and any respect in which it is purely in flux? Write a one-page reflection connecting your observations to Plato's distinction between the Forms (unchanging originals) and temporal things (changing images).
