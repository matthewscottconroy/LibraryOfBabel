# Chapter 4: Further Reading and Bibliography

---

## Annotated Further Reading

**Cornford, F. M. 1937. *Plato's Cosmology: The Timaeus of Plato*. London: Kegan Paul, Trench, Trübner.**
The classic English translation and commentary on the *Timaeus*. Cornford's commentary is philosophically engaged and historically informed. His discussion of the distinction between *aion* and *chronos* and his analysis of "time as a moving image of eternity" are still required reading, even if some of his interpretive conclusions have been challenged.

**Leftow, Brian. 1991. *Time and Eternity*. Ithaca: Cornell University Press.**
The most rigorous contemporary philosophical treatment of the distinction between temporal and eternal modes of existence. Leftow carefully distinguishes timelessness from sempiternity, addresses the theological implications of divine eternity, and provides detailed analysis of the arguments for and against various views. Demanding but essential for anyone interested in the eternity-time distinction.

**Plotinus. *Enneads* III.7: "On Eternity and Time." In *The Enneads*, translated by Stephen MacKenna. London: Faber and Faber, 1969.**
Plotinus's most important text for the philosophy of time. He argues that eternity is the simultaneous totality of Intellect's life, while time is the restless, sequential unfolding of the World Soul's activity — a temporal image of what Intellect holds all at once. The analysis is philosophically subtle and reads as a creative development of, rather than mere commentary on, the *Timaeus*.

**Plato. *Timaeus*. Translated by Donald Zeyl. In *Complete Works*, edited by John M. Cooper. Indianapolis: Hackett, 1997.**
The standard contemporary scholarly translation. Read alongside *Timaeus* 27c–38e for the cosmogony and the account of time; 48e–52d for the Receptacle; 37c–38e for the definition of time.

**Plato. *Meno*. Translated by G. M. A. Grube. In *Complete Works*, edited by John M. Cooper. Indianapolis: Hackett, 1997.**
Essential for the doctrine of recollection. The dialogue raises the paradox of inquiry (how can you learn what you don't know?) and provides the slave boy episode as an illustration of recollection. Read especially 80d–86c.

**Plato. *Phaedo*. Translated by G. M. A. Grube. In *Complete Works*, edited by John M. Cooper. Indianapolis: Hackett, 1997.**
The fullest treatment of the soul's immortality and its implications for knowledge. Particularly important for the philosophy of time is the discussion at 72e–77d of recollection and the soul's pre-temporal knowledge of the Forms.

**Sorabji, Richard. 1983. *Time, Creation and the Continuum: Theories in Antiquity and the Early Middle Ages*. London: Duckworth.**
The definitive scholarly treatment of ancient and early medieval philosophy of time. Sorabji covers every major figure from the Pre-Socratics through Boethius with rigorous scholarship and philosophical clarity. His chapters on Plato's *Timaeus*, on the creation-eternity debate, and on Aristotelian time are each worth reading in full.

**Stump, Eleonore, and Norman Kretzmann. 1981. "Eternity." *Journal of Philosophy* 78(8): 429–458.**
The most influential contemporary philosophical analysis of the distinction between timeless eternity and temporal existence. Stump and Kretzmann argue that an eternal being exists in a mode in which all of its life is simultaneously present (ET-simultaneous with every temporal moment). Though the paper focuses on divine eternity, it illuminates the philosophical content of the *aion*-*chronos* distinction directly.

**Vlastos, Gregory. 1964. "Creation in the *Timaeus*: Is It a Fiction?" In *Studies in Plato's Metaphysics*, edited by R. E. Allen, 401–419. London: Routledge and Kegan Paul.**
The classic statement of the metaphorical/structural interpretation of the *Timaeus* creation account. Vlastos argues that "creation" in the *Timaeus* should not be read as implying a temporal first moment; rather, it is a philosophical device for displaying the structural dependence of temporal order on eternal being.

**White, Nicholas P. 1976. *Plato on Knowledge and Reality*. Indianapolis: Hackett.**
An accessible and philosophically careful account of Plato's epistemology, including the theory of Forms and the Divided Line. White's treatment of the relationship between Platonic knowledge and temporal existence is particularly useful for understanding the epistemological dimensions of Plato's philosophy of time.
