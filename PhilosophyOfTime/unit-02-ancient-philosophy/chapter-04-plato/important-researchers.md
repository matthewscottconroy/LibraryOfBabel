# Chapter 4: Important Researchers and Thinkers

---

**Plato (428–347 BCE)**
The central figure of the chapter. Plato founded the Academy in Athens — the first institution of higher learning in the Western tradition. His dialogues, essentially all of which survive, constitute one of the most rich and philosophically productive bodies of work in the history of thought. The *Timaeus* is his main cosmological dialogue, central to the philosophy of time; the *Republic* provides the epistemological framework (Divided Line, Allegory of the Cave); the *Phaedo*, *Meno*, and *Phaedrus* develop the doctrine of the immortal soul and recollection; the *Parmenides* and *Sophist* engage critically with the theory of Forms.

**Timaeus of Locri**
The character who delivers the cosmological account in Plato's dialogue. He is described as a Pythagorean philosopher from Locri in southern Italy, expert in astronomy and mathematics. Whether he is based on a historical person of this name is uncertain; what matters philosophically is the Pythagorean character of the account he gives — its mathematical precision, its cosmological scope, and its treatment of the cosmos as a mathematically ordered living being.

**Plotinus (204–270 CE)**
The founder of Neoplatonism. Plotinus's *Enneads*, edited by his student Porphyry, contains the most systematic and philosophically sophisticated development of Platonic philosophy in antiquity. His treatise on time and eternity (*Enneads* III.7) is one of the greatest texts in the philosophy of time: it distinguishes Eternity as the simultaneous totality of Nous's intellectual life from Time as the sequential unfolding of the World Soul's restless activity. Plotinus's influence on subsequent philosophy of time — particularly through Augustine and medieval Christian thought — was enormous.

**Boethius (ca. 480–524 CE)**
Roman philosopher and statesman, author of the *Consolation of Philosophy*, written while awaiting execution on charges of treason. Book V of the *Consolation* contains the most influential medieval treatment of divine eternity and temporal existence. Boethius's definition of eternity — *interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio* — took up the Platonic *aion* and gave it a formulation that dominated medieval theology. He also addressed the problem of divine foreknowledge and free will, arguing that God's knowledge of future events is knowledge of a timeless present, not foreknowledge in the temporal sense.

**Gregory Vlastos (1907–1991)**
American philosopher and classicist. His 1964 paper "Creation in the *Timaeus*: Is It a Fiction?" is the definitive modern treatment of the debate about whether the *Timaeus* creation account is meant literally or metaphorically. Vlastos argued for the metaphorical interpretation — that the creation narrative is a device for revealing the structural dependence of temporal order on eternal being, not a claim about a historical act of creation.

**Richard Sorabji (1934–)**
British philosopher and classicist. His *Time, Creation and the Continuum* (1983) is the most comprehensive scholarly treatment of ancient philosophy of time, covering Aristotle, Plato, and their successors in depth. Essential reading for anyone who wants to study ancient philosophy of time seriously. Sorabji's treatment of the question of temporal creation is particularly valuable.

**F. M. Cornford (1874–1943)**
British classical scholar. His *Plato's Cosmology* (1937), a translation and commentary on the *Timaeus*, was the standard English-language treatment for decades. Cornford's commentary is philosophically sensitive and his translation remains readable, though it has been superseded in some respects by more recent scholarship.

**Donald Zeyl (1940–)**
American classicist. His translation of the *Timaeus* in the Hackett *Complete Works of Plato* (1997, edited by John M. Cooper) is the current standard scholarly translation in English. Zeyl's translation is accurate, readable, and accompanied by a useful introduction that situates the dialogue in Plato's overall philosophical project.

**Julia Annas (1946–)**
British-American philosopher. Her article "Aristotle, Number and Time" (1975) and related work provide important analysis of the relationship between number, time, and the *Timaeus* in the context of Aristotle's response to Plato. More generally, her work on ancient ethics and epistemology helps situate Plato's theory of knowledge — including its temporal dimensions — in the broader framework of ancient moral philosophy.

**Harold Cherniss (1904–1987)**
American classical scholar. Author of *Aristotle's Criticism of Plato and the Academy* (1944), a magisterial defense of Plato against Aristotle's criticisms. Cherniss's work is important for understanding the relationship between Plato's cosmogony and Aristotle's natural philosophy, and for assessing the Aristotelian objections to the *Timaeus* that recur throughout the tradition.

**Brian Leftow (1956–)**
American philosopher of religion. His *Time and Eternity* (1991) is the most rigorous and comprehensive contemporary treatment of the philosophical theology of divine eternity, building on both the Platonic tradition and analytic philosophy. Leftow carefully distinguishes timeless eternity from sempiternal existence and defends a version of the timeless eternity view.
