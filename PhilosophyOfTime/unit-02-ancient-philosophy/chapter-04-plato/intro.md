# Chapter 4: Plato on Time and Eternity

---

## Chapter Introduction

Plato (428–347 BCE) is the first philosopher to give a sustained, systematic account of time as part of a larger theory of reality, and his account in the *Timaeus* has exercised a defining influence on the Western tradition's way of framing the relationship between time and eternity. To understand Plato on time is to understand a whole philosophical picture — one that encompasses cosmology, metaphysics, epistemology, and theology — and to understand that picture is to understand many of the concepts and distinctions that have structured philosophical discussion ever since.

The central text is the *Timaeus*, a cosmological dialogue written in roughly 360 BCE in which a character named Timaeus (a Pythagorean philosopher from Locri, Italy) delivers a long account of the creation of the universe. The dialogue is a creation myth — but it is a philosophical creation myth, designed not merely to tell a story but to illustrate how the rational mind makes sense of temporal existence in relation to eternal truth.

The *Timaeus* is a difficult dialogue — Plato himself calls it a "likely story" (*eikôs logos* or *eikôs mythos*: *Timaeus* 29d, 59c), meaning a plausible account of a domain that does not permit the kind of strict demonstration available for mathematics. The physical world is the realm of becoming, not being; our best account of it will be probable, not certain. This epistemic humility about the physical world is not weakness but a reflection of Plato's fundamental distinction between the realm of eternal, unchanging being (accessible to knowledge) and the realm of temporal, changing becoming (accessible only to belief).

The chapter is organized around four interconnected themes.

First, we examine the *Timaeus* cosmogony itself: the figure of the Demiurge (the divine craftsman), the eternal model (the Living Being), and the creation of the ordered cosmos as an imperfect but genuine likeness of the eternal model. Understanding why the world was made and what it was made from gives the context for Plato's account of time.

Second, we examine Plato's famous definition of time as "a moving image of eternity" (*Timaeus* 37d) — one of the most beautiful and most philosophically dense sentences in the entire tradition. What does it mean for time to *image* eternity? What is the relationship between the numerical structure of time (measured by the celestial motions) and the eternal model it images?

Third, we examine the distinction between *aion* (eternity) and *chronos* (time) — two Greek words for temporal matters that Plato deploys with philosophical precision. Understanding this distinction requires understanding Plato's metaphysical hierarchy: the Forms, the Demiurge, the created cosmos, and the *Receptacle* — the strange, third kind of thing that underlies all temporal becoming.

Fourth, we turn to epistemological questions: what does Plato's temporal ontology imply about the possibility of knowledge? Can we have genuine knowledge (*episteme*) of temporal things, or only opinion (*doxa*)? And how does the famous doctrine of recollection (*anamnesis*) give time a role in the soul's relationship to eternal truth?

A note on method: throughout this chapter, citations to the *Timaeus* follow the standard Stephanus pagination, using Donald Zeyl's translation as the base text (Plato 1997).
