# 1.1 The Demiurge

---

## Why Was the World Made?

Before asking what time is, Plato asks why the world exists at all. The answer given in the *Timaeus* is among the most philosophically remarkable in all of ancient thought: the world was made by a divine craftsman who looked at an eternal model and fashioned the best possible temporal likeness of it, motivated simply by goodness.

Here is the key passage:

"Let us therefore state the reason why the maker of this universe of change framed it at all. He was good, and what is good has no particle of envy in it. Being without envy, he wished everything to be as like himself as possible. This is the supremely valid principle of becoming and of the ordered world, as we shall do well to believe on the testimony of men of insight. God, wishing that all things should be good and so far as possible nothing imperfect, and finding the visible universe in a state not of rest but of inharmonious and disorderly motion, reduced it to order from disorder, as he considered that order was in every way better than disorder." (Plato, *Timaeus* 29d–30a, trans. Cornford 1937: 33–34)

Several features of this passage repay close attention.

### The Demiurge's Nature

The Demiurge (*dêmiourgos* — literally "craftsman" or "artisan") is not the omnipotent creator-god of Abrahamic theology. He is a *craftsman*, not a creator ex nihilo: he works with pre-existing material (the *Receptacle* or *chora*, discussed in Section 3) and looks to a pre-existing model (the Living Being). His creativity is not unlimited production from nothing but the rational imposition of order on prior disorder.

This matters philosophically. The Demiurge is constrained: by the nature of the model (which he cannot violate, only approximate) and by the nature of the material (which resists complete ordering). The result is not a perfect reproduction of the eternal model but the best possible temporal likeness of it, given the constraints of material and change. Temporal reality is genuinely good — genuinely ordered, genuinely intelligible — but it is imperfect goodness, imperfect order, imperfect intelligibility compared to the eternal original.

### Goodness Without Envy

The reason for creation is the Demiurge's goodness. He lacks envy (*phthonos*): there is nothing in him that would want to keep goodness to himself or to withhold it from things that could partake of it. A less good maker might have been satisfied with a static, isolated perfection. The Demiurge, being entirely good, wants the good to be shared, to be expressed, to be realized as fully as possible in the material domain.

This is a remarkable claim. It makes creation an expression of the diffusive nature of the good — the principle that goodness, by its very nature, wants to spread and share itself. This principle — that the good is self-diffusive — became one of the most important ideas in Neoplatonist philosophy (Plotinus, *Enneads* V.1.6; V.2.1) and was later taken up by Christian theology in accounts of divine creation. Creation, on this view, is not an arbitrary act or a response to divine need but an expression of the essential nature of goodness.

For the philosophy of time, this has an important implication: time, as the medium of the created temporal cosmos, is not evil or defective in its nature. It is imperfect by comparison with eternity, but it is genuine and good — a real (if incomplete) expression of the eternal good. This distinguishes Platonic temporal ontology from purely dualistic views that see the temporal world as simply bad or illusory.

### The Model: The Living Being

What model does the Demiurge look to? It is "the Living Being," the eternal Form that encompasses all the intelligible living forms (*Timaeus* 30c–31a). The Living Being is the most perfect and comprehensive of the Forms — an intelligible totality that contains within itself the formal principles of all living things.

The Demiurge makes the cosmos in the likeness of this model — as "one visible living thing that embraces all the visible living things within it" (*Timaeus* 30d, Zeyl trans.). The cosmos is a *living animal* (the Greek *zôon* means both "living creature" and "animal"), not a dead machine. It is alive, it is rational, and it is temporal because it is made in the likeness of an eternal model that it can only imperfectly reproduce.

### Why Not Simply Make an Eternal World?

A natural question arises: if eternity is better than time, why didn't the Demiurge simply make an eternal world? Why make something temporal at all?

Plato does not address this question explicitly, but the answer is implicit in the account. The Demiurge makes the cosmos from pre-existing material (the Receptacle), and material — by its very nature — is subject to change. The Receptacle is the medium of becoming, of spatial location and material realization; it is not the medium of eternal, changeless being. A temporal material world is the only kind of world that can be made from material at all. Eternity is the condition of pure, immaterial being; anything material and physical is, by necessity, temporal.

This connects to a deep theme in Plato's metaphysics: matter is the source of imperfection, instability, and temporal change; form is the source of intelligibility, stability, and approximation to eternity. A perfectly material world cannot be eternal; a perfectly eternal being cannot be material. The created cosmos is a compromise — the best possible material image of the eternal model — and time is the medium of this compromise.

### The Cosmogonic Myth and Philosophical Interpretation

Scholars have debated whether the creation narrative in the *Timaeus* should be understood literally (as describing an actual act of creation at a specific time) or metaphorically (as a way of analyzing the structural features of the cosmos without implying that there was a first moment). Aristotle took Plato to be a literal creationist; Speusippus and Xenocrates argued for a metaphorical reading; the debate continues in modern scholarship (Vlastos 1964; Sorabji 1983: 268–283).

For the philosophy of time, the question is significant: if the creation is literal, then time has a genuine first moment and was genuinely absent before. If it is metaphorical, then the account is saying something about the *structural* dependence of temporal order on eternal being, not about a temporal origin. In either case, the central philosophical claim — that time is ontologically dependent on and derivative of eternity — stands.
