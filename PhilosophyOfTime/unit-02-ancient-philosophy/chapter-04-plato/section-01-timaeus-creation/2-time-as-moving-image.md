# 1.2 Time as a Moving Image of Eternity

---

## The Most Beautiful Sentence in the Philosophy of Time

At *Timaeus* 37d, Plato gives what is arguably the most celebrated definition of time in the entire philosophical tradition:

"So he [the Demiurge] began to think of making a moving image of eternity: at the same time as he brought order to the universe, he would make an eternal image, moving according to number, of eternity remaining in unity. This is, of course, what we call 'time.'" (Plato, *Timaeus* 37d, trans. Zeyl)

The sentence deserves slow, careful unpacking. Every word is doing philosophical work.

### "A Moving Image"

The Greek word for "image" is *eikôn* — from which the English "icon" is derived. An eikon is not a copy in the degraded sense of a counterfeit; it is a *likeness*, a representation that genuinely participates in or reflects the nature of what it represents. A portrait of a person is an eikon: it is genuinely like the person (it represents their features), even though it differs from the person in being flat, static, and not alive.

Time is a *moving* image — *kinêton tina aiônos eikona* — not a static one. This is essential. Eternity is the realm of stable, unchanging being; you cannot make an image of it that is also unchanging, because that would be another eternal, not an image. The image must be temporal — it must move, change, cycle — and yet in its motion it must genuinely represent the structure of eternity.

Think of a rotating clock hand. The hand moves in a circle, returning periodically to its starting point. In this motion, it approximates the unchanging completeness of a circle — returning to the beginning, recreating a whole, cycling through all its positions. The completeness of the circle is timeless; the clock hand's traversal of it is temporal. The hand's motion is a temporal image of the circle's timeless completeness.

This is, roughly, what Plato means by time as a moving image of eternity: the cyclical motions of the celestial bodies — the sun, moon, planets, and stars — cycle through their positions, returning periodically, and in this periodicity they approximate the timeless completeness of the eternal model.

### "Of Eternity Remaining in Unity"

The eternal model remains in unity — *menontôs aiônos en heni* — it does not move, does not change, does not spread itself across time. Eternity is whole, complete, self-identical. Time, by contrast, proceeds "according to number" — sequentially, one moment after another, spreading out what is in eternity simultaneously concentrated in a single, undivided whole.

Consider the number 1 as an analogy. The number 1 is simply 1 — complete, whole, undivided. Now suppose you want to represent 1 in base 10 notation by adding fractions: 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... converging to 1. The process is infinite; 1 itself is complete and simple. The infinite series "images" 1 in the sense that it approaches it, approximates it, takes it as its limit — but it spreads out over infinitely many terms what 1 simply is all at once.

Time is like the infinite series; eternity is like 1. Time approximates eternity by proceeding sequentially through what eternity encompasses all at once. The temporal cosmos cycles through its seasons, its astronomical periods, its Great Year — and in doing so, it approximates the completeness of the eternal model. But it can never fully achieve it: there is always another cycle ahead, always another moment not yet arrived.

### "Moving According to Number"

The phrase *kat' arithmon iousan* — "moving according to number" — gives Platonic time its mathematical character. Time is not just any motion; it is *numbered* motion. The celestial bodies move according to precise numerical ratios; their periods can be expressed as numbers; the Great Year is the period after which all the numbers come back into alignment. Time, in this sense, is the mathematical dimension of the temporal cosmos's movement toward its eternal model.

This connects to the Pythagorean inheritance in the *Timaeus*. The Demiurge, in constructing the World Soul (the intelligent animating principle of the cosmos), uses mathematical ratios — the same ratios that govern musical harmony — to structure its composition (*Timaeus* 35a–36d). The World Soul is literally a mathematical structure; its motion produces the celestial orbits; and the orbits produce time. Time is therefore mathematically structured at its very foundation.

For Plato, this mathematical character of time is not a contingent feature; it is what makes time genuinely representative of eternity. The eternal Forms are objects of mathematical knowledge: they have precise, necessary structure, accessible to reason. Time approximates them by having a mathematical structure of its own — a structure that can be known by reason, not just perceived by the senses.

### The Asymmetry Between Eternity and Time

Several important asymmetries follow from Plato's definition.

**Ontological asymmetry.** Eternity is more real than time. The eternal Forms — including the Living Being — are the originals; the temporal cosmos is a copy. Copies are ontologically dependent on originals; they exist insofar as and because the originals exist. Time therefore depends on eternity ontologically, not the reverse.

**Epistemic asymmetry.** Knowledge of eternal things is stable, certain, and accessible to reason. Knowledge of temporal things is unstable, merely probable, and accessible primarily to perception. This is why the *Timaeus* account of the cosmos is a "likely story" — because its subject matter (the temporal, material cosmos) does not support the kind of certain knowledge that mathematics or philosophy of Forms can provide.

**Evaluative asymmetry.** Eternity is better than time. The goal of the Demiurge was to make something as good as possible; the constraint was the material, which is inherently temporal. Time represents a genuine good — it is the best possible temporal approximation of eternity — but it is imperfect goodness by comparison.

### Philosophical Assessment

Plato's definition of time as a moving image of eternity is one of the most influential and most debated in the history of philosophy. Its strengths include: it gives time a positive ontological status (not mere illusion but genuine image); it connects temporal structure to mathematical necessity (time is not arbitrary, it is governed by numerical ratios); and it explains the cosmological significance of astronomical cycles (they are the most visible expression of time's approximation to eternity).

Its difficulties are equally notable. The notion of "imaging" eternity is metaphorical and needs unpacking: in what precise sense does numerical, cyclical motion represent timeless being? The account seems to imply that temporal passage is, in some sense, a defect — an inability to be all at once — rather than a positive feature. And the close identification of time with celestial motion raises questions: what happens to time if the celestial bodies stop moving? Is time simply identical with those motions, or are the motions merely the measure of something else?

These questions were taken up with great energy by Plotinus in the Neoplatonist tradition and by Augustine, each of whom developed sophisticated responses to the Platonic account.
