# Section 1: The Timaeus Cosmogony

---

## Section Introduction

The *Timaeus* begins in the middle of a conversation — we are told that Socrates had the previous day given an account of the ideal city, and now Timaeus, Critias, and Hermocrates have gathered to respond with an account of the origin of the cosmos. Timaeus speaks first, and what he delivers is not a refutation of anything but a construction: a full-scale account of how the universe came to be, what it is made of, and why it has the temporal structure it does.

The cosmogony is explicitly presented as a "likely story" (*eikôs logos*) rather than a demonstration (*apodeixis*). Plato is clear that the physical world, being in the domain of becoming rather than being, can only be described with probable accounts, not strict knowledge (*Timaeus* 29c–d). This epistemic humility is part of the philosophical content: the story of creation is as accurate as any story about a temporal, changing world can be.

The heart of the cosmogony is a distinction between three kinds of things: the eternal model (the Living Being, the Form of all forms), the Demiurge (the rational craftsman who looks to the model and makes the cosmos in its likeness), and the product (the created cosmos, a temporal image of the eternal model). Understanding each of these — and their relations to each other — is essential for understanding Plato's philosophy of time.

This section has two subsections. The first examines the Demiurge: his nature, his motivations, and the role he plays in the cosmogony. The second introduces the relationship between the eternal model and its temporal copy, leading into the discussion of time as a moving image.
