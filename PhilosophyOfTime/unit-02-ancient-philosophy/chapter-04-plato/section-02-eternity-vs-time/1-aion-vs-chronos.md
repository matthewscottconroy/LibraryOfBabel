# 2.1 Aion vs. Chronos

---

## Two Greek Words for Time

Philosophical vocabulary is never entirely innocent, and the Greek vocabulary for time repays careful attention. The two primary Greek words for time are *aion* and *chronos*, and their relationship is philosophically significant in the *Timaeus* in a way that is easily lost in translation.

*Chronos* is the ordinary Greek word for time: the time of the clock and calendar, the time that is measured and counted, the time in which events occur and are located relative to each other. It is the time of ordinary human experience — the time that divides past from present from future, the time that is measured by the motions of the sun and moon, the time that can be "wasted" or "saved."

*Aion* has a more complex semantic history (Onians 1951: 200–228). In Homer, *aion* means something like "vital force" or "life span." In the fifth-century lyric poets, it can mean "lifetime" or "generation." But in philosophical usage — beginning decisively with the *Timaeus* — it acquires the meaning of *eternity*: not endless duration, but a mode of being that is categorically distinct from temporal existence.

In the key passage of the *Timaeus*, Plato uses *aion* for the eternal model and *chronos* for its temporal image. The sentence at 37d reads: "he decided to make a moving image of *aion* ... this is what we call *chronos*." The distinction is explicit and deliberate.

### What Makes Aion Different from Endless Chronos

The crucial question is: what exactly is the difference between (a) *aion* as *eternity* — a mode of being utterly outside time — and (b) *aion* as *endless time* — an infinite temporal duration?

Plato's answer is in the grammar of the eternal. The eternal model, Plato says, "was and is and will be" — but wait, Plato immediately corrects himself: "only 'is' applies to it in strict truth; 'was' and 'will be' are forms of time that have come to be" (*Timaeus* 37e, Zeyl trans.). The past tense ("was") implies that something is no longer; the future tense ("will be") implies that something has not yet come to be. Both carry a reference to a present moment relative to which they are defined. And the eternal has no relationship to a present moment — it is not "now" rather than "then"; it simply *is*.

The distinction, then, is between:

(a) **Eternal being (aion)**: Being that is wholly present to itself, not distributed across time, without before or after in its own nature. The word "is" applies to it, but "was" and "will be" do not. It is not that it exists at all times; it is that it exists in a mode to which temporal location is inapplicable.

(b) **Temporal existence (chronos)**: Existence that is distributed across time, in which the past is gone, the future not yet arrived, and the present is a brief transition between them. The words "was," "is," and "will be" all apply to temporal things, shifting in their application as time passes.

This distinction anticipates the medieval philosophical debate about the nature of divine eternity. Boethius, in the *Consolation of Philosophy*, draws the contrast in terms that are explicitly Platonic: eternity is not the *whole* of an infinite life spread out through time, but the *simultaneous* possession of a complete life (*interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio*) — all of it at once, not extended over time (Boethius, *Consolatio* V.6, Relihan trans.). The contrast is between a *tota simul* eternity (all-at-once) and a sempiternal existence (existing at all times in sequence).

### The Forms and Their Timeless Existence

The Forms — the eternal, unchanging objects of Platonic knowledge — exist in the mode of *aion*. The Form of Beauty is not beautiful *now* (as opposed to being ugly yesterday or tomorrow). It is simply, unqualifiedly beautiful — at all times, outside of time, in a mode of being to which temporal qualifications do not apply. It is not in time at all.

This is why knowledge of the Forms is not temporal knowledge. When you genuinely know that the sum of the angles of a Euclidean triangle equals 180 degrees, you are knowing something that is true regardless of when you know it, that does not change over time, that does not depend on any particular moment of temporal existence. Mathematical truths are timeless in this sense; and for Plato, mathematical knowledge is a model for knowledge of the Forms in general.

The philosopher who achieves genuine knowledge of the Forms has, in that moment of knowledge, a direct cognitive relationship with the eternal — with *aion* rather than *chronos*. This is not a merely academic accomplishment; it is, for Plato, a transformation of the self, a moment in which the soul turns away from the temporal, changing world and toward the eternal, unchanging reality that underlies it.

### Aion in Later Philosophy

The *Timaeus* distinction between *aion* and *chronos* was enormously influential. The Neoplatonists — particularly Plotinus in the *Enneads* — developed it into a full metaphysical hierarchy. For Plotinus, Eternity (*aion*) is a property of Nous (Intellect or Mind) — the eternal intellectual activity of contemplation in which all the Forms are simultaneously present and active. Time (*chronos*) is a property of Soul — the World Soul's perpetual, sequential activity that cannot grasp all the Forms at once but cycles through them, producing temporal succession (*Enneads* III.7).

This Neoplatonist development takes up the Platonic insight that time is the Soul's way of approximating Intellect's timeless completeness — a temporal spread-out of what is, in Intellect, gathered up in a single, atemporal act of knowing.
