# 2.2 Becoming and the Ontology of the Image

---

## Why Temporal Existence Is Ontologically Inferior

Plato's metaphysics is hierarchical. Some things are more real, more good, and more knowable than others. The Forms are at the top: fully real, fully good, fully knowable. The temporal world of becoming is at the bottom of the ontological scale (above pure formlessness, but well below the Forms). Understanding why Plato places temporal existence so low in the hierarchy is crucial for understanding his philosophy of time.

### The Divided Line

In the *Republic* (509d–511e), Plato introduces the famous image of the *Divided Line* to illustrate his epistemological and ontological hierarchy. The line is divided into two major sections: the Intelligible (the realm of the Forms, known through reason) and the Visible (the realm of images and ordinary objects, known through perception and belief). Each major section is in turn divided into two sub-sections, giving four levels in total.

In ascending order:
1. **Images** (reflections, shadows): the lowest level of the visible realm. Known through imagination or conjecture (*eikasia*).
2. **Ordinary visible things** (animals, plants, artifacts): the level of ordinary perceptual objects. Known through perception-based belief (*pistis*).
3. **Mathematical objects** (numbers, geometric figures): the lower level of the intelligible realm. Known through hypothetical reasoning (*dianoia*).
4. **The Forms** (including the Form of the Good): the highest level. Known through pure rational insight (*noêsis*).

Temporal things — the things that come to be and pass away — fall at levels 2 and possibly 3 of this hierarchy. They are genuine objects of perception-based belief; they are real enough to be seen, touched, and interacted with. But they are not objects of the highest knowledge; they are not fully real; they are more like images of Forms than like Forms themselves.

### Why Temporal Things Are Ontologically Inferior

The reason temporal things are ontologically inferior is that they lack the stability, unity, and self-identity that full reality requires. Consider a beautiful face: it is beautiful now, but it was not beautiful in infancy and will not be beautiful in extreme age. It is beautiful only *to some* observers and under some conditions of light and angle. It is beautiful in one respect (the eyes) and not in another (some feature that does not please). In all these ways, it falls short of the Form of Beauty, which is beautiful always, to everyone, in every respect, unconditionally (Plato, *Symposium* 211a–b).

The same applies to all temporal predicates. A temporal thing is F at one time and not-F at another. A Form is simply F, without temporal qualification, without exception, without the hedging that temporal predication requires. The Forms "always are" (*aei onta*) what they are; temporal things "come to be" (*gignomena*) what they temporarily are, and they pass away.

This instability — the characteristic of temporal things — is what makes them less real, less good, and less knowable than the Forms. Reality, for Plato, is a matter of *what you can count on*: the Forms can always be counted on to be exactly what they are; temporal things cannot.

### The Image and Its Original

The metaphysics of the image (*eikôn*) is philosophically subtle. An image is not an illusion; it is not nothing. The reflection of a tree in still water is a real image — it is really there, it has real properties, it can be seen. But it is ontologically dependent on the tree: if the tree were destroyed, the reflection would vanish. The tree can exist without the reflection; the reflection cannot exist without the tree.

Temporal things stand to the Forms as images stand to originals. They have genuine existence — they are really there, they have real properties — but they are ontologically dependent on the Forms that they image. They exist *because of* and *by participation in* the Forms. A temporal beautiful thing is beautiful insofar as it participates in (shares in, imitates, images) the Form of Beauty.

And crucially: as images, temporal things have the defects of images. A shadow of a tree looks like the tree but lacks its texture, color, three-dimensionality, and life. A temporal beautiful thing approximates the Form of Beauty but lacks its stability, universality, and necessity. The defects of images — imprecision, instability, context-dependence — are the defects of temporal things.

### Temporality and Opinion

This ontological hierarchy has direct epistemological consequences. The *Republic*'s Divided Line indicates that perception-based belief (*doxa*) — opinion — is the cognitive state appropriate to temporal things. We do not *know* (in the strict Platonic sense) temporal things; we form beliefs about them, beliefs that may be accurate and well-justified but that can always turn out to be wrong because their objects can always turn out to have changed.

This is not a counsel of cognitive despair. Plato does not say that beliefs about temporal things are worthless or that we should ignore the temporal world. He says that the cognitive achievement appropriate to temporal things is belief, not knowledge; and that beliefs about temporal things, however accurate, do not achieve the stability and certainty of genuine knowledge of the eternal.

The practical implication is that the philosopher should not confuse accurate perception of temporal things with genuine understanding. Understanding requires knowledge of the Forms; knowledge of the Forms is not temporal. The philosopher who mistakes a beautiful face for the Form of Beauty has mistaken an image for an original — and, for Plato, this is not only an intellectual mistake but a moral and existential one: a life lived in pursuit of temporal beauties, rather than eternal Beauty, is a life oriented toward the inferior rather than the superior, toward becoming rather than being.

### Philosophical Assessment

Plato's ontological hierarchy has been enormously influential and enormously contested. The standard objection — the "Third Man Argument" that Aristotle raised and that appears in Plato's own *Parmenides* — questions the coherence of the participation relationship: if a beautiful thing is beautiful because it participates in the Form of Beauty, what makes the Form of Beauty itself beautiful? Another Form? And then another? (Plato, *Parmenides* 132a–b; Aristotle, *Metaphysics* 990b17–991a8.) This regress threatens the whole framework.

More relevant to the philosophy of time is the question of whether temporal existence is really as defective as Plato suggests. Is it true that we cannot "count on" temporal things in the way we count on mathematical truths? Many philosophers, including Aristotle, have thought that temporal existence has its own kind of reliability and its own kind of knowability — that science of temporal, changing things is possible, even if it differs from mathematical knowledge. We will examine this in Chapter 5.
