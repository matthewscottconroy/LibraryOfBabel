# Section 2: Eternity vs. Time

---

## Section Introduction

Plato's philosophy of time cannot be understood apart from his account of eternity. The two concepts are defined in relation to each other: time is what eternity is *not*, and yet time is what it is precisely because it *approximates* eternity. To understand what it means for time to image eternity, we need to understand what eternity is.

The Greek philosophical tradition distinguishes two ways in which something can be "without temporal limitation." Something is *sempiternal* if it exists at all times — if it has always existed and always will exist, but within time. The sun, on a certain picture, might be sempiternal: it has existed since the universe began and will exist until the universe ends. Something is *eternal* in the stronger sense if it exists *outside* time altogether — not "at all times" but in a mode of being to which temporal predications (before, after, duration) simply do not apply.

Plato's Forms are eternal in the stronger sense. They are not just very old; they are not temporal at all. The Form of Beauty does not have a duration; it is not located in time at all; it has not existed since some particular moment and will not cease to exist at some future moment. Its being is radically different in kind from temporal being.

This section examines two aspects of Plato's eternity-time distinction. The first concerns the Greek terms *aion* and *chronos* and what Plato intends by them. The second concerns the ontology of the realm of *becoming* — why temporal existence is ontologically inferior to eternal existence — and what this implies for Plato's system.
