# 3.1 The Receptacle (Chora)

---

## The Third Kind

The Receptacle (*chora* — literally "space" or "room," though in the *Timaeus* it functions as more than just physical space) is introduced by Plato with unusual philosophical caution. It is "difficult and vague" (*argalepê kai amydra*); it is known "by a kind of bastard reasoning" (*logismô tini nothô*); it is "perceptible to a sort of perception that is illegitimate" (*Timaeus* 52b, Zeyl trans.). These warnings are themselves philosophically significant: the Receptacle is at the limit of what Platonic philosophy can handle with its normal tools.

Here is Plato's main description:

"It is invisible and characterless, all-receiving, partaking in some very puzzling way of the intelligible and very hard to catch: we shall not be wrong if we call it the receptacle and, as it were, the nurse of all becoming and change." (*Timaeus* 49a, Taylor trans.)

What is being described here? Let us work through the main features.

### "All-Receiving" and "Characterless"

The Receptacle is *all-receiving* (*pandechês*): it receives all the impressions of the Forms. Think of it as a wax tablet that receives the impressions of seals: the wax itself has no shape of its own, but it takes on whatever shape is pressed into it. The Receptacle is the "wax" of the cosmos: it has no inherent qualities of its own, but it can receive and display the characteristics that correspond to the Forms.

It is *characterless* precisely because it receives all characters. If the Receptacle were, say, hot by nature, it would resist receiving "cold" — the Form of Cold could not appear in it. If it were blue, the Form of Red could not fully appear. To receive all qualities equally, it must be intrinsically colorless, temperatureless, textureless. It must be a kind of pure receptivity, without any positive nature that would interfere with the reception of Form.

This creates a philosophical puzzle. If the Receptacle has no positive character, what kind of thing is it? Is it a *substance* (a thing in the Aristotelian sense)? It does not seem to be: it has no essential properties, no natural kind. Is it a *property* or *accident*? No, because it is the *substrate* of properties, not a property itself. Is it simply *space* — the spatial extension in which things are located? This is tempting (and *chora* does mean "space"), but the Receptacle seems to be more than just space: it is the *material* dimension of temporal existence, the *stuff* out of which temporal things are made, not just the place in which they are located.

### The Gold Analogy

Plato illustrates the Receptacle with an analogy to gold (*Timaeus* 50a–b). Suppose a goldsmith makes many different shapes — animals, vessels, tools — out of gold. When asked "what is that?" referring to the gold that takes all these shapes, the best answer is "gold" rather than naming any of the particular shapes, since the gold persists through all the transformations. The shapes come and go; the gold remains.

The Receptacle is like the gold: it is what persists through all the transformations of the temporal world. As the Forms impress themselves on the Receptacle, temporal things appear and disappear — like shapes being made and remade in the gold. The Receptacle is the permanent, characterless substrate of all this change.

The analogy is illuminating but imperfect. Gold is a specific substance with specific properties (it is yellow, heavy, malleable). The Receptacle has no such properties; it is gold stripped of all its qualities, leaving only pure material receptivity.

### The Receptacle and Space-Time

Modern readers have naturally wondered about the relationship between the Receptacle and modern concepts of space and time. The *chora* is clearly spatial — it provides the *where* of temporal things, their location. But it also seems to be temporal — it provides the *when*, the medium of temporal change.

Julia Annas (1975: 116–119) and others have argued that the Receptacle is better understood as something like a *spatial substrate* (a "space" in the modern sense) into which Forms are projected. But Harold Cherniss (1954) and later scholars have argued that the Receptacle has a temporal as well as spatial dimension — that it is the medium of *becoming* in general, not just spatial location.

This question has been taken up in modern physics. In relativity theory, spacetime is a four-dimensional manifold that provides the "container" for all events — somewhat analogous to the Receptacle. And in some interpretations of quantum field theory, the vacuum (the ground state of quantum fields, which is not "empty" in the ordinary sense) plays a role analogous to the Receptacle: it is the characterless substrate from which particles appear and into which they disappear. These parallels are suggestive but should not be over-stressed; Plato is not doing physics.

### "A Kind of Bastard Reasoning"

Perhaps the most interesting feature of the Receptacle's description is Plato's claim that we know it "by a kind of bastard reasoning" — by an illegitimate, not-quite-rational mode of cognition. Why?

The Receptacle cannot be known as the Forms are known (by pure rational intuition), because it is not an intelligible Form — it has no positive rational structure for the intellect to grasp. It cannot be known as ordinary sensible objects are known (by perception), because it has no perceptible qualities. It is known only by a kind of inference from what we observe: we see temporal things changing, and we infer that there must be a substrate — something that receives the changing impressions — even if we cannot directly observe or rationally grasp it as such.

This is philosophically important: it reveals the limits of Platonic epistemology. The Receptacle is necessary to account for the material, spatial, temporal character of the world — but it resists both the intellectual gaze (which requires a positive Form to grasp) and the perceptual gaze (which requires perceptible qualities to detect). It is at the margins of the Platonic system, a necessary but recalcitrant element.

### Philosophical Significance for the Philosophy of Time

The Receptacle's relevance to the philosophy of time is threefold.

First, it shows that temporal existence is not merely a degraded copy of eternal existence; it also has a material dimension — the Receptacle — that the Forms alone cannot account for. Temporal things are constituted by both Form (the intelligible structure they instantiate) and the Receptacle (the material medium in which they are realized). The material dimension introduces an irreducible element of contingency, instability, and particularity.

Second, the Receptacle is the "nurse of becoming": it is what makes temporal change possible. Forms do not change; the Demiurge does not change. What changes is the pattern of Form-impressions in the Receptacle. Temporal change is change *in* the Receptacle — a succession of different patterns appearing in the same underlying characterless medium.

Third, the Receptacle raises one of the deepest questions in metaphysics: what is the relationship between form and matter, between intelligible structure and physical realization? This question will preoccupy Aristotle (who transforms it into the hylomorphic framework of form and matter), medieval philosophy (in discussions of prime matter and substantial form), and modern philosophy (in discussions of abstract versus concrete objects and the mind-body problem).
