# 4.1 Knowledge, Eternity, and Recollection

---

## Can We Know Temporal Things?

Plato's answer to the question "can we know temporal things?" is nuanced and sometimes appears contradictory. The strong answer, suggested by the *Republic*, is no: knowledge (*episteme*) is of the unchanging, and temporal things change, so they can only be objects of opinion (*doxa*). The more nuanced answer, suggested by the *Meno* and *Phaedo*, is that temporal experience can be the occasion for genuine knowledge — not because the temporal things themselves are known, but because encountering them triggers the soul's recovery of knowledge it already has from a pre-temporal condition.

To understand this position, we need to examine both the *Republic*'s epistemology and the doctrine of recollection.

### The Divided Line and Temporal Knowledge

In the *Republic*'s Divided Line (509d–511e), Plato assigns different cognitive faculties to different levels of reality:

- *Noêsis* (intellectual intuition, dialectic): the highest cognitive state, directed at the Forms, including the Form of the Good.
- *Dianoia* (mathematical reasoning): the cognitive state of the mathematician, using hypotheses and diagrams.
- *Pistis* (perception-based belief): the cognitive state directed at ordinary perceptible things — animals, plants, artifacts.
- *Eikasia* (conjecture, imagination): the lowest cognitive state, directed at images and reflections of perceptible things.

Temporal things — the perceptible objects of ordinary experience — fall under *pistis*. We form *beliefs* about them, beliefs that can be more or less accurate, more or less well-justified. But *pistis* is not *episteme* in the strict sense: it is not knowledge of the necessary, the unchanging, the eternal. It is tracking the world as it temporarily is rather than grasping how things must be.

This seems to imply that science of temporal things — natural science, including what we would call physics, chemistry, and biology — cannot achieve the status of genuine knowledge in Plato's strictest sense. And indeed, the *Timaeus*'s "likely story" acknowledges this: the best cosmological account is probable, not certain, precisely because its subject matter is temporal and changing.

However, Plato is not dismissing natural science as worthless. He is categorizing it: it occupies a genuine cognitive level, appropriate to its objects, below the mathematical sciences and the philosophy of Forms but above mere unstructured perception.

### The Meno and the Paradox of Inquiry

The *Meno* (80d–e) raises a paradox about knowledge and time: how can you inquire after what you do not know? If you know what you are looking for, the inquiry is unnecessary (you already have the answer). If you do not know what you are looking for, you would not recognize it when you found it. Therefore inquiry is impossible.

Plato's response is the doctrine of recollection (*anamnesis*): "Learning is nothing but recollection" (*Meno* 81d, Grube trans.). The soul, being immortal, has existed before its present embodied life. In its pre-embodied state, it had direct acquaintance with the Forms. When it enters the body and undergoes temporal experience, it forgets what it knew. But the knowledge is not lost — it is dormant, latent, recoverable.

Temporal experience — seeing, touching, experiencing beautiful or just or equal things — serves as a *trigger* for recollection: it reminds the soul of the eternal Forms it once knew directly. When you see two sticks of approximately equal length and recognize that they are *almost but not quite* equal, you are recognizing them as imperfect instances of a standard — the Form of Equality itself — that you know from before your temporal existence (*Phaedo* 74b–75a).

### Anamnesis as a Temporal-Epistemic Structure

The doctrine of recollection is a deeply interesting temporal-epistemic structure. It reverses the ordinary direction of knowledge acquisition: instead of learning things from temporal experience (as empiricists suggest), the soul *recovers* knowledge that precedes and survives temporal experience. Temporal experience is not the source of knowledge but the occasion for its recovery.

This has several philosophical implications.

**The soul's temporal and atemporal dimensions.** The soul in its full nature transcends temporal existence: it was present before this temporal life and will be present after it. Its genuine cognitive life — the life of *noêsis*, of direct acquaintance with the Forms — is atemporal. But its embodied, temporal life includes the experience of recollection: the temporal process of recovering what was anemporally known. Recollection is thus a temporal process oriented toward an atemporal content.

**The significance of temporal ignorance.** The soul's forgetting upon embodiment is not merely unfortunate; it is, within the Platonic framework, almost necessary: embodiment in the material world involves immersion in the temporal, changing, sensible realm, and this immersion naturally produces forgetfulness of the eternal. The temporal world and the eternal world are not merely ontologically distinct; they are in tension cognitively. Temporal experience pulls the soul toward the particular, the changing, the sensory; philosophical inquiry redirects it toward the eternal.

**The asymmetry between memory and recollection.** Ordinary memory is the recovery of past temporal events: I remember what happened yesterday. But *anamnesis* is the recovery of atemporal knowledge — knowledge that was never temporally acquired, because it was acquired in a pre-temporal condition. This is not temporal memory; it is a different kind of cognitive recovery. The *Meno* (82b–85e) illustrates this with the famous episode in which Socrates draws out geometrical knowledge from an uneducated slave boy through questioning: the boy did not learn the geometry from temporal experience; he recovers it through reflection prompted by the questioning.

### Philosophical Assessment

The doctrine of recollection faces serious objections. It presupposes the pre-existence of the soul — a substantial metaphysical commitment that requires independent argument. It does not obviously explain the *content* of what is recollected: if all knowledge is recollection, how was the knowledge acquired in the first place? Aristotle raises this objection in the *Prior Analytics* (67a21–26): if all learning is recollection, you would need to have known things before knowing them, which seems impossible.

But the doctrine illuminates something philosophically genuine: the distinction between temporal acquisition and atemporal validity. Mathematical truths are not "learned" in the same sense that historical facts are learned; they are more like truths that, once understood, seem to have been always true. The *Meno*'s slave boy episode illustrates how reflection — not experience — can produce genuine understanding of necessary truths. Whatever we make of the metaphysics of pre-existence, the epistemological point stands: some knowledge is not temporal in the sense of being acquired from temporal experience, but in the sense of grasping structures that are themselves atemporal.

For the philosophy of time, Plato's account suggests a rich and complex relationship between temporal existence and temporal knowledge. We are temporal beings; our ordinary cognitive life is temporal — memory of the past, perception of the present, anticipation of the future. But we are also capable of a kind of knowing that transcends temporal limitation — knowledge of eternal mathematical and philosophical truths — and this capacity is, for Plato, the highest expression of what we are.
