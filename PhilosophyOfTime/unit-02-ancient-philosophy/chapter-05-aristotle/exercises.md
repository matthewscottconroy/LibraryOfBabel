# Chapter 5 Exercises

---

## Comprehension and Analysis

**Exercise 1.** Explain Aristotle's definition of time as "the number of motion with respect to before and after" (*Physics* 219b1–2). What does "number" mean in this context? What does "before and after" refer to? How is time related to motion on this account — why is time *not* identical with motion?

**Exercise 2.** Aristotle argues that time cannot be identical with motion because different motions can be faster or slower, but time cannot (*Physics* 218b13–18). Assess this argument. Does the conclusion follow? Could there be a conception of time on which "faster time" and "slower time" make sense? (Consider the B-theory: is there any B-theoretic sense in which time could go faster or slower?)

**Exercise 3.** Explain Aristotle's account of the Now (*to nun*) as a boundary. How does the Now both divide time (past from future) and connect it (as a shared boundary between intervals)? Use the moving-point analogy to illustrate your explanation.

**Exercise 4.** Aristotle says the Now is "the same in what it is but different in being" at different moments (*Physics* 219b10–220a4). What does this distinction mean? Does it solve the puzzle of whether the Now is always the same or always different? Reconstruct the worry that this solution generates a regress, and assess whether Aristotle has the resources to avoid it.

**Exercise 5.** Explain Aristotle's distinction between potential and actual infinity, and show how it is supposed to resolve Zeno's Dichotomy and Achilles paradoxes. Does this resolution succeed? Identify any philosophical residue that the distinction fails to address.

**Exercise 6.** In *Physics* 223a16–29, Aristotle asks whether time would exist without a soul. Carefully reconstruct the argument that it would not. Then present the "weaker realist" interpretation, which holds that the before-and-after structure of motion exists independently of any soul. Which interpretation do you find more consistent with the rest of Aristotle's account of time? Why?

**Exercise 7.** Explain Aristotle's argument for an eternal world in *Physics* VIII.1. What assumption does the argument rely on, and how might someone challenge that assumption? How does Aquinas attempt to reconcile Aristotle's eternal world with the Christian doctrine of creation?

**Exercise 8.** Explain the concept of the Prime Mover (*to prôton kinoun akinêton*). How does Aristotle argue for its existence? In what sense does the Prime Mover "cause" the eternal celestial motion without itself being in motion or in time? Evaluate whether the notion of a "timeless final cause" of temporal motion is philosophically coherent.

## Critical Thinking

**Exercise 9.** Compare Aristotle's account of time as the number of motion with Plato's account of time as a moving image of eternity. What does each account say about: (a) the relationship between time and eternity/the eternal; (b) the relationship between time and motion; (c) the mind-dependence or mind-independence of time? Which account do you find more philosophically satisfying, and why?

**Exercise 10.** Consider the following objection to Aristotle: "The definition 'time is the number of motion' entails that without motion, there is no time. But surely it is at least conceivable that time could pass with no change at all — imagine a completely static universe in which nothing moves or changes, but time passes. Therefore Aristotle's definition is wrong." Reconstruct the strongest Aristotelian response to this objection. Then evaluate: does the possibility of "static time" (time without motion) constitute a genuine problem for Aristotle, or can it be resisted?

## Lab Exercises

**Lab 1: Counting Motion.** Choose a simple, repetitive physical process: a pendulum swing, a dripping tap, a metronome, your own resting pulse. Time yourself counting 100 repetitions. Then: (a) Record the total time (in seconds) and calculate the period (seconds per repetition). (b) Now use this process as your "clock" — use it to measure the duration of another activity (e.g., reading a page of text, walking to the end of the hall and back). (c) Reflect on what you have done in Aristotelian terms: you have used "the number of motion" (the count of a regular, repeating process) to measure the duration of another motion. Write a one-page reflection on how this activity illuminates Aristotle's definition of time, including the relationship between the "clock" motion and the "measured" motion.

**Lab 2: The Now and Temporal Boundaries.** This lab explores the experience of the Now as a boundary between past and future. Find a quiet place to sit. Set a timer for five minutes. During this time, try to observe the experience of "now" as precisely as you can. When does "now" end? Can you catch the moment when "now" becomes "just past"? Is there a sense in which the present moment has any duration, or does it always seem to vanish as soon as you try to grasp it? After the five minutes, write a one-page reflection on what this observation reveals about the phenomenology of the Now, connecting it to Aristotle's claim that the Now is a boundary (not an interval) and to the puzzle about whether the present has any duration.

**Lab 3: Motion, Rest, and the Perception of Time.** This lab explores the relationship between perceived motion and perceived time. Conduct the following observations on three separate occasions: (a) Sit in a quiet room with nothing happening — no phone, no music, no activity — for five minutes. Record your estimated duration when the time is up. (b) Watch a film or engage in an absorbing activity for five minutes. Record your estimated duration. (c) Observe a complex, dynamic environment (a busy street corner, a crowded cafeteria) for five minutes without participating. Record your estimated duration. Compare your three estimates to the actual five-minute duration. Reflect: which condition produced the most accurate estimate? Which the most distorted? What does this reveal about the relationship between perceived motion (or activity) and perceived duration? Connect your observations to Aristotle's claim that time is the number of motion — and to the phenomenological tradition's distinction between clock time and lived time.
