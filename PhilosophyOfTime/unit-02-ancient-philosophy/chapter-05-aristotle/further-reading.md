# Chapter 5: Further Reading and Bibliography

---

## Annotated Further Reading

**Coope, Ursula. 2005. *Time for Aristotle: Physics IV.10–14*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.**
The definitive contemporary philosophical treatment of Aristotle's theory of time. Coope goes through the text systematically, addressing every major interpretive issue with rigor and clarity. Her analysis of the definition of time as the number of motion, the Now's identity, and the "time and soul" passage are all superior to anything else in the secondary literature. If you read only one scholarly work on Aristotle's theory of time, it should be this one.

**Hussey, Edward. 1983. *Aristotle's Physics Books III and IV*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.**
The standard English scholarly translation with commentary. The introduction provides a lucid overview of Books III and IV, including the account of time. The notes are detailed and philosophically engaged. Indispensable for close reading of the text.

**Sorabji, Richard. 1983. *Time, Creation and the Continuum*. London: Duckworth.**
The most comprehensive scholarly treatment of ancient and early medieval philosophy of time. The chapters on Aristotle cover the definition, the Now, continuity, and the "time and soul" passage with exceptional depth and historical range. Sorabji's survey of the ancient reception of Aristotle's theory is also valuable.

**Waterfield, Robin, trans. 1996. *Aristotle: Physics*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
The most readable modern English translation of the *Physics*. Waterfield's introduction and notes make the text accessible to non-specialists while remaining philosophically responsible. Used for the translations throughout this chapter.

**Annas, Julia. 1975. "Aristotle, Number and Time." *Philosophical Quarterly* 25(99): 97–113.**
A careful analytical article that explains what Aristotle means by calling time a "number" of motion. Annas distinguishes the number-by-which-we-count from the number-that-is-counted, and applies this distinction to clarify the definition of time. Essential for understanding the technical content of the definition.

**Bergson, Henri. 1889. *Time and Free Will: An Essay on the Immediate Data of Consciousness*. Translated by F. L. Pogson. London: Swan Sonnenschein, 1910.**
Bergson's first major work, in which he introduces the concept of *durée* (duration) as a qualitative, lived time that is radically different from the measurable, quantitative time of science and Aristotelian philosophy. Bergson's critique is a sustained and philosophically stimulating challenge to the Aristotelian framework; engaging with it is essential for assessing the limits of the "time as number" account.

**Bostock, David. 1988. "Time and the Continuum." *Oxford Studies in Ancient Philosophy* 6: 255–270.**
An analytical reconstruction of Aristotle's argument for the continuity of time and its relationship to the continuity of motion and space. Bostock assesses the logical structure of the argument and identifies its strengths and weaknesses with precision.

**Graham, Daniel W. 1999. *Aristotle: Physics Book VIII*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.**
The Clarendon Aristotle translation and commentary on *Physics* VIII, covering the arguments for the eternity of motion, the first mover arguments, and the Prime Mover. Essential for the material covered in Section 5 of this chapter.

**Judson, Lindsay, ed. 1991. *Aristotle's Physics: A Collection of Essays*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.**
A collection of important scholarly essays on the *Physics*, including essays by Ursula Coope, Jonathan Lear, and others on topics central to the philosophy of time: change, continuity, motion, the infinite, and the Now. A useful anthology for advanced students.

**Owen, G. E. L. 1976. "Aristotle on Time." In *Motion and Time, Space and Matter: Interrelations in the History and Philosophy of Science*, edited by Peter Machamer and Robert Turnbull, 3–27. Columbus: Ohio State University Press.**
An important essay by one of the major twentieth-century Aristotle scholars, addressing the definition of time, the role of the Now, and the relationship between Aristotle's account and contemporary philosophy of time.
