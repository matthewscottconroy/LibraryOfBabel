# Chapter 5: Important Concepts

---

**Physics (Physis)**
The Greek word *physis* (nature) gives the *Physics* its title. For Aristotle, physis is the internal principle of motion and rest that characterizes natural things (as distinguished from artifacts, which have their principles of motion imposed from without). The *Physics* is a systematic investigation of natural motion and its presuppositions, including time.

**Kinesis (Change / Motion)**
Aristotle's general term for any kind of change. In the *Physics*, Aristotle identifies four kinds of kinesis: locomotion (change of place), qualitative change (change of quality or property), quantitative change (change of size), and substantial change (coming-to-be and passing-away). Time is the number of kinesis in general, though it is primarily measured by the most regular and universal kinesis (celestial rotation).

**Time as the Number of Motion (Arithmos Kinêseôs)**
Aristotle's definition of time: "the number of motion with respect to before and after" (*Physics* 219b1–2). Time is not motion itself (different motions can be faster or slower; time cannot) but the *countable quantity* of motion — what you get when you measure how much motion has occurred. This definition makes time relational (dependent on motion) but also universal (there is one unified temporal framework for all motions).

**Before and After (Proteron kai Husteron)**
The ordering of the parts of a motion from earlier to later. This ordering in motion is what time numbers: time is the measure of how much of this before-and-after ordering has been traversed. The before-and-after in motion corresponds to the before-and-after in space (the path); the two orderings are analogous and related.

**The Now (To Nun)**
Aristotle's term for the present moment — the moving boundary between past and future. The Now is not a part of time (it has no duration) but a *limit* or *boundary* of temporal intervals, analogous to a geometric point on a line. The Now is both divisive (separating past from future) and connective (joining the past interval and future interval into a continuous whole). The Now is type-identical (always the present) but token-different (a different particular moment at each time).

**Potential vs. Actual Infinity**
Aristotle's crucial distinction for responding to Zeno. Something is potentially infinite if it can always be further extended or divided, in principle, without limit. Something is actually infinite if it is simultaneously and completely divided into or contains infinitely many parts. Aristotle holds that continuous magnitudes (space, time, motion) are potentially but not actually infinite in their divisibility. Zeno's paradoxes confuse potential divisibility (which is harmless) with actual division into infinite parts (which is impossible).

**Continuity**
For Aristotle, a magnitude is continuous when its parts share a common boundary — when adjacent parts have the same limit. Time is continuous because any two adjacent intervals of time share a common Now at their junction. Continuity implies infinite divisibility but not actual infinite division. The continuity of time is related to and supported by the continuity of spatial paths and of motion through those paths.

**Celestial Motion (Rotation of the Outermost Sphere)**
For Aristotle, the rotation of the outermost celestial sphere (the sphere of the fixed stars) is the natural measure of time. It is preferred as a temporal measure because it is the most regular (uniform speed), most continuous (never stopping), most universal (observable from everywhere on Earth), and eternal (it has always occurred and always will). All other motions are measured against this standard.

**Mind-Dependence of Time (Time and Soul)**
The question Aristotle raises in *Physics* 223a16–29: would there be time without a soul to count it? If time is the number of motion, and number requires a counter (a soul or intellect), then time seems to require a soul. Aristotle hedges: the *substrate* of time (the before-and-after ordering of motion) might exist without soul, even if the *actualized number* requires a counter. The passage anticipates later debates about temporal idealism.

**Eternal World**
Aristotle's cosmological claim that the cosmos has no beginning and no end: motion has always existed and always will, and the cosmos with it. The argument turns on the claim that a first motion would require a prior motion to cause it, generating a regress. The eternal world is in direct conflict with Platonic creation and Abrahamic theology.

**Prime Mover (To Prôton Kinoun Akinêton)**
The first unmoved mover: Aristotle's solution to the question of what causes eternal celestial motion. The Prime Mover is pure actuality (no potentiality), pure intellect thinking itself (*noêsis noêseôs*), and the final cause of all cosmic motion — it moves the celestial spheres by being the ultimate object of their "attraction," not by pushing or acting. The Prime Mover is not in time: it is atemporal, unchanging, without before-and-after in its own nature.

**Final Cause**
One of Aristotle's four causes (*aitiai*): the *telos* (end or goal) toward which a natural process is directed. The Prime Mover is the final cause of eternal celestial motion: the celestial spheres move eternally in circles because a circle (returning perpetually to itself) is the best approximation available to the timeless, perfect self-completeness of the Prime Mover. Final causation is teleological (end-directed) rather than mechanical.

**Noêsis Noêseôs**
"Thought thinking itself" (*Metaphysics* 1074b34): Aristotle's description of the Prime Mover's activity. The Prime Mover is pure intellect engaged in the highest possible intellectual activity — self-contemplation — without any admixture of lower cognitive processes or any engagement with the changing temporal world. This activity is the most complete, most perfect, and most continuous — and as such, it is the ultimate object of cosmic attraction.

**De Caelo (On the Heavens)**
Aristotle's treatise on the structure of the cosmos, the nature of the celestial bodies, and the elements. Book I establishes the eternal, unchanging nature of the celestial region; Books II–IV discuss the earth, water, air, and fire. The arguments for the eternity and indestructibility of the heavens in *De Caelo* I.10–12 are closely related to the arguments for an eternal world in *Physics* VIII.

**Hylomorphism**
Aristotle's general metaphysical framework: all natural substances are composites of *matter* (*hylê*) and *form* (*morphê* or *eidos*). Matter is the material substratum that underlies change; form is the determining structure that makes a thing what it is. Hylomorphism provides the framework for Aristotle's account of change, which requires a substratum (matter) that persists through the change and acquires a new form. It also provides the background for the account of time as the numbered form of motion.

**Aporia**
"Impasse" or "puzzle" — Aristotle's methodological starting point. In the *Physics*, Aristotle begins with the aporiai about time: puzzles about whether time exists, what the Now is, and how time relates to motion. The dialectical resolution of these puzzles is what generates the positive account. Attending to the aporiai ensures that the account addresses the genuine difficulties rather than talking past them.
