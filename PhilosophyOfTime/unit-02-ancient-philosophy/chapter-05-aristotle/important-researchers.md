# Chapter 5: Important Researchers and Thinkers

---

**Aristotle (384–322 BCE)**
The central figure of the chapter. Aristotle studied at Plato's Academy for twenty years, traveled and conducted biological research in Asia Minor and Lesbos, tutored the young Alexander of Macedon, and founded the Lyceum in Athens. His *Physics* remains one of the most important philosophical texts ever written — not merely as a historical document but as a rigorous investigation of the nature of natural change. His account of time in *Physics* IV.10–14 set the terms of the discussion for two millennia.

**Edward Hussey (1939–)**
British philosopher and classicist. His translation and commentary on *Physics* III and IV (*Aristotle's Physics Books III and IV*, 1983, Clarendon Aristotle Series) is the standard English-language scholarly treatment of the text. Hussey's introduction and notes are philosophically acute and remain essential for anyone studying Aristotle's account of time seriously.

**Ursula Coope (1969–)**
British philosopher at Oxford. Her book *Time for Aristotle: Physics IV.10–14* (2005) is the most philosophically sophisticated and up-to-date monograph on Aristotle's theory of time. Coope argues for a careful reading of the definition of time as the number of motion and addresses the major interpretive controversies with rigor and originality. Essential reading for any serious student of Aristotle's temporal philosophy.

**Richard Sorabji (1934–)**
British philosopher and classicist. Already mentioned in Chapter 4, Sorabji's *Time, Creation and the Continuum* (1983) is equally important for Chapter 5. His chapters on Aristotle's definition of time, the Now, and the continuity of time provide the fullest available scholarly treatment. His analysis of the "time and soul" passage (Chapter 8) is particularly valuable.

**Robin Waterfield (1952–)**
British translator and ancient historian. His Oxford World's Classics translation of the *Physics* (1996) is the most readable modern English translation and the one used for quotations throughout this chapter. Waterfield's introduction provides accessible context for non-specialists.

**St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)**
Medieval Dominican philosopher and theologian. Aquinas wrote a detailed *Commentary on Aristotle's Physics* that was the standard medieval treatment of the text. He also addressed the tension between Aristotle's eternal world and Christian creation theology in the *Summa Theologica* (Ia.46.1–2), arguing that neither the eternity nor the temporality of the world can be demonstrated by reason alone — both require revelation or argument beyond pure metaphysics. Aquinas's synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian theology was the dominant intellectual framework of the medieval Latin West.

**Averroes / Ibn Rushd (1126–1198)**
Andalusian philosopher and physician. Averroes wrote voluminous commentaries on Aristotle — long, middle, and short commentaries on most of the corpus — and was known in the Latin West simply as "the Commentator." His defense of Aristotelian eternal world against religious objections, and his sophisticated treatment of the Prime Mover, were enormously influential on both Islamic and Latin medieval philosophy. His long commentary on the *Physics* is a major source for Aristotle's account of time in the medieval tradition.

**Richard McKirahan (1943–)**
American philosopher and classicist. In addition to his *Philosophy Before Socrates* (cited in Chapter 3), McKirahan's work on Aristotle's physics provides useful analytical reconstructions of Aristotle's arguments. His more recent work on pre-Socratic responses to Parmenides is particularly helpful for understanding the dialectical context of Aristotle's account.

**Henry Bergson (1859–1941)**
French philosopher and Nobel laureate (literature). Though not primarily an Aristotle scholar, Bergson's critiques of Aristotelian and mechanistic accounts of time in *Time and Free Will* (1889) and *Creative Evolution* (1907) are philosophically important. Bergson argued that Aristotelian (and scientific) time — time as a measurable quantity, the "number of motion" — is an abstraction from real *durée* (lived duration), which is qualitative, flowing, and not reducible to spatial measurement. Bergson's critique is the most influential twentieth-century challenge to the Aristotelian framework.

**J. L. Ackrill (1921–2007)**
British philosopher and classicist at Oxford. His essays on Aristotle's categories and metaphysics, collected in *Essays on Plato and Aristotle* (1997), provide incisive analyses of many of the concepts central to Aristotle's account of time, including actuality, potentiality, and form. His analysis of Aristotle's concept of *energeia* (actuality) is particularly useful for understanding the Prime Mover.

**Julia Annas (1946–)**
British-American philosopher. In addition to her work mentioned in Chapter 4, Annas's article "Aristotle, Number and Time" (*Philosophical Quarterly*, 1975) provides the clearest available analysis of what Aristotle means by calling time a "number" — distinguishing the number that counts from the number that is counted, and relating this to Aristotle's general philosophy of number.
