# Chapter 5: Aristotle's Physics of Time

---

## Chapter Introduction

If Plato's treatment of time is cosmological and mythological — oriented toward the big picture of creation, eternity, and the soul's relationship to eternal truth — Aristotle's treatment is systematic, analytical, and aggressively deflating of mythological pretension. Aristotle is not interested in the soul's journey toward eternity; he is interested in the question "what is time?" — and he pursues it with the patience and precision of a scientist.

Aristotle (384–322 BCE) was Plato's student for twenty years at the Academy, and then — after Plato's death and a period of travel and biological research — he returned to Athens to found his own school, the Lyceum. His philosophical relationship to Plato was one of profound respect and radical disagreement: he took Plato's problems with full seriousness while systematically dismantling the metaphysical framework in which Plato had set them. The Forms are gone; there is no realm of eternal intelligible objects beyond the world of nature; nature itself is the primary domain of philosophical inquiry. Time, change, and motion are features of the natural world, not defective images of a higher reality.

The primary text for Aristotle's philosophy of time is *Physics* Books III and IV, particularly Chapters 10–14 of Book IV (*Physics* 217b29–224a17). This passage — roughly thirty pages in modern editions — is among the most dense, careful, and philosophically productive analyses in the entire ancient corpus. Aristotle proceeds dialectically: he begins with the *aporia* (the puzzles and apparent contradictions), examines previous views, and gradually works toward his own positive account.

Here is what he argues, in brief:

Time is the *number of motion with respect to before and after* (*Physics* 219b1–2). This definition packs an enormous amount of philosophical content into a short phrase, and unpacking it occupies much of this chapter. It implies that time is not motion itself (motions differ in speed; time does not), but it also implies that time without motion is impossible (there is nothing to count). Time is continuous, because motion is continuous. Time is measured primarily by the circular motion of the celestial spheres — the most regular, the most knowable, and the most universal of all motions. Time is related to the mind: without a soul to do the counting, Aristotle suggests, there would be no time strictly speaking, though there would be what time counts.

The chapter traces these ideas through five sections. Section 1 examines the definition of time as the number of motion; Section 2 examines the concept of the Now (*to nun*); Section 3 treats temporal continuity and the response to Zeno; Section 4 takes up the famous and difficult question of whether time requires a soul; Section 5 examines Aristotle's cosmology — his defense of an eternal world and his account of the Prime Mover — and its relationship to his account of time.

Aristotle's account of time is not perfect. It faces serious difficulties, some of which Aristotle himself acknowledges. But it is the most sustained, rigorous, and philosophically rich account of time produced in antiquity, and it set the terms of the discussion for the next two thousand years.
