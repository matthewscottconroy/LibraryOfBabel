# 1.1 Setting the Context: Physics IV and the Study of Nature

---

## What Is Aristotle Doing in the Physics?

To understand Aristotle on time, you need to understand the project of the *Physics* as a whole. The *Physics* is not what we today call "physics" — it is not a mathematical theory of the behavior of matter and energy. It is closer to what we might call "philosophy of nature": a systematic investigation into the most fundamental features of natural things, the principles that make natural change possible and intelligible.

The central subject of the *Physics* is *kinesis* — motion or change. Book I investigates the principles of natural change (form, matter, privation). Book II examines the concept of nature (*physis*) and the four causes. Books III and IV analyze motion itself, together with the concepts of infinity, place, void, and time that are required to make sense of it. Books V through VIII pursue further questions about the structure of change and culminate in the argument for the Unmoved Mover — the ultimate cause of all natural motion.

Time appears in *Physics* IV.10–14 as a topic that must be treated as part of the analysis of motion. The connection is necessary: motion occurs *in* time; we measure motion *by* time; and we cannot understand what time is without understanding its relationship to motion. Time and motion are, for Aristotle, mutually implicated concepts that illuminate each other.

### The Dialectical Method

Aristotle's philosophical method in the *Physics* is dialectical: he begins with the *aporia* — the puzzles, contradictions, and difficulties that arise from ordinary experience and from previous philosophical accounts — and then works toward a resolution that preserves what is most defensible in the competing views while eliminating their defects.

This method is explicitly stated in *Nicomachean Ethics* VII.1 (1145b2–7) and is operative throughout the *Physics*: "We must start with what is familiar to us." What is familiar includes the views of previous philosophers (his predecessors and especially Plato) and the judgments of ordinary experience. A good philosophical account must account for what is right in both, and it must do so in a principled, non-ad-hoc way.

For time, the aporiai include: Does time exist at all, since the past is gone and the future is not yet? Is there always a now? Is there one now or many? What is the relationship between time and motion? These are the puzzles that Aristotle's analysis of *Physics* IV.10–14 attempts to resolve (Aristotle, *Physics* 217b29–218b29).

### The Place of Time in the Natural World

One way to see what Aristotle is doing is to contrast his approach with Plato's. For Plato, time is a moving image of eternity — its significance lies in its relationship to something outside and above the natural world. For Aristotle, time is immanent in the natural world: it is a feature of natural motion, not a reflection of anything beyond nature. There is no eternal model that time approximates; there is no Demiurge who created it. Time is simply what it is — the quantitative dimension of natural change — and it can be analyzed without reference to anything transcendent.

This is a characteristic Aristotelian move: naturalizing. Where Plato looked beyond nature for explanations, Aristotle looks within it. Forms are not transcendent objects; they are the immanent forms of natural things. The good is not a Form beyond all forms; it is the end at which natural and rational activities aim. Time is not an image of eternity; it is the number of motion. This naturalistic orientation shapes every aspect of Aristotle's account.

### The Key Questions

Aristotle's analysis of time addresses a cluster of related questions:

1. What is time? (The definition)
2. Does time exist? (The aporiai about the existence of the past and future)
3. What is the Now (*to nun*)? (Its identity, its function as boundary and limit)
4. Is time continuous? (Its relationship to spatial and kinematic continuity)
5. Does time require a soul? (The problem of temporal idealism)
6. What is the best measure of time? (The relationship between time and celestial motion)

We address questions 1–3 in this section, 4 in Section 3, 5 in Section 4, and 6 in connection with the broader account of celestial motion in Section 5.
