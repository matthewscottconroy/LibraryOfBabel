# 1.2 The Definition: Time as the Number of Motion

---

## Unpacking the Definition

At *Physics* 219b1–2, Aristotle gives his definition of time: *touto gar estin ho chronos, arithmos kinêseôs kata to proteron kai husteron* — "This is what time is: the number of motion with respect to before and after." This is one of the most discussed definitions in the history of philosophy, and it rewards very careful analysis.

The definition has three key components: "number," "motion," and "before and after." Understanding what Aristotle means by each is the key to understanding the whole.

### "Motion" (Kinesis)

Motion (*kinesis*) for Aristotle is not merely locomotion — spatial movement — but any kind of change. In *Physics* V.1–2, Aristotle distinguishes four kinds of change: change of place (locomotion), change of quality (alteration), change of quantity (growth and diminution), and change of substance (coming-to-be and passing-away). Any of these can be the motion whose number constitutes time.

But Aristotle also holds that time is primarily measured by the most regular, continuous, and universal motion available — and this turns out to be the circular motion of the celestial spheres (specifically the sphere of the fixed stars, the outermost sphere) (*Physics* 223b12–224a2). This motion is regular (it never varies in speed), continuous (it never starts and stops), universal (it is the measure for all other motions), and eternal (it has always been occurring and always will). The celestial rotation is the standard against which all other motions are measured.

### "Number" (Arithmos)

The claim that time is the *number* of motion is the philosophically most distinctive feature of the definition. Aristotle is not saying that time *is* motion — he will argue against this explicitly (see subsection 1.3). He is saying that time is the *number* or *measure* of motion: what you get when you count how much motion has occurred.

But Aristotle's notion of number (*arithmos*) is subtle. In *Metaphysics* XIII.7 (1082b16–19), Aristotle distinguishes the number *by which we count* from the number *which is counted* — the counting number from the counted number. Which of these is time?

Time is the *counted* number — the number that motion *has*, the measuredness of the motion. When you count the seconds during which a ball falls, "forty-two" is the number that the ball's fall *has*; forty-two is not an abstract counting number but the number that characterizes the temporal extent of this particular motion.

This is a delicate point. Numbers, for Aristotle, do not exist independently of things that have them (unlike Platonic numbers, which exist in the intelligible realm). The number of a pile of horses is not a separate entity; it is the pile's having-a-certain-number. Similarly, the number of a motion is not a separate entity; it is the motion's having-a-certain-temporal-extent. Time is not a separate entity over and above motion; it is motion's having-a-certain-temporal-extent — its being measurable by before and after.

### "Before and After" (To Proteron kai Husteron)

The "before and after" in the definition refers to the ordering of motion's parts. Aristotle holds that the order of points in space (the path of a motion) corresponds to an order in the motion itself (*Physics* 219a14–b1). As an object moves from A to B, the parts of its motion are ordered: there is a first part (the beginning of the motion, near A) and a last part (the end, near B), and the intermediate parts are ordered in between. This ordering of parts of the motion is the "before and after" in the motion.

Time is then the numbering of this before-and-after structure. The sequence of the motion's parts — the order in which they occur — provides the before-and-after framework; time is what results when we measure or count how much motion occurs within this framework.

Here is a helpful way to think about it. Imagine a race. The race has spatial parts (the first stretch, the second stretch, the finish) and temporal parts (the first minute, the second minute, etc.). The spatial parts give the before-and-after in *space* (which parts of the track come earlier and later in the path). The temporal parts give the before-and-after in *time* (which parts of the race occur earlier and later). These are analogous but distinct: spatial order corresponds to position on the track; temporal order corresponds to position in time. Aristotle's point is that temporal order — the before-and-after of time — is derived from and corresponds to the before-and-after of the motion itself.

### The Definition and Aristotle's Theory of Number

The definition connects directly to Aristotle's general theory of number, which holds that number is always the number *of* something — there are no free-floating numbers that exist independently (Aristotle, *Metaphysics* 1073a17–22). This contrasts sharply with Plato, for whom numbers are eternal, independent objects. For Aristotle, numbers are always dependent on and derived from the things that have them.

Applied to time, this means: time is always the time *of* some motion. There is no pure, free-floating time independent of any motion whatsoever. This is a relational account of time: time is a feature of motions (their having-a-certain-extent), not a container in which motions occur. In this respect, Aristotle's account is much closer to Leibniz's relationism than to Newton's absolutism.

But there is a complication. Aristotle holds that time is a *single*, *universal* measure — not multiple, with different motions having their own separate times. The celestial motion provides a single universal standard against which all particular motions are measured. This suggests that time is not merely relative to each motion individually but is a unified framework within which all motions are located. How exactly to reconcile the relational character of time (as the number *of* motion) with its universal, unified character is one of the more difficult interpretive questions in Aristotle's account (Hussey 1983: xxxvi–xlii; Coope 2005: 93–116).
