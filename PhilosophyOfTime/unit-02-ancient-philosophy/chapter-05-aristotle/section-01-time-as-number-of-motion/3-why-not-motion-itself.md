# 1.3 Why Time Is Not Motion Itself

---

## The Negative Argument

Before arriving at his positive definition, Aristotle clears the ground by arguing that time cannot simply be identified with motion or change. The argument is brief but important (*Physics* 218b9–219a10).

### The Argument from Differential Speed

Aristotle's main argument for distinguishing time from motion is this (*Physics* 218b13–18):

"The motion of any individual thing is confined to that thing, or to the place and time in which the thing happens to move. But time is present equally and simultaneously everywhere and with all things. Furthermore, change can be faster or slower, but time cannot. In fact, the fast and the slow are defined by time — the fast is what moves a large amount in a small time, and the slow is what moves a small amount in a long time. But time is not defined by time." (Waterfield trans., slightly modified)

The crucial point: motions can be faster or slower, but time cannot. You can walk faster or slower; you cannot make time faster or slower (in any ordinary sense). If time were identical with some particular motion, then that motion's speed would determine time's "speed" — but this is incoherent. Time is not faster or slower; it is the standard against which speed is measured.

Moreover, different motions occur at different rates — a fast runner covers the same distance as a slow runner in less time. If time were identical with motion in general, then different motions would each have their own time, and these would differ in speed from each other. But there is only one time, not multiple times running at different rates.

### Time and the Measure of Motion

The distinction between time and motion does not mean that time is entirely independent of motion. Aristotle is clear that time *requires* motion: "when we do not notice any change at all, time does not seem to have passed" (*Physics* 218b21–219a2). If we were somehow unaware of change — if we were anaesthetized, or like the sleepers in the myth who fall asleep in Sardinia and wake up unchanged — we would not perceive any time as having passed, even if it had.

This suggests that time is not detectable in the absence of motion, and raises the question of whether time exists in the absence of motion. Aristotle's answer is carefully formulated: time would not exist without motion (*Physics* 223a26–29), but time is not identical with motion. Time is the number of motion — the measured quantity that motion has — and this is different from motion itself.

An analogy: the weight of a stone is not the stone, and it is not the scales used to measure it. The weight is the stone's having-a-certain-heaviness — a relational property of the stone. Similarly, time is motion's having-a-certain-temporal-extent — a property of the motion, not the motion itself, and not the measuring instrument.

### The Normative Role of Celestial Motion

One consequence of distinguishing time from any particular motion is that the question of which motion best measures time becomes open. Aristotle's answer (*Physics* 223b12–224a2) is that the circular motion of the outermost celestial sphere — the sphere of the fixed stars — is the natural measure of time, because:

1. **It is the most regular**: it never varies in speed, producing a uniform standard.
2. **It is the most knowable**: it is observable from everywhere on Earth.
3. **It is the simplest**: circular motion is the purest and most continuous form of motion.
4. **It is eternal**: it has always been occurring and always will be (on Aristotle's cosmology).

All other motions are measured against this standard. When we say a human generation is thirty years, or a day is twenty-four hours, we are using the celestial rotation as the base unit. The celestial sphere's motion is not itself *identical* with time, but it provides the best available *measure* of time.

This normative role is philosophically interesting. It presupposes that some measures of time are better than others — not merely more convenient, but more accurate — and that the celestial motion is the most accurate of the available natural measures. This is a kind of temporal realism: there is a fact about how much time has passed, and the celestial motion gives the best available access to that fact.

The claim that the celestial motion is uniquely privileged as a temporal measure was challenged by Simplicius and other ancient commentators, and the issue remains philosophically alive. In modern physics, we use atomic oscillations (the cesium atom's hyperfine transition) as our standard of time — a motion that Aristotle could not have known about but that fits his criteria of regularity, universality, and knowability. The philosophical principle he articulates — that the best temporal measure is the most regular and universal available motion — is still operative.

### The Transition to the Now

Once Aristotle has established that time is the number of motion and not motion itself, he faces the question of *how* we count motion. The answer involves the concept of the Now (*to nun*) — the moving boundary between past and future that serves as the unit of temporal counting. This leads to Section 2.
