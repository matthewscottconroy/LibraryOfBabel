# 2.1 The Now as Boundary

---

## The Now and the Structure of Time

Aristotle develops the concept of the Now by analogy with a moving point on a line (*Physics* 219b9–220a4). Think of the path of a moving object. The object has a position at each moment; its position at a given moment corresponds to a point on the path. The point is both a *boundary* (it divides the path into what came before and what comes after) and a *connector* (it links the before-part and the after-part into a single, continuous path).

The Now plays the analogous role in time. It is the moving "position" of temporal process — the present moment — and it is both a boundary (dividing past from future) and a connector (holding past and future together as parts of a single, continuous temporal reality). As the object moves from earlier positions to later ones, the Now moves from earlier times to later times.

This analogy illuminates two functions of the Now that might otherwise seem contradictory.

### The Now as Divisive (Past/Future Boundary)

The Now marks the distinction between past and future. Everything on one side of the Now is past (no longer real in the sense of no longer occurring); everything on the other side is future (not yet real). The Now is the boundary that separates what has happened from what has not yet happened.

This is the Now as we ordinarily experience it. "Now" picks out the present moment, and everything before now is past, everything after now is future. The Now is the edge of the past and the edge of the future simultaneously.

### The Now as Connective (Temporal Continuity)

But the Now also *holds time together*. This is less obvious but equally important. Consider time as a line. A line is not merely a heap of points; it is a continuous whole. What makes it continuous — what makes it a *line* rather than scattered points — is that every point connects to adjacent points: every point is both the end of the segment before it and the beginning of the segment after it.

The Now plays this role in time. It is the "bridge" between the stretch of time that just occurred (the recent past) and the stretch that is about to occur (the near future). Without the Now, time would fall apart into disconnected pieces — the past and the future would be separate and unrelated. The Now connects them into a continuous whole.

Aristotle puts this in terms of the Now's dual role: "The 'now' connects and divides in that it is both the end of one time and the beginning of another" (*Physics* 220a4–5, Waterfield trans.). The Now is simultaneously the terminal boundary of the past interval and the initial boundary of the future interval — the same boundary from two sides.

### The Analogy with the Moving Point

The analogy with a point on a moving object is precise. Consider a point P on the surface of a moving ball. P has a position at each moment: it is at location L₁ at time t₁ and at location L₂ at time t₂. The point is the *same* point (P), but its *position* differs from moment to moment.

Now consider the Now. The Now is the present moment — the temporal "position" of the moving present. At each moment, the Now is at a different position in time (a different moment). It is the same *kind of thing* (the present) at each moment, but it is always at a *different* moment.

This analogy suggests that the Now is not a fixed point but a *moving point*: it is the temporal position of a kind of moving boundary, not a fixed location in time. The Now is always "here" in time, but "here" keeps moving.

### The Aporiai about the Now's Existence

Before developing this account positively, Aristotle confronts an *aporia* about whether the Now exists (*Physics* 218a8–30). The worry is: the past no longer is, the future is not yet, and the Now is a durationless instant that has no extent — it is not a period with beginning, middle, and end. So the present seems to have no real existence: it is a mere boundary between a past that is gone and a future that has not arrived.

Aristotle does not fully dissolve this worry; he holds that the Now is a genuine entity — not an interval but a genuine part of time's structure, analogous to a geometric point. The Now has no duration, but it is not nothing: it is a limit or boundary, and limits are real even if they are not themselves the things they limit.

This is not entirely satisfying, and Aristotle knows it. But the alternative — denying that the Now exists, and therefore denying that time has any present — is even more unacceptable. A time with no present would be a time with no relationship to experience, no anchor in reality. The Now must exist, even if its mode of existence is that of a limit rather than an interval.
