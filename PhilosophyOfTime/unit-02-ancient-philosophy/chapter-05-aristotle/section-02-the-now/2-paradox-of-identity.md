# 2.2 The Paradox of the Now's Identity

---

## Is the Now Always the Same, or Always Different?

Here is a puzzle that anyone who thinks carefully about the present moment runs into: the present moment is always "now." No matter when you think about it, the present is now. But surely the present moment at noon today is *different* from the present moment at 3:00 PM yesterday. These are different times; they cannot be numerically the same present. So the present changes — it is always a different now.

But if the present always changes, then in what sense is "the present" the same thing at all? Is "the present" simply a description that applies to different moments at different times, like "the current president" applies to different people in different years? Or is there a single thing called "the present" that moves through time, maintaining its identity as the present while occupying different temporal positions?

Aristotle confronts this puzzle at *Physics* 219b10–220a4 and offers a careful, if compressed, solution.

### Aristotle's Distinction

Aristotle draws a distinction between the Now considered *as what it is* (a boundary, a present) and the Now considered *in its being* (at a particular temporal position). In what it is — as a boundary between past and future, as the present — the Now is always the same. There is only one present; "the present" refers to the same thing whenever you use it. In this sense, the Now is one.

But in its *being* — in which particular moment it occupies, which temporal position it marks — the Now is always different. The present at noon is not the same temporal position as the present at 3:00 PM. Each now occupies a different position in time, and this positional difference makes them numerically different instances of the same *kind* of thing (the present).

The analogy Aristotle uses is the point again: the moving point on the surface of a ball. "The point on this ball's leading edge" always refers to the same *kind* of thing (the leading-edge point), but the point occupies different spatial positions as the ball moves. Similarly, "the now" always refers to the same *kind* of thing (the present), but it occupies different temporal positions as time advances.

### The Sporting Analogy

Another helpful analogy is the starting position in a running race. At any given race, there is one starting position — it is the same starting position in the sense that it is always the starting position. But the starting positions of different races are numerically different (they may be at different physical locations, or the same physical location at different times). "The starting position" is a role-description that picks out different instances in different contexts.

"The now" works similarly: it is always *the present* (same role), but it is always a *different particular moment* occupying that role (different instances). The present is a temporal role, not a fixed occupant of that role.

### Assessment: Does This Solve the Puzzle?

Aristotle's solution is elegant and has been influential. It introduces a distinction — between type-identity (the same *kind* of thing) and token-identity (the same *particular* thing) — that is important throughout metaphysics. The Now is always type-identical (always the present) but never token-identical across different moments (each moment is a different particular now).

But does this solution fully satisfy? Several philosophers have worried that it does not (Roark 2011: 66–78; Coope 2005: 48–52).

The worry is this: if different token-nows are genuinely different particulars, then the "movement" of the now from one token-now to the next is just the replacement of one token by another — and this replacement requires a further time in which it occurs. But then there would have to be a meta-time — a second temporal dimension — in which the now moves from one position to another. This threatens an infinite regress of times.

This is essentially a version of McTaggart's argument (Unit V) applied to the Aristotelian Now. Aristotle's framework does not obviously have resources to avoid it. His solution identifies the type-identity of the present (always the same role) without fully explaining how a single "moving present" is possible without a regress.

Aristotle's account is best understood not as a complete solution to the puzzle of temporal passage but as a step toward one. He identifies the right conceptual distinction (type vs. token identity), but he does not give a full metaphysics of how the type moves through its token instances. That problem is still open.

### Connection to Modern Debates

The puzzle about the Now's identity connects directly to the contemporary debate between A-theories and B-theories of time (Unit V). An A-theorist holds that there is a genuinely moving present — a single now that advances through time — and faces the challenge of explaining how this is possible without a regress. A B-theorist denies the moving present: there is no privileged "now" in the objective world; different moments are all equally real, and "now" is a perspectival indexical (like "here"). Aristotle is clearly more sympathetic to something like the A-theory — the Now is a genuine, moving boundary — but his account of its identity has the difficulties we have just noted.
