# 3.1 Time as Infinitely Divisible and Continuous

---

## What Continuity Means

For Aristotle, a magnitude is *continuous* if and only if any two adjoining parts share a common boundary (*Physics* 227a10–13). A line is continuous because any two adjacent segments share an endpoint: the right endpoint of the left segment is the same as the left endpoint of the right segment. This shared endpoint is the connecting boundary.

This is different from mere *contiguity* (things touching at a boundary but having separate endpoints) and from *succession* (things following each other with a gap or interval between them). Continuity requires genuine sharing — the boundary is one, not two.

Time is continuous in this sense. Any two adjacent temporal intervals share a common Now at their junction: the final Now of the earlier interval is the same as the initial Now of the later interval. This shared Now is what makes time a continuous whole rather than a sequence of discrete, separate periods.

### The Relationship Between Temporal and Spatial Continuity

Aristotle holds that temporal, spatial, and kinematic continuity are intimately related — they are not three independent kinds of continuity but one underlying continuous structure expressed in three ways (*Physics* 219a10–b9).

The argument is this: motion occurs through a spatial path, and the order of positions in the path determines the order of parts in the motion. The path is spatially continuous; therefore the motion through it is kinematically continuous. And if the motion is continuous, then time — the number of that continuous motion — is temporally continuous.

This three-way relationship is expressed in *Physics* 219a10–14: "the motion corresponds to the magnitude, and the time to the motion." Just as the spatial magnitude is continuous (indefinitely divisible), so the motion is continuous (no smallest temporal unit of motion), and so time is continuous (no smallest temporal duration).

The relationship runs in both directions. Not only does the continuity of space imply the continuity of time; the continuity of time also implies the continuity of space. A time with discrete minimal units would correspond to a space with discrete minimal units — and Aristotle argues against spatial atoms in *Physics* VI.1–2 on purely geometrical grounds (any magnitude can be divided, at least conceptually, without limit).

### Potential vs. Actual Infinity

Aristotle's continuity does not imply that time (or space or motion) is *actually* infinitely divided. It implies only that it is *potentially* infinitely divisible: at any level of division, further division is always possible in principle. But the infinite divisions are never all simultaneously actualized.

This distinction — between *potential* and *actual* infinity — is Aristotle's central tool for responding to Zeno (*Physics* 233a21–31; 263a4–b9). Zeno's paradoxes assume that completing a task with infinitely many sub-tasks requires *actually* completing infinitely many separate acts. Aristotle denies this: the journey from A to B is potentially divisible into infinitely many sub-journeys, but these sub-divisions are only potential, not actual. The journey is completed in one continuous act, not in infinitely many separate acts.

The potential/actual distinction does real philosophical work here. A continuous motion does not consist of infinitely many distinct acts; it is a single continuous process that can be analyzed (divided in thought) into as many sub-processes as we like. The analysis is always in principle extendable, but this does not mean the motion itself is composed of infinitely many actual parts.

### Aristotle's Response to Zeno

How does temporal continuity respond to Zeno's paradoxes?

**Dichotomy and Achilles**: These paradoxes assume that to complete a journey, you must complete infinitely many sub-journeys, each requiring a positive amount of time. Aristotle's response: the sub-journeys are only *potentially* many, not *actually* many. The continuous journey is completed in a single continuous act; the infinite divisibility of the journey does not require infinitely many actual acts of traversal. The sum of potentially infinitely many sub-durations is finite, because the sub-durations converge to zero.

**Arrow**: This paradox assumes that time is composed of durationless instants, and that being "at rest" in an instant makes sense as a state of the arrow. Aristotle's response: instants are limits of temporal intervals, not parts of time. Motion is a continuous process defined over intervals; it is not composed of instantaneous states. The arrow is moving in the sense that it is *in the midst of* a continuous motion over an interval; the question "is it moving at an instant?" is malformed, because motion is defined for intervals, not points.

**Stadium**: This paradox targets discrete space and time. Aristotle's response: space and time are continuous, so there is no smallest spatial atom or temporal atom whose multiplied extent could generate a contradiction.

### Continuity and the Real Number Line

In modern terms, Aristotle's continuity corresponds to the continuum of the real numbers: the temporal line is isomorphic to the real number line, on which between any two numbers there are infinitely many others. This is the mathematical model for Aristotelian temporal continuity, made rigorous by the development of the theory of the real line in the nineteenth century (Dedekind 1872; Cantor 1883).

However, modern physics raises a challenge. There is theoretical and some empirical evidence suggesting that at the Planck scale (ca. 10⁻⁴³ seconds), the continuum description of spacetime may break down and be replaced by something discrete. Loop quantum gravity, causal set theory, and other approaches to quantum gravity posit that spacetime has a discrete structure at the smallest scales (Penrose 2004: 961–966; Sorkin 2003). If these theories are correct, then Aristotle's (and the continuum's) account of time as infinitely divisible is an approximation that holds at the scales relevant to ordinary experience but fails at the fundamental level.

This is a philosophically interesting situation: the conceptual argument for temporal continuity (Aristotle's) seems compelling at the macroscopic scale, but quantum gravity may require a revision of the underlying ontology. The relationship between the continuous macroscopic account and the potentially discrete fundamental account remains an open problem in physics and philosophy of physics.
