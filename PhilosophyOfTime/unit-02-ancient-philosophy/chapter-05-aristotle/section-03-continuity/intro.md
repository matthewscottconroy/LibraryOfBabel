# Section 3: The Continuity of Time

---

## Section Introduction

Zeno's paradoxes (Chapter 3, Section 3) raised the question of whether time and motion are continuous magnitudes or compositions of discrete units. Aristotle's account of time is deeply and deliberately continuous: time, like motion and spatial extension, is a continuous magnitude — infinitely divisible, with no smallest unit, no temporal atom below which further division is impossible.

This commitment to continuity is not incidental; it is central to Aristotle's response to Zeno and to his understanding of the relationship between time, motion, and space. In *Physics* VI, Aristotle develops a systematic account of continuous magnitude that underlies his treatment of time.

This section's single subsection examines the continuity of time: what Aristotle means by it, how it relates to spatial and kinematic continuity, and how it functions as a response to Zeno. We also consider the modern challenge to temporal continuity from quantum mechanics and quantum gravity.
