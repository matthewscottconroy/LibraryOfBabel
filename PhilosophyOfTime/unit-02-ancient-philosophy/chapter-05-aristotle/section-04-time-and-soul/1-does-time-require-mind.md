# 4.1 Does Time Require a Soul?

---

## The Passage

Here is *Physics* 223a16–29 in Waterfield's translation:

"One might raise the question of whether without soul there could be time. If nothing can count, then nothing can be counted either; hence there can be no number, since number is either what has been counted or what can be counted. But if only soul, or intellect as a faculty of soul, naturally counts, then there can be no time without soul — although the substrate of time (i.e. that aspect of motion which can be prior or posterior) could exist without soul. But if being prior and posterior in motion is what we identify as time, then time must depend on soul."

This is a careful, hedged passage, and it admits of at least two readings.

### The Strong Idealist Reading

The strong reading is that time is mind-dependent: without a counting soul, there is no time at all. The argument is clean: time is number; number requires a counter; a counter is a mind (soul, intellect); without a mind, there is no number, and therefore no time.

If this reading is correct, Aristotle is endorsing a form of *temporal idealism*: time is a feature of how minds represent and count motion, not a feature of mind-independent reality. This would make Aristotle's position similar to Kant's, who held that time (and space) are forms of intuition imposed by the mind on the matter of experience, not features of things-in-themselves.

### The Weaker Realist Reading

But Aristotle himself immediately hedges. He says that the *substrate* of time — "that aspect of motion which can be prior or posterior" — could exist without soul. And he ends with a conditional: "if being prior and posterior in motion is what we identify as time, then time must depend on soul." This conditional leaves open whether we should identify time with that: perhaps we should identify time with the prior-and-posterior in motion itself, not with the counting of it.

On this weaker reading, what depends on soul is the *counting* — the actualization of time's number in a cognitive act. But the *countable* — the before-and-after structure of motion — exists independently of any soul. Time, on this reading, is like a book before it is read: the text is there, but its being *read* requires a reader. Similarly, motion's before-and-after structure is there, but its being *counted* requires a counter.

This weaker reading preserves the realism of Aristotle's account: the temporal structure of the world exists independently of any mind, even if its actualization as a counted number requires a mind.

### Scholarly Debate

The scholarly debate about this passage is extensive and not fully resolved. Hussey (1983: lii–lvii) argues for the weaker, realist reading: what depends on soul is the *actualized* number, not the objective before-and-after structure that is countable. Coope (2005: 154–164) argues that Aristotle's account is genuinely ambiguous here and that he was not fully settled on the question of time's mind-dependence.

Sorabji (1983: 90–93) suggests a middle position: Aristotle distinguishes two senses of time — a mind-independent sense (the before-and-after ordering of motion) and a mind-dependent sense (the number actually counted). Both senses are legitimate; the definition is primarily of the second sense, but the first sense underpins it.

### The Connection to Modern Debates

The question of time's mind-independence is one of the central issues in contemporary philosophy of time. The *A-theory* — particularly in its "moving spotlight" version — holds that there is an objective present that moves through time independently of any observer. The *B-theory* holds that the temporal ordering of events is mind-independent (the before-and-after structure exists objectively), but that the sense of a moving present is a perspectival, possibly mind-dependent feature of temporal experience (Mellor 1998; Skow 2015).

Aristotle's tentative position is closest to the B-theory in its realist dimension: the before-and-after ordering of motion is objective and mind-independent. But in suggesting that the *number* of motion (time strictly speaking) requires a counter, he anticipates the idealist tendency that will be developed more fully by Augustine and, much later, by Kant.

### The Lesson for Philosophy of Time

This passage illustrates a general point about time: it has both objective and subjective dimensions, and it is not always clear how to separate them. The before-and-after structure of motion is objective; it does not depend on any mind to be ordered in a certain way. But the identification of this ordering as a *number* — the assignment of a quantity to the ordering — does seem to involve an act of measurement, of counting, that requires a cognitive subject.

Modern discussions of time measurement make a similar distinction: the events that constitute the temporal order (physical processes, causal relations) are objective; but the assignment of numbers to those events — the metric of time — involves a choice of standard (which clock? which physical process?) that is not simply read off the world but involves a judgment about which standard best serves our theoretical and practical purposes (Poincaré 1898; Dummett 2000).

Aristotle did not have the resources to articulate this distinction clearly, but he sensed it. The *Physics* 223a16–29 passage is evidence of a philosopher probing the limits of his own account and acknowledging, honestly, where the account becomes less secure.
