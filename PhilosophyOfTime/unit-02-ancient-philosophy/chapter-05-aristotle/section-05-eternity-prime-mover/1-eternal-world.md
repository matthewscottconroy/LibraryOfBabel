# 5.1 The Eternal World

---

## Was the Cosmos Always There?

One of the sharpest disagreements between Aristotle and Plato concerns the temporal status of the cosmos. Plato, in the *Timaeus*, described the world as *created* — made by the Demiurge at a specific time (or, on the metaphorical reading, as ontologically dependent on an eternal model in a way that is analyzed as "creation"). Aristotle rejected this picture entirely: the world has no beginning and no end; it has always existed and always will. There was no creation event, no first moment.

The arguments for an eternal world are developed primarily in *Physics* VIII.1 (*Physics* 250b11–252b6) and *De Caelo* I.10–12 (*De Caelo* 279b5–283b22).

### The Argument from Motion

The main argument in *Physics* VIII.1 proceeds from the nature of motion itself:

1. Motion is either eternal or it had a beginning.
2. If motion had a beginning, there was a first change — the transition from rest to motion.
3. But the transition from rest to motion is itself a change — which requires prior motion to explain it.
4. Therefore, if motion had a beginning, there would have to be motion before the supposed first motion. Contradiction.
5. Therefore motion has no beginning; it is eternal.

The argument is an instance of the general Aristotelian principle that every change requires something that initiates it. A transition from complete stasis to motion requires a cause; but the cause must already be in motion (or be a moved mover), which means motion was already occurring. Therefore there can be no absolute first motion.

This argument has been criticized on several grounds. The most obvious is that it assumes a causal regress: every change requires a prior cause. But why could there not be a *spontaneous* beginning — a first change with no prior cause? Aristotle's answer would appeal to the Principle of Sufficient Reason: there can be no change without a cause, because change requires an agent that changes something, and an agent acting requires a reason for acting at that time rather than another.

### De Caelo and the Indestructibility of the Cosmos

In *De Caelo* I.10–12, Aristotle argues that the heavens (the outermost celestial sphere and its contents) are neither generated nor destructible. The argument is partly empirical — there is no evidence of any part of the heavens being generated or destroyed; "in the whole time of our life, according to tradition, no perceptible change has occurred in the whole heaven or in any of its proper parts" (*De Caelo* 270b12–16) — and partly conceptual.

The conceptual argument appeals to the relationship between natural potentiality and actuality: a naturally destructible thing is something that *can* not-be, and therefore something that has a *tendency toward* not-being. The heavens show no such tendency; there is nothing in their nature toward dissolution or destruction. Therefore they are naturally indestructible.

Moreover, Aristotle holds that the natural motion of the heavens — circular motion — has no contrary motion (locomotion can be up or down, left or right; but circular motion has no opposite direction in the relevant sense). Without a contrary, there is nothing that could "act against" the circular motion and eventually overcome it. Therefore the circular motion is eternal and the heavens are eternal.

### The Conflict with Plato and Theology

Aristotle's eternal world is in direct conflict with Plato's cosmogony and with all versions of creation theology. For Plato, the world was made at a specific time (or in a specific metaphysical act of dependence). For the Abrahamic traditions, the world was created by God from nothing at a definite first moment.

The conflict with Christian theology was particularly important in the medieval period. The condemnation of 1277 at the University of Paris explicitly targeted (among other Aristotelian claims) the doctrine of the eternity of the world, which was seen as contradicting the doctrine of creation ex nihilo. Aquinas attempted to reconcile Aristotle and Christian theology by arguing that while revelation establishes that the world has a temporal beginning, it cannot be demonstrated by philosophical reason alone that the world could not have been eternal — Aristotle's argument for the eternity of the world is not a decisive proof (*Summa Theologica* Ia.46.1–2).

Whether Aquinas succeeded in this reconciliation is a matter of continuing debate. The philosophical issue — whether the universe must have had a temporal beginning — remains alive in modern cosmology. The Big Bang model describes an initial singularity approximately 13.8 billion years ago; whether this constitutes a genuine temporal "beginning" — whether there is any meaningful sense in which time itself began — is a question that physics and philosophy have not yet definitively resolved (Hawking 1988: 133–140; Carroll 2010: 37–55).

### The Theological Significance of Aristotle's Position

Despite rejecting the Platonic creation narrative, Aristotle does not hold that the world is entirely without a transcendent dimension. The eternal cosmos requires an eternal cause: the Prime Mover, which is the subject of the next subsection. Aristotle's eternal world is not a world without God; it is a world with a God that is radically different from the creator-God of either Plato or the Abrahamic traditions.
