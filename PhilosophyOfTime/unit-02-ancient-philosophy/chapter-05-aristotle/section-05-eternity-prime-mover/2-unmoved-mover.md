# 5.2 The Unmoved Mover

---

## A Timeless Cause of Temporal Change

The argument for an eternal, continuously moving cosmos requires a solution to a pressing problem: if motion is eternal, what keeps it going? What is the ultimate cause of eternal motion?

Aristotle's answer, developed in *Physics* VIII.6 and *Metaphysics* XII.6–9, is the Prime Mover (*to prôton kinoun akinêton*) — the first mover that is itself unmoved. This is not a mover that acts by pushing or pulling — it does not exert force in a mechanical sense. It is a mover that causes motion by being the *final cause* of motion — the object of desire and thought that the celestial spheres pursue, not through deliberate rational decision, but through a kind of metaphysical attraction.

### The Argument for a First Unmoved Mover

The argument in *Physics* VIII.5–6 is a form of the causal regress argument:

1. Every mover is either moved or unmoved.
2. If every mover is moved, there is an infinite causal regress of movers — every mover requires a prior mover.
3. An infinite causal regress is impossible (Aristotle argues this from the nature of causal efficacy: an infinite chain of intermediaries cannot transmit causal efficacy to the thing at the beginning).
4. Therefore, there is a first mover that is itself unmoved: the Prime Mover.
5. The Prime Mover causes the eternal circular motion of the outermost celestial sphere; this motion is transmitted through the sphere system to the sublunary world.

The Prime Mover is the cause of all temporal motion without itself being in motion — and therefore without itself being *in* time. As Aristotle says in *Metaphysics* XII.7: "And it produces motion by being loved" (*kinei dê hôs erômenon*: 1072b3). The unmoved mover is the object of cosmic love — the final end toward which all natural motion is ultimately oriented.

### The Nature of the Prime Mover

What kind of thing is the Prime Mover? *Metaphysics* XII.7–9 develops the account:

The Prime Mover is pure actuality (*energeia*) without any potentiality. Potentiality implies the possibility of change (you can actualize what is potential); but the Prime Mover cannot change, since change would require it to go from one state to another, which would imply being in time. The Prime Mover is always fully actual — there is no possible state it is not yet in.

The Prime Mover is also *noêsis noêseôs* — "thought thinking itself" (*Metaphysics* 1074b34). Its activity is pure intellectual contemplation of itself. It does not think about the world or exercise providential care; it is absorbed in self-contemplation, and this activity is so perfect and so complete that it serves as the final cause — the ultimate object of attraction — for all cosmic motion. The celestial spheres move in eternal circular motion because they are, in their way, "attracted toward" the perfection of the Prime Mover's self-contemplation.

### The Paradox of a Timeless Cause of Temporal Change

The Prime Mover is the most philosophically challenging element in Aristotle's cosmology. It is the cause of eternal temporal motion — without it, the celestial spheres would not move, and without their motion, there would be no time. Yet the Prime Mover itself is not in time: it has no before-and-after, no earlier-and-later, no duration. Its "activity" is timeless self-contemplation, not a temporal process.

How can something timeless cause temporal effects? This is a version of the problem that confronts anyone who posits a timeless ground of temporal existence — whether the Platonic Forms, Aristotle's Prime Mover, or the eternal God of Christian theology.

Aristotle's implicit answer seems to be that the Prime Mover is the *final cause* of motion, not the *efficient cause*. An efficient cause produces an effect by acting — by doing something, which takes time. A final cause produces an effect by being *the end toward which something is oriented* — and this orientation can be eternal even if the process of orientation is temporal. The celestial sphere moves in a circle because a circle is the best approximation to the Prime Mover's timeless perfection; this "because" is the relation of final causation, which does not require the Prime Mover to do anything in time.

This is a subtle but important distinction. The Prime Mover does not act in time; it does not push the celestial sphere around its orbit; it does not initiate temporal events. It is the ultimate *why* of temporal motion — the ground of its intelligibility — without being a temporal agent.

### The Conflict with Christian Theology

Aristotle's Prime Mover differs from the Abrahamic creator-God in several important ways. First, it did not create the world ex nihilo: the world is eternal, not created. Second, it has no knowledge of the world: the Prime Mover thinks only about itself; it does not exercise providence or hear prayers. Third, it is not personal in any way that would support religious worship: it is a philosophical principle, not a God who enters into covenantal relationships.

Medieval philosophers, particularly Averroes (Ibn Rushd) and Maimonides, developed sophisticated analyses of the relationship between Aristotelian and Abrahamic conceptions of God (Averroes, *Long Commentary on De Caelo*, passim; Maimonides, *Guide of the Perplexed* I.69–76). The synthesis they attempted — identifying the Aristotelian Prime Mover with the Abrahamic God while managing the differences — was one of the great intellectual projects of the medieval period.

### The Prime Mover and the Philosophy of Time

For the philosophy of time, the Prime Mover raises a question that is still unresolved: what is the relationship between a ground of temporal existence that is itself atemporal and the temporal world it grounds?

In Aristotle's case, the answer is that the Prime Mover is the *final cause* of the eternal celestial motion, which is itself the ground (measure and standard) of time. The Prime Mover does not create time; it makes possible the eternal regularity of the celestial motions that serve as time's measure. Time, in this sense, is as eternal as the cosmos — it has no beginning and no end — but it is ultimately grounded in something that is not itself temporal.

This picture — temporal process grounded in something atemporal — recurs in very different forms in later philosophy. Plato's temporal cosmos grounded in eternal Forms, Kant's temporal phenomenal world grounded in atemporal things-in-themselves, modern physics's temporal evolution governed by atemporal laws — all exhibit the same structural feature that Aristotle's cosmology first articulates clearly: temporal reality, intelligible and ordered, must ultimately rest on something that is not itself subject to temporal change.
