# Section 5: Eternity, the Prime Mover, and the Eternal World

---

## Section Introduction

Aristotle's account of time is embedded in a larger cosmological picture that is, in several respects, radically different from Plato's. Where Plato's cosmos was created by a Demiurge and is a temporal image of an eternal model, Aristotle's cosmos is eternal: it has always existed, it always will exist, and there was no first moment at which it began.

This commitment to an eternal, uncreated cosmos generates a distinctive account of time's relationship to eternity. For Aristotle, there is no eternity in Plato's sense — no realm of timeless Forms over and above the natural world. But there is something eternal within the natural world: the circular motions of the celestial spheres, which are eternal and uniform, and the Prime Mover (*to prôton kinoun akinêton* — the first, unmoved mover), which is the ultimate cause of those motions.

The Prime Mover is itself unmoved and unchanged: it is the cause of eternal motion without itself being in motion, and it is therefore atemporal in a sense — not located in time, not subject to change. But it is also not "outside" the natural world in Plato's sense; it is at the limit of the natural world, the final cause of natural motion, the ultimate ground of temporal existence.

This section examines the cosmological background of Aristotle's temporal philosophy: the argument for an eternal world (subsection 1) and the paradoxical status of the Prime Mover as a timeless cause of temporal change (subsection 2).
