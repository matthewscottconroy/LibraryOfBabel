## Comprehension and Analysis

**Exercise 6.1**
In your own words, explain the Stoic distinction between bodies and incorporeals. Why does time qualify as incorporeal rather than corporeal? In your response, make sure to specify what the Stoics understood as the criterion for being a body.

**Exercise 6.2**
Reconstruct Chrysippus's argument for the claim that the present moment is a durationless instant. What premise about time generates this conclusion? Do you think the conclusion is correct? If not, where does the argument go wrong?

**Exercise 6.3**
Compare the Stoic definition of time as "the interval of the world's motion" with Aristotle's definition of time as "the number of motion with respect to before and after." What philosophical difference does the substitution of "interval" for "number" signal? Which definition do you find more defensible, and why?

**Exercise 6.4**
Epicurus describes time as an "accident of accidents." Explain in detail what this means. In what sense is time "ontologically secondary" on this account? How does this view differ from both the Stoic view and a view that treats time as an independently existing substance?

**Exercise 6.5**
Reconstruct Lucretius's symmetry argument in your own words. Present it as a formal argument with numbered premises and a conclusion. Then identify the premise you think is most vulnerable to challenge and explain why.

**Exercise 6.6**
Nagel argues that there is a genuine asymmetry between prenatal and post-mortem non-existence. Explain his argument. Then write a response defending Lucretius against Nagel's objection. Does Lucretius have an adequate reply?

**Exercise 6.7**
The Stoics held that only the present exists, while past and future *subsist*. How does this position relate to contemporary presentism? Is the Stoic position a version of presentism, or is it importantly different? Explain your answer.

**Exercise 6.8**
James Warren argues that the symmetry argument may be defensible even if Nagel's metaphysical asymmetry is correct. Explain Warren's distinction between metaphysical symmetry and evaluative symmetry. Is this distinction tenable?

## Observational and Experimental Exercises

**Lab Exercise 6.A — The Shrinking Present**

Try to identify the duration of "the present moment" through careful introspection. Start with a broad present ("this afternoon"), then narrow: this hour, this minute, this second, this half-second, this tenth of a second. At each step, ask yourself: is this interval purely present, or does it contain a "just-passed" part and a "just-coming" part? Keep narrowing until you cannot reliably distinguish present from immediate past. Record the smallest interval you can identify as "clearly present" and reflect: does your experience support or challenge Chrysippus's claim that the strict present is durationless?

**Lab Exercise 6.B — Time Without Events**

Find a quiet place and spend five minutes trying to attend to time itself — not to events, sensations, or thoughts, but to pure time. Keep a brief journal of what you find. Then consider: Is Lucretius's claim that "no one has a sense-experience of time itself apart from the motion of things" confirmed or disconfirmed by your experiment? Report your findings, and discuss what methodological limitations affect this kind of introspective experiment.

**Lab Exercise 6.C — The Mirror Argument in Ordinary Life**

The Lucretian symmetry argument has an everyday version: we regularly look back at periods of our past with equanimity that we do not extend to the corresponding future periods (for example: you don't regret being asleep for the last eight hours, but you might fear being in a coma for the next eight hours). Identify three examples of this temporal asymmetry in your own experience or observation. For each example, ask: is the asymmetry rational? Does it support or challenge Lucretius's argument? Write up your findings as a brief philosophical reflection.

## Essay Questions

**Exercise 6.9 (Essay)**
Is Chrysippus's account of the present self-defeating? If the present is a durationless instant, can anything be said to "exist" strictly in the present? Write an essay of 800–1000 words exploring this question, drawing on both the Stoic text and at least one contemporary discussion of presentism.

**Exercise 6.10 (Essay)**
The Epicurean relational account of time holds that there is no time without events. Newton held that time flows even in a universe with no events. Write an essay of 800–1000 words evaluating these competing views. Consider the following question: if a universe with no events is coherently conceivable, what follows for the Epicurean view?

**Exercise 6.11 (Essay)**
How does the fear of death relate to one's conception of time? Drawing on Epicurus, Lucretius, and at least one contemporary philosopher (Nagel, Warren, or McMahan), write an essay of 1000–1200 words arguing for a position on whether Lucretius's symmetry argument should lead us to revise our attitudes toward death.
