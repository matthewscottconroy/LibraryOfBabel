## Annotated Suggestions for Further Reading

**Long, A. A., and D. N. Sedley. *The Hellenistic Philosophers*. 2 vols. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1987.**
The indispensable resource for primary texts. Volume 1 contains translations organized by topic; Volume 2 contains the original Greek and Latin with commentary. Chapter 51 ("Time") contains the key Stoic sources; Chapter 7 contains the Epicurean sources. Any serious study of Hellenistic philosophy of time begins here.

**Sorabji, Richard. *Time, Creation and the Continuum*. London: Duckworth, 1983.**
The most thorough scholarly treatment of ancient and early medieval theories of time. Sorabji combines meticulous textual scholarship with genuine philosophical acuity. The chapters on the Stoics and Epicureans are particularly valuable for establishing the relationship between their positions and Aristotle's. Highly recommended for any student who wants to go beyond the introductory level.

**Warren, James. *Facing Death: Epicurus and His Critics*. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 2004.**
The best contemporary treatment of Epicurean arguments about death, including the fullest analysis of the symmetry argument available. Warren is both careful about the ancient sources and genuinely engaged with the contemporary philosophical debates. Essential for Exercise 6.11.

**Nagel, Thomas. "Death." In *Mortal Questions*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1979.**
The most influential modern engagement with the Epicurean view of death. Short (about 10 pages) but dense. Nagel takes the Epicurean position seriously rather than dismissing it, which is one reason the essay has been so important. Accessible to undergraduates.

**Long, A. A. *Hellenistic Philosophy: Stoics, Epicureans, Sceptics*. 2nd ed. London: Duckworth, 1986.**
A readable introduction to Hellenistic philosophy as a whole, with good coverage of the metaphysical and physical views of both schools. A good starting point before tackling the primary sources directly.

**Sedley, David. *Lucretius and the Transformation of Greek Wisdom*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1998.**
A detailed study of how Lucretius adapted and transformed Epicurean philosophy in his poem. Essential for understanding what Lucretius adds to Epicurus and what he changes. Particularly good on the structure of *De Rerum Natura* as a philosophical argument.

**White, Michael J. "Stoic Natural Philosophy." In *The Cambridge Companion to the Stoics*, edited by Brad Inwood. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2003.**
A reliable survey of Stoic physics and cosmology, including the cosmological framework within which the Stoic theory of time was developed. Accessible to undergraduates and a good reference for the ekpyrosis doctrine.

**Brunschwig, Jacques. *Papers in Hellenistic Philosophy*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1994.**
A collection of sophisticated essays by one of the leading historians of Hellenistic philosophy. The essay on the supreme genus and Stoic ontology is directly relevant to understanding the incorporeal/corporeal distinction.

**Feldman, Fred. "Some Puzzles About the Evil of Death." *Philosophical Review* 100 (1991): 205–227.**
A useful complement to Nagel, examining the deprivation account of death's badness in more detail and with more philosophical rigor. Accessible and clear.

**McMahan, Jeff. *The Ethics of Killing*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2002, chap. 2.**
McMahan's analysis of death's badness in relation to personal identity and time is among the most sophisticated available. Though the broader book is not primarily about the philosophy of time, Chapter 2 on death and deprivation rewards careful study.

**Caston, Victor. "Something and Nothing." *Oxford Studies in Ancient Philosophy* 17 (1999).**
A careful philosophical analysis of Stoic ontology, focused on the status of universals and concepts but illuminating for the broader question of what "subsistence" means in the Stoic framework. Best for students with some background in contemporary analytic metaphysics.

**Markosian, Ned. "A Defense of Presentism." *Oxford Studies in Metaphysics*, vol. 1 (2004).**
One of the best contemporary defenses of the view that only the present exists. Reading this alongside the Stoic material helps to identify both the anticipations of contemporary presentism in ancient thought and the ways in which the positions differ.
