# Chapter 6: Important Concepts

---

**Incorporeal** (*asomaton*)
In Stoic metaphysics, an incorporeal is something that is real but lacks the capacity to act or be acted upon — the defining characteristic of bodies (*somata*). The four Stoic incorporeals are lekta (sayables), void, place, and time. Being incorporeal does not mean being nonexistent; incorporeals *subsist* rather than fully *exist*.

**Subsistence** (*hyphestanai*) vs. **Existence** (*hyparchein*)
The Stoics drew a distinction within the category of "the real" between things that fully exist (bodies, which can cause and be caused) and things that merely subsist (incorporeals, which are real but causally inert). This is not the same as the distinction between the real and the fictional; it is a distinction within the real itself, based on degree or mode of ontological robustness.

**Lekta** (Sayables)
The propositional contents of statements — the meanings expressed by utterances. Lekta are incorporeal because they are not bodies and cannot act on or be acted upon. They belong to a class of "subsisting" things that play genuine roles in reasoning and communication without being material constituents of the world.

**Time as Interval** (*diastema*)
The Stoic definition of time as the interval of the world's motion. Unlike Aristotle's definition in terms of "number" (which implies a counting mind), the Stoic "interval" is meant to be objective and mind-independent. The interval is not the motion itself but the extent or dimension along which motion unfolds.

**Ekpyrosis** (Cosmic Conflagration)
The Stoic cosmological doctrine that the universe periodically undergoes total dissolution into fire, from which it reconstitutes itself identically. This cycle of conflagration and reconstitution is the cosmic process whose "interval" constitutes time; time on this view is coextensive with the entire cyclical life of the cosmos.

**Eternal Recurrence**
The Stoic cosmological thesis that each cycle of the cosmos is an exact repetition of every previous cycle, down to the smallest detail. Every philosophical argument, every person, every event recurs in exactly the same order in every cosmic cycle. This doctrine is importantly different from Nietzsche's eternal recurrence, which has ethical rather than cosmological import.

**Durationless Instant**
Chrysippus's conclusion about the nature of the strict present: because any positive temporal duration can be divided into a past part and a future part, the present can only be a durationless limit between past and future, analogous to a mathematical point on a line. This is a rigorous consequence of combining temporal continuity with the demand that the present be "purely" present.

**Presentism (Ancient Version)**
The Stoic view that only the present fully exists, while past and future merely subsist. This anticipates the contemporary metaphysical position called presentism (the view that only present entities and events exist), but with the important difference that Stoic presentism involves a graduated ontology (full existence vs. subsistence) rather than a simple binary between existing and not existing.

**Accident of Accidents** (*symbebêkos symbebêkotôn*)
Epicurus's description of the ontological status of time. Ordinary accidents (properties) belong directly to bodies; time is an accident that belongs to events (motions, changes, rests), which are themselves accidents of bodies. This makes time ontologically second-order — real but doubly derivative.

**Relational Theory of Time**
The view, held in different forms by the Epicureans and later by Leibniz, that time is not an independently existing container but is constituted by the temporal relations between events. There is no time "left over" once all events are removed; time is a feature of the event-structure of the world, not a background within which events occur.

**The Symmetry Argument**
Lucretius's argument (*De Rerum Natura* III.830–842) that the post-mortem period of non-existence is structurally symmetric with the prenatal period, and that since we do not regret or fear the latter, consistency requires that we not fear the former. The argument aims to liberate readers from the fear of death by revealing it as a temporal inconsistency.

**Deprivation Account**
The view (defended by Nagel and others) that death is bad primarily because it deprives a person of the goods they would otherwise have enjoyed — further experiences, relationships, achievements. On this account, death's badness is not about the experience of dying (which may be painless) but about the foreclosed future. This account motivates Nagel's challenge to Lucretius: the prenatal period does not deprive anyone of anything, while death deprives a specific existing person.

**Temporal Asymmetry of Attitudes**
The widely observed phenomenon that people are more distressed about future non-existence than about past non-existence, and generally more future-directed in their personal concerns than past-directed. Whether this asymmetry is rational or a bias to be corrected is one of the central questions raised by Lucretius's symmetry argument.

**Specious Present**
The psychologically extended "now" of conscious experience — the temporal interval within which experience presents itself as unified and simultaneous, even though it has positive duration. The specious present was analyzed by William James and Edmund Husserl and is relevant to the Stoic problem of the present: how can consciousness have a present if the strict present is durationless?

**Temporal Atomism vs. Temporal Continuity**
Temporal atomism is the view that time is composed of indivisible minimal units — temporal atoms that cannot be further divided. The Stoics and Epicureans both rejected temporal atomism in favor of temporal continuity (the view that any temporal interval, however small, can be further divided). This commitment to continuity is what drives Chrysippus's analysis of the durationless present.
