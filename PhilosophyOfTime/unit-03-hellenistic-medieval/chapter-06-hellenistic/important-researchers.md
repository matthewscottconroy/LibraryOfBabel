# Chapter 6: Important Researchers

---

**Zeno of Citium** (c. 334–262 BCE)
The founder of Stoicism, who taught at the Stoa Poikile ("painted porch") in Athens. Zeno established the basic Stoic ontological framework, including the distinction between bodies and incorporeals and the classification of time among the incorporeals. His original writings survive only in fragments preserved by later authors, principally Stobaeus and Diogenes Laertius. The definition of time as "the interval of the world's motion" is attributed to Zeno's circle, though it was elaborated by later Stoics, particularly Chrysippus.

**Chrysippus of Soli** (c. 280–207 BCE)
The third head of the Stoic school and by all ancient accounts the philosopher who gave Stoicism its mature systematic form. He wrote prolifically — ancient sources credit him with over 700 books — but nothing survives intact. His analysis of the present moment as a durationless limit is preserved only in Plutarch's hostile report in *On Common Conceptions*. Chrysippus was also central to Stoic logic and ethics; his breadth and rigor made him the dominant intellectual figure of the Hellenistic period.

**Epicurus** (341–270 BCE)
The founder of the Epicurean school, who established his community ("the Garden") in Athens. Epicurus's philosophical project was explicitly therapeutic: philosophy should liberate humans from fear, above all the fear of death and divine punishment. His physics — atomism derived from Democritus but revised — was the theoretical basis for this therapy. The *Letter to Herodotus* is our best source for his philosophy of time; it survives in Diogenes Laertius's *Lives of the Eminent Philosophers*.

**Titus Lucretius Carus** (c. 99–55 BCE)
Roman poet and philosopher, author of *De Rerum Natura* (On the Nature of Things), the most complete surviving exposition of Epicurean philosophy. Lucretius's distinctive contribution was not only to preserve and transmit Epicureanism in Latin but to make it vivid and compelling through brilliant poetic imagination. His treatment of time in Book I and his symmetry argument in Book III are philosophically precise as well as aesthetically powerful. Almost nothing is known of his life.

**A. A. Long** (b. 1937)
British philosopher and classicist, now Emeritus Professor at the University of California, Berkeley, and one of the foremost authorities on Hellenistic philosophy. His collaborative work with David Sedley, *The Hellenistic Philosophers* (2 vols., 1987), is the indispensable scholarly resource for primary texts on Stoic and Epicurean philosophy, including the philosophy of time. Long's earlier study *Hellenistic Philosophy* (1974) remains an accessible introduction to the period.

**David Sedley** (b. 1947)
British classicist and philosopher, Laurence Professor of Ancient Philosophy at Cambridge University. Co-author with Long of *The Hellenistic Philosophers*, Sedley has done extensive work on Epicurean atomism, Stoic philosophy, and the reception of ancient philosophy in later periods. His book *Lucretius and the Transformation of Greek Wisdom* (1998) is essential for understanding Lucretius's philosophical project.

**Richard Sorabji** (b. 1934)
British philosopher and historian of ancient philosophy. His book *Time, Creation and the Continuum* (1983) is one of the most thorough studies of ancient and medieval theories of time, covering Aristotle, the Stoics, Plotinus, Augustine, and their medieval successors. Sorabji's patient reconstruction of arguments from fragmentary sources is an exemplary model of historical philosophy.

**Thomas Nagel** (b. 1937)
American philosopher at New York University, known for contributions to philosophy of mind, ethics, and political philosophy. His essay "Death" (in *Mortal Questions*, 1979) is the most influential modern response to the Epicurean and Lucretian view that death is not bad for the person who dies. Nagel argues that death is bad as a deprivation and challenges the Lucretian symmetry argument by pointing to an asymmetry between pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence.

**James Warren**
British philosopher and classicist at Cambridge University, author of *Facing Death: Epicurus and His Critics* (2004), the most careful recent analysis of Epicurean arguments about death, including Lucretius's symmetry argument. Warren carefully distinguishes the philosophical logic of the Lucretian argument from the historical and philological questions about what Lucretius intended, and he defends the argument more fully than most contemporary philosophers are willing to do.

**Jeff McMahan** (b. 1954)
American philosopher at Oxford University, known for his work in ethics, political philosophy, and the ethics of killing. His *The Ethics of Killing: Problems at the Margins of Life* (2002) contains an extensive analysis of the nature and degree of death's badness, including a careful discussion of the Lucretian symmetry argument and the grounds for temporal asymmetry in our attitudes toward past and future goods.
