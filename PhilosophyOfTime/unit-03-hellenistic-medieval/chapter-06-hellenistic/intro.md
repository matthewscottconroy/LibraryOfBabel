# Chapter 6: Hellenistic Schools

---

## Chapter Introduction

When Aristotle died in 322 BCE, his students and successors faced the task of inheriting one of the most systematic bodies of philosophical thought ever produced. But philosophy does not simply pass from one generation to the next like a deed of property. It transforms in the passage. The Hellenistic period — roughly from Alexander's death in 323 BCE to the rise of the Roman Empire — was one of the most creative and underappreciated eras in the history of philosophy, and its contributions to the philosophy of time deserve far more attention than they typically receive in introductory treatments.

Two schools dominate this chapter: the Stoics and the Epicureans. They were intense rivals, competing not only in the lecture rooms of Athens but for the allegiance of the educated classes across the Mediterranean world. They disagreed about physics, ethics, logic, and almost everything else. But they shared a practical orientation — philosophy was not an academic exercise for them but a way of life — and they shared a concern with the nature of time that expressed itself in ways both technically precise and existentially urgent.

The Stoics (Sections 6.1 and 6.2) developed perhaps the most sophisticated ancient ontology. Their framework divided reality into corporeal things — things that can act or be acted upon — and four incorporeals that are real but not bodies: sayables (*lekta*), void, place, and time. Understanding what it means for time to be "incorporeal" in this technical sense is the first task of this chapter, and it opens onto a much larger discussion of what "existence" means in Stoic metaphysics. From there, we examine Chrysippus's remarkably radical analysis of the present moment — an analysis that anticipates debates about presentism that are very much alive today.

The Epicureans (Section 6.3) approached time from a different direction entirely. Starting from the atomist physics inherited from Democritus and Leucippus, Epicurus arrived at a view of time as an "accident of accidents" — not a thing that exists alongside bodies and events, but a higher-order property that supervenes on them. Lucretius, the first-century BCE Roman poet whose *De Rerum Natura* is our most complete surviving source for Epicurean natural philosophy, argued for this view with arguments of striking clarity and modern resonance.

Section 6.4 examines what is perhaps the single most discussed argument about time and death in the entire history of philosophy: Lucretius's symmetry argument. If the prenatal period of non-existence causes us no concern in retrospect, why should the post-mortem period of non-existence cause us concern in prospect? The argument is elegant, counterintuitive, and philosophically serious — and the responses to it (from Thomas Nagel, Jeff McMahan, and James Warren, among others) have generated some of the best work in contemporary philosophy of death.

This chapter covers material that is often treated only briefly in histories of philosophy, partly because the primary sources are fragmentary and reconstruction is required. That fragmentation is not a defect but a kind of philosophical invitation: the work of piecing together a position from evidence, weighing the reliability of sources, and distinguishing what a philosopher actually said from what was reported of them is excellent philosophical training. Where we rely on second-hand sources, we will say so and explain why.

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: articulate the Stoic distinction between corporeal and incorporeal and explain why time was classified as incorporeal; reconstruct Chrysippus's analysis of the present and its difficulties; explain the Epicurean relational account of time and contrast it with the Stoic view; and reconstruct and critically evaluate Lucretius's symmetry argument. These are not small achievements. The Hellenistic schools thought harder about time than most subsequent philosophical traditions have given them credit for.
