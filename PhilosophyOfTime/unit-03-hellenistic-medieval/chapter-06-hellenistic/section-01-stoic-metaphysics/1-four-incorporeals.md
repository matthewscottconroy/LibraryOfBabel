# 6.1.1 — The Four Incorporeals: The Ontological Framework

---

## Something and Nothing

One of the oldest temptations in metaphysics is to think that the question of what exists admits of a clean binary answer: a thing either exists or it doesn't. The Stoics resisted this temptation with unusual sophistication. They recognized that there are things that are real — that play genuine roles in the world and in our thought — without qualifying as full-blooded existents. Their framework for capturing this graduated ontology is among the most interesting in ancient philosophy.

The Stoics distinguished at the highest level between the *ti* (literally "something," the most general positive ontological category) and the *outhen* (nothing). Everything that falls under *ti* is real in some sense. Below *ti*, they drew a fundamental distinction between *somata* (bodies) and *asomata* (incorporeals). Bodies are the only things that truly *exist* (*hyparchein*) — they have the capacity to act and be acted upon. The Stoics were prepared to say that even qualities, souls, and the commanding faculty of the mind (*hegemonikon*) are corporeal. Incorporeals, by contrast, *subsist* (*hyphestanai*) — they are real but do not possess the full-blooded causal efficacy that characterizes bodies (Long and Sedley 1987: 27–28 = §27A–C).

The four incorporeals are:

1. **Lekta** (sayables): the meanings or propositional contents of utterances, distinct from both the physical utterance and the mental act of understanding
2. **Void** (*kenon*): infinite empty space surrounding the finite cosmos, occupied by nothing
3. **Place** (*topos*): the extension occupied by a body
4. **Time** (*chronos*): the interval of the world's motion

This is a carefully curated list. Each of the four incorporeals had to pass a test: it had to be genuinely real (not nothing), but it had to lack the power to act or be acted upon that characterizes bodies. Let us verify that time passes this test.

## What "Incorporeal" Means for Time

Can time act on anything? Can anything act on time? In the ordinary sense, no. Time does not push or pull; it does not heat things or cool them; it cannot be increased or decreased by any physical operation. In this sense, time is causally inert: it is not an agent of change. Nor can physical processes alter time itself. The cosmic conflagration that periodically destroys and reconstitutes the Stoic world (on which more below) does not affect the interval within which it occurs — time frames the event rather than participating in it.

But time is not nothing. The Stoics insisted that time is real. It can be measured. Events occur *in* it and can be ordered by it. It provides the framework within which causal sequences unfold. If time were simply nothing — a mere concept with no foothold in reality — these features would be inexplicable. The Stoics were not tempted by an eliminativist view of time any more than they were tempted by an eliminativist view of meanings.

Our primary evidence here is assembled in Long and Sedley's collection. Stobaeus preserves the report that "the Stoics say there are four incorporeals: sayable, void, place, time" (Long and Sedley 1987: §51A). Arius Didymus, cited by Stobaeus, gives slightly more detail: these four "subsist" (*hyphestanai*) alongside bodies but do not themselves exist in the robust sense (Long and Sedley 1987: §27D). The philosophical task is to understand what this subsistence consists in.

## The Distinction Between Existence and Subsistence

The Stoic distinction between existence and subsistence is not the same as modern debates about "being" and "existence" in the philosophy of logic (where, for example, Meinong's theory of objects posited non-existent objects). It is closer to the distinction some contemporary metaphysicians draw between things that are "part of the furniture of the world" and things that are "real but derivative" — ontologically second-class but not fictional.

A helpful analogy: consider the equator. The equator is not a body — it occupies no space, has no mass, cannot be seen or touched. But it is not fictitious. Ships cross it. It separates hemispheres. It plays a determinate role in geography and navigation. The equator is real without being a physical thing. It is, we might say, a real feature of the world that is not itself a constituent of the world in the way bodies are. Something similar is true, for the Stoics, of time. Time is a genuine feature of the world — the interval of the cosmic process — without itself being a body that could act or be acted upon.

This analogy has limits. The equator is defined by our conventions of mapping, while the Stoics would insist that time is mind-independent. But the analogy captures the ontological middle ground that the Stoic framework is trying to mark out.

## Lekta and Their Significance

It is worth pausing on the *lekta* — the sayables — because they illuminate the broader logic of Stoic incorporeals. Lekta are the propositional contents expressed by utterances. When I say "Socrates walks," the sounds I produce are physical; the mental act of understanding is physical (since mind is corporeal for the Stoics); but the *meaning* — the proposition that Socrates walks, which can be expressed in different languages, understood by different people, and which can be true or false — is incorporeal. It subsists without being a body.

The importance of lekta for time is indirect but real. The Stoics were prepared to posit incorporeals whenever they had systematic theoretical reasons to do so — when eliminating the posit would leave something genuinely unexplained. Time, place, and void were posited for the same reason: because the physical world cannot be fully described without reference to the framework within which bodies operate, and that framework, while real, is not itself corporeal.

## Bodies, Qualities, and the Cosmos

One last feature of the Stoic framework deserves mention. The Stoics held that the cosmos as a whole is a living organism — an ordered whole pervaded by *pneuma* (a fiery, vital breath) that holds everything together and gives each part its characteristic qualities. This cosmic organism undergoes periodic total conflagrations (*ekpyrosis*): the entire cosmos is consumed in fire and then reconstitutes itself identically. This cyclical cosmology has implications for the nature of time, which we will examine in the next subsection.

What matters here is that the Stoic ontological framework is not arbitrary. The distinction between bodies and incorporeals follows from the core Stoic thesis that causal power is the mark of genuine existence. Bodies are causally active and passive; incorporeals are neither. Time is incorporeal because, while it is the framework within which causation operates, it does not itself cause or undergo change. It is the interval of the world's motion — a feature of that motion — but not an agent in it.

This is a philosophically serious and defensible position. Whether it is ultimately correct is a different question, and one to which we will return. But the Stoic framework shows that the question "does time exist?" is not as simple as it sounds. How we answer depends on what we mean by existence — and getting clear on that is part of what philosophy of time is for (Long and Sedley 1987: 162–163).
