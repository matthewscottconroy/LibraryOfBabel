# 6.1.2 — Time as the Interval of the World's Motion

---

## The Definition

The Stoic definition of time survives in a report preserved by Stobaeus, drawing on an early Stoic doxography that almost certainly reflects the views of Zeno of Citium, the founder of Stoicism, and was elaborated by his successors. The report reads: "Time is the interval of motion according to which the measure of speed and slowness is spoken of" (Stobaeus, *Eclogae* I.105 = Long and Sedley 1987: §51B). A related formulation appears in the same passage: time is "the interval of the motion of the cosmos."

This is a compact definition that rewards unpacking. Let us take it phrase by phrase.

**"The interval"** (*diastema*): The Greek word *diastema* can mean extension, stretch, or interval. It is the same word used in music for the interval between notes. Time is not the motion itself but the *extent* or *measure* of the motion — the dimension along which the motion unfolds. This marks an important distinction: time is not identical with the physical process it measures; it is the framework within which that process can be measured.

**"Of the world's motion"**: The Stoics specified that this is the interval of *cosmic* motion, the overall movement of the cosmos taken as a unified whole. This is significant because it anchors time to the physical world without identifying it with any particular motion within it. The cosmic conflagration and reconstitution — the entire life-cycle of the Stoic universe — are what time primarily measures.

## Comparison with Aristotle

The Stoic definition is clearly in dialogue with Aristotle's, and comparison is illuminating. Aristotle had defined time as "the number of motion with respect to before and after" (*Physics* IV.11, 219b1–2, trans. Hussey 1983). For Aristotle, time is what we count when we count the before-and-after of motion; it requires a counting mind, a soul capable of numbering. Plotinus will later make much of this mentalist implication in Aristotle's definition, but the Stoics had already drawn a different lesson.

The Stoic definition replaces "number" (*arithmos*) with "interval" (*diastema*). This is not a trivial substitution. Number suggests a discrete, mind-dependent counting operation. Interval suggests a continuous, mind-independent extent. For the Stoics — who, unlike Aristotle, held that time exists even if no mind is present to count it — this substitution reflects a genuine commitment to the objective, mind-independent character of time as an incorporeal framework.

There is another difference: Aristotle's definition is general — it applies to any motion, and time is defined relative to any motion whatsoever. The Stoic definition anchors time specifically to the motion of the cosmos. This gives Stoic time a distinctly cosmological flavor. Time is not the interval of any arbitrary process but of the world-process itself.

## The Cosmic Conflagration (*Ekpyrosis*)

To fully understand the Stoic definition, we need to know something about Stoic cosmology. The Stoics held that the universe undergoes periodic total conflagrations: at the end of each cosmic cycle, everything — all the bodies that make up the cosmos — is consumed in fire, which is the primal element from which all others derive. This *ekpyrosis* is not destruction in the sense of annihilation; it is a return to the source. From this cosmic fire, the entire world reconstitutes itself, and it reconstitutes itself *identically*: every event, every person, every philosophical debate recurs in exactly the same order in the next world-cycle. This is the Stoic doctrine of eternal recurrence.

What happens to time during the ekpyrosis? This is a question on which our sources are not entirely clear, but the most plausible reading is that time, as an incorporeal framework, persists across the conflagration. The motion of the cosmos is interrupted — there is a phase of pure fire that is not articulated into the complex motions of ordinary cosmic life — but the interval that constitutes time is not destroyed. It continues to be the interval within which even the conflagration occurs. If this reading is right, time in Stoic cosmology is strictly speaking unbounded — it extends before the current cosmic cycle and will extend after it.

This has an interesting implication. The Stoic definition ties time to the world's motion, but the world's motion includes not just the orderly processes of the current cosmic epoch but the entire cyclical life of the cosmos across conflagrations and reconstitutions. Time is thus cosmic in scope in a very strong sense.

## The Relationship Between Time and the Cosmos

Commentators have sometimes asked: is time identical with the world's motion, or merely coextensive with it? The Stoic definition says time is the *interval* of the motion — not the motion itself. This suggests the following picture: the world's motion provides the content of time, but time as interval is distinct from that content. It is the dimension along which the content is ordered, not the content itself. This is analogous to the way that spatial extension is not identical with the matter that occupies it, even though matter is what gives spatial extension its determinate character.

This analogy is imperfect, since place (not extension as such) was the Stoic incorporeal that corresponded to space, but the structural point stands: time is a real framework, not a body, within which the world's motions are ordered and measured.

## Infinite Divisibility and the Problem of the Present

The definition of time as the interval of cosmic motion has a further implication that the Stoics were fully aware of: if time is an interval, it is divisible. And if it is divisible, then any temporal interval, however small, can be further divided. There is no smallest possible duration — no temporal atom. This is not a problem unique to the Stoics; it is a feature of any continuous conception of time.

But it creates a puzzle specifically about the present: if every duration can be divided, and if the present has some positive duration, then it can be divided into a past part and a future part, neither of which is the present. So the present, if it exists at all, must be durationless — an instantaneous knife-edge between past and future. Chrysippus pressed this point with great force, as we will see in Section 6.2. The definition established in this section sets up the problem that the next section addresses.

## Long-Standing Resonances

The Stoic definition anticipates several features of modern relational theories of time. Like Leibniz and unlike Newton, the Stoics held that time is not an absolute container independent of the world's processes but rather an interval inherently tied to those processes. There is no time in a universe with no motion — not because time would be undetectable but because there would be nothing for time to be. Time is not a thing that could persist in a motionless universe; it is the structure of cosmic process itself.

This is a position with significant contemporary defenders. The substantivalist-relationist debate in philosophy of time — which we will examine in detail in Chapter 10 when we reach Leibniz and Newton — has Stoic roots. The Stoics were among the earliest thinkers to articulate a clear relationist intuition about time and to argue for it from metaphysical principles about what kinds of things are real. That they arrived at this position while still maintaining that time is genuinely real and not merely a mental construct marks them as careful and sophisticated metaphysicians (Long and Sedley 1987: 304–306; White 2003: 151–153).
