# Section 6.1: Stoic Metaphysics and the Incorporeals

---

## Section Introduction

Before we can understand what the Stoics said about time, we need to understand the ontological framework within which they said it. Stoic metaphysics is unlike anything in Plato or Aristotle, and it rewards careful study in its own right. The Stoics divided everything that is real into two fundamental kinds: bodies (*somata*) and incorporeals (*asomata*). This division is not merely a taxonomic convenience; it carries deep philosophical weight.

For the Stoics, only bodies can act or be acted upon. This is their criterion for being a body: causal efficacy. Mind, for the Stoics, is itself corporeal — the soul is a special kind of air, the *pneuma* or "breath" that pervades and animates the body. Even virtues and vices are corporeal on this account, since they have genuine causal powers. This is a thoroughgoing materialism in one sense — everything with genuine causal powers is material — but it is not a reductive materialism that denies reality to non-material things. The incorporeals are real. They just have a different mode of reality: they do not exist (*hyparchein*) in the full sense but *subsist* (*hyphestanai*).

Time falls into this category of the incorporeal. It is not nothing. It cannot be dismissed. But it is not a body, and it does not exist in the same robust sense that a stone or a person exists. Understanding exactly what this means, and whether it is coherent, is the central task of this section.

The two subsections that follow examine: first, the four incorporeals and the metaphysical framework that generates the distinction (6.1.1); and second, the specific Stoic definition of time as the interval of the world's motion and the cosmological context in which that definition operates (6.1.2).
