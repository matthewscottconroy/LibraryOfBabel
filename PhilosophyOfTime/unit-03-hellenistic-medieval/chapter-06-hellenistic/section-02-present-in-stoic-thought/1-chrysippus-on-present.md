# 6.2.1 — Chrysippus on the Present

---

## The Radical Thesis

Chrysippus of Soli (c. 280–207 BCE), the third head of the Stoic school and arguably its greatest systematic thinker, held an astonishing view about the ontological status of the present. According to a report preserved by Plutarch, Chrysippus maintained that of the three temporal divisions — past, present, and future — only the present *exists* in the strict sense, while past and future merely *subsist*. But he then went further: the present itself, when analyzed rigorously, turns out not to be a positive temporal interval with genuine duration but rather an infinitely thin boundary — a limit that has no positive extent at all.

Our primary source is Plutarch's *On Common Conceptions* (1081E–F), which Long and Sedley catalogue as §51C. The passage is hostile in intent — Plutarch is criticizing Stoic inconsistency — but it preserves the doctrine with what appears to be genuine fidelity. Plutarch reports that Chrysippus said the present time is never entire (*holon*) but is always being divided; the present is "a part neither of the past nor of the future" but is nothing more than the moving boundary between them. Chrysippus compares the present to a point — a boundary that has position but no magnitude (Long and Sedley 1987: §51C; Plutarch, *On Common Conceptions* 1081E–F).

## The Argument for Infinite Divisibility

Why did Chrysippus think the present was infinitely divisible? The argument, as best we can reconstruct it, runs as follows.

Suppose the present has some positive duration, however small — say, one second. Then that second can be divided into two halves: the first half-second has already passed (it is now past), and the second half-second has not yet occurred (it is future). So neither half-second is wholly present. The same reasoning applies to any sub-interval we might choose as "the present." Any positive duration contains a past part and a future part and is therefore not purely present.

This is a reductio ad absurdum of the idea that the present has positive duration. If the present cannot be any positive duration, then the only candidate for the present is a durationless instant — what mathematicians call a point on the temporal line, which has position but no extension.

The Stoics were committed to the continuity of time — they had no temporal atomism, no minimum possible duration. Given this commitment, the argument is valid. If time is a continuum and we define the present as the boundary between past and future, the present has zero positive duration.

## What Exists, What Subsists

Here the peculiarity of Chrysippus's position becomes vivid. The Stoics held that only the present exists in the full sense (*hyparchein*). But if the present is a durationless instant with zero duration, what exactly is it that fully exists? An instantaneous boundary has no extent — it is nowhere "thick" enough to contain any event, any motion, any occurrence. Events take time; even the fastest physical process lasts for some positive duration. A durationless instant cannot contain any event.

Chrysippus's response, insofar as we can reconstruct it, seems to have been to accept both horns of this strange position: the present, in the strict metaphysical sense, is indeed a durationless limit; but in common parlance, we use "the present" loosely to refer to some temporal neighborhood of that limit — "today," "this year," "this century" are all loose present-talk, not strict metaphysical claims. The strict present is an ideal boundary; the practical present is whatever temporal span is salient for the purposes at hand.

This distinction between strict and loose senses of "present" is philosophically important but also somewhat unsatisfying. It does not quite resolve the problem; it redefines it. The strict present, being durationless, cannot really be the locus of what exists, since existence requires some positive temporal extent. Chrysippus seems aware of this tension but does not have a fully worked-out response to it (Sorabji 1983: 21–23).

## Infinite Divisibility and the Regress

There is a further difficulty. If the present is always already divisible — if any period I call "the present" can be divided into a past-half and a future-half — then there is no terminus to the analysis. I never reach a genuine present; I only ever reach smaller and smaller intervals, each of which admits the same division. The present is, in this sense, always receding. What I call "now" is really a neighborhood of now that is being constantly evacuated.

Plutarch, hostile witness though he is, puts the point with force: Chrysippus seems committed to the view that the present is being perpetually divided, so that we are always standing between a past and a future with no genuine present to stand in (*On Common Conceptions* 1081F). The present is like a mathematical limit — it can be approached arbitrarily closely but never reached.

Modern philosophers of time will recognize this as a precursor to the problem of the "specious present" — the psychologically extended "now" that William James, Edmund Husserl, and William Earle analyzed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The specious present is the temporally extended interval within which experience occurs as a unified whole, and it does not seem to be reducible to a durationless instant. Chrysippus's strict present raises the question of what grounds the specious present: how can we experience a "now" at all if the real now has no duration?

## The Present Moment and What "Exists"

The most interesting philosophical legacy of Chrysippus's analysis is its relevance to the contemporary debate between presentism and eternalism. Presentism is the view that only the present moment exists — past and future things and events do not exist, they merely did or will exist. This sounds like a natural commonsense view, but Chrysippus's analysis reveals a problem: if the present is a durationless instant, then presentism seems committed to saying that the only things that exist are things that exist in a durationless instant — which is to say, nothing at all exists, since existence requires positive duration (Sider 2001: 11–12).

The Stoic response — that past and future subsist even if they do not exist in the full sense — is one way of addressing this problem. But it does so at the cost of complicating the ontology: we now have three modes of being rather than a simple exist/not-exist binary. Whether this is a refinement or a retreat is a question worth debating. What is clear is that Chrysippus saw the problem clearly and was not willing to paper over it with comfortable imprecision (Long and Sedley 1987: 164–165).
