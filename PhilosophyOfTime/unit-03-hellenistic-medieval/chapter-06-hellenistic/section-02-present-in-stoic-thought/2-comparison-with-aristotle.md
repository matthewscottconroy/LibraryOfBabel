# 6.2.2 — Chrysippus and Aristotle: Comparing Analyses of the Now

---

## Common Ground

Aristotle and Chrysippus approached the present moment from different directions and arrived at positions that are, on the surface, quite different. But they shared a problem: how to give an account of the "now" — the present moment — that does not dissolve it into inconsistency. Comparing their solutions reveals both what they had in common and where they genuinely parted ways.

Aristotle, in *Physics* IV.10–14, devoted sustained attention to time and the now. His starting analysis was that time is divisible — into hours, minutes, and so on — but he resisted the idea that time is made up of discrete nows, as if a series of instantaneous moments were strung together like beads. Time is continuous (*syneches*), which means it flows smoothly rather than jumping from one now to the next. The now (*to nun*) is not a part of time but the boundary of temporal intervals — it is what separates the past from the future, and it is in this sense like a point on a line: it bounds the line without being a segment of it (Aristotle, *Physics* IV.11, 219b9–22, trans. Hussey 1983).

This is strikingly close to what Chrysippus said. Both thinkers hold that the now is not a part of time but a limit. Both hold that time is continuous and infinitely divisible. Both deny that the now has positive duration. The convergence is not coincidental: the Stoics were deeply engaged with Aristotle, and the parallels here reflect genuine philosophical inheritance.

## Where They Diverge: Number vs. Interval

The first and most important divergence is in the definition of time itself. Aristotle defines time as "the number of motion with respect to before and after" — a definition that seems to require a counting mind. The Stoics define time as "the interval of the world's motion" — a definition that is structurally analogous but deliberately non-mentalist. As we saw in Section 6.1.2, this shift from "number" to "interval" reflects a different view of time's relation to mind: Aristotle seems to hold that time would not exist without a soul to count it; the Stoics seem to hold that time is an objective feature of the cosmos (Sorabji 1983: 73–79).

This divergence is philosophically significant. If Aristotle is right, then questions about time are inseparable from questions about consciousness. If the Stoics are right, time is objective and mind-independent — it belongs to the cosmos, not to the observer. The twentieth century's phenomenological tradition, from Husserl through Heidegger to Merleau-Ponty, tends toward the Aristotelian side; the physicalist tradition tends toward the Stoic side. The debate was already live in antiquity.

## What Only the Present "Exists" Means for Aristotle vs. the Stoics

Aristotle does not say that only the present exists. His analysis is of time's structure, not primarily of time's ontological status. He does not develop a presentist metaphysics in the way Chrysippus does. The Aristotelian treatment of the now is part of an analysis of how continuous quantities have limits, and it does not generate a general thesis about what temporal intervals exist and what they don't.

Chrysippus, by contrast, makes existence-claims about temporal regions: only the present exists; the past and future merely subsist. This is a more explicitly ontological thesis. It anticipates what contemporary metaphysicians call *presentism* — the view that the present is the only temporal region that genuinely exists — though it differs from most contemporary presentism in holding that the present is a durationless instant rather than a temporal interval (Sider 2001: 11–15; Markosian 2004: 47–49).

The Stoic version of presentism is, in this sense, more radical than most contemporary versions. Contemporary presentists typically allow that the present has some positive extent — that it includes at least the contents of the specious present, or that "now" picks out a suitably short interval. Chrysippus's version does not allow this escape: the present is, strictly speaking, durationless, and what exists strictly speaking is nothing more than this durationless boundary.

## What the Stoic Doctrine Anticipates

The resonances with contemporary debates are worth spelling out.

**Presentism and the growing block**: The contemporary debate between presentism (only the present exists), the growing block theory (the past and present exist, the future does not), and eternalism (past, present, and future equally exist) has structural analogies in Stoic thought. The Stoic view — only the present *exists*, past and future *subsist* — is closest to presentism but with a more nuanced ontology. The notion of subsistence plays a role analogous to the growing-block theorist's claim that the past has some kind of being even though it is not "present."

**The problem of temporal ontology**: Modern presentists face a notorious problem: how can we make true past-tensed statements ("Caesar crossed the Rubicon") if the past does not exist? One response appeals to the idea of truthmakers in the present; another appeals to the claim that past-tensed statements are made true by present facts that "trace back" to past events. The Stoic notion of the past as *subsisting* — having some mode of reality without fully existing — offers an interestingly different framework: past things are real enough to be the semantic referents of past-tensed statements but not real enough to be genuine causes.

**The specious present problem**: As noted above, Chrysippus's durationless present creates the problem of how experience is possible. Aristotle has the same problem, but he does not develop it in the direction of a phenomenology of temporal experience. That development will be Augustine's contribution, and it will require reconceiving what the present is — not as a mathematical limit but as the structure of conscious attention.

## A Tentative Assessment

The Stoic analysis of the present is more philosophically radical than Aristotle's, and its radicalism is both a strength and a liability. The strength is clarity: if time is continuous and the present is not a part of time, then the present can only be a durationless limit. This follows rigorously from premises the Stoics are committed to, and they deserve credit for following the argument rather than fudging it.

The liability is that this clean logical result is very difficult to reconcile with the phenomenology of temporal experience. We do not experience time as a series of durationless instants. We experience it as flowing, as having a present that spans at least a brief interval. Any account of time that leaves no room for this experienced present is missing something important — even if it is hard to say exactly what. The solution, as we will see, required a more radical rethinking of the relationship between time and mind than either Aristotle or the Stoics attempted.
