# Section 6.2: The Present in Stoic Thought

---

## Section Introduction

If you had to identify the single most philosophically acute contribution of Stoic thought to the philosophy of time, it would be Chrysippus's analysis of the present. This analysis is brief — it survives only in a hostile report by Plutarch — but it is extraordinarily dense, and it raises problems that philosophy has never fully resolved. The Stoics were not content to treat the "now" as a simple, obvious thing; they pressed the question of what the present actually is with a rigor that exposes deep tensions in any continuous conception of time.

The problem, in brief, is this: on any account of time as a continuous interval that is infinitely divisible, the present faces a dilemma. If the present has duration — if it lasts for any positive stretch of time — then it contains a past part (what has just occurred) and a future part (what is about to occur), and so it is not purely present. But if the present has no duration — if it is a durationless instant — then it seems to be a kind of nothing, a boundary between past and future with no thickness of its own. Chrysippus confronted this dilemma directly and arrived at a remarkable conclusion.

Section 6.2.1 reconstructs Chrysippus's position and its implications. Section 6.2.2 sets it alongside Aristotle's analysis of the Now, drawing out both the parallels and the differences, and considers what the Stoic position anticipates in contemporary debates about presentism.
