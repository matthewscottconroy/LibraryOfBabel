# 6.3.1 — Epicurus: Time as Accident of Accidents

---

## The Atomist Framework

To understand Epicurus's account of time, you need to begin with the atomist universe. For Epicurus — following and revising Leucippus and Democritus — the fundamental constituents of reality are atoms (*atomoi*) and void (*kenon*). Atoms are indivisible, indestructible material particles moving through infinite void. Everything that exists is composed of atoms; everything that happens is the result of atomic collisions, aggregations, and dispersions. There is no matter other than atoms, no space other than void, and no agency other than atomic motion.

Within this framework, Epicurus recognized a category of things that are real but that are not atoms and not void: what he called *symbebêkota* — properties, accidents, or attributes. Accidents are features that belong to bodies (aggregates of atoms) rather than to atoms directly. They are real in the sense that they genuinely characterize the bodies they attach to; but they do not have independent existence. Remove the body and the accident goes with it.

Epicurus's *Letter to Herodotus*, our most important source for his physics, contains the key passage on time. He writes:

> "Time is a peculiar thing: we must not say 'time is' in the same way as substances... Time is an accident of accidents — it is what accompanies the motions and rests and changes of things, and we must not seek to grasp it as if it were a substance in its own right." (Epicurus, *Letter to Herodotus* §73, in Long and Sedley 1987: §7A, trans. adapted from Inwood and Gerson)

The phrase "accident of accidents" (*symbebêkos symbebêkotôn*) is technically precise. Ordinary accidents — properties like "red" or "hot" — belong directly to bodies. Time is an accident not of bodies but of the *events* (changes, motions, rests) that bodies undergo. It is, as it were, one ontological level further removed from basic reality than ordinary accidents.

## What "Accident of Accidents" Means

This locution deserves careful unpacking. Consider a particular event: Socrates walks across the agora. This event involves Socrates (a complex of atoms), his motion (an accident of Socrates), and a duration (the time the walk takes). Epicurus's claim is that the duration is an accident of the motion — it characterizes the motion rather than directly characterizing Socrates. And the motion is itself an accident of Socrates. So the duration is an accident of an accident: a second-order property.

This means that time, on Epicurus's account, cannot exist without events (processes, motions, changes). There is no time "left over" when all events are removed. Time is not an empty container that persists even when nothing is happening; it is a feature of what happens, and it exists only as long as there is something happening to be its subject.

This is a *relational* view of time in the strongest sense: time is constituted by the temporal relations between events, and those relations supervene on the events themselves. There is no additional "temporal substance" that exists alongside events and within which events are located.

## The Phenomenological Starting Point

It is worth noting how Epicurus arrives at this position. He does not begin with abstract metaphysical principles and deduce the nature of time from them (though his conclusions cohere with his atomist metaphysics). He begins, characteristically, with what is evident to experience: "We must apprehend time, using an evident standard, just as we apprehend everything else — we must apprehend it by our clearest impression, which is that of days and nights, and the changes and motions and rests that we observe in things" (Epicurus, *Letter to Herodotus* §72–73, Long and Sedley 1987: §7A).

The method here is empiricist. Our concept of time is derived from experience of temporal processes — the alternation of day and night, the observation of change and motion. We do not have an experience of time itself, independent of these processes. If this is right, it suggests that time is not the kind of thing that can be independently experienced; it is always given through our experience of temporal events. This phenomenological point supports the metaphysical conclusion: if time cannot be experienced independently of temporal events, then time is not an independently existing thing.

This is an Epicurean version of an argument that appears, in different forms, in Berkeley, Hume, Leibniz, and twentieth-century relationist philosophers of physics. The argument from the conditions of temporal experience to the relational nature of time is one of the more durable threads in the history of the philosophy of time.

## Implications for the Ontology of Time

Epicurus's account has several distinctive implications.

**Against absolute time**: If time is an accident of accidents, there is no absolute temporal container. There is no "void" of time, analogous to the void of space, within which events are located. Events are not in time the way balls are in a box. Rather, time is in events — it characterizes them, and through them, characterizes the relations between them.

**Against temporal vacuum**: On Epicurus's view, a period of time with no events occurring is strictly speaking impossible — or at least, the concept of such a period is empty. There is no time between events if no events connect them. This contrasts with Newton, for whom absolute time flows whether or not anything is happening in it.

**For temporal pluralism**: If time supervenes on events, then there is a question whether there is one unified time or many local times — one for each system of interacting events. Epicurus does not develop this implication, but it is worth noting that the modern physicist's concern about whether there is a global time for the universe (as opposed to local times relative to reference frames) resonates with an Epicurean concern, even if the physics is utterly different.

## Comparison with Stoic Time

A comparison with the Stoic view is instructive. Both Stoics and Epicureans held that time is not an independently existing body. But they differed in how they characterized what time is. For the Stoics, time is an incorporeal — real but not corporeal, a genuine feature of the cosmos. For Epicurus, time is an accident of accidents — a second-order property that supervenes on events. These are not the same view.

The Stoic view seems more willing to accord time a kind of robust reality: time subsists, it is a genuine cosmic interval, it structures the entire life of the cosmos. The Epicurean view is more deflationary: time is not quite a thing at all, but a feature of things. The Stoics give time an objective status that the Epicureans seem reluctant to grant.

This difference matters because it bears on the question of whether time could exist in a universe with no change. The Stoic answer seems to be: yes, time would persist even in a universe with no change, as the interval of a cosmic process that was itself motionless (though this is not a scenario Stoic cosmology makes room for). The Epicurean answer is: no, time cannot exist without change, because time is constituted by change.
