# 6.3.2 — Lucretius on Time: *De Rerum Natura* I.459–463

---

## The Poet-Philosopher

Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99–55 BCE) wrote one of the most ambitious poems in Latin — *De Rerum Natura* (On the Nature of Things) — as a vehicle for Epicurean philosophy. The poem is a tour de force: it presents a complete atomist physics, a philosophy of mind, an account of the evolution of civilization, and a sustained argument against the fear of death, all in the meter of Homer. It is at once a work of philosophy and a work of art, and unlike many philosophical poems (which use the verse as a thin wrapper for prose argument), Lucretius's verse actively serves his philosophical purposes. The poem is intended to be beautiful because beauty is evidence of truth, and because only beauty can hold the reader's attention long enough to deliver the philosophical medicine.

Lucretius's treatment of time in Book I, lines 459–463, is brief but philosophically pointed. It comes in the context of his general argument about what kinds of things exist. He has established that everything that can be said to exist is either a body or void. He then considers what to say about things that are clearly real but that are neither bodies nor void — such as time, events, and the past history of things. His answer is that these are *accidentia* or *eventa*: accidents and events. They are real, but their reality is derivative — they cannot exist except insofar as they belong to or characterize bodies and the interactions of bodies.

## The Key Passage

The passage in question reads, in a literal translation:

> "Time also does not exist by itself; but from things themselves sense-experience makes clear what happened in the past, what is happening now, and what is going to follow upon that. One must admit that no one has a sense-experience of time itself apart from the motion of things, or their restful quiet." (Lucretius, *De Rerum Natura* I.459–463, trans. Melville 2009, adapted)

The argument has a simple but elegant structure. It proceeds from an empiricist premise about experience to a metaphysical conclusion about existence. Let us lay it out:

1. We have no sense-experience of time itself — we only ever experience time as the time of some event or process (the pastness of what happened, the presentness of what is occurring, the futurity of what will come).
2. If we had no sense-experience of time-in-itself, this would naturally be explained by the fact that time does not exist in itself — it only exists as a feature of events and processes.
3. Therefore, time does not exist by itself.

The premise is a conceivability-cum-experiential claim: can you conceive of, or have any sensory access to, time that is not the time of something? Lucretius's answer is no. Strip away all events, motions, and processes — imagine a universe with nothing happening — and you have no grip on time at all. Time is not something over and above the events that occur in it; it is the temporal character of those events.

## Testing the Argument

Lucretius's argument depends on the conceivability premise, and it is worth pressing on it. Can we conceive of time apart from events?

The hardest case for Lucretius is this: we can surely imagine a universe in which nothing is happening for some period, and then something begins to happen. Don't we then have a conception of time during which nothing occurred? Newton would say yes: absolute time is precisely this empty temporal container that persists even when nothing is in it. If we can coherently imagine such a period, the conceivability premise seems false.

Lucretius's response, implicit in the context, would be: what you are imagining is not time itself but the *before* and *after* of some event sequence — you are borrowing temporal structure from the events and projecting it into the gap between them. You are not really conceiving of time independent of all events; you are conceiving of time relative to events by imagining a region of the temporal framework that they define but do not fill.

Whether this response is convincing depends on how we think about the relationship between temporal vacua and relational time. Leibniz later made exactly this argument with greater technical precision: the concept of "empty time before the creation" is not coherent because temporal order is constituted by event sequences, and there is no event sequence to constitute temporal order in a period of absolute nothing (Leibniz, *Fifth Letter to Clarke* §4, in Alexander 1956: 75). Lucretius anticipates this by two millennia.

## The Modern Parallel: Relationalism in Philosophy of Physics

The view Lucretius is defending has a direct descendant in contemporary philosophy of physics. The debate between substantivalists and relationists about the nature of spacetime is, at its core, a debate about whether spacetime is an independently existing entity (an absolute container, like Newton's absolute space and time) or merely the totality of spatiotemporal relations between events (with no "container" over and above the relata). Lucretius is clearly a relationist.

Contemporary physicists have found Machian relationism attractive in the context of general relativity, which makes spacetime geometry dynamical — the structure of spacetime is not a fixed background but is affected by the distribution of matter and energy. In this sense, Einstein's general relativity leans Leibnizean/Lucretian rather than Newtonian (Barbour 1999: 1–10; Earman 1989: 12–15). Lucretius did not, of course, anticipate the detailed physics; but he anticipated the philosophical stance that has proven most compatible with it.

## The Rhetorical Function

It is worth noting that Lucretius's treatment of time is not primarily a contribution to metaphysics for its own sake. It is part of a larger argument aimed at removing the fear of death. If time has no independent existence — if it is merely a feature of events — then the "time after death" is not a real temporal container waiting to be empty and lonely. Death is not an event that occurs in time; it is the cessation of the events whose temporal character constitutes time for the person who dies. This metaphysical point supports the therapeutic conclusion: there is no "time after death" in which you will be absent and missing things, because there is no time for you once the events of your life have ceased. We will examine this connection more carefully in Section 6.4.
