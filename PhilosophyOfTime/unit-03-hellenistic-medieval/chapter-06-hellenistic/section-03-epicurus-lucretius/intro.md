# Section 6.3: Epicurus and Lucretius on Time

---

## Section Introduction

The Epicureans and the Stoics shared a world — the Hellenistic Mediterranean — and a conviction that philosophy must do practical work in human lives. But their philosophical systems could hardly have been more different. The Stoics were rationalists who believed in a providential cosmic order woven through with divine reason; the Epicureans were materialists who believed the universe was nothing but atoms falling through void, with no divine supervision and no cosmic purpose. These are not small differences, and they generated very different approaches to the philosophy of time.

For the Epicureans, the metaphysics of time followed directly from their atomism. If everything is either atoms or void, where does time fit? It is not an atom — it has no shape, size, or weight. It is not void — it is not space waiting to be filled. Epicurus's solution was to treat time as an "accident of accidents" — a property that belongs not to bodies directly but to the complex events and processes that bodies constitute. Time, on this view, is real but ontologically secondary: it supervenes on events without being a thing in its own right.

Lucretius, writing in Latin roughly two centuries after Epicurus, articulated this position with a poet's clarity in *De Rerum Natura* (On the Nature of Things). His treatment of time is brief — a few dozen lines — but philosophically precise. He offers what amounts to a conceivability argument for the relational nature of time: we cannot conceive of time apart from events and processes, and this inability reflects a genuine ontological dependence.

Section 6.3.1 examines Epicurus's original position and its philosophical basis. Section 6.3.2 turns to Lucretius and the specific argument he developed, its structure, and its modern parallels.
