# 6.4.1 — Lucretius's Symmetry Argument

---

## The Argument in Context

Lucretius's symmetry argument appears late in *De Rerum Natura*, after he has laid out the Epicurean view that the soul is mortal — that it is, like everything else, composed of atoms, and that at death these atoms disperse, ending the person as definitively as a fire is extinguished when its fuel runs out. Death, on this account, is simply the cessation of the atomic configuration that constitutes a person. There is no afterlife, no continuation of consciousness, no shade in the underworld. Death is — exactly — nothing.

Given this picture, why do people fear death? Lucretius argues that they fear it because they implicitly conceive of their own death as a kind of deprivation — an ongoing absence of the goods they currently enjoy. They imagine themselves lying in the grave, missing the warmth of the sun, the pleasures of friendship, the satisfactions of work. This implicit picture is, Lucretius argues, incoherent: it requires imagining oneself present to experience one's own absence, which is precisely what death rules out. The famous argument, from slightly earlier in Book III (line 830), is: "Where death is, I am not; where I am, death is not." Death and the person are mutually exclusive; there is no subject of the deprivation.

The symmetry argument is a supplementary argument aimed at a different source of fear: the sheer temporal extent of non-existence that follows death. Even if death is not experienced as a deprivation, it might be feared simply because of the infinite void of non-being that it introduces. Lucretius addresses this by drawing a temporal comparison.

## The Text

The key lines are:

> "Look back at the eternity that passed before we were born, and mark how utterly it counts to us as nothing. This is a mirror that Nature holds up to us, in which we may see the time that shall be after our death. Is there anything terrifying in it? Is there anything that looks like gloom? It is more peaceful than any sleep." (*De Rerum Natura* III.972–977, trans. Bailey, adapted)

The parallel passage that is most cited is III.830–842, in which Lucretius explicitly draws the symmetry:

> "Death, then, is nothing to us and concerns us not one jot, since the nature of mind is found to be mortal... For in the days of old we felt no anguish, when the Carthaginians came from all sides to the conflict; when all the world, shaken by the alarming tumult of war, shudderingly quaked beneath the high coasts of heaven, and mortal men were in doubt to which domain they must fall by land and sea. So, when we shall be no more — when there shall have been a separation of body and soul, out of both of which we are each formed into a single being — to us, who shall then be nothing, nothing by any hazard will happen any more at all, nothing will excite our feeling; no, not if earth shall be mingled with sea, and sea with heaven." (*De Rerum Natura* III.830–842, trans. Bailey 1910)

The formal structure of the argument is this:

1. There is a long period of non-existence before your birth (the "prenatal" period).
2. This period does not distress you — you do not regret or fear the eternity of non-existence that preceded your life.
3. The period after your death is structurally identical to the period before your birth: in both, you do not exist; in both, the world goes on without you; in both, you are present to no experiences.
4. By consistency, you should not fear the post-mortem period of non-existence any more than you regret the prenatal period.
5. Therefore, death is not to be feared.

This is a symmetry argument: if two things are structurally symmetric, they call for symmetric evaluative attitudes.

## The Argument's Logical Force

The argument has genuine force. The prenatal period was not distressing. We do not lie awake at night regretting that we were not alive to witness the construction of the Colosseum, or that we missed the philosophical debates in Athens in the fifth century BCE. We have no felt sense of loss about the infinity of time before we existed. If this is right, and if the post-mortem period is really structurally symmetric with the prenatal period, then consistency demands that we not fear the post-mortem period either.

Philosophers who have defended the Lucretian view (roughly) include Stephen Rosenbaum (1986, 1989) and Fred Feldman (1991). They argue that the symmetry is genuine and that Lucretius's conclusion follows. The fear of death is, on this view, a kind of temporal parochialism — an irrational attachment to the near-future that would not survive clear-eyed temporal impartiality.

## Nagel's Challenge: Why the Symmetry May Break Down

Thomas Nagel's influential essay "Death" (1979) argues that the symmetry argument fails, though he takes it seriously rather than dismissing it. Nagel's challenge operates at two levels.

At the first level, Nagel questions whether the prenatal period is really parallel to the post-mortem period. The prenatal period is one in which you did not yet exist — there was no subject to suffer deprivation. But the post-mortem period is one in which you have ceased to exist — there *was* a subject (you), and it is that subject who no longer enjoys the goods it previously did. The post-mortem period is the deprivation of a specific individual who existed; the prenatal period is not the deprivation of anyone, since the subject who would be deprived had not yet come into existence (Nagel 1979: 7–8).

At the second level, Nagel argues that what makes death bad is the deprivation of life — the goods that life contains that death forecloses. Death is bad not because dying is painful (it need not be) but because it cuts short a life that would otherwise have contained more good things: more experiences, more relationships, more achievements. This "deprivation account" of death's badness makes no direct reference to temporal direction; what matters is not *when* the non-existence occurs but whether the non-existence deprives a subject of goods they would otherwise have had (Nagel 1979: 6–7). But if that is what makes death bad, it is not obviously symmetric: the prenatal non-existence does not deprive you of goods you would have had, because there is no you prior to your birth to be deprived.

## Warren's Analysis

James Warren (2004) offers the most careful sustained analysis of the symmetry argument in recent literature. Warren argues that the Lucretian argument is more sophisticated than it appears and that Nagel's asymmetry objection, while pointing to something real, does not fully undermine it.

Warren makes a key distinction: there is an asymmetry between pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence at the level of *personal identity* (there is no pre-natal you to be deprived), but there may be a symmetry at the level of *evaluative attitude* (what reasons do you have to care about your non-existence, in either direction?). The Lucretian point, Warren argues, is not primarily about metaphysical symmetry but about the rationality of temporal preferences: is there any reason to prefer that your non-existence occur before rather than after your life? And Lucretius's answer — that there is no such reason — may be defensible even if the metaphysical asymmetry Nagel points to is genuine (Warren 2004: 58–67).

Jeff McMahan (2002: 75–77) argues that birth and death are genuinely asymmetric: we have a strong interest in our future existence that is not matched by a corresponding interest in our past existence. This future-directedness of personal concern is not irrational; it reflects the fact that future goods are open to our agency in ways past goods are not. The asymmetry in our attitudes toward pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence may thus be rational rather than a bias to be corrected.

## The Deeper Temporal Issue

What the symmetry argument ultimately probes is our relationship to our own temporal position. The argument asks: why does it matter *when* one's non-existence occurs? Why is non-existence after life worse than non-existence before life? The various responses to Lucretius have converged on the view that temporal position matters because of what it means for personal identity, narrative, and agency: the goods of life are not timeless goods that can be redistributed across time indifferently, but are goods of a specific kind of temporal existence — of being a persisting subject with a past and a future.

This is a profound point about the philosophy of time. Time is not merely a neutral container in which goods and ills are located; it structures what goods and ills *are* for beings like us. The Stoic insight that the present has a special ontological status, the Epicurean insight that time supervenes on events, and the Lucretian argument about temporal symmetry all converge on the same deep theme: time is not separable from what it means to be the kind of being we are.
