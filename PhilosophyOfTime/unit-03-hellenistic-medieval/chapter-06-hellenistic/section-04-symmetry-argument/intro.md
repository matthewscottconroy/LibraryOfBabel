# Section 6.4: The Symmetry Argument

---

## Section Introduction

Philosophy at its best does double duty: it clarifies abstract metaphysical questions and it illuminates how we should live. The Epicurean tradition excelled at this double work. The metaphysics of atoms and void was not only a contribution to natural philosophy; it was the foundation of a therapeutic project aimed at liberating human beings from their most destructive fears. Of those fears, the most pervasive and the most philosophically tractable was the fear of death.

Lucretius's symmetry argument — also sometimes called the "mirror argument" — appears in Book III of *De Rerum Natura* (lines 830–842). It is brief, elegant, and philosophically challenging. The argument turns on a temporal comparison: the period after your death is structurally symmetric with the period before your birth. Since you do not regard the latter with regret or terror — since the eternity of non-existence before you existed causes you no grief — you should, by consistency, not regard the former with terror either. The pre-natal and post-mortem darknesses are the same kind of thing.

The argument seems compelling on first encounter. And yet something about it resists. Most people who hear it feel that the symmetry cannot be complete — that there is some genuine asymmetry between the prospect of one's own non-existence and the retrospect of it. The philosophical question is whether this felt asymmetry can be articulated and defended.

Section 6.4.1 reconstructs the argument carefully and examines what it actually claims. The discussion of challenges — particularly Thomas Nagel's influential response and James Warren's analysis — follows within the same section.
