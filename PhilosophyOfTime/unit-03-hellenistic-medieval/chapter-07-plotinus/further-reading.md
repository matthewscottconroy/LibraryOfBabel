## Annotated Suggestions for Further Reading

**Plotinus. *Enneads* III.7 ("On Eternity and Time"). Translated by A. H. Armstrong. Loeb Classical Library, 1967.**
The primary source for Plotinus's philosophy of time. Armstrong's translation is reliable and his introductory note to the treatise is helpful. The treatise is demanding but manageable if read slowly and with attention to the structure of the argument. All serious students of this chapter must engage with this text directly.

**Emilsson, Eyjólfur K. *Plotinus*. London: Routledge, 2017.**
The best single-volume introduction to Plotinus currently available. Emilsson writes clearly, engages seriously with the philosophical arguments, and situates Plotinus within both ancient and contemporary debates. Chapter 4 on Soul and time is directly relevant; Chapters 2–3 on the One and Intellect are needed background.

**Sorabji, Richard. *Time, Creation and the Continuum*. London: Duckworth, 1983.**
The indispensable scholarly treatment of ancient and medieval theories of time. Chapters 8–9 on Plotinus are particularly thorough. Sorabji's ability to identify the precise philosophical move at stake in dense ancient texts is unparalleled. Best for students who want to go beyond the introductory level.

**Gerson, Lloyd P. *Plotinus*. London: Routledge, 1994.**
A systematic account of Plotinus's metaphysics, organized around the central arguments of the *Enneads*. Chapter 7 on Soul and the physical world is directly relevant. More philosophically focused and argumentative than Emilsson, and somewhat more demanding.

**Corrigan, Kevin. *Reading Plotinus*. Purdue University Press, 2004.**
An unusually student-friendly introduction that moves through Plotinus's major treatises with commentary. An excellent choice for a first encounter with the primary texts, since Corrigan explains what to look for and why it matters.

**Armstrong, A. H. "Eternity, Life and Movement in Plotinus' Accounts of Nous." In *Plotinian and Christian Studies*. London: Variorum, 1979.**
A short but important essay by the leading Plotinus translator of the twentieth century. Armstrong clarifies the relationship between the eternal life of Intellect and the temporal life of Soul, and helps to explain what makes Plotinus's account distinctive compared to Plato.

**Ricoeur, Paul. *Time and Narrative*, vol. 1. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.**
Ricoeur's monumental three-volume work on time and narrative engages extensively with the ancient and modern philosophical tradition. Volume 1 discusses Augustine and Aristotle but also frames the broader question of the relationship between temporal experience and narrative structure in ways that illuminate the Plotinian background. Best read alongside the Augustine chapter.

**Plato. *Timaeus*. Translated by Donald Zeyl. Indianapolis: Hackett, 2000.**
The primary Platonic source for the account of time as a "moving image of eternity" that Plotinus is engaging with and transforming. The relevant passage is 37b–38c. Reading the *Timaeus* alongside *Enneads* III.7 makes Plotinus's transformations of Plato visible.

**Gerson, Lloyd P., ed. *The Cambridge Companion to Plotinus*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1996.**
A valuable collection of essays covering the major aspects of Plotinus's thought. The introduction by Gerson and essays by Armstrong, Emilsson, and O'Brien are especially worth reading alongside this chapter's material.

**Aristotle. *Physics* IV.10–14. Translated by Edward Hussey. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1983.**
The source for Aristotle's account of time, which Plotinus is critiquing. Hussey's translation comes with excellent notes and a commentary that helps to clarify the arguments Plotinus is engaging. Reading Aristotle *Physics* IV alongside *Enneads* III.7 reveals exactly what Plotinus is responding to and where his critique bites.
