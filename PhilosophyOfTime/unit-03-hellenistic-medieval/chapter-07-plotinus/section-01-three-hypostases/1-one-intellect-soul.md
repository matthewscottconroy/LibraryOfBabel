# 7.1.1 — The One, Intellect, and Soul: The Procession Structure

---

## The One (*to Hen*)

At the apex of Plotinus's ontology sits the One — the absolutely simple, undifferentiated, self-sufficient source of all being. The One is not a being among beings; it is beyond being (*epekeina tes ousias*), beyond thought, beyond any determination whatsoever. If you predicate anything of the One — that it is good, that it exists, that it is one — you have already compromised its absolute simplicity, because predication involves a kind of duality (the thing said and the thing of which it is said). The One is strictly beyond all predication; what we say about it is said not to characterize it but to gesture toward it (Plotinus, *Enneads* V.3.13–14, trans. Armstrong 1984).

The One is beyond time in the most radical sense: time does not even arise as a question with respect to the One. The One does not persist through time, does not endure, is not "at" any moment. It simply is — absolutely and without temporal qualification. This is not eternity-as-endless-duration; it is being that is not even the right kind of thing to have a duration, endless or otherwise.

The metaphysical relationship between the One and everything else is one of *emanation* (*prohodos*): everything that is proceeds from the One as light proceeds from a luminous body, not by deliberate creation (which would require a prior intention and thus temporal priority) but through the sheer superabundance of the One's being. The One does not lose anything in this emanation; it remains what it is. But from it, Intellect proceeds.

## Intellect (*Nous*)

Intellect is the first emanation from the One. It is also called Nous, Mind, or Pure Intelligence. Intellect is the level at which being and thought first coincide: Intellect is pure thinking thinking itself, where the thinker and the thought are identical (Plotinus, *Enneads* V.1.4, trans. Armstrong 1984). It is the realm of the Platonic Forms — the eternal, perfect archetypes of which sensible things are imperfect copies — and it grasps all the Forms in a single, simultaneous act of thought.

This simultaneity is crucial for Plotinus's account of time. Intellect does not move from one Form to another sequentially, considering them one at a time. It contemplates all the Forms at once, in a single atemporal act of comprehension. Its "life" (if we can call it that) is complete and entire at every "moment" — though "moment" is the wrong word, because Intellect is not in time. This mode of being is what Plotinus means by *aion* — eternity. We will examine the concept of *aion* in more detail in Section 7.1.2.

The important point for now is that Intellect is outside time not merely in the sense that it lasts forever but in the more radical sense that the categories of before and after, earlier and later, do not apply to it at all. It is atemporal through and through. As Plotinus writes: "Eternity [*aion*] belongs to the things around Intellect and has its being there" (*Enneads* III.7.3, trans. Armstrong 1967).

## Soul (*Psychê*)

Soul is the second emanation — it proceeds from Intellect as Intellect proceeded from the One. Soul has a complex status in Plotinus's system. On the one hand, Soul is ontologically dependent on Intellect and the One; it is "lower" in the hierarchy. On the other hand, Soul is the principle that generates the physical world and animates it; it is, in a sense, the creative force responsible for everything below Intellect.

Most importantly for the philosophy of time: Soul is where time originates. Unlike Intellect, which grasps everything simultaneously, Soul cannot hold all its knowledge and activity in a single act. It must move through its knowledge discursively, considering one thing after another, passing from one state to another. This sequential, discursive character of Soul's activity is the source of time (Plotinus, *Enneads* III.7.11, trans. Armstrong 1967).

Soul is also the principle of life in the physical cosmos. The physical world is ordered and animated by Soul, which is why the cosmos is not dead matter but a living, rational whole. The temporal succession that characterizes the physical world is an expression of Soul's own temporal character — the physical world is temporal because Soul, which animates it, is temporal.

## The Procession Structure and Time

The structure of procession — from One to Intellect to Soul to the physical world — is thus also a structure of increasing temporality. The One is beyond time; Intellect is outside time but within eternity; Soul is within time as the generator of time; the physical world is within time as Soul's temporal expression.

This is a profoundly original metaphysical picture. Time, for Plotinus, does not come "from outside" to attach itself to a pre-existing world. It is generated from within — from the nature of Soul itself. Understanding why Soul generates time requires understanding what Soul is trying to do, and why it cannot do it all at once. That is the subject of Section 7.2.

It is worth noting a contrast with Plato here. In the *Timaeus*, Plato says that the Demiurge created time as "a moving image of eternity" — time was made by an external divine craftsman to be an imperfect copy of eternal being (Plato, *Timaeus* 37d5–7, trans. Zeyl 2000). For Plotinus, time is not an artifact made by a demiurgic mind; it is the natural expression of Soul's own activity. This is a genuine departure from Plato, not merely a commentary on him (Armstrong 1967: 301–302).
