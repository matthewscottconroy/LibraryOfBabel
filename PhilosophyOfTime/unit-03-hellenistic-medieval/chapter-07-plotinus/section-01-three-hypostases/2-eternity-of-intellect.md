# 7.1.2 — Eternity (*Aion*) and the Life of Intellect

---

## What Eternity Is Not

Before we can say what Plotinian eternity (*aion*) is, it helps to say what it is not. Two misunderstandings are common.

The first misunderstanding is to think that eternity is just time stretched to infinity — a temporal duration without beginning or end. This conception of eternity, sometimes called "sempiternity," describes something that has always existed and will always exist — something that endures through time but does so without limit. A God who has existed since before the Big Bang and will exist after the heat death of the universe would be sempiternal, not eternal in Plotinus's sense. Sempiternity is a property of things that are *in* time; it just says something about how long they are in it.

The second misunderstanding is to think that eternity is a frozen instant — a single timeless "now" that is somehow outside the flow of time but is nevertheless a kind of temporal position. This is closer to the truth but still not quite right.

Plotinus's eternity is neither of these. It is the mode of being of a life that is *complete, all at once, and without succession* — a life whose entire reality is present simultaneously, without any part of it being "not yet" or "no longer." This life is Intellect's life.

## The Definition of Eternity in *Enneads* III.7

The key text is *Enneads* III.7.3, where Plotinus defines eternity (*aion*) as "the life which belongs to that which exists and is in being, all together and full, completely without extension or interval" (Plotinus, *Enneads* III.7.3.7–8, trans. Armstrong 1967). He continues: eternity is "what was and is and will be — not what 'was' and 'will be' as if these referred to different things — but what always is, all together, without any division" (*Enneads* III.7.3.16–18, trans. Armstrong 1967).

Several features of this definition deserve attention.

**"Life"**: Eternity is not a static, dead timelessness. It is a *life* — a complete, active, self-aware existence. The analogy might be to the experience of understanding something with perfect clarity, all at once: not working through it step by step but grasping the whole simultaneously. Intellect's life is like that, except that what is grasped simultaneously is not a theorem but the entire intelligible universe.

**"All together"** (*homou*): The characteristic mark of eternity for Plotinus is simultaneity. Everything that Intellect is and knows is present to it all at once. There is no "first I think of the Form of Beauty, then I think of the Form of Justice" — Intellect comprehends all Forms in a single, undivided act.

**"Without extension or interval"**: Eternity has no temporal parts. It is not a long time; it is no time at all. The spatial metaphor of interval (*diastema*) is precisely what is absent in eternity, even though interval is exactly what characterizes time.

## Contrast with Platonic Eternity

Plato introduced the concept of eternal Forms in the *Phaedo*, *Republic*, and *Timaeus*, and he used the language of eternity (*aei*) to characterize them. For Plato, the Forms are eternal in the sense that they do not change — they are always what they are, without beginning or end. But Plato does not develop a sophisticated concept of eternity as a mode of being in the way Plotinus does.

Plotinus's *aion* is a significant elaboration of the Platonic idea. What Plotinus adds is the emphasis on *simultaneous completeness*: not just that the Forms persist without change, but that they are grasped by Intellect in a single undivided act that has no temporal parts. Eternity is not just unchangingness; it is the positive character of a life that is complete without succession.

This concept of eternity becomes enormously influential. Boethius (Chapter 9) will adapt it to characterize God's eternal mode of being: "the complete, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unlimited life" (*Consolation of Philosophy* V, prose 6) — a formulation that is almost certainly indebted to Plotinus. Augustine (Chapter 8), though he does not follow Plotinus directly, arrives at a structurally similar notion of the divine *nunc stans* — the eternal standing now — that echoes Plotinian eternity.

## The Priority of Eternity over Time

For Plotinus, eternity is not derived from time; time is derived from eternity — or rather, time arises when the completeness and simultaneity of eternity are "stretched out" into succession. Time is what eternity looks like when it is encountered by a nature (Soul) that cannot hold it all at once.

This priority of eternity over time is metaphysical, not merely temporal. Eternity is not "earlier" than time (that would be a temporal relationship); it is the ontological model of which time is an imperfect realization. Time is the moving image of eternity — Plotinus endorses Plato's phrase (*Timaeus* 37d) but interprets it more deeply: the "movement" is not physical motion but the movement of Soul's discursive activity, and the "image" is a genuine ontological image, not merely a resemblance.

## Why This Matters for Philosophy of Time

Plotinus's account of eternity is important for the philosophy of time for two reasons.

First, it provides a genuine alternative to the view that time is simply the most fundamental feature of reality. If time is derived from something deeper — from the nature of Soul's activity, which is in turn a realization of Intellect's eternal life — then time is not ontologically basic. This is a significant departure from any view that treats time as an irreducible feature of the universe.

Second, it raises the question of what it would mean to "get outside" time — to experience time from the perspective of eternity. For Plotinus, this is not merely an abstract possibility; it is the highest form of human activity. In moments of genuine philosophical or mystical insight, the soul may briefly share the atemporality of Intellect, grasping something entire and complete rather than moving through it sequentially. Whether this is psychologically possible — and what it would feel like — are questions that Plotinus takes seriously, and that later thinkers from Augustine to Boethius to the medieval mystics will pursue.
