# 7.2.2 — Implications: Subjectivity, Augustine, and the Difference from Aristotle

---

## Is Plotinian Time Subjective?

The first question a philosophically alert reader will ask about Plotinus's account is: if time is "the life of Soul," does this make time merely subjective? Does Plotinus's position collapse into the view that time is a feature of minds — that the physical world, in itself, is not really temporal at all?

Plotinus does not think so, and his reasons are important. The Soul in question is not any individual human soul but the cosmic Soul — the principle that animates and orders the entire physical universe. The physical world is temporal not merely because you or I experience it temporally but because the cosmic Soul generates temporal succession in the very act of animating the world. Individual temporal experience is real, but it is a participation in a more fundamental temporal reality that belongs to the cosmos as a whole.

This means Plotinus's account is not a simple subjectivism. The temporal order of the physical world — the fact that causes precede effects, that the sun rises and sets in a regular sequence, that organisms go through stages of development — is a genuine feature of the world, not merely a feature of how we represent it. It is a feature of the world, however, because the world is animated by a Soul whose nature is essentially temporal (Emilsson 2017: 125–127).

There is a genuine tension here, though. If we were to abstract away from Soul entirely — if we imagined the physical world without any animating soul — would there be any time left? On Plotinus's account, presumably not. The atoms of Democritus and Epicurus, if they fell through void with no soul animating them, would produce a world with no genuine temporal order. Time requires Soul. This is a substantial dependence of time on mind, even if it is not a simple subjectivism.

## The Relationship to Augustine's *Distentio Animi*

Augustine's account of time in *Confessions* XI (Chapter 8) is the most famous inner-directed account of time in Western philosophy. Augustine argues that time is a "distension of the soul" (*distentio animi*) — the soul's stretching between memory and expectation, with the present as the moment of attention connecting them. This is a thoroughly psychological account: time is located in the soul's structure, not in the world.

Plotinus and Augustine are connected but not identical. Their similarities are real: both locate the source or nature of time in soul or mind rather than in the external world; both see temporal succession as a consequence of the sequential, discursive character of mental activity; both contrast temporal succession with the eternal simultaneity of a higher mode of being.

The differences are equally significant. Plotinus is talking about the *cosmic* Soul; Augustine is talking about the *individual human* soul. Plotinus's account is primarily ontological (what is time?); Augustine's is primarily phenomenological (how do we experience time? what do we measure when we measure time?). Plotinus sees the temporal character of Soul as a metaphysical necessity that follows from Soul's place in the hierarchy of being; Augustine sees the temporal structure of the soul as a feature of *creaturely* existence — time is the mode of being of creatures, as opposed to the eternal mode of being of the Creator.

Despite these differences, Augustine's account is intelligible as a further development and internalization of themes already present in Plotinus. Plotinus says time is the life of Soul; Augustine goes further and asks what the structure of *individual* temporal experience is, and how that structure relates to measurement and language. Plotinus gives the cosmological picture; Augustine provides the phenomenological close-up.

## How Plotinus Differs from Aristotle

The surface similarities between Plotinus and Aristotle are misleading. Both thinkers connect time to motion and to soul. But they mean very different things by these connections.

For Aristotle, time is "the number of motion with respect to before and after." Aristotle's soul enters the picture because *counting* — the activity that reveals time — requires a soul to do the counting. But the motion that is counted is real and objective: celestial motion, the rotation of the heavens, the physical processes of the world. Time is, for Aristotle, primarily a feature of the physical world that the soul then measures.

For Plotinus, this gets things exactly backwards. Time is not a feature of the physical world that Soul measures; time is a feature of Soul that is expressed in the physical world. Soul does not number the world's motion; Soul generates the temporal succession that the world exhibits. Time flows from Soul downward into the physical world, not from the physical world upward into Soul's awareness.

Plotinus makes this inversion explicit in *Enneads* III.7.9, where he argues that Aristotle's definition puts the cart before the horse: you cannot understand what time is by starting from physical motion, because the temporal character of physical motion is itself dependent on Soul. To define time through motion is to explain the explanandum by the phenomenon that itself needs explaining (Sorabji 1983: 98–100).

Whether Plotinus's inversion is correct is a deep question. One way to assess it: consider a physical universe without any animating soul. Would it have temporal succession? Common sense says yes — the stars would still rise and set, causes would still precede effects. Plotinus says no — in the absence of Soul, there would be no genuine temporal order, only the appearance of such order from the perspective of a soul that projects it. This is a strong metaphysical claim, and it is not obviously right. But it is not obviously wrong either, and the question it raises — whether temporal order is genuinely objective or depends on minds — is one of the deepest in the philosophy of time.
