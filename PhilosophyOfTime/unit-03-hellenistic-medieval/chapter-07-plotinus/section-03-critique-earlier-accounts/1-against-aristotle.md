# 7.3.1 — Against Aristotle: The Critique of *Enneads* III.7.9

---

## Aristotle's Position Revisited

To understand Plotinus's critique of Aristotle, we need to be clear about what Aristotle actually said. Aristotle's definition of time as "the number of motion with respect to before and after" (*Physics* IV.11, 219b1–2) involves two components: the *numerical* character of time and its connection to *motion*.

On the numerical side: Aristotle says that time is what we count when we count the before-and-after of motion. If you count the number of complete rotations of the heavens, you are counting time. Time is not the same as the motion — the celestial sphere moves spatially, while time is a quantity. Time is the number — the measure — that we impose on motion by counting it.

On the motion side: time, for Aristotle, is specifically connected to motion. Rest has no time of its own; a body at rest is still located in time, but its location in time is defined by the motion of other things (including the heavens). Motion is the "substrate" of time — the material in which time exists, as it were.

Aristotle also makes the famous comment that "if there were no soul, there would be no time" (*Physics* IV.14, 223a25–28, trans. Hussey 1983). This is because time requires counting, and counting requires a soul. This comment opens the door to a mind-dependent account of time, though Aristotle did not develop it in that direction.

## Plotinus's Critique: Motion Cannot Number Itself

The central argument in *Enneads* III.7.9 runs as follows. Suppose we define time as the measure of motion (following Aristotle). What does the measuring? Not the motion itself — motion is what is measured, not the measurer. It must be something external to the motion. But what is that external thing?

If it is another motion, then we have a regress: what measures the second motion? Another motion? And what measures that? This regress cannot be stopped by positing an infinite series of measuring motions; that just pushes the problem back without solving it. We need something that can measure motion without itself being measured by another motion — something that is the standard rather than the thing to be measured.

Plotinus's answer is Soul. Soul provides the standard against which motion is measured. But if Soul provides the standard, then the real ground of time is not motion but Soul. Motion is what is measured; Soul is what measures; and time is the measure itself — which is a feature of Soul's activity, not of motion.

Plotinus makes this argument at *Enneads* III.7.9.69–79: "If time is the measure of movement, how is it a measure? One can say 'this much movement corresponds to this length of time,' but what is 'this length of time'? It is nothing other than the Soul's extension of itself... the motion is measured by the Soul, which has time as its own extension, and time is not the number of motion but the form by which Soul extends itself" (trans. Armstrong 1967, paraphrased).

## The Regress Problem

The regress argument is important, and it is worth spelling out more carefully. The problem is not just that motion is measured by Soul but that any definition of time in terms of motion seems to generate a regress.

Suppose time = the measure of motion. What is the measure? Call it M. What is M? If M is itself a motion or a temporal process, then M itself takes time, and we need M to be measured by some prior measure M'. But M' also takes time... and so on. There is no non-question-begging way to halt the regress within a purely motion-based account of time.

Plotinus's proposal is that the regress is stopped by Soul: Soul's activity is temporal in a way that does not require any further measurer, because time just *is* the form of Soul's activity. Soul does not measure time the way a ruler measures length; Soul generates time in the very act of its sequential activity. The measuring is not a further operation on something else; it is what Soul is and does.

Whether this response is fully satisfying is debatable. One might ask: if Soul's activity is temporal, what makes it temporal? Does that not require another level of explanation? Plotinus's answer would presumably be that Soul's temporality is primitive — it is not explained in terms of something else but is just the character of what Soul is. This is not evasion; it is the claim that Soul is the *explanatory terminus* for temporality. Time traces back to Soul, and Soul's temporal character is a brute feature of Soul's nature, not a further phenomenon requiring explanation.

## The Soul Must Number, Not Motion

A closely related point: Aristotle's definition says time is the *number* of motion, where "number" implies an act of counting. Plotinus agrees that counting is involved, but draws the implication that Aristotle downplays: if time requires counting, and counting requires a soul, then time depends essentially on Soul. Not just for its measurement but for its existence.

This is a stronger claim than Aristotle intended. Aristotle meant to say that time would not exist "for us" without a counting soul — that time is what we count, and counting requires a soul. He seems to have thought that the before-and-after character of motion is mind-independent, even if time as an explicit count requires a counter. Plotinus argues that the before-and-after character itself is not mind-independent — it is a feature of motion *as experienced or generated by Soul*.

If Plotinus is right, then Aristotle's definition is confused: it appeals to counting but then tries to locate time in the world rather than in the counting subject. Aristotle noticed the mind-dependence implication ("if there were no soul, there would be no time") but did not follow it to its conclusion. Plotinus follows it to its conclusion and arrives at the view that time is genuinely a feature of Soul.

## Lingering Difficulties

Plotinus's critique of Aristotle is powerful but not without difficulties of its own.

The most pressing is: if time is a feature of Soul, and Soul is a metaphysical principle that animates the cosmos, what is the relationship between cosmic time (Soul's time) and the time measured by physical processes (clocks, seasons, planetary motions)? Plotinus seems to think that physical temporal order is an expression of Soul's time — that the regularity of the heavens reflects the regularity of Soul's activity. But this relationship is not fully developed in *Enneads* III.7, and it raises questions about how we are to understand the physical processes that we actually use to measure time.

A second difficulty: if Soul's temporal activity is the ground of physical time, what happens when individual souls are out of sync with the cosmic Soul? If I am deeply absorbed in thought and lose track of time — or if my sense of duration is distorted by emotion — does this alter the temporal order of the cosmos? Plotinus's answer would presumably be no: individual soul's distortions do not affect the cosmic Soul's regular activity, which sets the objective temporal framework. But this response reveals that Plotinus's "time as the life of Soul" is really "time as the life of the *cosmic* Soul," not any individual mind. Individual temporal experience is participation, not generation.
