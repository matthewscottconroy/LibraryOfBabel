## Annotated Suggestions for Further Reading

**Augustine. *Confessions*, Book XI. Translated by Henry Chadwick. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1991.**
The primary text. Book XI is self-contained enough to be read without the rest of the *Confessions*, though reading the whole enriches the context. Chadwick's translation is philosophically sensitive and accurate. The analysis of time occupies chapters 10–31 of the book. Every student of this chapter should read the primary text.

**Ricoeur, Paul. *Time and Narrative*, vol. 1. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1984.**
The most important contemporary philosophical engagement with Augustine's analysis of time. The first part of Volume 1 (chapters 1–2) offers a detailed reading of *Confessions* XI alongside Aristotle's *Poetics*. Ricoeur's concept of narrative as the resolution of temporal aporias is both original and important. Accessible to undergraduates who have read Augustine.

**Husserl, Edmund. *On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time*. Dordrecht: Kluwer, 1991.**
The most important phenomenological development of Augustine's analysis. Demanding reading, but the first twenty sections (§§1–22) are the most important and relatively manageable. Husserl's analysis of retention, primal impression, and protention clarifies and systematizes what Augustine identified but did not fully develop.

**Sorabji, Richard. *Time, Creation and the Continuum*. London: Duckworth, 1983.**
Chapters 2–3 on Augustine are the most careful scholarly treatment of his philosophy of time. Sorabji situates Augustine within the ancient tradition while identifying what is original. Essential for serious students.

**Matthews, Gareth B. *Augustine*. Oxford: Blackwell, 2005.**
The most accessible philosophical introduction to Augustine from an analytic perspective. Matthews treats Augustine as a serious philosopher whose arguments deserve engagement on their merits. The chapter on time is a good place to start before reading the primary text.

**Stump, Eleonore, and Norman Kretzmann. "Eternity." *Journal of Philosophy* 78 (1981): 429–458.**
The most influential modern philosophical analysis of the concept of divine eternity as developed in Augustine and Boethius. Stump and Kretzmann argue that the concept of "ET-simultaneity" (eternity-time simultaneity) allows a timeless God to be causally related to temporal events. Essential for the philosophical theology of divine timelessness.

**Dainton, Barry. *Stream of Consciousness*. London: Routledge, 2000.**
The best contemporary analytic engagement with the phenomenology of temporal experience, including the specious present. Dainton reviews and synthesizes the Augustinian, Husserlian, and contemporary analytic traditions. Chapter 6 on the specious present is directly relevant.

**Heidegger, Martin. *Being and Time*. New York: Harper and Row, 1962. §§65–66.**
The existential transformation of the Augustinian analysis. Demanding reading, but §§65–66 on "Temporality as the Ontological Meaning of Care" can be read alongside this chapter to see how Heidegger develops the threefold structure of past, present, and future into a characterization of human existence as such.

**Craig, William Lane, and Quentin Smith. *Theism, Atheism, and Big Bang Cosmology*. Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1993.**
A debate between a theist and an atheist philosopher about the Big Bang and the beginning of time. Craig's papers draw explicitly on the Augustinian argument that time was created and connect it to contemporary physics and cosmology. Useful for Exercise 8.9.

**Wolterstorff, Nicholas. "God Everlasting." In *God and the Good*, 1975.**
An important paper arguing that the timelessness of God is philosophically indefensible and that God should be understood as sempiternal (everlasting in time) rather than eternal (outside time). A useful counterpoint to Augustine's *nunc stans* and Stump and Kretzmann's eternity account.
