# Chapter 8: Important Concepts

---

***Distentio Animi***
"Distension of the soul" — Augustine's central concept for the nature of temporal existence (*Confessions* XI.26.33). The soul is "stretched" between memory and expectation, with attention to the present connecting them. This stretching is not a defect but the positive structure of what it means to be a temporal, finite being. The apparent "thickness" of the present in experience derives from this distension rather than from any objective extended present in the world.

**The Threefold Present**
Augustine's analysis that past, present, and future are not three separate temporal regions but three modes in which the present moment is structured by consciousness: the present of past things (memory), the present of present things (attention), and the present of future things (expectation) (*Confessions* XI.20.26). Time, as we experience it, is the unity of these three modes.

**Memory (*Memoria*)**
For Augustine, memory is not merely the storage of past experiences but the present activity of the soul through which the past is made available to consciousness. When I remember a conversation from last week, the memory is happening *now* — it is a present mental act whose content is the past conversation. This analysis distinguishes the present act of memory from the past event it represents.

**Attention (*Contuitus*)**
The direct, present awareness of what is currently occurring — the middle term of Augustine's threefold present. Attention is what connects memory and expectation: it is the present moment of the soul's engagement, neither retrospective (like memory) nor anticipatory (like expectation) but directly focused on the current content of experience.

**Expectation (*Expectatio*)**
The present orientation of the soul toward what is yet to come. Like memory, expectation is a present activity — it is happening now — but its content is directed toward the future. Expectation is what makes future events available to the experiencing subject before they occur, allowing temporal experience to have the structure of "coming toward" as well as "receding from."

**The *Nunc Stans* (Standing Now)**
The divine eternal present: a mode of existence in which the entire life is present all at once, without succession, passing, or temporal division. God's eternity is not endless time but a non-temporal completeness. Augustine contrasts the *nunc stans* with human temporal existence, which is always marked by the absence of what is past and what is not yet (*Confessions* XI.11.13).

**Created Time**
The theological and philosophical claim that time was created along with the world — that there is no temporal framework that pre-exists creation, into which the world was then placed. This means that the question "what was before creation?" contains a false presupposition: there was no temporal "before" prior to the creation of time (*Confessions* XI.13.16).

**Sempiternity vs. Eternity**
A distinction Augustine implicitly uses: a sempiternal being exists at all times (has no beginning and no end) but is still *in* time; an eternal being (like God, for Augustine) is outside time altogether. The distinction matters because a sempiternal God would still face the question "what was God doing before creation?" whereas a truly eternal God is not located in any temporal framework at all.

**The Impression (*Affectio*)**
The mental trace or modification left in the soul by passing events. When a sound occurs and passes away, it leaves an impression in the mind. When we measure how long the sound lasted, what we are measuring is not the sound itself (which is gone) but this present impression. The mind measures time by measuring its own modifications — it measures itself rather than the world (*Confessions* XI.27.36).

**The Durationless Present**
Augustine's argument that the strict present has no positive duration: any interval proposed as "the present" can be divided into a past part and a future part, so the present (strictly) is only a durationless limit between past and future. This is consistent with the Stoic analysis (Chrysippus) but creates the puzzle: if the present is durationless, how do we experience duration at all? The answer lies in *distentio animi*.

**The Before-Creation Question**
The question "what was God doing before he created the world?" (*Confessions* XI.10.12). Augustine treats this as a philosophically important challenge that requires a genuine metaphysical response: there was no "before" because time was created with the world. This is a relational view of time's ontological status and an assertion of God's genuine atemporality.

**Phenomenological Time vs. Objective Time**
A distinction implicit in Augustine's analysis, though not using these terms: there is time as experienced by consciousness (phenomenological time, involving memory and expectation, giving rise to felt duration and temporal order) and time as an objective feature of the physical world (objective time, the temporal order of events regardless of whether any consciousness is present). Augustine's analysis primarily concerns phenomenological time.

**The Living Present**
The extended now of conscious experience — the present that has genuine breadth because it includes retention of the just-past and anticipation of the just-coming. Husserl used this term, but the concept is already present in Augustine's *distentio animi*: the experienced present is not a durationless instant but a temporally extended activity of consciousness.

**Narrative Time**
The organization of temporal experience through the structure of stories — beginnings, middles, and ends. Ricoeur argues that narrative is the medium through which human beings "live" Augustine's threefold present in a coherent way: stories make the distension of the soul into a structured, meaningful whole. This concept connects Augustine's analysis to the philosophy of literature and history.

**Divine Timelessness**
The theological doctrine, expressed in Augustine's account of the *nunc stans* and developed by Boethius and Aquinas, that God is not in time at all — not even in an infinite or sempiternal time — but exists in a mode of being that is genuinely atemporal. This raises philosophical puzzles about how a timeless God can act in time, know temporal facts, and respond to temporal prayers.
