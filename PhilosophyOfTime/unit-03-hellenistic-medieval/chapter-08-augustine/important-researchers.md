# Chapter 8: Important Researchers

---

**Augustine of Hippo** (354–430 CE)
Bishop of Hippo (in North Africa), one of the most influential figures in the history of Christianity and of Western philosophy. Augustine studied rhetoric in Carthage and Rome, converted to Christianity in 386, and returned to North Africa, where he became bishop of Hippo in 396. His philosophical formation was primarily Neoplatonist; he read Plotinus and Porphyry (in Latin translations), and their influence on his metaphysics and theory of the soul is profound. *Confessions* (c. 397–401), *City of God* (413–426), and *On the Trinity* (399–422) are his major works. Book XI of the *Confessions* is the locus classicus for his philosophy of time.

**Henry Chadwick** (1920–2008)
British patristics scholar and theologian. His translation of the *Confessions* (Oxford University Press, 1991) is widely regarded as the best English translation — philosophically sensitive, accurate, and elegantly written. His brief notes and introduction are useful for understanding the philosophical context of Augustine's arguments. Any serious student of Augustine's philosophy of time should use this translation.

**Richard Sorabji** (b. 1934)
As in Chapter 7, Sorabji's *Time, Creation and the Continuum* is essential for the Augustine chapters. His analysis of Augustine's arguments about temporal measurement and the relation between soul-time and world-time is among the most careful available. Sorabji situates Augustine precisely within the ancient tradition while identifying what is genuinely original in his analysis.

**Edmund Husserl** (1859–1938)
Founder of phenomenology, who explicitly acknowledged Augustine as the first thinker to identify the central problem of time-consciousness. His *On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* (lectures c. 1905, published 1928, translated by J. B. Brough, Kluwer 1991) develops the Augustinian structure of memory/attention/expectation into a rigorous phenomenological analysis of retention, primal impression, and protention. Husserl's work is the direct descendant of Augustine's and the starting point for all subsequent phenomenological work on time.

**Paul Ricoeur** (1913–2005)
French philosopher whose three-volume *Time and Narrative* (*Temps et récit*, 1983–85) is the most sustained engagement with Augustine's analysis of time in contemporary philosophy. Ricoeur argues that the "aporias of time" — the puzzles Augustine identifies — are resolved not by further philosophical analysis but by narrative, the activity through which human beings give temporal meaning to their experience. He is also the author of a major study of Augustine's *Confessions* (*Memory, History, Forgetting*, 2004).

**Martin Heidegger** (1889–1976)
German philosopher who transformed the Augustinian-Husserlian analysis of time into an ontological account of human existence. His early lectures on Augustine (1921, published as *The Phenomenology of Religious Life*, 1992) show the direct influence of Augustinian temporal analysis on his own developing thought. *Being and Time* (1927) develops temporality as the fundamental structure of *Dasein* — the kind of being that humans are.

**Gareth B. Matthews** (1929–2011)
American philosopher at the University of Massachusetts, Amherst. His book *Augustine* (Blackwell, 2005) is one of the best introductions to Augustine's philosophy from a broadly analytic perspective. Matthews takes Augustine's philosophical arguments seriously without reducing them to theology, and his treatment of the *Confessions* XI analysis is clear and accessible.

**Barry Dainton** (b. 1958)
British philosopher at the University of Liverpool. His *Stream of Consciousness: Unity and Continuity in Conscious Experience* (Routledge, 2000) engages seriously with both the Augustinian and the Husserlian traditions in the context of contemporary analytic philosophy of mind. His treatment of the specious present and the phenomenology of temporal experience is the best contemporary analytic engagement with these questions.

**William Lane Craig** (b. 1949)
American philosopher and theologian. His extensive work on the nature of divine eternity, the beginning of time, and the relationship between theology and physics includes rigorous philosophical analysis of the Augustinian concept of created time. His debate with Quentin Smith in *Theism, Atheism, and Big Bang Cosmology* (1993) is a valuable reference for the contemporary relevance of Augustine's claim that time was created.
