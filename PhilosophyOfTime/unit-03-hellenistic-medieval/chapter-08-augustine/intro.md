# Chapter 8: Augustine

---

## Chapter Introduction

Some philosophical texts are important because they are first — they introduce a problem that later thinkers must deal with. Some are important because they are careful — they analyze a question with a precision that no subsequent treatment has surpassed. Augustine's *Confessions*, Book XI, is rare in being both. Written around 397–401 CE, it contains the first sustained phenomenological analysis of time in the Western tradition, a set of arguments about what time is and how we experience it that remain philosophically alive today, and a theological framing that gives the whole inquiry an urgency that purely academic treatments often lack.

Augustine of Hippo (354–430 CE) was a bishop, a theologian, and one of the most powerful prose stylists in the Latin language. He was also a philosopher of the first rank — trained in the rhetorical and philosophical traditions of late antiquity, deeply influenced by Neoplatonism (particularly Plotinus and Porphyry, though he read them in Latin translation), and driven by an intellectual passion that did not stop at the boundaries of any established discipline. The *Confessions* is autobiography, prayer, and philosophy simultaneously — and the philosophy of time in Book XI emerges from all three of those modes at once.

The occasion for Augustine's inquiry into time is a theological question: what was God doing before he created the world? (*Confessions* XI.10.12). This is not an idle curiosity. It bears on whether God is genuinely eternal — outside time entirely — or merely very old, sempiternal. If God has an eternity that is radically different from temporal duration, then the question "what was God doing before creation?" is malformed — there was no "before." Augustine's philosophical analysis of time is in service of this theological claim, but it leads to results that stand entirely on their own as philosophy.

Chapter 8 proceeds through five sections. Section 8.1 examines the framing question — what was God doing before creation? — and Augustine's response that time itself was created. Section 8.2 analyzes Augustine's famous "threefold present": the present of past things (memory), the present of present things (attention), and the present of future things (expectation). Section 8.3 examines the *distentio animi* — the stretching or distension of the soul — which is Augustine's positive account of what time is. Section 8.4 presses the puzzle of the present: even the present has no duration, which leads to the question of how measurement of time is possible at all. Section 8.5 briefly considers Augustine's legacy in the medieval and modern traditions.

A note on the text: the *Confessions* is one of the most beautiful and accessible texts in the entire history of philosophy. It is also one of the most carefully argued. Do not let the lyrical, prayerful surface of the prose lead you to underestimate the precision of the philosophical moves. Augustine knows exactly what he is doing, and the prayers to God with which he punctuates the analysis are not ornamental interruptions but part of the argument's structure: they express the relationship between the finite, temporal human mind and the infinite, eternal divine mind that Augustine's philosophy of time is designed to illuminate.
