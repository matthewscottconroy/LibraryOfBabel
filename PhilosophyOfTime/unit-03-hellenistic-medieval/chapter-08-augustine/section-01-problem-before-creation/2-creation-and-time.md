# 8.1.2 — The Eternal Present: God's *Nunc Stans* and Created Time

---

## What God's Eternity Is

If time was created along with the world, and if God exists "before" creation only in the non-temporal sense of being the creator and ground of time, then God's mode of being must be radically different from any temporal mode. Augustine articulates this difference through the concept of the *nunc stans* — the "standing now," the eternal present that does not flow or pass.

The key passage is *Confessions* XI.11.13: "Your years do not come and go. Our years pass and others come after them... But your years are all simultaneously present since they stand still; nor do they depart when they come since they do not pass away. But our years will all be complete only when they have all ceased to be. Your years are one day, yet your day is not a daily recurrence but simply Today, because your Today does not yield to Tomorrow nor did it follow on Yesterday. Your Today is Eternity" (trans. Chadwick 1991: 229–230).

This passage is theologically intense, but the philosophical content is clear. God's existence has the following features:
1. It is not successive — there is no before and after in God's life
2. It is completely present — not "all times" spread out but a single, all-at-once completeness
3. It is not measurable by time — it has no duration in the ordinary sense

This is remarkably close to Plotinus's concept of eternity (*aion*) as the "complete, simultaneous, and full life" of Intellect. Augustine had read the Platonists (though probably in Latin translation, and the books attributed by some scholars to Victorinus's translations of Plotinus), and the influence is clear. But Augustine transforms the Plotinian concept by making it specifically theological — it is the mode of being of the Christian God, not of an abstract metaphysical principle.

## The Structure of the Contrast

Augustine's analysis sets up a fundamental contrast that runs through the rest of Book XI:

**God's eternity**: complete, simultaneous, non-successive, non-flowing, the eternal *Today*
**Human temporal existence**: successive, flowing, divided into past, present, and future, never complete

This contrast is not merely descriptive. It is normative: God's eternal mode of being is the standard against which human temporal existence is measured and found wanting — not in a moralistic sense, but in the sense that human temporal existence is an ontologically dependent, incomplete realization of something more fully real. Time is, for Augustine as for Plotinus, the mode of being of creatures — and creatures are by nature limited, sequential, partial. Eternity is the mode of being of the Creator, whose unlimited life is complete all at once.

## What "Created Time" Implies about the World

If time was created along with the world, then the world does not exist in a pre-existing temporal framework that it then fills. Time and the world came together. This has a remarkable implication: the age of the world is not "how long the world has been in existence" in any absolute sense — there is no absolute temporal framework in which the world has been sitting for some duration. The world's age is the temporal order internal to the world — the relations of before and after among events in the world.

This is a profoundly relational account of time. Time is not a container; it is the internal order of the created world's events. The world does not have a temporal location in some larger temporal framework; it simply is a temporally structured whole. The creator of the world is not located in the world's temporal framework at all.

## The Contrast with Human Experience

Human beings are temporal creatures — we exist in succession, not in the eternal standing now of God. Our years come and go; what was future becomes present becomes past, and the present is always slipping away from us. This is not accidental to being human; it is constitutive. We are the kind of beings whose existence is spread out across time — who have a past that is no longer and a future that is not yet, with a present that is always disappearing in the act of occurring.

This temporal constitution of human existence is what Augustine will analyze in Sections 8.2 through 8.4. The *distentio animi* — the stretching of the soul — is not an accident or a defect; it is what it means to be a finite, created being as opposed to the infinite, uncreated one. But understanding it clearly requires pressing hard on the questions: what exactly are the past and future? What do we measure when we measure time? How is memory and anticipation possible at all?

These questions are not merely theological; they are genuinely philosophical, and Augustine addresses them with the same rigor that any careful philosopher would. The theological framing of Book XI does not make the philosophical content less serious; if anything, it gives the analysis an urgency that purely academic treatments lack.
