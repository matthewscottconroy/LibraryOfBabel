# 8.2.1 — The Three Presents: Memory, Attention, Expectation

---

## The Preliminary Puzzle

Augustine begins the analysis of time in *Confessions* XI with a puzzle about existence: "There are three times: a present of things past, a present of things present, a present of things to come. In the soul there are these three aspects of time, and I do not see them anywhere else. The present considering the past is memory, the present considering the present is immediate awareness, the present considering the future is expectation" (*Confessions* XI.20.26, trans. Chadwick 1991: 235).

Before arriving at this formulation, Augustine establishes why we cannot straightforwardly say that past and future exist. The past "no longer is" — it has ceased to be. The future "is not yet" — it has not come to be. Only the present exists, strictly speaking. But then how can we speak of the past and future at all? How can they be *spoken* of with true or false claims ("Caesar crossed the Rubicon," "the sun will rise tomorrow") if they do not exist?

The puzzle is not merely verbal. It bears on whether anything like temporal extension is real. If only the present instant exists, and the present instant has no duration (as Chrysippus showed), then there is no temporal extension at all — only a succession of durationless points, each of which is real only at the moment of its occurrence. But this seems to make the passage of time unintelligible.

## The Solution: Three Modes of the Present

Augustine's solution is to recognize that the past, present, and future, as we experience them, are not three temporal regions at different locations in a temporal framework. They are three aspects of *how the present moment is structured by consciousness*. The past exists in memory — not as an actual past region of time that persists alongside the present, but as a present mental state (the memory) that carries the content of past experience. The future exists in expectation — not as an actual future region that already exists somewhere, but as a present mental state (the anticipation) directed toward what will come. The present exists in attention — the direct awareness of what is occurring now.

This is a phenomenological insight of genuine depth. Memory is not a record stored in some separate temporal region; it is a present activity of the mind. When you remember what happened yesterday, the remembering is happening *now*, even though its content is of something past. Similarly, expectation is a present activity directed toward the future: the anticipation is present even though its object is not yet.

What we call "the past" is, for purposes of analysis, a name for the content of current memories. What we call "the future" is a name for the content of current expectations. This does not mean that past events never happened or that future events will not happen; it means that our *access* to past and future is always through present mental states. We are always in the present, but the present as structured by consciousness contains within it a complex orientation toward what has been and what will be.

## The Phenomenological Precision

Augustine's analysis is phenomenologically precise in a way that earns admiration even from readers unsympathetic to his theology. Consider the following observations he makes in support of the threefold present:

**Memory is not the past, but about it**: When you remember a past experience — say, a conversation you had last week — what is present in your mind is not the conversation itself (that is over) but an "image" (*imago*) of it. The memory is a present reality; the event it represents is past. Augustine is careful to distinguish the present reality of the memory from the past reality of what is remembered (*Confessions* XI.18.23, trans. Chadwick 1991: 234).

**Narration as tripartite attention**: Augustine uses the example of reciting a psalm to illustrate how memory, attention, and expectation operate together in temporal experience. Before you start, the whole psalm is in expectation. As you recite, the earlier parts pass into memory while the later parts remain in expectation, and each syllable is in the immediate focus of attention. The recitation is a temporal whole that is given to you not all at once but through the movement of attention from expectation into the present and then into memory (*Confessions* XI.28.38, trans. Chadwick 1991: 242).

**Extension in the soul**: What makes temporal experience possible is not the objective temporal extension of events in the world but the extension (*distentio*) of the soul — the soul's capacity to hold memory and expectation together with present attention. Without this extension, time would be experienced as an unintelligible succession of durationless instants. The soul's extension is what makes temporal experience coherent.

## Why This Is Not Simple Subjectivism

A reader might worry that Augustine's account reduces time to something purely mental — that it makes the temporal order of the world a projection of the mind's activities rather than a genuine feature of things. This would be a misreading. Augustine is not claiming that events do not really happen in temporal succession outside the mind. He is claiming that our *experience* of time — our ability to perceive duration, to know that something happened earlier and something will happen later — is possible only because the mind structures experience through memory, attention, and expectation.

The soul does not create temporal succession; it makes temporal succession intelligible. Without memory and expectation, temporal succession would be a series of isolated instants, each of which was present for a moment and then gone, with no connection to what preceded or followed. The soul's tripartite structure is what makes the succession of instants into experienced time — time with duration, direction, and narrative coherence.

This is a subtle and important distinction, and it anticipates the phenomenological tradition's key insight that experience of time requires a structure of temporal intentionality — the mind's directedness toward past and future within the present moment (Husserl 1991: 29–35). Augustine does not use the language of intentionality, but he identifies the same structure.
