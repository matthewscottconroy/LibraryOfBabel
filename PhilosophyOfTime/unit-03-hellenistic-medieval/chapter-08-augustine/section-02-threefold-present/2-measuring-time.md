# 8.2.2 — Measuring Time: What Do We Measure?

---

## The Puzzle of Temporal Measurement

Having established that time is, experientially, a threefold present — memory, attention, expectation — Augustine turns to what he calls the "second great difficulty": the puzzle of temporal measurement. We measure time. We say this period lasted longer than that one, that this event took two hours, that this journey was shorter than that one. Temporal measurement is not only possible; it is indispensable to everyday life. But what exactly are we measuring?

The puzzle, as Augustine articulates it in *Confessions* XI.27.36: "How do I measure time? Do I measure the longer time by the shorter, as we measure the length of a beam by a foot? Is it the same as when we measure the length of a syllable by the length of a long syllable, and say it is double? So we measure the extension of poems by the lines, the lines by feet, the feet by syllables, the shorts by longs. But we do not measure poems in pages — that would be a spatial measure — but in time as we speak them. I say: 'That is a long poem, since it has so many verses,' and 'That is a long verse, since it consists of so many feet,' and 'That is a long foot, since it is double the short.' But even so I do not grasp a firm measurement of time; it may be that a short verse, spoken slowly, takes more time than a long verse spoken quickly" (trans. Chadwick 1991: 241, paraphrased).

The puzzle is that ordinary temporal measurement (comparing durations) does not seem to measure anything that exists. The future does not yet exist; the past no longer exists; the present has no duration. So what is being measured?

## Augustine's Reductio of External Measures

Augustine considers and rejects the view that we measure time by measuring something external to the mind. Perhaps we measure time by measuring the motion of the heavens — the rotation of the stars, the movement of the sun? But this cannot be right, as Joshua's prayer in the Old Testament illustrates: Joshua prayed for the sun to stand still, and the battle continued — time continued — even as the sun was motionless. Time is not the motion of the sun.

More generally: we do not measure time by measuring any particular motion, because time and any particular motion are distinct. A motion could speed up or slow down while time continued at the same rate; or time could speed up while a particular motion remained constant. The measure of motion and the measure of time are not the same thing.

This argument parallels Aristotle's point that time is not identical with motion, and Plotinus's point that motion cannot be the ultimate ground of time. But Augustine draws a different conclusion: the independence of time from external processes drives him not to a cosmological source (Soul) but to a psychological source (the mind itself).

## The Resolution: The Mind Measures Itself

Augustine's solution is as elegant as it is radical: "It is in you, O mind of mine, that I measure time. Do not shout me down that it exists [objectively]; do not shout yourself down with the turbulent flood of your impressions. In you, as I have said, I measure time. The impression which passing events make on you remains, and it is this which I measure, not the things which have passed and caused the impression. This is what time is" (*Confessions* XI.27.36, trans. Chadwick 1991: 241).

The key move is the identification of what is measured with the "impression" (*affectio*, sometimes translated as "affection" or "modification") that passing events leave in the mind. When I experience a sound and then measure how long it lasted, what I am measuring is not the sound itself (which is now past) but the impression of the sound — the trace that the sound has left in my memory. The measuring is a present activity operating on a present mental state (the memory or impression), not on any past or future entity.

This is a decisive internalization of the measurement of time. Aristotle said that time requires a soul to count it, but he left the being of time in the external world (the before-and-after of motion). Augustine brings time itself into the soul: what is measured is the soul's own modification, not any external process. The soul, measuring time, is measuring itself.

## The Philosophical Significance

This conclusion has enormous philosophical consequences. If what we measure when we measure time is the soul's impressions — its memories and expectations — then temporal measurement is fundamentally a self-referential activity. The mind is not a disinterested observer recording the passage of objective time; it is the very medium in which temporal order is constituted. The "length" of a duration is not a property out there in the world waiting to be measured; it is the depth of the impression that the duration leaves in the mind.

This does not mean that all temporal claims are subjective in the sense of being person-relative and potentially varying from one individual to another. Augustine is not saying that two hours feel like two hours to me but three hours to you, so there is no fact of the matter. He is saying that the *ground* of temporal measurement is the mind's capacity to retain impressions and compare them — and this capacity, while operating differently in different individuals, tracks something real about the temporal order of the world because the world's events really do leave impressions in the mind that accurately represent their order and duration.

Whether this fully resolves the puzzle is debatable. A skeptic might ask: if temporal measurement is ultimately measurement of impressions, what ensures that our impressions accurately track the objective temporal order of the world? Is there not a gap between the phenomenal duration of experience (how long something *seems* to have lasted) and the objective duration (how long it actually lasted)? This gap is familiar — time "flies" when we are absorbed and "drags" when we are bored. Augustine acknowledges the variability of felt duration but does not fully address the question of how subjective impression connects to objective order.

This connection — between phenomenal time and objective time — will be one of the central puzzles of the phenomenological tradition (Husserl 1991; Heidegger 1962) and remains unresolved today.
