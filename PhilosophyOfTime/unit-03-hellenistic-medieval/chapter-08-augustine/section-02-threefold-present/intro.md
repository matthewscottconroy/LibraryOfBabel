# Section 8.2: The Threefold Present

---

## Section Introduction

Having established that time was created and that God's eternal being contrasts with human temporal existence, Augustine turns to the analysis of time itself. The famous diagnosis — "if no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to one that asks, I do not know" — is followed not by despair but by a careful philosophical investigation. Augustine begins by questioning the naive division of time into three parts: past, present, and future.

The puzzle he identifies is acute. The past no longer exists — it has ceased to be. The future does not yet exist — it has not begun. The present, strictly speaking, has no duration — as soon as any moment is identified as present, it is already passing into the past. How can there be three times — past, present, future — if two of them do not exist and the third has no extent? And yet we speak of all three with perfect confidence and they play essential roles in our lives: we remember the past, attend to the present, and anticipate the future. Something must be real that makes these activities possible.

Augustine's solution is the "threefold present" — the insight that what we call past, present, and future are not three separate temporal regions but three aspects of the present as it is structured by consciousness. The present of past things is memory; the present of present things is attention; the present of future things is expectation. These are not three times but three modes of the mind's engagement with time.

Section 8.2.1 examines this analysis and its phenomenological precision. Section 8.2.2 takes up the connected question of how we measure time.
