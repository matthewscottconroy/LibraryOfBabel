# 8.3.1 — Time as *Distentio Animi*: The Distension of the Soul

---

## The Core Concept

The phrase *distentio animi* appears in *Confessions* XI.26.33: "I am about to repeat a psalm that I know. Before I begin, my expectation is extended over the whole; but when I have begun, as much of it as I have removed into the past is extended in my memory; and the life of this action of mine is extended both ways, into my memory, so far as I have repeated already, and into my expectation, so far as I am about to repeat. But the attention is present and present is that which, as future, passes over into the past" (trans. Chadwick 1991: 243, slightly adapted).

The Latin *distentio* means a stretching, an extension, a being pulled in different directions. The soul is stretched — pulled out, as it were, from a point into an extension — by its temporal existence. This stretching is not a distortion or a defect; it is the positive structure of what it means to be a temporal being. Without *distentio*, time would not be experienced as having duration at all.

What is the soul stretched between? It is stretched between:
- **Memory** (*memoria*): the soul's retention of the past as a present mental content
- **Attention** (*contuitus* or *attentio*): the soul's direct awareness of what is occurring now
- **Expectation** (*expectatio*): the soul's anticipation of what is to come as a present orientation toward the future

These three are not three temporal locations strung along a line; they are three aspects of the present moment as it is experienced by a soul with the capacity for memory and expectation. Memory carries the past into the present; expectation carries the future into the present; attention is the active connection between them.

## The Psalm Example

Augustine's most vivid illustration of *distentio animi* is the example of reciting a psalm — an activity familiar to any monk or bishop in late antiquity, and one that all his readers would recognize. Before beginning, the entire psalm is in expectation — the soul holds the whole of what it is about to do in a kind of forward-directed attention. As recitation proceeds, the earlier portions pass from expectation into presence into memory, while the later portions remain in expectation. Throughout, attention operates in the present, transferring content from expectation into memory through the "action" of reciting.

The stretching is dynamic: the relative proportions of memory and expectation change continuously as the recitation proceeds. At the beginning, expectation predominates; at the end, memory predominates; in the middle, they are roughly balanced. But throughout, the soul is extended in both directions simultaneously. The recitation is not experienced as a series of isolated instants but as a unified temporal whole — a "now" with genuine breadth — because the soul's distention gives it coherence.

This example is beautiful because it applies not just to psalm-recitation but to all temporal experience. Any activity — a conversation, a journey, a day's work, an entire life — has the same structure: it is a temporal whole that is experienced through the soul's extension between memory and expectation. The philosopher's task, Augustine suggests in *Confessions* XI.29.39, is to "extend" the distension to encompass an entire human life: "even the life of a man, the parts of which are all at the same time in expectation, in attention, and in memory" (trans. Chadwick 1991: 243).

## *Distentio* as a Mark of Finitude

There is a poignant edge to Augustine's account of *distentio animi* that it would be philosophically dishonest to ignore. The stretched soul — the soul extended between past and future, never fully present to itself — is, for Augustine, a soul in a condition that falls short of what it should be. The perfect mode of being would be the eternal present of God: complete, simultaneous, without the division and partial absence that characterize temporal existence.

Augustine writes at *Confessions* XI.29.39: "Let me be gathered together from the dispersion in which I was torn to pieces, while I was turned away from thee, the One, and wasted myself on the Many" (trans. Chadwick 1991: 244). The *distentio* of the soul — its stretching through time — is connected to a kind of fragmentation or dispersion (*distentio* can also carry the sense of distraction, pulling-in-different-directions). The soul that is fully engaged with the eternal One would not be scattered across time in this way; it would be collected, unified, complete.

This theological coloring does not undermine the philosophical analysis; it enriches it. Augustine is identifying a genuine feature of temporal existence — that to be in time is to be partially absent from yourself, because your past is no longer present and your future is not yet present — and connecting it to the broader question of what it means to be a finite creature. Time is not merely an objective framework; it is the condition of a particular kind of existence.

## The Triadic Structure of Temporal Experience

What *distentio animi* provides is an account of the triadic structure of temporal experience that goes beyond simple memory and anticipation. The three elements — memory, attention, expectation — are not three separate faculties operating independently. They are three aspects of a single, unified temporal orientation. Memory without attention to the present would be mere archival retrieval; expectation without a present to proceed from would be empty projection; attention without memory and expectation would be punctual awareness without duration or anticipation.

The unity of the three is precisely the *distentio* — the extension that holds them together. The soul is not first in the present, then in the past (memory), then in the future (expectation). It is simultaneously in all three — or rather, the three are all dimensions of what it means to be in the present. The present, for Augustine, is not a durationless instant (as Chrysippus argued) but a temporally extended activity of the soul — one that is thick with memory and anticipation.

This is the deepest answer to the Stoic problem of the present. Chrysippus showed that if we define the present as a temporal region, it must be durationless. Augustine shows that the phenomenological present — the present as experienced by a soul with memory and expectation — is not a durationless point but an extended activity. The extension is not temporal extension in the external world; it is the extension of the soul itself through its structural orientation toward past and future.
