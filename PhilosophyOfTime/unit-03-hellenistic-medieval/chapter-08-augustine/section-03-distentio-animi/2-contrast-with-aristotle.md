# 8.3.2 — Augustine vs. Aristotle: World-Time and Soul-Time

---

## The Fundamental Disagreement

The contrast between Aristotle's account of time and Augustine's is one of the most instructive confrontations in the history of philosophy. The two accounts are not simply rivals; they are answers to different questions — or rather, they identify time's primary locus differently. For Aristotle, time is primarily a feature of the physical world. For Augustine, time is primarily a feature of the soul's structure. Getting clear on this difference, and assessing which account is more adequate, is one of the best exercises in the philosophy of time.

Aristotle defines time as "the number of motion with respect to before and after" (*Physics* IV.11, 219b1–2). His account places time in the world: time is what we count in motion, and the motion that grounds time is primarily the regular motion of the heavens. The soul is required to count — time would not exist without a counting mind — but the primary locus of time is out there in the world's motions, not in here in the soul.

Augustine implicitly rejects this. His argument is that when we claim to measure time, we are not measuring external processes (the rotation of the sun, the flow of water through a clepsydra) but the impressions that processes leave in the mind. The external process has already passed by the time we measure it; what remains, and what we actually compare, is the mental trace. Time's primary locus is in the soul, not in the world.

## Whose Answer Is Right?

Both accounts capture something important, and it is worth asking whether they are genuinely inconsistent.

Aristotle's account is right that time is not merely in individual minds. The temporal order of the world — the succession of days and seasons, the before-and-after of causes and effects — is not a product of any individual consciousness. The world would have temporal structure even if there were no human observers. In this sense, objective time — the time described by physics and astronomy — is more naturally accounted for by something like Aristotle's approach.

Augustine's account is right that our *experience* of time — temporal awareness, the sense of duration, the ability to measure temporal intervals — requires the structures of memory and expectation that he describes. A being with no memory and no anticipation would be unable to experience time as having duration; it would be locked in a perpetual present with no sense of how long things last or what comes before and after. In this sense, phenomenological time — time as it figures in experience — requires the soul's tripartite structure.

The question is whether these two levels of analysis — objective time and phenomenological time — are genuinely independent or whether one is prior.

## The Phenomenologist's Response

The phenomenological tradition, following Husserl and Heidegger, has generally sided with Augustine's emphasis on the soul (or consciousness) as the locus of time's constitutive structure. Husserl, in his *Lectures on the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time*, argues that objective time — the time of clocks and events — is itself constituted by the time-consciousness of the experiencing subject. This does not mean that objective time is illusory, but it means that our grasp of objective time is mediated by the temporal structure of consciousness (Husserl 1991: 3–10). This is a sophisticated development of the Augustinian insight.

Heidegger, in *Being and Time*, argues that temporality is the fundamental structure of human existence (*Dasein*): to be human is to be projected forward toward one's future while being thrown back upon one's past, with the present as the moment of authentic or inauthentic resolution between these two. This tripartite structure of temporality echoes Augustine's threefold present in a recognizably transformed key (Heidegger 1962: §65).

Ricoeur, in *Time and Narrative*, argues that the opposition between Aristotle's cosmic time and Augustine's phenomenological time is irreducible — neither can be derived from the other — and that narrative is what bridges the gap: narrative is the form through which human beings make the succession of objective events into a meaningful personal and collective time (Ricoeur 1984: 52–77).

## The Genuine Tension

The tension between world-time and soul-time is not merely academic. It bears on practical questions about what we are measuring when we measure time and what would be lost if all conscious beings were extinguished.

If Aristotle is right, time would continue after the extinction of all conscious life: the galaxies would still rotate, causes would still precede effects, the universe would still age. If Augustine is right — or if we take the full phenomenological lesson — there is something important missing from time without consciousness: not the objective temporal order, which continues, but the *experienced* temporal order — duration, direction, narrative. Whether this matters depends on what you think time fundamentally is.

The most defensible position may be a pluralism: there is world-time (the objective temporal order of events and processes, the time described by physics) and there is phenomenological time (the experienced temporal structure that requires consciousness). These two are related — phenomenological time tracks world-time, and world-time provides the material that phenomenological time organizes — but neither is simply reducible to the other. Augustine and Aristotle each got half of the story.
