# 8.4.1 — The Present Has No Duration

---

## The Regressive Analysis

Augustine applies to the present an analysis that is both careful and relentless. He begins from the common observation that the present "is" — it is the one genuine temporal region. The past no longer exists; the future does not yet exist; only the present is. But what is the present? How long does it last?

In *Confessions* XI.15.20, Augustine runs the following argument. Take the present as "this year." The year is present. But the year contains twelve months: eleven of them are either past or future, and only one is present. Take that month: it contains thirty days, of which only one is present. Take that day: it contains hours, of which only one is present. Take that hour: it contains minutes. Take that minute: it contains seconds. And so on.

"Even a single hour passes in fugitive little pieces. Everything which has been part of it has gone into the past, and what remains is future. Even if we can conceive of a point in time that cannot be divided into any further parts, however tiny — that alone is what can be called 'present.' And that rushes past from the future into the past so quickly that it has no duration (*nulla morula*). For if it has duration, it is divisible into past and future. But the present has no extension" (*Confessions* XI.15.20, trans. Chadwick 1991: 232).

The argument is essentially the same as Chrysippus's: any positive duration can be divided into a past part and a future part; therefore the present, if it is a temporal region at all, must be durationless. And a durationless present is essentially nothing — it "rushes past" from future to past without pausing to exist.

## The Double Bind

This creates what might be called a double bind for any account of time. On one hand, the present is the only temporal region that exists. On the other hand, the present is durationless — it has no extent, no "space" in which anything can be or occur. If the present is the only time that exists, and the present has no duration, then it seems that nothing can exist in the present: existence requires some positive temporal extent, and the present has no positive temporal extent.

But this cannot be right. Things obviously exist and events obviously occur. The double bind reveals that our ordinary concept of "the present" is systematically ambiguous: we use it sometimes to mean the strict mathematical limit (a durationless boundary between past and future), and sometimes to mean some contextually relevant temporal neighborhood (this moment, this afternoon, this era). The strict present has no duration; the practical present has whatever duration is relevant to the context.

Augustine does not resolve this tension but acknowledges it openly. The honest thing to say is that the present, when analyzed rigorously, is not a temporal region that things can be *in*. It is a limit — a moving boundary that separates the no-longer from the not-yet. And yet we experience ourselves as *in* the present, as living through a present that has genuine duration.

## The Role of *Distentio*

This is precisely where the concept of *distentio animi* does its crucial work. If the objective present — the present as a temporal region in the world — has no duration, then the experienced present — the present as a structure of consciousness — must get its apparent duration from somewhere else. Augustine's answer is that it gets it from the soul's extension through memory and expectation.

The soul is not *in* a durationless present; it is extended through time by means of memory and expectation. What appears to be the "thickness" of the present — the sense that the present has some positive duration, enough to hold an experience — is actually the soul's distension, its stretching backward into memory and forward into expectation. There is no objective thick present; there is only the soul's tripartite orientation, which makes temporal experience possible in the absence of any extended objective present.

This is a brilliant move. It explains how we can experience time as having duration even though the strict present is durationless, by locating the apparent thickness of the present in the structure of consciousness rather than in the objective temporal framework. The experienced present is as thick as the soul's memory and anticipation, not as thick as any objective temporal interval.

## The Anticipation of Husserl

This move anticipates Husserl's distinction between the "now-point" (the strict present of the objective temporal stream, analogous to Augustine's durationless instant) and the "living present" (the extended structure of consciousness that includes retention and protention — the holding-in-mind of the just-past and the just-coming-into-being) (Husserl 1991: §§10–16). Husserl's retention and protention are structurally analogous to Augustine's memory and expectation, and the "living present" is structurally analogous to the temporally extended experience that Augustine's *distentio animi* makes possible.

The similarity is not coincidental: Husserl had studied Augustine, and his Lectures on Internal Time-Consciousness explicitly acknowledge the Augustinian analysis as a forerunner of his own (Husserl 1991: 4). What Augustine achieved through theological reflection and personal meditation, Husserl developed into a systematic phenomenology of time-consciousness — but the core structure was already present in *Confessions* XI.

## What Remains Unresolved

Augustine is honest that his account does not fully resolve the puzzle of the present. He knows he has identified the structure of temporal experience rather than given a final account of what time is. The soul's distension explains how we experience duration; it does not explain why the objective world has the temporal structure it does, or why physical processes succeed one another in the order they do.

These questions — which fall more naturally under the Aristotelian or Newtonian heading — are questions that Augustine's phenomenological analysis does not address. This is not a failing; it is a division of labor. Augustine is doing phenomenology before there was a word for it, and doing it brilliantly. The physical questions are left for others.

What he has established, however, is this: the temporal experience that we take to be fundamental — the experience of a present with genuine duration, of a past that we remember, of a future that we anticipate — is not a simple readout of objective temporal structure but a complex achievement of consciousness. The soul, stretched between memory and expectation, makes temporal experience possible. Without this *distentio*, the durationless present would be all there was, and we would have no experience of time at all.
