# 8.5.1 — Augustine's Influence: Medieval Theology to Modern Phenomenology

---

## The Medieval Reception

Augustine's analysis of time in *Confessions* XI was not the primary text through which medieval thinkers engaged with the philosophy of time. The dominant tradition was Aristotelian — mediated through Boethius, Avicenna, and Averroes — and it was primarily concerned with the metaphysics of time, eternity, divine knowledge, and the relationship between temporal succession and divine action. The *Confessions*, while universally known and respected, was read primarily as a spiritual autobiography and a treatise on grace and conversion, not as a work of technical philosophy.

Nevertheless, Augustine's influence can be traced in specific medieval discussions. Aquinas, in his account of divine eternity and its relation to time, draws on the Augustinian/Boethian concept of the eternal present — God's knowledge of all times in a single atemporal act (Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, Q.10, a.2). The concept of the *nunc stans* — the standing now — is thoroughly Augustinian in spirit, even when it is formulated in Boethian language. Bonaventure, in his *Journey of the Mind to God*, uses Augustinian temporal imagery in connection with the soul's ascent toward the eternal (Bonaventure 1956: chap. 3).

The tension between the Aristotelian emphasis on objective time (time as the measure of motion) and the Augustinian emphasis on soul-time (time as the distension of consciousness) is a productive fault line running through medieval philosophy of time. Aquinas tries to hold both together: objective time is measured by the motion of the heavens, but time requires a soul to measure it (echoing Aristotle's remark about soul and time). The deeper Augustinian insight — that what we measure when we measure time is the soul's own impressions — is less developed in Aquinas than the Aristotelian framework.

## Husserl's Transformation

The most important modern development of Augustine's analysis is Edmund Husserl's *Lectures on the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time*, delivered around 1905 and published in edited form in 1928. Husserl begins his analysis with an explicit acknowledgment of Augustine: "The analysis of time-consciousness is an age-old crux of descriptive psychology and the theory of knowledge. The first thinker who deeply felt the immense difficulties was Augustine" (Husserl 1991: 4). He then proceeds to develop a systematic phenomenology of time-consciousness that addresses the questions Augustine raised.

Husserl's key concepts — the "living present," "retention" (the holding-in-mind of the just-past), "protention" (the anticipation of the just-coming-into-being), and the "primal impression" (the direct awareness of the now-point) — are direct developments of Augustine's memory, attention, and expectation. Where Augustine was working in literary and theological prose, Husserl works in the language of transcendental phenomenology — but the structural analysis is recognizably related.

The significance of Husserl's development is that it makes the Augustinian analysis technically precise and philosophically systematic. Where Augustine established the basic structure of temporal consciousness, Husserl worked out the details: the precise relationship between retention and primary memory, the character of protention as distinct from full expectation, the way that the living present is itself a continuously changing complex structure. These developments enable a much finer-grained analysis of temporal experience than Augustine achieves.

## Heidegger and the Existential Transformation

Heidegger, in *Being and Time* (1927), inherits the Augustinian-Husserlian analysis and transforms it in an existential direction. For Heidegger, the tripartite structure of temporality — having-been (*Gewesenheit*), coming-toward (*Zukunft*), and the Present (*Gegenwart*) — is not primarily a feature of time-consciousness but the ontological structure of human existence (*Dasein*). To be human is to be thrown into a past, projected toward a future, and making decisions in the present. This is not merely a description of how we experience time; it is a characterization of what it means to exist as the kind of beings we are (Heidegger 1962: §65).

The connection to Augustine is not merely structural. Heidegger gave lectures on Augustine and Neoplatonism early in his career (1921), and his reading of Augustine's account of the soul's "distension" through care (*cura*) is explicitly acknowledged as a precursor of his own concept of care (*Sorge*) as the fundamental structure of human existence (Heidegger 1992: §§10–12).

## Ricoeur: Narrative and Temporal Experience

Paul Ricoeur, in the three volumes of *Time and Narrative* (1984–1988), offers perhaps the most sustained engagement with the Augustinian analysis of time in contemporary philosophy. Ricoeur begins with the "aporia of time" as Augustine formulates it — the puzzle that time has no being when analyzed literally — and argues that this aporia is partially resolved, not by philosophy, but by narrative. Narrative is the form of human action and life that makes the succession of events into a meaningful temporal whole; it is how human beings live time rather than merely being in it (Ricoeur 1984: 52–77).

For Ricoeur, the Augustinian threefold present is not merely a description of how we experience time but a characterization of how we *live* time — and narrative is the medium through which this living becomes intelligible. Stories impose beginnings, middles, and ends on the temporal flow; they make the distension of the soul into a structured whole with meaning. This is a profound extension of Augustine's insight.

## Contemporary Relevance

Augustine's analysis remains philosophically relevant in several contemporary contexts:

**Philosophy of mind and temporal experience**: The question of how consciousness generates the experience of temporal extension — the "specious present" with its retentional and protentional structure — is an active area of research in both phenomenology and cognitive science. Augustine's formulation of the problem, and his basic answer, is still the starting point for this research (Dainton 2000: chap. 6).

**Philosophy of time and the A/B-series debate**: The contemporary debate between A-theorists (who hold that there is an objective present, past, and future) and B-theorists (who hold that time is simply a dimension with earlier-later relations and no privileged present) can be illuminated by Augustine's analysis. If Augustine is right that the past, present, and future are features of soul-time rather than objective time, then the B-theory may capture objective time while the A-theory captures phenomenological time — and both may be right at their own level (Dainton 2000: 5–8).

**Cognitive science and memory**: Research on the neuroscience of memory and temporal perception has increasingly converged on the view that temporal experience requires active cognitive processes — memory and anticipation — of exactly the kind Augustine describes. The brain does not passively receive temporal information; it actively constructs temporal experience through processes of retention and anticipation (Eagleman 2011: 74–79).
