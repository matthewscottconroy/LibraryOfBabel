## Comprehension and Analysis

**Exercise 9.1**
Analyze Boethius's definition of eternity: "*interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio*." Explain each component of the definition. What work does *tota simul* do that "interminabilis" (unlimited) alone does not? How does this definition differ from the concept of sempiternity?

**Exercise 9.2**
Explain Boethius's solution to the problem of divine foreknowledge and human freedom. What is the role of the analogy of sight? Does Boethius's solution require that God exists outside time entirely? Could a similar response be made by a defender of divine sempiternity?

**Exercise 9.3**
Explain Avicenna's account of the Necessary Being and its relationship to time. What distinguishes the Necessary Being from contingent, temporal creatures? Is Avicenna's account of divine timelessness the same as Boethius's, or are there important differences?

**Exercise 9.4**
Aquinas distinguishes three modes of duration: *tempus*, *aevum*, and *aeternitas*. Explain what distinguishes each mode from the others. What philosophical work does the intermediate category of *aevum* do? Could you think of any non-theological application for the concept of aeveternal existence?

**Exercise 9.5**
Explain Ockham's nominalism and its implications for the ontology of time. In what sense does Ockham hold that time does not "really exist"? Is this eliminativism about time, or something more nuanced? Compare his position with the Stoic account of time as incorporeal.

**Exercise 9.6**
Explain Ockham's distinction between hard and soft facts about the past. What makes a fact about the past "hard"? Construct two original examples of hard past facts and two examples of soft past facts. How does this distinction support Ockham's response to the foreknowledge problem?

**Exercise 9.7**
Aquinas argued that reason alone cannot demonstrate whether the world had a beginning. Reconstruct his argument for this position. Do you agree that the question is genuinely undecidable by reason? What would count as a philosophical demonstration that time had a beginning?

**Exercise 9.8**
The Condemnations of 1277 condemned the proposition that the world is eternal. Some historians have argued that this helped rather than hindered the development of modern science. Explain this claim. Do you find it convincing?

## Observational and Experimental Exercises

**Lab Exercise 9.A — What Would Eternal Vision Be Like?**

Boethius describes God's eternal knowledge as analogous to seeing a landscape all at once, rather than traversing it sequentially. Try the following experiment: pick a route you know well (to class, to a shop, to a friend's home). First, mentally traverse the route step by step, noting each landmark in sequence. Then try to "see" the whole route all at once, as if on a map or from a great height. Write a brief account of the differences between these two modes of knowing the route. What does this experiment suggest about the contrast between temporal, sequential knowledge and something like "eternal," simultaneous knowledge? What are the limits of this analogy for understanding divine eternity?

**Lab Exercise 9.B — The Hard/Soft Fact Distinction in Ordinary Life**

The distinction between hard and soft facts is not unique to theology. Consider: "Yesterday, I predicted correctly that you would arrive on time." Is this a hard or soft fact about yesterday? Its truth seems to depend on what you did today. Find three ordinary (non-theological) examples of apparent past facts whose truth seems to depend on future or present facts. For each, discuss whether the "softness" shows that the fact is not genuinely fixed, or whether there is a way to reformulate it as a hard fact.

**Lab Exercise 9.C — Mapping Duration Ontologies**

Create a visual diagram or table comparing the three medieval accounts of duration surveyed in this chapter: Aquinas's *tempus/aevum/aeternitas*, Ockham's nominalist account (time as not a real entity), and the Boethian *tempus/aeternitas* binary. For each account, note: (1) what kinds of beings exist in each mode of duration; (2) what changes are possible within each mode; (3) what the account implies about the relationship between God and time. Use this comparison to identify which account you find most philosophically defensible, and explain why.

## Essay Questions

**Exercise 9.9 (Essay)**
Is Boethius's solution to the problem of divine foreknowledge and freedom successful? Present the problem clearly, explain Boethius's solution, and then evaluate at least one serious objection (from Wolterstorff, Fischer, or Pike). Write an essay of 900–1100 words.

**Exercise 9.10 (Essay)**
Ockham holds that time is not a real entity distinct from temporal things and events. Does this make time "merely subjective" — a feature of how we talk about the world rather than a feature of the world itself? Write an essay of 800–1000 words comparing Ockham's position with the Stoic account of time as incorporeal and with Leibniz's relational account of time (previewing Chapter 10 if you have read ahead).

**Exercise 9.11 (Essay)**
Aquinas argued that the question of whether the world had a temporal beginning cannot be resolved by reason alone. Contemporary cosmologists argue about whether the Big Bang constitutes a genuine beginning of time. Write an essay of 1000–1200 words assessing whether contemporary physics has resolved the question that Aquinas thought was undecidable by reason. Consider both what physics can and cannot tell us about whether time had a beginning.

**Exercise 9.12 (Essay)**
The concept of eternity (*aeternitas* in the Boethian/Thomistic sense) requires a timeless being that can somehow be related to temporal events. Identify and analyze the three most serious philosophical problems for divine timelessness (from among: inability to act in time, inability to know tensed truths, inability to be causally related to temporal events, incompatibility with divine simplicity). Then either defend timelessness against these objections or explain why you think the problems are fatal.
