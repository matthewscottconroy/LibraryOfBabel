## Annotated Suggestions for Further Reading

**Boethius. *Consolation of Philosophy*, Book V. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1999.**
The primary source for Boethius's definition of eternity and his response to the foreknowledge problem. Prose 3–6 of Book V are the relevant sections. Walsh's translation is clear and his introduction is helpful. Book V can be read without the rest of the *Consolation*, though the earlier books provide context.

**Stump, Eleonore, and Norman Kretzmann. "Eternity." *Journal of Philosophy* 78 (1981): 429–458.**
The most important modern analysis of divine eternity and foreknowledge in the Boethian tradition. Stump and Kretzmann develop the concept of ET-simultaneity and argue that it provides a coherent account of how a timeless God can be related to temporal events. Essential for anyone engaging seriously with the theological philosophy of time.

**Stump, Eleonore. *Aquinas*. London: Routledge, 2003.**
The most comprehensive philosophical treatment of Aquinas's system currently available. Rigorous and deeply learned but accessible to advanced undergraduates. The chapters on time, eternity, and divine knowledge are directly relevant to this chapter's material.

**Leftow, Brian. *Time and Eternity*. Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 1991.**
A systematic philosophical examination of divine eternity and its relationship to time, drawing on both the medieval tradition and contemporary analytic philosophy. Leftow defends a version of divine timelessness against numerous objections. Demanding but important.

**Fischer, John Martin. *God, Foreknowledge, and Freedom*. Stanford: Stanford University Press, 1989.**
A careful analytic treatment of the foreknowledge problem and its various solutions, including Ockhamism. Fischer is skeptical of Ockhamist responses but engages them fairly. A useful guide to the contemporary analytic literature on the problem.

**Plantinga, Alvin. "On Ockham's Way Out." *Faith and Philosophy* 3 (1986): 235–269.**
The most important defense of Ockhamism in contemporary analytic philosophy. Plantinga argues that the hard/soft fact distinction can be given a principled characterization and that it dissolves the foreknowledge problem. Essential reading alongside Ockham's own text.

**Davidson, Herbert A. *Proofs for Eternity, Creation, and the Existence of God in Medieval Islamic and Jewish Philosophy*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1987.**
The most thorough scholarly treatment of the eternity-of-the-world debate in the Islamic and Jewish traditions. Essential for placing Avicenna, Averroes, and Maimonides in philosophical context. Somewhat technical but indispensable for serious students.

**McGinnis, Jon. *Avicenna*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2010.**
The best contemporary introduction to Avicenna's philosophy. Chapter 5 on natural philosophy and Chapter 7 on metaphysics are most relevant. McGinnis makes Avicenna's complex system accessible without oversimplifying it.

**Sorabji, Richard. *Time, Creation and the Continuum*. London: Duckworth, 1983.**
Chapters 9–18 cover the medieval material, including Boethius, the Islamic tradition, and Aquinas. Sorabji's patient, historically informed analysis is indispensable for understanding how medieval accounts of time were shaped by and departed from the ancient tradition.

**Grant, Edward. "The Condemnation of 1277." *Viator* 10 (1979): 211–244.**
The best treatment of the 1277 Condemnations and their philosophical implications. Grant examines how the condemnations affected natural philosophy and created space for non-Aristotelian accounts of nature and time. Essential for understanding the context in which early modern philosophy emerged.
