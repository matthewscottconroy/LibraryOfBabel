# Chapter 9: Important Concepts

---

***Aeternitas* (Divine Eternity)**
The mode of existence that belongs to God, as defined by Boethius: "the complete, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unlimited life" (*interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio*). *Aeternitas* is distinguished from both sempiternity (endless temporal duration) and ordinary time by the *tota simul* — the all-at-once-ness of divine existence. God does not endure through time; God exists in a mode where all temporal distinctions (before/after, past/future) are simply absent.

***Aevum* (Aeveternity)**
Aquinas's intermediate category of duration, appropriate to beings (like angels) that are substantially unchanging but whose activities are successive. The *aevum* is more temporal than *aeternitas* (because there is succession of activities) but less temporal than *tempus* (because there is no substantial change or corruption). This three-tier account (*tempus/aevum/aeternitas*) is Aquinas's most original contribution to the metaphysics of duration.

***Tempus* (Ordinary Time)**
The mode of duration appropriate to material creatures subject to change, growth, and corruption. Time in this sense follows Aristotle's definition — it is the measure of motion — and it is the kind of duration within which all physical and biological processes occur. Creatures in *tempus* have genuinely past, present, and future phases of their existence.

**Sempiternity**
The mode of existence of a being that has always existed and will always exist — that has no beginning and no end — but that is still *in* time. A sempiternal being endures through all of time rather than existing outside it. This is distinct from Boethian eternity (*aeternitas*), which involves genuine atemporality. The debate about whether God should be understood as eternal (atemporal) or sempiternal (always in time) is a central controversy in the philosophy of religion.

***Tota Simul* (All at Once)**
The crucial phrase in Boethius's definition of eternity: God's life is possessed "all at once" — simultaneously complete — rather than spread out over temporal succession. This phrase marks the key difference between divine eternity and any temporal mode of existence, however long: even a sempiternal being lacks the *tota simul* character of genuine eternity.

**Divine Foreknowledge Problem**
The problem of reconciling God's omniscience (including knowledge of future free actions) with human freedom. If God knew in advance what you would freely do, and if this knowledge is fixed (as all past facts seem to be), then it seems that you could not have done otherwise — which seems to undermine freedom. The problem has generated several medieval and contemporary responses: divine timelessness (Boethius, Aquinas), Ockhamism (hard/soft facts), and Molinism (middle knowledge).

**Hard vs. Soft Past Facts**
Ockham's distinction: hard past facts are about the past simpliciter — they are entirely about what happened, and their truth does not depend on anything in the future. Soft past facts have a temporal location in the past but their truth depends on future or present facts. "Caesar crossed the Rubicon" is a hard past fact; "God knew in 1000 CE that you would eat breakfast on [today's date]" is a soft past fact (its truth depends on what you do today). Ockham argues that divine foreknowledge involves only soft past facts, which are not fixed in the relevant sense.

**Eternity-Time Simultaneity (ET-Simultaneity)**
A technical concept developed by Stump and Kretzmann (1981) to give content to the Boethian claim that God's eternal knowledge is "simultaneous" with temporal events. Standard simultaneity is temporal: two events are simultaneous if they occur at the same time. ET-simultaneity is a non-standard relation that holds between an eternal entity (God's knowledge) and a temporal event: the eternal knowledge is co-present with the temporal event in a way that does not require temporal co-occurrence.

**The Necessary Being (*Wajib al-Wujud*)**
Avicenna's concept of the ultimate ground of contingent existence: a being whose existence is necessary — it cannot not exist — as opposed to contingent beings, which exist but might not have. The Necessary Being is the source from which all contingent existence proceeds. For Avicenna, the Necessary Being is timeless: temporal categories (before, after, earlier, later) do not apply to it.

**Apophatic (Negative) Theology**
The theological method, defended by Maimonides and others, of characterizing God only negatively — saying what God is *not* rather than what God is. God is not temporal, not corporeal, not composite, not limited. Positive assertions about divine nature risk anthropomorphism and misrepresentation. In the philosophy of time, apophatic theology supports divine timelessness by arguing that we cannot say positively what God's eternal mode of being consists in — only that it is not temporal.

**Creation *ex nihilo***
The doctrine that God created the world "from nothing" — not from pre-existing matter but from no prior material substrate whatsoever. This doctrine, affirmed by Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theology, was philosophically controversial because Aristotle (and Averroes) argued that nothing comes from nothing: production requires a prior matter to be shaped. The debate about *creatio ex nihilo* is directly connected to the eternity of the world debate.

**The Condemnations of 1277**
The list of 219 propositions condemned by the Bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, as heretical. The condemned propositions included the eternity of the world, the impossibility of creation from nothing, and other Aristotelian-Averroist theses. The condemnations forced Latin Christian philosophy to develop accounts of nature, time, and motion that were not simply Aristotelian — creating philosophical space that some historians see as important for the later development of modern science.

**Temporal Nominalism**
The position, associated with Ockham, that time is not a real entity distinct from the temporal things and events that are "in" it. Time is a connotative term that picks out the temporal order and duration of individual events without designating any abstract temporal substance or framework. This position is a form of temporal relationism and anticipates Leibniz's relational account.

**The Eternity of the World (Debate)**
The medieval debate about whether Aristotle was right that the world has always existed (is eternal) or whether the world had a temporal beginning (was created). The debate was philosophically serious and generated rigorous arguments on both sides. Aquinas's position — that the question cannot be resolved by reason alone — is the most philosophically nuanced response.

**Molinism**
A position in philosophy of religion, named after Luis de Molina (1535–1600), that attempts to reconcile divine foreknowledge and human freedom through the concept of "middle knowledge" — God's knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: what each possible creature *would* freely do in each possible circumstance. This is a later development of Ockhamist themes and remains an active position in contemporary analytic philosophy of religion.
