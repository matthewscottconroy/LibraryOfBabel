# Chapter 9: Important Researchers

---

**Boethius** (Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius, c. 480–524 CE)
Roman philosopher, statesman, and theologian. Executed on charges of treason under Theodoric, he wrote the *Consolation of Philosophy* while awaiting death. His definition of eternity in Book V, prose 6 — "*interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio*" — is the most influential formulation of divine timelessness in the Western tradition. His translation of and commentaries on Aristotle's logic were the primary means by which Aristotelian thought was transmitted to the early medieval Latin West.

**Avicenna (Ibn Sina)** (c. 980–1037 CE)
Persian philosopher and physician, whose *Kitab al-Shifa'* (Book of Healing) is a comprehensive philosophical encyclopedia. Avicenna synthesized Aristotelian natural philosophy with Neoplatonist metaphysics and Islamic theology. His concept of the Necessary Being and his treatment of time in the Physics of the *Shifa'* were enormously influential on both Islamic and Latin Christian philosophy. He is the most important philosopher in the Islamic tradition for the transmission and transformation of Aristotelian natural philosophy.

**Averroes (Ibn Rushd)** (1126–1198 CE)
Andalusian philosopher and jurist, known in the Latin West as "the Commentator." His detailed commentaries on Aristotle were translated into Latin in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and became standard reference works. His rigorous defense of the eternity of the world set the terms for the Latin debate, and his *Incoherence of the Incoherence* (*Tahafut al-Tahafut*) — a defense of Aristotelian philosophy against al-Ghazali's attacks — is one of the great works of medieval philosophy.

**Maimonides (Moses ben Maimon)** (1138–1204 CE)
Jewish philosopher, physician, and codifier of Jewish law. His *Guide for the Perplexed* (*Moreh Nevukhim*, c. 1190) is the major work of medieval Jewish philosophy, addressing the educated Jew who has studied Aristotelian philosophy and is troubled by apparent conflicts with Torah. His argument that the eternity of the world cannot be demonstrated by reason, and his apophatic approach to divine nature, are philosophically original contributions of lasting importance.

**Thomas Aquinas** (1225–1274 CE)
Dominican friar and the most systematic philosopher-theologian of the medieval period. Born near Aquino in Italy, he studied in Paris and taught there and in Rome. His *Summa Theologiae*, *Summa Contra Gentiles*, *On the Eternity of the World*, and *Commentary on Aristotle's Physics* are all relevant to his philosophy of time. His three-tier account of duration (*tempus*, *aevum*, *aeternitas*) and his position on the eternity of the world are his most original contributions to the philosophy of time.

**John Duns Scotus** (c. 1266–1308 CE)
Franciscan philosopher and theologian known as "the Subtle Doctor." Scotus developed important innovations in logic and metaphysics, including a careful analysis of modal concepts (possibility, necessity, contingency) and their relationship to divine will and temporal facts. His work on contingency and the "possible worlds" framework that underlies it was influential on later medieval and early modern discussions of freedom and foreknowledge.

**William of Ockham** (c. 1287–1347 CE)
Franciscan philosopher and theologian known as "the Venerable Inceptor." Ockham's nominalism — the denial that universals are real entities — had wide-ranging implications for metaphysics, natural philosophy, and theology. His distinction between hard and soft past facts, developed in his *Tractatus de praedestinatione*, is one of the most important contributions to the foreknowledge problem. His influence on the development of early modern philosophy is considerable.

**Eleonore Stump** (b. 1947)
American philosopher at Saint Louis University, known for her work in medieval philosophy and philosophy of religion. Her joint paper with Norman Kretzmann, "Eternity" (1981), is the most important modern analysis and defense of the Boethian concept of divine eternity. Her subsequent book *Aquinas* (2003) is the most comprehensive philosophical treatment of Aquinas's system available in English.

**Norman Kretzmann** (1928–1998)
American philosopher at Cornell University. With Eleonore Stump, Kretzmann wrote "Eternity" (1981), arguably the most influential paper in contemporary philosophy of religion. He also edited and contributed to *The Cambridge Companion to Aquinas* (1993) and wrote extensively on medieval logic and natural philosophy.

**Herbert A. Davidson** (b. 1927)
Emeritus Professor at UCLA, specializing in medieval Jewish and Islamic philosophy. His *Proofs for Eternity, Creation, and the Existence of God in Medieval Islamic and Jewish Philosophy* (1987) is the most thorough scholarly treatment of the eternity-of-the-world debate in the Islamic and Jewish traditions. Essential for understanding the philosophical background of the medieval Christian discussion.
