# Chapter 9: Medieval Philosophy of Time

---

## Chapter Introduction

The thousand years of philosophy between Augustine and Descartes are poorly served by the popular image of medieval thought as derivative, repetitive, and philosophically unoriginal. The reality is almost exactly the opposite. Medieval philosophy was one of the most creative periods in the history of philosophical thought, generating original arguments in metaphysics, logic, philosophy of language, philosophy of mind, and — for our purposes — philosophy of time. It did so in three languages (Latin, Arabic, Hebrew) and across three religious traditions (Christian, Islamic, Jewish) that were simultaneously in dialogue and in conflict, borrowing arguments and concepts from each other while arriving at genuinely different conclusions.

The central organizing challenge for medieval philosophy of time was a problem that Aristotle could not have anticipated: the requirements of monotheistic theology. Aristotle had argued that the world is eternal — it has no beginning and no end. But Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theology all affirmed that God created the world, and creation implies a temporal beginning. Was the Aristotelian account of time compatible with creation? Could reason decide the question of the world's eternity, or was it a matter for revelation? What is God's relationship to time — does God exist in time, outside time, or in some third mode? If God foreknows all future events, how can human actions be genuinely free? These questions animated medieval philosophy of time with an urgency and precision that purely theoretical inquiry rarely achieves.

Chapter 9 proceeds through five sections. Section 9.1 examines Boethius's definition of eternity and his influential response to the problem of divine foreknowledge and human freedom. Section 9.2 surveys the contributions of Islamic and Jewish philosophy — Avicenna, Averroes, and Maimonides — as they adapted and criticized Aristotle's account of time. Section 9.3 examines Aquinas's sophisticated three-tiered account of temporal duration. Section 9.4 turns to the late medieval tradition, particularly Ockham's nominalism and its implications for the ontology of time and the problem of foreknowledge. Section 9.5 examines the medieval debate about the eternity of the world — whether the world could have existed forever — and its implications for the philosophy of time.

One caution deserves emphasis: medieval philosophy of time is inseparable from medieval theology. This is not because medieval philosophers were uncritical; on the contrary, many of the most penetrating objections to theological claims in the philosophy of time were raised by medieval thinkers themselves. It is because the problems that concern medieval philosophy — the nature of divine eternity, the compatibility of foreknowledge and freedom, the beginning of time — are problems that arise at the intersection of philosophical and theological inquiry, and that cannot be handled well if the theological dimension is artificially bracketed.
