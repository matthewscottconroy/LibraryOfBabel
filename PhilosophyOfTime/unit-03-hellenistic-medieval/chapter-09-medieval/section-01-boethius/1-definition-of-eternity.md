# 9.1.1 — Boethius's Definition of Eternity

---

## The Definition

The most precise and influential definition of eternity in the Western philosophical tradition appears in Boethius's *Consolation of Philosophy*, Book V, prose 6. The definition is given in Latin with a precision that translation struggles to convey: "*aeternitas est interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio*" — "eternity is the complete, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unlimited life" (Boethius, *Consolation* V.6.4, trans. Walsh 1999: 100).

This definition has six components, and each carries philosophical weight:

**"Eternity"** (*aeternitas*): Boethius is defining not "being eternal" in the loose sense of "lasting a very long time" but eternity as a specific mode of existence — the mode of existence that belongs to God.

**"Interminabilis"** (unlimited): God's life is not bounded by any temporal limits — not by a beginning, not by an end. This corresponds to what others have called sempiternity. But "unlimited" is only one component; it is not sufficient by itself for genuine eternity.

**"Vitae"** (of life): Eternity is a mode of *life*, not of abstract existence. Boethius follows the Plotinian tradition in insisting that the divine mode of being is living, active, and rich — not a static, dead persistence.

**"Tota simul"** (all at once / simultaneously complete): This is the crucial phrase. God's life is not spread out over time, with some parts past and others future. It is all present simultaneously. The past and future are not absent from God's mode of being; they are present to God at once. This rules out even a sempiternal being: a being that has existed from before the beginning and will exist forever still has, at any given moment, parts of its life that are past and parts that are future. God's life has no such parts.

**"Perfecta"** (perfect, complete): The word *perfecta* carries the sense of "having arrived at the end" (*perfectum* = completed). God's life is not an ongoing, still-being-accomplished process but a completed whole. This is not a limitation (God is not "done" and now idle) but a mark of the fullness of divine being.

**"Possessio"** (possession): God possesses this life — it is fully present to God, fully available, not in the process of being attained or risked of being lost. The metaphor of possession suggests security and completeness.

## Analysis: What the Definition Achieves

Boethius's definition does three important things simultaneously.

**First, it distinguishes genuine eternity from sempiternity.** A sempiternal being exists at all times — it has no beginning and no end — but it still exists *in* time. At any given temporal moment, its past is over and its future has not yet arrived. Boethius's God, by contrast, possesses all of life *tota simul* — all at once. This is a genuinely atemporal mode of being, not merely an unlimited temporal one.

**Second, it preserves the positive, living character of God's eternity.** Some conceptions of divine timelessness make God sound inert and static — a frozen moment, a changeless block of being. Boethius's "life" (*vita*) prevents this: God's eternity is not death-in-timelessness but a mode of living that is supremely active because it encompasses everything at once without strain or incompleteness.

**Third, it provides a framework for the problem of foreknowledge and freedom.** If God possesses all time tota simul, then God does not "foreknow" future events in the way that a fortuneteller might predict what will happen. God sees all times — past, present, and future from our perspective — from an eternal vantage point that is simultaneous with all of them. This is not prediction but eternal perception. We will examine this response to the foreknowledge problem in Section 9.1.2.

## Comparison with Plotinus and Augustine

Boethius's definition is clearly indebted to Plotinus's concept of eternity (*aion*) as the "complete, simultaneous, and full life" of Intellect (see Chapter 7). The phrase *tota simul* is essentially the Latin equivalent of Plotinus's *homou* — the "all-at-once" character of Intellect's eternal life. Whether Boethius read Plotinus directly or inherited the concept through intermediaries is debated, but the structural parallel is undeniable.

The difference from Plotinus is theological: Boethius's eternal being is the Christian God, a personal being who created the world and is provident over human affairs, not an impersonal metaphysical principle. This makes the problem of God's relationship to time more pressing than it was for Plotinus: a personal, providential God who acts in time and responds to prayer cannot easily be treated as simply having no relationship to time at all.

Augustine's concept of the *nunc stans* (Chapter 8) is also clearly related to Boethius's definition, though there is no direct dependence. Both thinkers are working from a common Platonist inheritance and developing it in a theological direction. Boethius's contribution is the precision of the definition — the six-component analysis that provides a formula to be cited, debated, and refined.
