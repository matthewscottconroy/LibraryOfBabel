# 9.1.2 — Divine Foreknowledge and Human Freedom

---

## The Problem

The problem of divine foreknowledge and human freedom is one of the most debated problems in medieval philosophy, and it has a direct connection to the philosophy of time. The problem can be stated simply: if God is omniscient, God knows in advance (from the perspective of human time) what you will freely do. But if God knows this in advance — if it is already determined, as a fact about the past, that God knew you would do it — then how can your action be genuinely free? A free action seems to require that it was genuinely possible for you to have done otherwise. But if God has always known you would do what you do, was it ever really possible for you to have done otherwise?

This is not merely a theological puzzle; it is a puzzle about the relationship between temporal facts and freedom. The specific temporal element is this: if there is a fact about the past (God's past knowledge of your future action), and if the past is fixed — if we cannot change the past — then there is a fact about the past that necessitates your present action. And if your present action is necessitated by a past fact, it seems not to be free.

Boethius presents this problem in *Consolation* V, prose 3, and addresses it in the famous response of prose 6.

## Boethius's Solution

Boethius's solution turns on his concept of divine eternity. If God is eternal in the Boethian sense — possessing all of time *tota simul*, from an atemporal vantage point — then God does not "foreknow" anything. Foreknowledge is knowledge of the future, and the future is in the future only from a temporal perspective. From God's atemporal perspective, past, present, and future are all equally present — all "simultaneously seen" in God's eternal vision.

This means that God's knowledge of what you will do tomorrow is not foreknowledge at all; it is eternal knowledge — the knowledge of a being who sees all times at once, as we might look at a landscape spread out before us. Boethius uses the famous analogy: "As you see things, walking at the same time, so God sees all things in the present of eternity... just as he sees that you are walking and sitting at the same time, so also he sees that the future things which have not yet happened will happen, and this happens without any determination" (Boethius, *Consolation* V.6.15–17, trans. Walsh 1999: 104–105, adapted).

The analogy of sight is important. When I see you walking, my perception does not *cause* or *necessitate* your walking; I am simply observing what you are doing. Similarly, God's eternal observation of your future actions does not cause or necessitate them; God is simply observing what you freely do — the only difference being that God's observation is atemporal.

## Does the Solution Work?

Boethius's solution is elegant, but philosophers have debated whether it is ultimately successful. The main challenge is what contemporary philosophers call the problem of "the fixity of the past" in a different guise.

If God's eternal vision includes seeing that you will freely do A tomorrow, and if God's eternal vision is (timelessly) necessary — if it is a necessary truth that God's vision contains what it contains — then it seems that it is necessarily true that you will do A. And if it is necessarily true that you will do A, then it is not possible for you to do otherwise, which means your action is not free.

The Boethian response would be: God's eternal vision does not make your action necessary; it makes the truth about your action present (eternally). What is eternally true is not thereby necessarily true in the sense that would undermine freedom. God's vision is contingent on what you freely do: if you had freely done otherwise, God's vision would have eternally seen that. The conditional "If you do A, God eternally sees you doing A" does not make A necessary; it merely records what happens.

This response has been extensively debated. Stump and Kretzmann (1981) defend a sophisticated version of the Boethian response, arguing that the concept of "eternity-time simultaneity" (ET-simultaneity) can be given a coherent technical definition that allows God to perceive all temporal events without thereby making them necessary. Critics, including Wolterstorff (1975) and Pike (1965), argue that the response fails because even an eternal perception of a future free action requires that the action was going to occur — which seems to undermine the genuine openness of the future that freedom requires.

## The Deeper Issue: Temporal Ontology and Freedom

The debate about foreknowledge and freedom is not merely theological; it raises deep questions about temporal ontology. The problem only arises if we accept two claims: (1) that God's knowledge of the future is fixed and cannot be changed; and (2) that freedom requires that the future is genuinely open — that it was genuinely possible for things to be otherwise.

These two claims presuppose different pictures of the ontological status of the future. If the future is "already there" — if future events already exist in some sense, even if not yet from our perspective — then freedom requires that even existing future events were "up to us." If the future is genuinely open — not yet determined, not yet existing — then the fixity of God's knowledge (if it extends to genuinely future events) seems to conflict with that openness.

The debate thus connects to the A-theory/B-theory debate in contemporary philosophy of time. On a B-theory (the "block universe," in which all times are equally real), the future is already there; on an A-theory (in which the future is genuinely not yet), the future is genuinely open. The foreknowledge problem is acutest for A-theorists, because their commitment to the genuinely open future conflicts most directly with the claim that God's knowledge is fixed (Pike 1965: 34–35).
