# Section 9.1: Boethius

---

## Section Introduction

Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (c. 480–524 CE) occupies a unique position in Western intellectual history. A Roman statesman and philosopher who served under the Ostrogothic king Theodoric, he was later imprisoned on charges of treason and executed. While awaiting execution, he wrote *The Consolation of Philosophy* — one of the most widely read books of the Middle Ages — in which Lady Philosophy appears to him in prison and argues, among other things, for the compatibility of divine providence with human freedom and the proper attitude toward fortune's reversals.

The *Consolation* is philosophical prose interspersed with verse, and it is genuinely philosophical: Boethius does not merely appeal to religious consolation but argues carefully. His analysis of eternity and foreknowledge in Book V, prose 6, is one of the most important philosophical texts of the early medieval period and was quoted, debated, and elaborated for the next thousand years.

Two closely related topics are treated in this section: Boethius's definition of eternity (9.1.1) and his response to the problem of divine foreknowledge and free will (9.1.2).
