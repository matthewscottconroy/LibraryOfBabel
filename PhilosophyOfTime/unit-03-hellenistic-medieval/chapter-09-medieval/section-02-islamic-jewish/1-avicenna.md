# 9.2.1 — Avicenna (*Ibn Sina*) on Time

---

## Avicenna and the *Shifa'*

Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Abd Allah ibn Sina (c. 980–1037 CE) — known in the Latin West as Avicenna — was the most influential philosopher and physician of the Islamic world. His encyclopedic *Kitab al-Shifa'* (Book of Healing) is not a medical text but a philosophical encyclopedia covering logic, natural philosophy, mathematics, and metaphysics. The section on natural philosophy (*al-Tabi'iyyat*) includes Book IV, which deals with time, and represents the most sophisticated Arabic engagement with Aristotle's physics up to that point.

Avicenna's treatment of time is Aristotelian in its basic framework but introduces important refinements. For Aristotle, time is the number of motion with respect to before and after; for Avicenna, time is the measure of motion with respect to the "before and after" of a continuous, potentially infinite magnitude. But Avicenna's version of Aristotelianism is not mere commentary; it is a creative synthesis that draws on Neoplatonist elements and develops distinctively Islamic philosophical themes.

## Time as the Measure of the Celestial Motion

Like Aristotle, Avicenna holds that time is fundamentally tied to motion — specifically to the motion of the celestial sphere (Avicenna, *Shifa'*, Physics IV.1–2; see McGinnis 2010: 197–200). The celestial sphere is the appropriate reference for time because its motion is uniform, continuous, and universal: everything that occurs in the sublunary world is encompassed by the celestial motion. This is the cosmic clock against which all other durations are measured.

Avicenna is careful to distinguish between the continuous motion of the celestial sphere and time itself. Time is not identical with the motion; it is the *quantity* of that motion — the amount of it, measured by a before-and-after relation. This distinction anticipates concerns in the philosophy of physics about the relationship between time and the physical processes used to measure it: the measuring process and the thing measured are different.

## The Necessary Being and Timelessness

Avicenna's most original contribution to the philosophy of time comes from his distinctive metaphysics. Avicenna distinguishes between *possible* beings (whose existence is not necessary — they might or might not exist) and the *Necessary Being* (*Wajib al-Wujud*), whose existence is necessary — it cannot not exist. God, for Avicenna, is the Necessary Being, the ultimate ground of all contingent existence.

The Necessary Being is timeless — not in the sense of sempiternal (existing at all times) but in the more radical sense that temporal categories do not apply to it. The Necessary Being is beyond the categories of before and after, earlier and later; it exists in a mode that is simply not temporal. This is structurally analogous to Plotinus's One (which is beyond time) and to the Boethian eternal God. Avicenna's philosophical framework thus supports a genuine divine timelessness that is not merely a theological assertion but an implication of his metaphysical system (Davidson 1987: 74–78).

## The Possibility of Eternal Creation

One of the most important — and controversial — aspects of Avicenna's account is his view that the world might be eternal: created by the Necessary Being from eternity, not at some particular moment in time. Avicenna holds that creation from the Necessary Being is a necessary overflow — the world proceeds from God necessarily, not as a result of a free temporal decision. This means the world has always existed, though it has always depended on God for its existence.

This was a controversial position in Islamic theology, and it was among the views criticized by al-Ghazali in his *Incoherence of the Philosophers* (Tahafut al-Falasifa). Al-Ghazali argued that the philosophers (including Avicenna) were wrong to hold that the world is eternal: this contradicts Islamic teaching about creation. Averroes replied to al-Ghazali in his *Incoherence of the Incoherence* (Tahafut al-Tahafut), defending the compatibility of eternal creation with proper Islamic theology. This debate about eternal creation becomes one of the central philosophical-theological controversies of the medieval period and directly shapes the Latin debates examined in Section 9.5.
