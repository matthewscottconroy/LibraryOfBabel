# 9.2.2 — Averroes and Maimonides on Time and Eternity

---

## Averroes: The Commentator

Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Rushd (1126–1198 CE) — known in the Latin West as Averroes — is called "the Commentator" by Aquinas and the scholastics, because his commentaries on Aristotle's works were taken to be definitive. He wrote "lesser," "middle," and "greater" commentaries on virtually all of Aristotle's works, and his commentaries on the *Physics* and *Metaphysics* were translated into Latin and became standard reference works in the thirteenth century.

On the philosophy of time, Averroes's most important contribution is his refinement of Aristotle's definition. For Averroes, time is not simply "the number of motion" — this definition is too imprecise. Aristotle's claim must be carefully interpreted: time is the number (measure) of the continuous motion of the outermost celestial sphere, taken as a whole (*Physics* commentary, book IV; see Taylor 2000: 192–193). The reason for specifying the outermost celestial sphere is that it is the most fundamental and encompassing motion; all other motions in the cosmos are "within" this motion and measured by it.

Averroes also clarifies the relationship between the continuity of motion and the continuity of time. Time is continuous — not a series of discrete instants — because the motion it measures is continuous. This is a Aristotelian point, but Averroes develops it with greater care, arguing that the continuity of time is derived from the continuity of the celestial motion, which in turn derives from the continuity of space.

## The Eternal World

Averroes was the most forthright of the Aristotelian commentators in defending Aristotle's position that the world is eternal — that it has no beginning and no end. This was not a position adopted uncritically; Averroes argued for it on systematic philosophical grounds. The reasons he gave were: (1) Motion and time are eternal, because their opposite (an absolute beginning or end of motion) would itself require explanation in terms of a prior or posterior change, generating a regress. (2) The Aristotelian concept of creation requires a pre-existing matter to be formed; creation from nothing (*ex nihilo*) is philosophically incoherent. (3) The concept of a "first moment" of time is incoherent, because any moment can be preceded by another.

These arguments were directly engaged by Aquinas (who accepted some and rejected others) and by later scholastic thinkers. Averroes's defense of the eternal world is philosophically serious and represents a genuine alternative to the theological doctrine of creation, even for those who ultimately rejected it.

## Maimonides and the *Guide for the Perplexed*

Moses ben Maimon (1138–1204 CE) — Maimonides, or RaMBaM in the Jewish tradition — was the most important Jewish philosopher of the medieval period. Born in Córdoba, he spent most of his adult life in Egypt, where he was a physician and the chief rabbi of the Jewish community. His *Guide for the Perplexed* (*Moreh Nevukhim*, c. 1190) is a philosophical masterpiece: it addresses the educated Jew who has studied philosophy (primarily Aristotle, mediated through the Islamic tradition) and is troubled by apparent conflicts between philosophical reason and the Torah.

On the question of time, Maimonides is principally concerned with the eternity of the world. He argues — in a position that surprised some contemporary readers — that neither the eternity of the world nor its creation in time can be *demonstrated* by reason alone. Both positions face philosophical difficulties; neither can be proved. Maimonides's argument is not agnosticism but a principled account of the limits of metaphysical demonstration.

If reason cannot settle the question, Maimonides argues that we should follow the teaching of the Torah: the world was created in time. But — and this is important — he does not mean that the Torah settles what reason cannot. Rather, he believes that the Torah's account of creation is the most plausible interpretation of the evidence once we recognize that the philosophical arguments for eternal creation are not demonstrative (Maimonides, *Guide* II.13–25; see Seeskin 2000: 49–53).

On the nature of divine eternity, Maimonides is a rigorous defender of negative theology: we cannot say positively what God is, only what God is not. We cannot say that God is eternal in the sense of possessing some positive attribute called "eternity"; we can only say that God is not temporal — not subject to the before-and-after structure that characterizes created things. This apophatic approach to divine eternity is importantly different from the positive theological accounts of Boethius and Aquinas, and it represents a third major approach to divine timelessness.
