# 9.3.1 — Aquinas's Three Modes of Duration: *Tempus*, *Aevum*, *Aeternitas*

---

## The Problem of Gradations of Temporality

The Boethian framework offers two modes of temporal existence: time (for creatures subject to change) and eternity (for God). But this binary faces an immediate theological problem: what about angels? Angels are spiritual beings that are not subject to the kind of material change that characterizes earthly creatures (they do not grow old, get sick, or die), but they are not eternal in the way God is — they are created beings whose existence has a beginning and whose activities involve successive operations.

Aquinas addresses this problem by introducing a third mode of duration: the *aevum* (sometimes translated "aeon" or "aeveternity"). The *aevum* is the mode of duration appropriate to beings that are not fully temporal but not fully eternal either. In doing so, he creates a three-tier ontology of duration: *tempus* (ordinary time), *aevum* (angelic duration), and *aeternitas* (divine eternity) (*Summa Theologiae* I, Q.10, art. 1–6).

## *Tempus*: Ordinary Time

Ordinary time (*tempus*) is the mode of duration of creatures subject to change — that is, material creatures, including human beings. Time, following Aristotle, is the measure of motion; it is the framework within which material change occurs. Creatures in *tempus* change in all their substantial properties: they come into being, grow, change, decay, and pass away. Their existence is thoroughly temporal: it has a beginning, it unfolds in successive moments, and for most creatures it has an end.

Aquinas accepts the Aristotelian analysis of *tempus* as the measure of motion but enriches it with Augustine's insight that the soul is required to make temporal measurement possible. In Aquinas's synthesis, objective time — the temporal order of the physical world — is grounded in the motion of the heavens, but this objective time is only measured by a soul that can count the before-and-after of that motion (*Summa Theologiae* I, Q.10, a.1, resp.).

## *Aevum*: Angelic Duration

The *aevum* is the most original element of Aquinas's three-tier account. An "aeveternal" being is one whose existence does not change in its substantial being — it does not undergo essential change, corruption, or growth — but whose activities are nevertheless successive. An angel does not change what it fundamentally *is* over time, but it does successive activities: it understands one thing, then another; it acts in one moment, then another. This succession of activities puts the angel in a kind of time — but it is not the same as ordinary temporal succession, because the angel's essential being is unchanging (Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, Q.10, a.5, resp.).

The *aevum* is thus intermediate between *tempus* and *aeternitas*: less temporal than ordinary creatures (because the aeveternal being does not undergo substantial change), but more temporal than God (because its activities are successive). Aquinas is careful to distinguish: the *aevum* is not simply a very long time; it is a qualitatively different mode of duration, appropriate to beings with a different kind of existence.

## *Aeternitas*: Divine Eternity

Divine eternity is, for Aquinas, precisely what Boethius defined: the complete, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unlimited life. Aquinas accepts the Boethian definition as correct and provides arguments for why it is the appropriate description of God's mode of being.

The key argument is from God's simplicity and immutability. God is absolutely simple — there are no real distinctions in God, no parts, no composition. And God is absolutely immutable — God does not change in any respect. From immutability, Aquinas argues that God has no before or after in the sense of real temporal succession: a God who changes would have different properties at different times, implying temporal distinctions within the divine being. Since God is immutable, God has no such distinctions: God's being is all at once, without temporal parts.

This argument connects divine timelessness to divine simplicity — a connection that is important in Aquinas's overall theology. The attribute of timelessness is not an independent claim but follows from more fundamental attributes (simplicity, immutability) that Aquinas argues for on metaphysical grounds (*Summa Theologiae* I, Q.10, a.2, resp.).

## The Philosophical Significance of the Three-Tier Account

Aquinas's three-tier account of duration has several philosophical virtues.

**It accommodates intermediate cases.** The binary of "time vs. eternity" is inadequate to the variety of beings that a rich ontology must recognize. By introducing the *aevum*, Aquinas creates conceptual space for beings that are neither wholly temporal nor wholly atemporal — a space that is useful not only for theology but for any account of gradations of temporality. (Contemporary discussions of "event time" vs. "state time" in cognitive science and linguistics have structural analogies.)

**It distinguishes modes of change.** The three tiers correspond to three modes of susceptibility to change: creatures in *tempus* change substantially; creatures in *aevum* are substantively unchanging but operationally successive; the eternal being is unchanging in every respect. This makes the temporal framework a function of the being's nature rather than an external framework imposed from outside.

**It articulates what divine timelessness means.** By situating divine eternity at one extreme of a spectrum, Aquinas's account makes clear what is being denied: not just that God's existence is very long, but that temporal categories (before, after, earlier, later) simply do not apply to God. This is more informative than a bare assertion of timelessness.
