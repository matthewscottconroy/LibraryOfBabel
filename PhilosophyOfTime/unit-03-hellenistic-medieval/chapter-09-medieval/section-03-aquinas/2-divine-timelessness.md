# 9.3.2 — Divine Timelessness and Its Problems

---

## Aquinas's Defense

Aquinas defends divine timelessness on the grounds that God is both simple (without real internal distinctions) and immutable (without any change). A being that genuinely changes must change from one state to another — it must be different at different times, which implies a real before-after distinction within the being's history. Since God is immutable, God has no such history; there is no before or after *within* God. And since God is timeless, God's existence is not parceled out across a temporal framework but is wholly and completely present in a single atemporal act.

Aquinas follows Boethius in describing this eternal presence as God's "seeing" of all times simultaneously. God does not foreknow the future; God knows all temporal events in a single eternal act of knowledge that is simultaneous (in a special, non-temporal sense) with all the events it knows (*Summa Theologiae* I, Q.14, a.13, resp.). This is the same framework that Boethius used to respond to the foreknowledge problem.

## Can a Timeless God Act in Time?

The most serious philosophical problem for divine timelessness is the question of divine action in time. If God is genuinely outside time — if temporal categories do not apply to God — then it seems that God cannot do anything *at* a time. God cannot *now* create the world, or *then* answer a prayer, or *in response to* some event cause another event. All these descriptions of divine action use temporal vocabulary. If God is timeless, none of them literally applies.

Aquinas's response is that these temporal descriptions describe the *effects* of God's action in time, not the action itself. God's creative act is eternal — it is not performed at any time but in a single atemporal act. What is temporal is the *result* of that act: the created world, which exists in time and which was (temporally) brought into being. The description "God created the world at the beginning of time" describes a temporal fact about the world (its beginning), not a temporal fact about God's act.

This response is ingenious but faces a difficulty. If God's creative act is timeless, then it is not something God *decided* to do at some point — decisions are temporal events. But then what explains God's choice to create rather than not create? Aquinas's answer is that God's will to create is identical with God's essence (a consequence of divine simplicity), not a separate event or decision. Whether this is coherent — whether the concept of "willing" something can be stripped of all temporal connotations — remains debated (Wolterstorff 1975: 186–193).

## Can a Timeless God Know Temporal Truths?

A related problem concerns temporal knowledge. If God is timeless, can God know facts expressed in tensed language? Can a timeless God know that "it is now raining in London"? The truth value of this sentence changes over time: it is true now but will be false in ten minutes. But a timeless God cannot know something that was not true ten minutes ago: God's timeless knowledge cannot change.

This problem was developed with force by Nicholas Wolterstorff (1975) and has generated substantial literature. Defenders of divine timelessness typically respond by arguing that tensed truths are reducible to tenseless truths (God knows the tenseless fact "rain-in-London at t₁" rather than the tensed fact "it is now raining in London") — but critics argue that this response eliminates something important about temporal knowledge that God, as omniscient, must have.

## The Alternative: Divine Sempiternity

The problems with divine timelessness have led some philosophers and theologians to prefer *sempiternity* — the view that God exists at all times without having a beginning or end but is nevertheless genuinely *in* time. This view, defended by Nicholas Wolterstorff and (in a more nuanced form) by Richard Swinburne, avoids the problems of timeless action and timeless knowledge but faces its own difficulties: a sempiternal God who is "in" time faces the problem that what God knows at one time may differ from what God knows at another (if the future is genuinely open), which seems to compromise omniscience.

Aquinas himself was aware of some of these difficulties and addressed them in his account of divine foreknowledge. His position remains the most sophisticated defense of divine timelessness before the contemporary period, and the objections to it are not obviously decisive. The debate is live and unresolved.
