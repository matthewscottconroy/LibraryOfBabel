# 9.4.1 — Ockham's Nominalism Applied to Time

---

## The Nominalist Challenge to Realism

William of Ockham's nominalism begins from a simple but far-reaching principle: we should not posit entities beyond necessity (the famous "Ockham's Razor," or the principle of parsimony: *entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*). If we can explain everything we need to explain without positing abstract, universal entities — if all our explanations can be cast in terms of individual things and their relations — then abstract entities are ontological luxuries we should dispense with.

Applied to time, this principle generates a challenge to any account that treats time as an entity in its own right — something that exists over and above the temporal things that are in it. Does time *exist* as a thing, or is it merely a name for certain relations among individual things?

## Time as Not a Real Entity

Ockham's answer is that time is not a real entity distinct from the things that are in time. It is a "connotative term" — a term that picks out individual things but connotates (implicitly refers to) their relations and properties. When we say "the battle occurred two hours ago," we are not referring to a thing called "two hours" and reporting that the battle was located in it. We are saying something about the battle, about now, and about the relationship between them — a relationship that can be fully understood without positing any abstract temporal entity.

This is a form of temporal nominalism or relationism. Time, on Ockham's account, does not exist as an independent substance, property, or Platonic abstract entity. It is a way of talking about the order and duration of individual events, things, and processes — a convenient shorthand for structural features of the world that are themselves constituted by individual things and their relations (Ockham, *Quodlibetal Questions* IV.25; see Leftow 1991: 36–38).

What does *exist*, for Ockham? Individual substances and their qualities. A particular horse exists; the individual motion of that horse exists (as an accident of the horse); the temporal relations between individual events exist (as relations among individuals). What does not exist, as a further entity over and above these, is "time itself" — the abstract temporal framework.

## Implications for the Ontology of Time

Ockham's nominalism has several important implications for the ontology of time.

**Against absolute time**: If time is not a real entity distinct from temporal things and events, then there is no absolute temporal container. There is no "empty time" — time in which nothing is happening. This parallels the Epicurean account of time as an accident of accidents, though Ockham arrives at the position from a different direction (nominalism rather than atomism).

**For temporal relationism**: Ockham's position anticipates the relational account of time that Leibniz will develop in the seventeenth century (Chapter 10). Time is the order of non-simultaneous events, not an entity that exists independently of those events.

**Against reified tenses**: Ockham is skeptical of accounts that treat the past, present, and future as distinct kinds of entity with distinctive modes of being. The Stoic distinction between existence (present) and subsistence (past, future) would be too metaphysically generous for Ockham: if we can describe temporal facts without positing entities that subsist rather than exist, then we should.

## Ockham's Razor and the Measure of Time

A practical implication of Ockham's approach: we should not say that there is a single, unified cosmic time in which all events are located. There are individual events with individual durations and individual temporal relations between them. Whether these individual temporal relations form a single coherent framework — whether there is a global time for the cosmos — is an empirical question, not something guaranteed by the metaphysics of time.

This anticipates, in a remarkably prescient way, the challenges to global time in modern physics. Einstein's special theory of relativity shows that there is no single global time; simultaneity is relative to reference frame, and the temporal order of events can differ between observers in relative motion. Ockham's nominalism, which resists the reification of time as a single global entity, is more compatible with this physical picture than Aquinas's Aristotelian realism about cosmic time.
