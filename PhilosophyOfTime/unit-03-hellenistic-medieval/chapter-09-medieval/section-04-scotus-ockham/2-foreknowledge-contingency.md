# 9.4.2 — Ockham on Foreknowledge and the Hard/Soft Fact Distinction

---

## The Problem of the Fixity of the Past

Ockham's most influential contribution to the problem of divine foreknowledge and freedom is his distinction between "hard" and "soft" (or "merely apparent") facts about the past. This distinction has been enormously influential in contemporary analytic philosophy and remains a live option in discussions of freedom and foreknowledge.

The problem Ockham is addressing can be stated precisely. It is standardly agreed that the past is fixed — we cannot change what has already happened. If Caesar crossed the Rubicon, that fact is now unalterable; nothing anyone does can make it the case that Caesar did not cross the Rubicon. Call this the "fixity of the past." Now consider the following fact: "God knew, five hundred years ago, that you would eat breakfast tomorrow." If God has foreknowledge, this is a fact about five hundred years ago. And since it is a past fact, it is fixed — unalterable. But if it is unalterable that God knew five hundred years ago that you would eat breakfast tomorrow, then it seems that you are not free to skip breakfast: your doing so would make it false that God knew you would eat breakfast, which would require altering the past.

This is the "Consequence Argument" for the incompatibility of foreknowledge and freedom. It relies crucially on the claim that the fact about God's past knowledge is genuinely fixed.

## Hard and Soft Facts

Ockham's response is to challenge the claim that all past facts are equally fixed. He distinguishes between two kinds of facts about the past:

**Hard facts**: Facts that are genuinely and entirely about the past — facts about what happened, what was the case, what was true — without any logical dependence on the future. "Caesar crossed the Rubicon on January 10, 49 BCE" is a hard past fact: it is about a specific past event and its truth does not depend on anything that happens after that event.

**Soft facts**: Facts that are nominally about the past but whose truth depends on (involves logical entailment with) some fact about the future. "God knew, five hundred years ago, that you would eat breakfast tomorrow" is a soft past fact: its truth depends on a future event (your eating breakfast). If you skip breakfast, this fact about five hundred years ago is false; if you eat breakfast, it is true. The "pastness" of the fact is genuine, but the fact's content involves the future.

Ockham's claim is that only hard past facts are fixed. Soft past facts — including divine foreknowledge — are not fixed in the relevant sense: their truth depends on what happens in the future, and since the future is (in some sense) open, so is the truth of soft past facts (Ockham, *Tractatus de praedestinatione*, Q.1; see Plantinga 1986: 238–240).

If this is right, then the fact "God knew five hundred years ago that you would eat breakfast" is not a fixed fact that constrains your freedom. It is a soft fact whose truth depends on what you do. If you freely choose to eat breakfast, God knew five hundred years ago that you would; if you freely choose to skip it, God knew five hundred years ago that you would skip it. God's knowledge "tracks" your free choices rather than determining them.

## Assessment of the Hard/Soft Distinction

The hard/soft distinction is clever and has attracted significant philosophical attention. Its defenders argue that it correctly identifies a category of past facts that are not genuinely fixed in the relevant sense, and that divine foreknowledge falls into this category. Its critics argue that the distinction is either (1) incoherent — all past facts are equally fixed once they are past — or (2) question-begging — it assumes rather than proves that foreknowledge does not determine future actions.

Plantinga (1986) defends the Ockhamist approach and argues that it is the most promising response to the foreknowledge problem. Fischer (1989) argues that the distinction is inadequately specified and that it is difficult to give a principled criterion for what makes a fact "merely soft." Flint (1998) defends a more developed version of Ockhamism using the Molinist framework of middle knowledge.

The hard/soft distinction also connects to questions about the nature of the past more generally: are all past facts equally "fixed"? There are cases outside of theology where the question arises. If a mathematical truth is proved tomorrow, does that make it now true that the theorem was provable yesterday? These questions about the logic of temporal facts are philosophically interesting independent of their theological application.

## The Influence on Subsequent Discussion

Ockham's distinction between hard and soft facts became an important tool in late medieval and early modern discussions of predestination, providence, and freedom. It was adapted and extended by later scholastics and eventually entered analytic philosophy through the work of Plantinga, Fischer, and others in the late twentieth century. The contemporary literature on "Ockhamism" in philosophy of religion is one of the more technically sophisticated areas of analytic philosophy, and it has connections both to formal logic and to the metaphysics of time.
