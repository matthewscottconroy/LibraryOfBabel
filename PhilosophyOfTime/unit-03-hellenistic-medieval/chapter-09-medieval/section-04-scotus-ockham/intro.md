# Section 9.4: Scotus and Ockham

---

## Section Introduction

The late medieval period saw a significant philosophical development that had profound implications for the ontology of time and the problem of foreknowledge: the rise of nominalism. Nominalism — the view that only individual things exist, and that general terms ("universals") are merely names that we apply to groups of similar things, not designations of real entities — challenged the Aristotelian-Thomistic realism that had dominated scholastic philosophy.

John Duns Scotus (c. 1266–1308) and William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347) are the two most important figures of late scholasticism for the philosophy of time. Scotus made important contributions to the logic of contingency and possibility, while Ockham's nominalism had radical implications for the ontology of time: if universals are not real, then time itself — as a universal or abstract entity — might not be real in the way Aristotle and Aquinas had supposed. Ockham also made a famous and important contribution to the debate about divine foreknowledge and freedom through his distinction between hard and soft facts about the past.
