# 9.5.1 — The Medieval Debate Over the Eternity of the World

---

## Aristotle's Arguments for Eternal Motion

Aristotle's argument for the eternity of the world, given in *Physics* VIII.1 and *De Caelo* I.10–12, is a philosophical argument from the nature of motion and time. The core argument runs: (1) If there was a first motion, there was a time before the first motion when there was no motion. (2) But if there was a "before" the first motion, then the world was in some state before the first motion — a state of rest, presumably. (3) But rest is the absence of motion; it cannot occur except in a world capable of motion. Therefore, even the "rest" before the first motion requires a prior world capable of motion. (4) This generates a regress: there is always a prior state of the world, and therefore no absolute first motion.

A parallel argument applies to time: if time had a beginning, there was a "before" the beginning of time. But "before" is a temporal concept; to speak of a "before" the beginning of time is incoherent. Therefore, time must be eternal — it cannot have a beginning.

These arguments are genuinely powerful, and they were taken seriously by medieval thinkers even when they ultimately rejected the conclusion.

## Aquinas's Position: Reason Cannot Decide

Aquinas's position on the eternity of the world is philosophically nuanced and deserves careful attention. He argues, in *On the Eternity of the World* (*De Aeternitate Mundi*, c. 1270–1271) and in *Summa Theologiae* I, Q.46, that *reason alone cannot demonstrate that the world had a beginning*, but also that *reason alone cannot demonstrate that the world did not have a beginning*. Both the eternalist position (Aristotle's) and the creationist position (the theologians') can be maintained without logical contradiction; neither can be conclusively demonstrated from purely philosophical premises.

This is a significant concession to Aristotle. Aquinas is not claiming that the eternalist argument fails; he is claiming that it does not succeed either. The philosophical arguments on both sides are genuinely inconclusive. Given this philosophical underdetermination, we should follow the teaching of Scripture: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" — which tells us that the world had a beginning, even though reason alone cannot establish this (Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, Q.46, a.2, resp.).

## Why This Position Matters Philosophically

Aquinas's position is philosophically important for several reasons.

**First, it is intellectually honest.** Aquinas does not pretend that the philosophical arguments against eternal creation are weak when they are strong. He engages Aristotle's arguments seriously and acknowledges their force. His conclusion — that they are not demonstrative — is a philosophical judgment, not a dismissal.

**Second, it establishes a principled division between philosophy and theology.** Questions that reason can settle are settled by reason; questions that reason cannot settle are deferred to revelation. This is not intellectual laziness; it is a carefully articulated account of the complementary roles of reason and faith. The question of the world's temporal beginning is one of those questions that fall in the middle: not clearly resolvable by reason, not clearly beyond reason, but ultimately determined by revelation.

**Third, it opens philosophical space.** If the eternity of the world cannot be demonstrated or refuted by reason, then the question remains philosophically open. This means that new philosophical arguments for or against eternal creation are possible and welcome. The history of subsequent philosophy and science — culminating in contemporary cosmology and the question of whether the Big Bang constitutes a genuine temporal beginning — is a continuation of this medieval debate.

## The Condemnations of 1277 and Their Implications

In 1277, Étienne Tempier, the Bishop of Paris, issued a list of 219 propositions that were condemned as heretical. Among them were several Aristotelian-Averroist positions, including the eternity of the world and the impossibility of creation from nothing. These condemnations were a direct response to the influence of Aristotelian natural philosophy at the University of Paris, particularly in the form mediated by Averroes.

The condemnations are philosophically significant because they forced a distinction between what Aristotle had argued and what Christian philosophy must maintain. By condemning certain Aristotelian positions as heretical, the condemnations created pressure to develop genuinely non-Aristotelian natural philosophy — to think about nature, time, and motion without assuming that Aristotle had the last word. Some historians of science (notably Pierre Duhem) have argued that the 1277 condemnations were a necessary precondition for the development of modern science, because they broke the grip of Aristotelian physics and created space for alternative accounts (Grant 1979: 211–214).

Whether or not this historical thesis is correct, the condemnations were certainly a turning point in the relationship between theology and natural philosophy — and they directly shaped the context in which early modern thinkers like Descartes, Galileo, and Newton developed their revolutionary accounts of time and motion.

## Contemporary Resonances

The medieval debate about the eternity of the world has direct resonances with contemporary cosmology. The standard model of cosmology (the Big Bang theory) posits that the universe had a temporal beginning approximately 13.8 billion years ago. Does this mean that the question Augustine raised — what was there before the beginning? — is settled?

The answer is complicated. Some physical models suggest that time itself began with the Big Bang, in which case the question "what was before the Big Bang?" makes no more sense than "what was God doing before creation?" — both questions contain a false temporal presupposition. Other models (eternal inflation, cyclic cosmology, the no-boundary proposal of Hawking and Hartle) suggest that the Big Bang was not an absolute beginning of time but a transition from an earlier phase of the universe. If these models are correct, then the medieval eternalists were right in spirit, even if the cosmology is entirely different from Aristotle's (Craig and Smith 1993: chap. 1; Hawking and Penrose 1996: 19–25).

The deep philosophical point is that the question of whether time had a beginning is not settled by physics alone. Physics can tell us that the observable universe had a certain kind of beginning at a certain time. Whether this constitutes a genuine absolute beginning of time — whether there was genuinely no "before" — is a philosophical question about the nature of time that physics, by itself, cannot answer.
