# Unit III: Hellenistic, Late Ancient, and Medieval Philosophy of Time

---

## Unit Introduction

Philosophy does not move in a straight line. It spirals — returning to the same questions but from greater depth, carrying more equipment, pressing harder against the places where earlier thinkers faltered. Aristotle gave us a systematic account of time that dominated ancient thought. But Aristotle's account, brilliant as it was, left questions unanswered: What is the nature of the present moment? Does time depend on the mind or on the world? What happens to time at the boundaries of creation — if there are such boundaries? What is the relationship between time and eternity? The four centuries following Aristotle produced a series of attempts to answer these questions, attempts that were not mere commentaries on the master but genuine philosophical innovations.

The story of this unit runs from the Hellenistic schools (Chapter 6) through Plotinus (Chapter 7) and Augustine (Chapter 8) to the rich medieval tradition that culminated in Aquinas, Scotus, and Ockham (Chapter 9). These thinkers were separated by centuries and by language — Greek, Latin, Arabic, Hebrew — and they inhabited vastly different intellectual and theological frameworks. What unites them is a shared seriousness about the problem of time: not as a footnote to other concerns but as a question that forced itself onto their attention and demanded genuine answers.

The Stoics (Chapter 6) brought to the question a metaphysical framework unlike anything in Plato or Aristotle. Time, for the Stoics, was one of four "incorporeals" — real but not corporeal, subsisting rather than fully existing. Chrysippus pressed the analysis of the present moment further than anyone had before, arriving at a position that is startling in its radicalism: the present, strictly understood, is not a thing at all but a knife-edge between two modes of non-being. The Epicureans took a different route, treating time as an "accident of accidents" — a second-order property that arises from events without being a thing in its own right. And Lucretius, the great Epicurean poet, gave us in the process one of the most beautiful and philosophically serious arguments in the ancient world: the symmetry argument, which asks why we fear death when we do not fear the prenatal non-existence that precedes it.

Plotinus (Chapter 7) represents a different kind of genius. Working within the Platonist tradition but transforming it beyond recognition, he developed a three-tiered ontology — the One, Intellect, and Soul — and located time precisely at the level of Soul. Time is not, for Plotinus, a feature of the physical world that the mind then measures. It is generated by the Soul itself, as the Soul moves restlessly through its activities rather than resting in the unified, simultaneous grasp that characterizes Intellect. This is a deeply interior account of time — one that anticipates Augustine in striking ways.

Augustine (Chapter 8) is the towering figure of this unit, and arguably the most important philosopher of time before Kant. His account in Book XI of the *Confessions* is not a philosophical treatise in the technical sense; it is a meditation on creation, eternity, and the nature of God that discovers, along the way, a set of insights about time that have never been superseded. Time, for Augustine, exists in the soul as *distentio animi* — the stretching of the soul between memory and expectation. The mind measures time not by measuring the world but by measuring itself. This is phenomenology before there was a word for it.

The medieval thinkers (Chapter 9) inherited this rich tradition and added their own framework: the demands of Christian, Islamic, and Jewish theology. Questions about divine eternity, the beginning of the world, the compatibility of foreknowledge and freedom, and the ontological status of created time were not merely academic exercises. They were matters of theological urgency, which is one reason why medieval philosophy of time is both unusually precise and unusually ambitious.

Throughout this unit, a single thread runs: the growing recognition that time cannot be understood in isolation from mind. Whether mind is the generator of time (Plotinus, Augustine), the measurer of time (Aristotle, carried forward), or the interpreter of a mind-independent temporal structure (the Stoics), the relationship between temporality and consciousness moves ever closer to the center of the philosophical picture. By the end of Unit III, you will be prepared to see why that relationship became the central philosophical problem of time in the modern and contemporary periods.
