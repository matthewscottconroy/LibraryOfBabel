## Annotated Suggestions for Further Reading

**Alexander, H. G., ed. *The Leibniz-Clarke Correspondence*. Manchester: Manchester University Press, 1956.**
The primary source for the debate between Leibniz and Newton/Clarke. Alexander provides a helpful introduction and notes. The correspondence is not very long, and every student of the philosophy of time should read the key letters — at minimum, Leibniz's Third Letter and Clarke's Third Reply, plus Leibniz's Fifth Letter and Clarke's Fifth Reply.

**Vailati, Ezio. *Leibniz and Clarke*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1997.**
The most detailed scholarly examination of the correspondence. Vailati reconstructs the arguments of both sides carefully and situates the debate within early modern intellectual history. Indispensable for advanced study.

**Earman, John. *World Enough and Space-Time*. MIT Press, 1989.**
The most thorough contemporary treatment of the absolute-relational debate in philosophy of physics. Chapter 1 covers the Newtonian and Leibnizian positions; later chapters examine how the debate plays out in special and general relativity. Demanding but rewarding.

**Della Rocca, Michael. *Spinoza*. Routledge, 2008.**
The best recent introduction to Spinoza's philosophy, with strong emphasis on the role of the PSR. Chapters on modes, attributes, and the three kinds of knowledge are directly relevant to the philosophy of time material. Philosophically rigorous and accessible.

**Leibniz, G. W. *New Essays on Human Understanding* II.14. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1996.**
Leibniz's critique of Locke, organized around Locke's *Essay*. Book II, chapter 14 deals explicitly with time and space. The *New Essays* is in dialogue form, which makes it more readable than some of Leibniz's other works. Essential for understanding Leibniz's relational theory.

**Spinoza, Baruch. *Ethics*, Part II and Part V. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1994.**
The primary source for Spinoza's account of time, eternity, and the kinds of knowledge. Part II, proposition 44 and its corollaries are the key passages for time as imagination; Part V, propositions 29–32 are the key passages for *sub specie aeternitatis*. Reading these alongside Curley's notes is highly recommended.

**Gorham, Geoffrey. "Descartes on Time and Duration." *Early Science and Medicine* 12 (2007).**
A careful scholarly article that provides the best available analysis of Descartes's philosophy of time. Accessible to advanced undergraduates. Useful for Exercise 10.9.

**Barbour, Julian. *The End of Time*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1999.**
A provocative and philosophically rich book arguing that time is not fundamental to physical reality. Barbour is a physicist as well as a philosopher, and his arguments are grounded in quantum gravity. Chapters 2–3 on Mach and the relational tradition are directly relevant to this chapter. Highly readable.

**Pooley, Oliver. "Substantivalist and Relationalist Approaches to Spacetime." In *Oxford Handbook of Philosophy of Physics*, 2013.**
The best current review article on the substantivalist-relationist debate. Pooley surveys the debate from Newton and Leibniz through general relativity and quantum gravity. An excellent map of the contemporary philosophical terrain.

**McTaggart, J. M. E. "The Unreality of Time." *Mind* 17 (1908).**
McTaggart's famous argument for the unreality of time anticipates some Spinozan themes and is the starting point for the contemporary A-series/B-series debate (covered elsewhere in this textbook). Reading it alongside the Spinoza material reveals structural connections.
