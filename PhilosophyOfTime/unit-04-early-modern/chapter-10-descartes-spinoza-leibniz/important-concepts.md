# Chapter 10: Important Concepts

---

**Duration as a Mode of Substance**
Descartes's account of what it is for a thing to persist through time: duration is the mode by which any created substance continues to exist. It is a real feature of substances, not a separate entity. Duration is intrinsic to things that persist; time, by contrast, is the abstract measure by which durations are compared.

**Continuous Creation**
Descartes's doctrine that God must re-create the world at every moment: the ontological independence of temporal moments means that a thing's existence at one moment does not entail its existence at the next, so God's continuous creative activity is required for the world to persist. This doctrine has implications for causation (apparent causal connections may be God's regular re-creations) and for temporal continuity (persistence is not intrinsic to things but depends on divine activity).

**The Ontological Independence of Temporal Moments**
Descartes's premise for continuous creation: the parts of time are not mutually dependent — each moment is ontologically self-contained, not connected by any logical necessity to adjacent moments. This is a temporal atomism about ontological dependence: though time may be continuous in extension, each moment requires a separate cause of existence.

**Time as Imagination (Spinoza)**
Spinoza's claim that "time" as ordinarily conceived — as a series of flowing moments, a past that is gone, a present that is now, a future that approaches — is a product of the first kind of knowledge (imagination) rather than adequate rational or intuitive knowledge. Duration (the persistence of finite modes) is real; the "flow" and the perspectival "now" are imaginative additions.

***Sub Specie Aeternitatis***
"Under the aspect of eternity" — Spinoza's characterization of the highest kind of knowledge (third kind, or intuitive knowledge): knowing things in their necessary connection to the whole of reality, stripped of all contingent, temporal, perspectival qualifications. This mode of knowing does not represent things as occurring "now" or "then" but as necessarily occupying their place in the eternal causal order.

**Relational Theory of Time (Leibniz)**
The view that time is not an independently existing container or framework but merely the order of non-simultaneous things: the relational structure constituted by the temporal relations among events. There is no time "over and above" the events and their relations. This position is opposed to Newton's absolute time and is supported by Leibniz's PSR and Identity of Indiscernibles arguments.

**Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR)**
Leibniz's fundamental metaphysical principle: nothing happens without a sufficient reason why it is so rather than otherwise. Leibniz uses the PSR to argue against absolute time: if time is absolute, there would be no sufficient reason why the world occupies its particular temporal location in absolute time rather than any other. Since the PSR forbids unexplained arbitrary facts, absolute time must be rejected.

**Identity of Indiscernibles**
Leibniz's principle that no two numerically distinct things can be intrinsically qualitatively identical. Applied to time: two possible worlds that differ only in their absolute temporal location are intrinsically qualitatively identical and thus — by this principle — the same world. Therefore, absolute temporal location is not a real feature of worlds.

**Absolute Time (Newton/Clarke)**
The view that time is an independently existing framework — a fixed background that would exist even in a universe with no events. Time "flows equably without relation to anything external." Each moment in absolute time is a specific temporal position that events can occupy, and the question of which events occur at which absolute times is a real question with a determinate answer.

**Haecceitism**
The view that there are primitive facts about which particular individual something is — "primitive thisness" — that are not reducible to qualitative properties. Clarke (on Newton's behalf) implicitly appeals to haecceitism in responding to Leibniz's Identity of Indiscernibles argument: two worlds differing only in absolute temporal location differ in *which* particular temporal moments their events occupy, and this is a brute haecceitistic fact.

**Mach's Principle**
Ernst Mach's thesis that the inertial properties of a body are determined by its relations to all the other matter in the universe, not by its relation to absolute space. If true, this would support Leibniz's relational program: inertia can be accounted for relationally, without absolute space or time. Whether general relativity fully implements Mach's Principle is disputed.

**The Mechanics Argument**
The Newtonian argument that absolute space and time are necessary for physics: Newtonian mechanics defines acceleration, inertia, and rotation in terms of reference to absolute space/time, and these physical concepts cannot be adequately defined in purely relational terms. This is the main challenge that Leibniz's relational theory must address.

**The Bucket Argument**
Newton's thought experiment (discussed in Chapter 11) designed to show that rotation is not relative to surrounding matter but to absolute space: spinning water in a bucket climbs the sides regardless of the bucket's rotation relative to surrounding objects. Clarke uses this as evidence for absolute space; Mach counters that the relevant frame is the totality of matter in the universe.

**Relational Spacetime**
The view, held by Leibniz and various modern relationist philosophers of physics, that spacetime is not an entity over and above its contents but consists only of the spatiotemporal relations among events and material objects. On this view, questions about "where" or "when" in absolute terms are meaningless; only questions about spatial and temporal relations among things have real answers.
