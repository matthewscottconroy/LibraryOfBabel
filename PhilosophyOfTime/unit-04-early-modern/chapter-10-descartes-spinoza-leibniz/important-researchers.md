# Chapter 10: Important Researchers

---

**René Descartes** (1596–1650)
French philosopher and mathematician, widely regarded as the founder of modern philosophy. His *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641), *Principles of Philosophy* (1644), and *Discourse on Method* (1637) defined the agenda of early modern philosophy. Descartes's distinction between duration and time and his doctrine of continuous creation are his main contributions to the philosophy of time. His substance dualism — the division of reality into thinking and extended substance — shaped the context in which all subsequent early modern philosophy of time was conducted.

**Baruch Spinoza** (1632–1677)
Dutch philosopher of Portuguese-Jewish descent, excommunicated from the Amsterdam Jewish community in 1656. His *Ethics Demonstrated in a Geometrical Manner* (*Ethica Ordine Geometrico Demonstrata*, completed c. 1675, published posthumously 1677) is the most systematic rationalist philosophical system of the early modern period. Spinoza's account of time as imagination and his concept of *sub specie aeternitatis* are his central contributions to the philosophy of time. His influence on subsequent philosophy has been enormous and continues to grow.

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1646–1716)
German philosopher, mathematician, and diplomat. Leibniz made major contributions to mathematics (he and Newton independently invented calculus), logic, metaphysics, and philosophy of physics. His relational theory of time, developed against Newton in the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence and in the *New Essays*, is one of the most important positions in the philosophy of time. His *Monadology* (1714), *Discourse on Metaphysics* (1686), and *Theodicy* (1710) are the major systematic works.

**Samuel Clarke** (1675–1729)
English philosopher and theologian, close associate and spokesperson for Newton. Clarke's contributions to the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence represent the most developed defense of Newtonian absolute space and time in philosophical form. He also translated Newton's *Opticks* into Latin and was involved in the priority dispute over the invention of calculus. Clarke's work in metaphysics and natural theology, including *A Demonstration of the Being and Attributes of God* (1705), made him one of the leading English philosophers of his generation.

**Ezio Vailati** (1954–2006)
Italian-American philosopher, author of *Leibniz and Clarke: A Study of Their Correspondence* (Oxford University Press, 1997). Vailati's book is the most detailed scholarly examination of the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence available. He analyzes the arguments of both sides with great care and situates the debate within the broader context of early modern philosophy and theology.

**John Earman** (b. 1942)
American philosopher of physics at the University of Pittsburgh. His *World Enough and Space-Time: Absolute versus Relational Theories of Space and Time* (MIT Press, 1989) is the most thorough contemporary examination of the substantivalist-relationist debate, from Newton and Leibniz through general relativity. Essential reading for anyone who wants to understand the modern form of the debate that Leibniz and Clarke initiated.

**Michael Della Rocca** (b. 1958)
American philosopher at Yale University. His *Spinoza* (Routledge, 2008) is one of the best recent introductions to Spinoza's philosophy, with particular attention to the role of the PSR in Spinoza's system. The discussion of Spinoza's account of time and eternity is clear and philosophically engaged.

**Julian Barbour** (b. 1937)
British physicist and philosopher of physics. His *The End of Time* (Oxford University Press, 1999) develops a radically relational account of time in physics, arguing (more radically than Leibniz) that time may not be fundamental to physical reality at all. Barbour's position represents the contemporary extreme of the relational tradition and is philosophically provocative and scientifically grounded.

**Geoffrey Gorham**
American philosopher at Macalester College. His paper "Descartes on Time and Duration" (*Early Science and Medicine* 12, 2007) is the most careful scholarly examination of Descartes's philosophy of time. Gorham situates Descartes within the broader context of late scholastic and early modern discussions of time and duration.
