# 10.1.1 — Duration as a Mode of Substance

---

## The Cartesian Framework

Descartes's metaphysics divides reality into two fundamental kinds of substance: thinking substance (*res cogitans*) and extended substance (*res extensa*). Mental substance has thought as its principal attribute; material substance has extension as its principal attribute. Everything that exists is either God (a third, infinite substance), a thinking thing, an extended thing, or a mode of one of these.

Duration fits into this framework as a mode — a specific determination or qualification of substance. Just as a particular shape is a mode of extended substance (the triangle is a mode of extension), duration is a mode that belongs to any created substance insofar as it continues to exist. Duration is what we attribute to a thing when we recognize that it persists through time.

In *Principles of Philosophy* I.55–57, Descartes articulates this account with characteristic precision. He distinguishes the *principal attributes* of substances (extension for bodies, thought for minds) from their *modes* (particular shapes, sizes, motions for bodies; particular thoughts, emotions, volitions for minds). Duration is described as a "universal" attribute that belongs to all modes of any substance: "whatever we regard as a thing may be attributed duration... even God may be said to have duration in the common use of the term" (Descartes, *Principles* I.55, trans. Miller and Miller 1983: 24).

## Time as Abstraction from Duration

Descartes draws a careful distinction between duration and time that is philosophically important. Duration is what a thing has when it continues to exist; it is an intrinsic feature of the thing. Time, by contrast, is an abstraction from duration — a general measure by which we compare the durations of different things:

> "For the sake of brevity, we call duration a part of that which endures, or a mode of that substance insofar as it continues to be. But time is nothing over and above the duration of things generally taken, so we do not distinguish it from the duration of things, except in thought (*cogitatione*)." (Descartes, *Principles* I.57, trans. Miller and Miller 1983: 25)

Time, in other words, is not a thing — not a separate entity existing alongside durations. It is the conceptual measure we use to compare and order durations. We "abstract" time from the durations of particular things by choosing some regular, recurring process (the sun's daily rotation, the swings of a pendulum) as a standard and measuring other durations against it.

This is a relational account of time in a specific sense: time is not an independently existing container but a way of comparing durations. The comparison is real — durations really do have relative lengths — but there is no "time itself" over and above the durations of individual things. This anticipates Leibniz's relational account in important ways.

## Substance Dualism and the Two Times

Descartes's substance dualism creates an interesting question: is there one time or two? If minds and bodies are fundamentally different substances, each with its own mode of duration, do they share a common temporal framework? Or does mental duration run on a different clock from physical duration?

Descartes does not develop this question, but it is significant. His account suggests that the durations of mental and physical things are both measured by "time" in the same abstract sense — the comparison is made by reference to some standard. But since the standard is always a physical process (the motion of some body), mental duration is measured in physical terms. This raises the question of whether mental time is genuinely comparable to physical time, or whether the comparison is only metaphorical.

This question becomes important in the context of Spinoza, who will develop Descartes's framework in a more radical direction, and in the context of phenomenology, which will insist that phenomenological time (the time of consciousness) is not reducible to physical time.

## Implications for the Philosophy of Time

Descartes's account of duration as a mode of substance has several implications worth noting.

**Time is real but not a substance.** Time, for Descartes, is not a thing in its own right — not a Newtonian absolute container existing independently of all substances. It is a mode of substances, a real feature of how things are, but dependent on substances for its existence.

**The independence of duration from measurement.** Descartes recognizes that the duration of a thing is not dependent on our measuring it: a thing continues to exist (and thus to have duration) whether or not we are counting it. But time, as a measure, is a human (or cognitive) invention — a way of comparing durations that would not exist without a mind to make the comparison. This distinction anticipates the later separation of objective duration from subjective temporal measurement.
