# 10.1.2 — Continuous Creation and Temporal Discontinuity

---

## The Doctrine

Perhaps the most philosophically striking claim in Descartes's philosophy of time is his doctrine of continuous creation. Descartes argues that God does not merely create the world once and then allow it to run on its own; God must re-create the world at every moment. Without God's continuous creative act, the world would not persist from one moment to the next. Temporal continuity — the fact that things persist through time — is not a feature of things themselves but requires God's ongoing creative support.

The doctrine appears in the Third Meditation: "For the whole time of my life may be divided into an infinite number of parts, none of which is in any way dependent on the others; and accordingly, from the fact that I was in existence a short time ago it does not follow that I must be in existence now, unless some cause at this instant, so to speak, produces me anew, that is, conserves me" (Descartes, *Meditations*, Third Meditation, AT VII.49, trans. Cottingham et al. 1984: 33). And in *Principles* I.21: "The nature of time is such that its parts are not mutually dependent, and never coexist. From this we can derive the fact that nothing but God alone can preserve anything, and that he must constantly create it anew" (trans. Miller and Miller 1983: 21, adapted).

## The Philosophical Argument

The argument for continuous creation runs as follows:

1. The parts of time are not mutually dependent — each moment is ontologically independent of every other.
2. Therefore, the existence of a thing at one moment does not by itself entail its existence at the next moment. There is no necessary connection between a thing's existence at t₁ and its existence at t₂.
3. But the world persists — things continue to exist through time. This persistence requires a cause.
4. The only sufficient cause of the world's continued existence at each moment is God's creative power, re-creating the world at every instant.

The key premise is (1): the ontological independence of temporal parts. Descartes treats this as a deep feature of time's nature — each moment is self-contained, not connected to adjacent moments by any logical or necessary tie. This is a temporal atomism about ontological dependence (not necessarily about the size of moments): each temporal "slice" is, in the relevant sense, a new creation.

## Implications for Temporal Continuity

The doctrine of continuous creation has a startling implication: at the most fundamental level, temporal continuity does not exist. There is no ongoing persistence of things through time; there are only a series of existences at successive moments, held together by God's creative activity. The "continuity" we observe in the world is God's perfectly regular re-creation of the same thing at each successive moment — not any intrinsic persistence of things themselves.

This is deeply counterintuitive, and Descartes seems aware of it. In practice, he does not apply this ontological picture to the details of his physics: his mechanics assumes that bodies have continuous trajectories through space and time. But at the level of metaphysics, continuous creation requires us to understand persistence as a series of re-creations rather than a genuine temporal extension of things.

## Causation and Time

The doctrine also has implications for Descartes's account of causation. If the world is re-created at every moment, then causation between successive moments is not a feature of the world itself — it is God's regular exercise of creative power. When billiard ball A strikes ball B and B moves, the "causal connection" is not a necessary link between A's motion and B's motion; it is God's regular habit of re-creating B in motion whenever it has been struck by A.

This has an obvious connection to Hume's later analysis of causation. Hume argued that we have no impression of necessary connection between cause and effect — only an impression of regular succession (*Treatise* I.iii.14). Descartes's continuous creation doctrine might be seen as a theological version of the same insight: there is no intrinsic necessary connection between successive states of the world; what looks like causation is the regular exercise of divine agency. This connection is not always made explicit in the secondary literature, but it is philosophically significant.

## Assessment

Is the doctrine of continuous creation philosophically defensible? It depends on whether the key premise — that the parts of time are ontologically independent — can be sustained. If temporal moments are genuinely independent, then continuous creation does seem to follow: nothing ensures the persistence of things through time except some ongoing cause. If temporal moments are not genuinely independent — if there are necessary connections between successive states of the world — then continuous creation is unnecessary.

Leibniz was critical of the doctrine: he argued that it makes every created thing entirely dependent on God's moment-to-moment re-creation, leaving no genuine causal power to created things themselves (Leibniz, *Theodicy* §382, in Huggard 1951: 370). This objection connects to Leibniz's broader concern about the relationship between God's activity and the genuine causal powers of creatures.
