# 10.2.1 — Time as Imagination

---

## Spinoza's Three Kinds of Knowledge

Spinoza distinguishes three kinds of knowledge in the *Ethics*, and understanding the role of time in his system requires understanding this hierarchy.

The first kind (*cognitio primi generis*) is knowledge through imagination: it is derived from the senses, from signs and testimony, and from casual observation. This kind of knowledge is inadequate — it represents things partially, in a confused manner, relative to particular circumstances and points of view. It is also the source of all error. Mathematical knowledge at the level of simple calculation belongs to this kind, as does ordinary temporal knowledge.

The second kind (*cognitio secundi generis*) is knowledge through reason: it grasps the common notions — the features that all bodies share — and derives their necessary connections. This is the kind of knowledge that mathematical physics achieves: it understands how things must be related given their natures.

The third kind (*cognitio tertii generis*) is intuitive knowledge: it grasps individual things directly in their relationship to God or Nature as a whole, understanding them as they necessarily are from the perspective of the whole of reality. This is the highest knowledge, characterized by what Spinoza calls knowing things *sub specie aeternitatis* — under the aspect of eternity.

## Duration is Real; "Time" is Imaginative

Within this framework, Spinoza draws a crucial distinction between *duration* and *time*. Duration — the property of things by which they continue to exist — is real. It is a genuine feature of finite modes, which have determinate but limited existences. When a particular thing persists, it genuinely has duration; duration is not an imaginative fiction.

Time, however, is something else. "Time" in Spinoza's usage refers to our way of *measuring* duration — of comparing and ordering durations abstractly. And this measurement introduces something that is not in reality itself: the succession of discrete moments, the "flow" from past to future, the idea of a "now" that divides what has been from what will be. These are features of how imagination represents duration, not features of duration as it is in itself (Spinoza, *Ethics* II.44, Corollary 2, and Scholium; see Donagan 1988: 89–93).

The key text is *Ethics* II.44, Corollary 2, with its scholium: "It is in the nature of Reason to regard things as necessary... Under this necessity alone does Reason perceive things, for Reason perceives things neither as contingent nor as present, but rather under a certain species of eternity (*sub quadam aeternitatis specie*)." The scholium adds that "time" and "duration" are aids to imagination rather than features of things as reason grasps them.

## What This Means

If duration is real but "time" as ordinarily conceived is imaginative, what exactly is the error in ordinary temporal thinking? Spinoza's answer seems to be this: ordinary temporal thinking represents things as contingently existing at particular moments — as if they might not have existed at those moments, or might have existed at different moments. It treats the particular temporal location of events as external to their natures, as if the same thing could in principle have occurred earlier or later.

Adequate knowledge — knowledge through reason or intuition — sees things as they necessarily are. It recognizes that everything that exists does so necessarily, given the nature of the whole of reality. There is no real contingency and no real temporal indifference: each thing exists when and how it necessarily exists, given the infinite causal order of Nature. To represent things as "happening now" rather than "necessarily happening at this place in the causal order" is to add a subjective perspectival element — the "now" — to what is in itself a timeless necessary order.

## Is This Temporal Eliminativism?

Is Spinoza eliminating time entirely — saying that time does not really exist? The answer is nuanced. Duration exists — finite modes really do persist for determinate periods. What is eliminated is not temporal existence but the perspective-relative "now" that ordinary temporal thinking adds: the sense that the present moment is *special*, that it divides an existing past from an open future, that things "flow" from one moment to another.

From the perspective of adequate knowledge — from the perspective of the whole of reality as God knows it — there is no "now" and no "then"; there is only the eternal necessary order of causes and effects, each thing occupying its place in that order. Duration is a real feature of this order (some things persist longer than others), but the temporal "flow" — the feeling that time is passing, that the future approaches and the past recedes — is an imaginative addition.

This is close to what contemporary philosophers call the "B-theory" or "eternalist" account of time: the view that past, present, and future are equally real, that the "flow" of time is an illusion, and that what exists is a four-dimensional block of events related by earlier-later rather than a three-dimensional world changing through time (see Chapter 5). Spinoza arrives at something like this view not through physics but through a metaphysics of necessity.
