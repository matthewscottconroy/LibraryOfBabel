# 10.2.2 — *Sub Specie Aeternitatis*: Knowing Under the Aspect of Eternity

---

## The Concept

The phrase *sub specie aeternitatis* — under the aspect of eternity — is one of the most evocative in all of philosophy. Spinoza uses it to characterize the highest form of knowledge: the kind of knowledge by which we grasp individual things in their necessary connection to the whole of reality, rather than in their accidental temporal and perspectival character.

The locus classicus is *Ethics* V.29: "The mind conceives under a certain species of eternity (*sub specie quaedam aeternitatis*) all things that it understands with the third kind of knowledge" (*Ethics* V.29, Scholium, trans. Curley 1994). And *Ethics* V.31: "The third kind of knowledge depends on the mind as its formal cause, insofar as the mind itself is eternal." (V.31, Demonstratio).

To know something *sub specie aeternitatis* is to know it in terms of its necessary nature — to understand it as it is from the perspective of the whole of reality, stripped of all contingent, perspectival, temporal qualifications. It is to see the thing as God sees it: not as occurring now or then but as necessarily connected to all other things in the eternal order of Nature.

## What This Means for Temporal Things

Does knowing things *sub specie aeternitatis* mean ignoring their temporal existence? Not quite. Spinoza is not saying that temporal things are not temporal — they are, insofar as they are finite modes with determinate durations. What he is saying is that the *adequate* understanding of temporal things does not represent them as occurring "now" or "then" but as necessarily occupying their place in the causal order.

Consider a historical event — say, the fall of the Roman Republic. Imaginative knowledge represents this as something that happened in 27 BCE, that is now far in the past, that might (for all we know) have happened otherwise. Rational/intuitive knowledge represents it as a necessary consequence of specific political, military, and economic causes that themselves follow necessarily from prior causes, and so on back to the beginnings of Nature. From the adequate perspective, the event is exactly what it had to be, given the nature of reality — and this necessity is as true now as it was then, and as it will be tomorrow.

## The Relationship to Plotinus and Later B-Theory

The Spinozan concept of *sub specie aeternitatis* has obvious structural resonances with Plotinus's concept of Intellect's atemporal, simultaneous knowledge and with Boethius's concept of God's eternal vision. In all three cases, the highest mode of knowledge is one that grasps things without temporal qualification — without the before-and-after, the past-and-future, the "now."

More recently, the concept has been associated with the B-theory of time in analytic philosophy. The B-theory holds that all times are equally real — that there is no metaphysically privileged "now," and that the "flow" of time is an illusion. Knowing things *sub specie aeternitatis* looks like knowing them as B-theory says they really are: as occupying positions in a four-dimensional block, not as occurring in a flowing present. McTaggart (1908), who first distinguished the A-series (past/present/future) from the B-series (earlier/later), was himself influenced by a broadly Spinozan metaphysics.

Whether Spinoza would have endorsed the B-theory in its modern formulation is debatable — the B-theory is about the metaphysics of time, while Spinoza's account is about the epistemology of knowledge. But the structural parallel is genuine: both views hold that the "now" is a perspectival, mind-dependent addition to a reality that is, in itself, neither flowing nor divided into past and future.

## The Ethical Dimension

Knowing things *sub specie aeternitatis* is not merely an intellectual exercise for Spinoza; it is the foundation of the highest form of human freedom and happiness. In Part V of the *Ethics*, Spinoza argues that understanding things under the aspect of eternity reduces our vulnerability to temporal goods and evils: we are less enslaved to fear and hope (which are both directed at the future) and less tormented by regret and nostalgia (which are both directed at the past) when we understand that what is, necessarily is (Spinoza, *Ethics* V.6 and V.20, Scholium).

This ethical use of the concept of eternity has an interesting parallel with Epicurus and Lucretius. For Lucretius, understanding that time is not an independent container — that there is no "after-death" for you to be absent from — liberates us from the fear of death. For Spinoza, understanding that temporal events are necessary parts of an eternal order liberates us from the anxieties that come from treating temporal contingency as real. In both cases, the metaphysics of time is put in service of a therapeutic project.
