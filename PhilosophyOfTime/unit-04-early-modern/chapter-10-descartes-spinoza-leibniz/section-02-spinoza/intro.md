# Section 10.2: Spinoza on Time and Eternity

---

## Section Introduction

Spinoza's philosophy of time is radically at odds with common sense — and it is intended to be. Spinoza holds that there is only one substance (God or Nature, *Deus sive Natura*), that all finite things are modes or modifications of this one substance, and that the adequate understanding of reality is achieved not through temporal, imaginative thinking but through knowing things "under the aspect of eternity" (*sub specie aeternitatis*). Time, as we ordinarily conceive it — as a series of flowing moments, a past that is gone and a future that approaches — is, for Spinoza, a product of the imagination rather than of adequate intellect. It is not an objective feature of reality but a way that finite minds represent what is, in itself, an eternal, necessary order.

This is a dramatic claim, and making sense of it requires understanding Spinoza's distinction between the three kinds of knowledge and his account of what adequate knowledge looks like.

Section 10.2.1 examines time as imagination. Section 10.2.2 examines the concept of *sub specie aeternitatis* and its philosophical implications.
