# 10.3.1 — Time as the Order of Non-Simultaneous Things

---

## The Positive Account

Leibniz's relational theory of time is developed in several places, but the clearest formulations appear in the *New Essays on Human Understanding* (a critique of Locke, written c. 1703–05 but not published until 1765) and in the Fifth Letter to Clarke (1716). The central passage in the *New Essays* reads:

> "Time is the order of inconsistent possibles. This is the order of successive things, that is, of things which are not at the same time and each of which exists only during a limited time... Time and space denote the order of things and not of any substances outside them." (Leibniz, *New Essays* II.14.16–17, trans. Remnant and Bennett 1996: 152)

And in the Fifth Letter to Clarke (§4): "As for my own opinion, I have said more than once, that I hold space to be something merely relative, as time is; that I hold it to be an order of coexistences, as time is an order of successions" (Leibniz, *Fifth Letter to Clarke* §4, in Alexander 1956: 75–76).

The position is clear: time is the *order* of non-simultaneous things (things that do not coexist). It is not a container into which events are placed; it is the relational structure constituted by the non-simultaneous relations among events. Just as a genealogy is not a place existing alongside families but the relational structure that connects them, time is not a framework existing alongside events but the relational structure that connects them temporally.

## The Principle of Sufficient Reason Against Absolute Time

Leibniz's most powerful argument against absolute time comes from the PSR. If time is an absolute container — an independently existing framework that has its own structure regardless of what happens in it — then a question arises that cannot be answered: why does the history of the world occur *now* in this absolute time rather than one hour earlier, or one day earlier, or one million years earlier?

If time is absolute, then "one hour earlier" picks out a specific temporal location that is distinct from the actual location of events. A world with exactly the same events, but located one hour earlier in absolute time, would be a different world — the events would be at different temporal locations. But nothing in the history of the world itself would distinguish the two worlds. They would be perfectly alike in every intrinsic respect; their only difference would be their position in absolute time.

But then: why does the actual world occupy its actual temporal location rather than the earlier one? There is no difference between the intrinsic characters of the two worlds; nothing about the events themselves determines which temporal location they occupy. Therefore, if absolute time is real, there is no sufficient reason why the world is where it is in time rather than somewhere else. The PSR is violated.

The PSR demands that every fact be explicable — that nothing be arbitrary. A universe floating free in absolute time, with no explanation of why it occupies its particular temporal location, would be an arbitrary universe. Since Leibniz takes the PSR to be a fundamental principle of rationality, he concludes that absolute time is impossible. If the temporal location of events is to have a sufficient reason, that reason must be provided by the events themselves and their relations — not by their position in an absolute framework. Therefore, time is nothing but those relations (Leibniz, *Fifth Letter to Clarke* §§4–6, in Alexander 1956: 75–76; Earman 1989: 123–124).

## The Relational Account of Temporal Order

On Leibniz's positive account, temporal order is constituted by the causal and quasi-causal relations among events. Events that are causally connected stand in temporal order; simultaneous events are causally unconnected (or connected only indirectly through a common cause). The "earlier/later" relation is grounded in the causal structure of the world, not in a primitive temporal framework.

This is an important claim with a modern counterpart in the causal theories of time developed in the twentieth century (notably by Hans Reichenbach, Adolph Grünbaum, and Bas van Fraassen). On these theories, the temporal order of events is reducible to the causal-counterfactual structure of the world: event A is earlier than event B if and only if A could cause B (or contributes to B's causal history). Leibniz does not develop this reduction explicitly, but his relational account implies it.
