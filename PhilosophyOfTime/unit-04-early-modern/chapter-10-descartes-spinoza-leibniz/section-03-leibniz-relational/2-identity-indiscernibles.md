# 10.3.2 — The Identity of Indiscernibles and the Argument Against Absolute Time

---

## The Principle

The Identity of Indiscernibles (sometimes called "Leibniz's Law" in its converse form) is the principle that there cannot be two numerically distinct things that are qualitatively identical in every intrinsic respect: if A and B share all the same intrinsic properties, then A and B are the same thing — not two things but one. Leibniz regarded this as an a priori principle of metaphysics, following from the PSR: if A and B were qualitatively identical but numerically distinct, there would be no sufficient reason why A is A and B is B rather than vice versa.

This principle has an important application to the philosophy of time. Suppose absolute time is real. Then consider two scenarios:

**Scenario 1**: The actual world, in which event E occurs at absolute time t₁.
**Scenario 2**: A world identical in all its intrinsic events and relations, but in which event E occurs at absolute time t₂, where t₂ ≠ t₁.

If absolute time is real, Scenarios 1 and 2 are genuinely different worlds — they differ in the temporal location of every event. But they are intrinsically qualitatively identical: every event in Scenario 1 has an exact counterpart in Scenario 2 with the same intrinsic properties, and every intrinsic relation between events (how long before E occurs event F occurs, etc.) is preserved. The two scenarios differ only in their absolute temporal location — in which bit of absolute time they occupy.

The Identity of Indiscernibles says that if two things are intrinsically qualitatively identical, they are the same thing. If Scenarios 1 and 2 are intrinsically qualitatively identical (differing only in absolute temporal location), then they are the same scenario — they describe the same possible world. But then absolute temporal location is not a real feature of worlds; it is something we are incorrectly treating as a real feature when it is not. Therefore, absolute time is not real (Leibniz, *Fifth Letter to Clarke* §§4–6, in Alexander 1956: 75–76; Saunders 2003: 300–303).

## Clarke's Response and Leibniz's Rejoinder

Samuel Clarke, representing Newton, had a natural response to this argument: he challenged the Identity of Indiscernibles itself. Clarke argued that two things could differ in their "numerical identity" — in which particular individual they are — even without differing in any intrinsic qualitative property. This is what contemporary philosophers call a "haecceitistic" view: there are primitive facts about which particular individual something is, over and above all its qualitative properties.

If haecceitism is true, then Scenarios 1 and 2 are genuinely different even if qualitatively identical: they differ in which particular temporal moments the events occupy, and this difference is a brute fact about numerical identity that does not reduce to qualitative facts.

Leibniz rejected haecceitism on principle: for Leibniz, all facts about things reduce to intrinsic qualitative facts. There are no brute facts about numerical identity; two things are identical or distinct solely in virtue of their qualitative properties. This is a deep metaphysical commitment, not merely a methodological preference, and it connects to Leibniz's broader account of possible worlds and individual essences (Leibniz, *Fifth Letter to Clarke* §26, in Alexander 1956: 90).

## The Contemporary Debate

The debate between Leibniz and Clarke about absolute vs. relational space and time has continued in various forms in contemporary philosophy of physics. The debate between substantivalism (the view that spacetime is an independently existing entity) and relationism (the view that spacetime consists only of spatiotemporal relations among events) is directly descended from the Leibniz-Clarke controversy (Earman 1989: chap. 6; Pooley 2013: 522–526).

General relativity complicates the picture. On one reading, general relativity supports relationism: the spacetime metric is determined by the distribution of matter and energy, not a fixed background. On another reading, the metric field is itself a physical entity — a "structure" of the world — and this suggests a sophisticated form of substantivalism. Neither side has decisively won, and the Leibniz-Clarke debate remains philosophically live.
