# Section 10.3: Leibniz's Relational Theory of Time

---

## Section Introduction

Leibniz's philosophy of time is one of the most important and most carefully argued positions in the history of the subject. In opposition to Newton's absolute time — a fixed, independent framework that would exist even if the universe contained no events — Leibniz argues that time is simply the order of non-simultaneous things: a relation among events, not an entity in its own right. There is no time "over and above" the temporal relations among events; there is no empty time, no temporal container waiting to be filled.

The argument for this position draws on two of Leibniz's most fundamental principles: the Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR), which states that nothing happens without a sufficient reason why it is so rather than otherwise; and the Identity of Indiscernibles (II), which states that no two distinct things can be perfectly alike in all their intrinsic properties. Both principles, Leibniz argues, are violated if time is absolute.

Section 10.3.1 develops Leibniz's relational theory of time. Section 10.3.2 examines how the Identity of Indiscernibles generates a specific argument against absolute time.
