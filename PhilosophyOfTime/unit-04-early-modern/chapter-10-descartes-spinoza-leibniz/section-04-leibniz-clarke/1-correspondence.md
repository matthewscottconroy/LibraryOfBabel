# 10.4.1 — The Leibniz-Clarke Correspondence: Key Arguments

---

## The Context

The correspondence began in November 1715 when Leibniz wrote to Caroline, Princess of Wales, criticizing the Newtonian natural philosophy for its implications about God and nature. Newton's physics, Leibniz argued, made God a bad craftsman: a God who needed to intervene from time to time to wind up the cosmic clock was not the perfect, omnipotent God of Christianity. Clarke responded on Newton's behalf, and the exchange continued through five letters on each side until Leibniz's death in November 1716.

The debate about time is not isolated within the correspondence but is embedded in a broader argument about the nature of God's relationship to the world, the meaning of absolute space and time, and the foundations of mechanics. This broader context is philosophically significant: the debate about time connects to the debate about miracles, divine providence, and the intelligibility of nature.

## Leibniz's Arguments (Summary)

Leibniz deploys three main arguments against absolute time in the correspondence:

**The PSR Argument** (discussed in Section 10.3.1): An absolute temporal framework would allow for states of affairs that have no sufficient reason — for example, a universe that could be displaced by one minute in absolute time without any detectable difference. Since the PSR demands sufficient reasons for all contingent facts, absolute time violates it.

**The Identity of Indiscernibles Argument** (discussed in Section 10.3.2): If two possible worlds differ only in their absolute temporal location, they are intrinsically qualitatively identical and thus — by the Identity of Indiscernibles — the same world. Therefore, absolute temporal location is not a real feature of worlds.

**The Theological Argument**: Absolute space and time were, for Newton, features of God's "sensorium" — the way that God is present to all things. Leibniz argued that this made space and time independent of God — existing alongside God as quasi-divine entities — which was theologically problematic. A truly perfect God would not be limited by or constituted by spatial and temporal dimensions (Leibniz, *Third Letter* §3, in Alexander 1956: 25–26).

## Clarke's Arguments (Summary)

Clarke's defense of absolute time on Newton's behalf centers on three points:

**The Mechanics Argument**: Newton's mechanics requires absolute time to define concepts like acceleration and rotation that cannot be defined relative to any particular frame. The bucket argument (Section 11.2 below) illustrates this: the behavior of a spinning bucket cannot be accounted for in terms of relations between the bucket and surrounding matter; it seems to require reference to something like absolute space or time.

**The Intelligibility Argument**: If time is merely the order of events, then a period of time with no events would be impossible — there would be no time "in" which events could occur before any events occur. But the idea of God creating the world at a particular time seems to require a prior temporal framework. Clarke argues that Leibniz's relational account makes creation itself unintelligible (Clarke, *Third Reply* §§4–5, in Alexander 1956: 31–32).

**The PSR Counter-Response**: Clarke argued that God, as an absolutely free agent, does not need a sufficient reason for *everything*. Some facts — including which particular absolute temporal location the world occupies — may be settled by God's free will rather than by any reason that favors that location over alternatives. Leibniz's PSR demand is too strong (Clarke, *Fourth Reply* §1–10, in Alexander 1956: 47–49).

## Leibniz's Response to the Mechanics Argument

Leibniz's response to the mechanics argument is less well developed than his positive position. He denies that inertia and rotation require absolute space or time: he claims that all motion is relative, and that the behavior described in Newton's bucket argument can be accounted for by reference to the relative motion of the bucket with respect to the totality of matter in the universe. But he does not provide a detailed account of how this would work within his relational framework.

This is the main weakness of Leibniz's position. The mechanics argument — the demand for a framework within which to define inertia, acceleration, and rotation — is a powerful challenge that Leibniz does not fully meet. It would take Mach (Section 11.2.2) and Einstein (whose general theory of relativity provides a relational account of inertia through the interaction of matter and the metric field) to address this challenge more adequately.
