# 10.4.2 — Why the Debate Was Not Resolved: Mach and Einstein

---

## What Was Left Open

The Leibniz-Clarke correspondence ended inconclusively. Leibniz died in November 1716, leaving Clarke's fifth letter unanswered. But even if the correspondence had continued, the debate was unlikely to have been resolved: each side had genuine strengths and genuine weaknesses.

Leibniz's relational theory was philosophically compelling — it avoided the mysterious absolute framework that seemed to float free of all physical detection, and it gave a principled account of why absolute temporal location is not real. But it could not provide a satisfactory account of inertia and rotation within a purely relational framework. Newton's mechanics needed an absolute framework to define acceleration, and no one in the seventeenth century knew how to provide that without positing absolute space and time.

Newton's absolute time was physically essential to his mechanics — it provided the framework within which force, acceleration, and conservation laws were defined. But it was philosophically mysterious: absolute time flowed at a fixed rate "without relation to anything external," which meant it was beyond any physical measurement or detection. Time passed whether anything happened or not, at a rate that nothing could speed up or slow down. This was a real entity that played a crucial role in mechanics — but how could we know anything about it, given that it was beyond all observation?

## Mach's Challenge

Ernst Mach, the Austrian physicist and philosopher, mounted the most important nineteenth-century challenge to Newtonian absolute time and space. In his *The Science of Mechanics* (1883), Mach argued that Newton's bucket argument did not demonstrate the existence of absolute space; it demonstrated that the behavior of rotating water was related to the distribution of the rest of the matter in the universe.

Mach's principle (as it came to be called): the inertial properties of a body — its resistance to acceleration, its tendency to maintain its state of motion — are determined not by its relationship to absolute space but by its relationship to all the other matter in the universe. A spinning bucket in an otherwise empty universe would, on Mach's view, show no concavity of the water surface: there would be nothing relative to which it was spinning. The concavity we observe is a result of the bucket's spinning relative to the totality of cosmic matter (Mach 1919: 284–290).

This is a genuinely relational account of inertia. It supports Leibniz's program: if inertia is relational, then we do not need absolute space or time to define it. But Mach's Principle was programmatic rather than precise — it did not provide the detailed physical account that would show how cosmic matter gives rise to local inertial properties.

## Einstein and General Relativity

Einstein was deeply influenced by Mach and attempted to incorporate Mach's Principle into general relativity. General relativity (1915) removes the fixed, absolute spacetime background of special relativity and Newtonian mechanics, replacing it with a dynamical spacetime metric that is influenced by the distribution of matter and energy. In this sense, general relativity is more "relational" than Newtonian mechanics: the structure of spacetime is not fixed but depends on what is in it.

Whether general relativity fully implements Mach's Principle is disputed. Some solutions to Einstein's field equations do not respect Mach's Principle — they allow empty universes with non-trivial spacetime geometry, which would mean that the inertial properties of matter are not fully determined by other matter. But regardless of whether Mach's Principle is fully implemented, general relativity represents a significant step in the direction of relational spacetime physics — a step that Leibniz's philosophical arguments anticipated without providing the physical mechanism (Barbour 1999: 191–215; Norton 1995: 264–268).

## The Contemporary Situation

The debate between substantivalist and relational accounts of spacetime is not settled in contemporary philosophy of physics. Sophisticated substantivalists argue that general relativity supports a form of substantivalism about the metric field — the spacetime structure is itself a genuine physical entity, not merely a set of relations among matter and events. Sophisticated relationists argue that the metric field is best understood as encoding relational facts about spacetime intervals rather than as a thing in its own right.

The Leibniz-Clarke debate, in its modern form, is one of the most technically demanding areas of philosophy of physics. But the philosophical questions at its core — does time exist independently of events? does inertia require an absolute framework? — are recognizably the same questions that Leibniz and Clarke debated in their letters of 1715–16. Philosophy at its best is cumulative, and this is a case where the philosophical analysis has been genuinely enriched by three centuries of scientific progress without the original philosophical questions having been dissolved.
