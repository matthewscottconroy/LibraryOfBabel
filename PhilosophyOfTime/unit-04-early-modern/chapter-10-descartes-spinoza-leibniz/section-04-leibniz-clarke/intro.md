# Section 10.4: The Leibniz-Clarke Correspondence

---

## Section Introduction

The Leibniz-Clarke correspondence of 1715–1716 is one of the great intellectual exchanges in the history of philosophy. Leibniz, in the last year of his life, engaged in a series of letters with Samuel Clarke, a theologian and philosopher who was Newton's close ally and spokesman. Leibniz died before the correspondence concluded, and the exchange is consequently unfinished — but what survives is philosophically rich. The correspondence covers space, time, the void, the nature of God's action, and the relationship between mathematics and physics.

For the philosophy of time, the correspondence is the single most important early modern document: it presents the two opposing positions — absolute time (Newton/Clarke) and relational time (Leibniz) — with clarity and force, and it demonstrates both the strengths and the limitations of each.

Section 10.4.1 examines the key arguments on both sides. Section 10.4.2 asks what was and was not resolved, and what Mach and Einstein later contributed to the same debate.
