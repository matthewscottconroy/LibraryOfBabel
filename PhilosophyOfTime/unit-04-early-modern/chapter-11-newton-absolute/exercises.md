## Comprehension and Analysis

**Exercise 11.1**
Explain Newton's distinction between absolute time and relative time. What does it mean for time to flow "without relation to anything external"? What does the phrase "absolute, true, and mathematical" add over the mere designation "absolute"?

**Exercise 11.2**
Reconstruct Newton's bucket argument. What is the argument supposed to show about absolute space? How does the argument also support the claim that absolute time is real? Is the argument successful?

**Exercise 11.3**
Explain Mach's Principle. How does it respond to Newton's bucket argument? What are the limitations of Mach's response? Does Mach's Principle vindicate Leibniz's relational theory of time?

**Exercise 11.4**
Explain Locke's account of how we acquire the idea of duration. What experience does Locke think underlies our concept of time? Does this account support or undermine Newton's absolute time?

**Exercise 11.5**
Berkeley argues that the concept of "absolute time" — time flowing independently of all mental succession — is philosophically incoherent. Reconstruct his argument. Is the argument effective against Newton?

**Exercise 11.6**
Hume argues that time cannot "make its appearance to the mind" without variation — without something changing from state to state. Explain this argument. Does it follow that a universe with no change would have no time? What are the implications for Newton's claim that absolute time flows even in a changeless universe?

**Exercise 11.7**
Hume includes "temporal priority of the cause" in his definition of causation. Kant challenged this inclusion, arguing that cause and effect can be simultaneous. Who is right? Can you think of a case where cause and effect are (or might be) simultaneous?

**Exercise 11.8**
Why do causes precede their effects? Consider three possible explanations: (1) temporal priority is conceptually required for causation; (2) the causal order constitutes the temporal order; (3) the asymmetry of causation reflects the thermodynamic arrow of time. Which explanation do you find most defensible?

## Observational and Experimental Exercises

**Lab Exercise 11.A — Looking for Absolute Time**

Spend 15 minutes in a quiet room with minimal external stimulation. During this time, try to attend to time passing "in itself" — not the succession of your own thoughts or the movement of light through the window, but the "pure" passage of time. Can you do it? Write a brief report on what you find. Does your experience support Hume's claim that there is no impression of time apart from the succession of perceivable events? What would it take to verify or falsify Newton's claim that absolute time flows independently of all events?

**Lab Exercise 11.B — Spinning and Inertia**

Find a swivel chair or office chair that spins freely. Sit in it and spin yourself around. Notice the sensation of rotation — the tendency to feel motion even as you slow down, the visual experience of the room spinning when you stop. Now ask: what determines that you are "really" spinning and the room is "really" stationary? If the room were rotating around you at the same rate, would the experience be any different from the inside? What does this suggest about Newton's argument for absolute rotation, and Mach's response?

**Lab Exercise 11.C — Documenting Temporal Variation**

Keep a time diary for one day. Every hour (or at each significant transition in activity), note: (a) your estimate of how much time has passed since the last entry; (b) the objective clock time; (c) what you were doing. At the end of the day, analyze the relationship between felt duration and clock time. Was there a pattern (e.g., engaged activities felt shorter; waiting felt longer)? Write a brief analysis of what this experiment suggests about Hume's claim that our idea of time is tied to the manner in which perceptions appear — and whether this is consistent or inconsistent with the existence of an objective absolute time independent of experience.

## Essay Questions

**Exercise 11.9 (Essay)**
Newton claims that time flows "equably without relation to anything external." Hume argues that our idea of time requires variation — change in our impressions. Are these two claims compatible, or does Hume's epistemological point undermine Newton's metaphysical claim? Write an essay of 800–1000 words.

**Exercise 11.10 (Essay)**
The bucket argument is supposed to show that not all motion is relative — that there is a real distinction between "true" rotation and apparent rotation. Does Mach's Principle adequately respond to this argument? Write an essay of 900–1100 words assessing whether a purely relational account of rotation and inertia is defensible.

**Exercise 11.11 (Essay)**
Hume treats temporal priority as a necessary component of our concept of causation. Contemporary philosophers argue that backward causation (causes succeeding their effects) is at least conceptually possible. Write an essay of 800–1000 words assessing whether temporal priority is conceptually required for causation, drawing on Hume, Kant, and at least one contemporary philosopher.
