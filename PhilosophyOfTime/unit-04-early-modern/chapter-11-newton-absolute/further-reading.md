## Annotated Suggestions for Further Reading

**Newton, Isaac. *Principia*, Scholium to the Definitions. UC Press, 1999.**
The primary source. The Scholium is a few pages long and can be read in isolation from the rest of the *Principia*. Cohen and Whitman's translation is excellent and their extensive introduction (over 350 pages) provides the best available scholarly context. All students of this chapter should read the Scholium itself.

**DiSalle, Robert. *Understanding Space-Time*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2006.**
The best philosophical history of the development of space-time concepts. DiSalle's account of what Newton was trying to achieve with his Scholium — and how the concept evolved through Mach and Einstein — is illuminating and philosophically sophisticated. Accessible to undergraduates with some philosophy of science background.

**Earman, John. *World Enough and Space-Time*. MIT Press, 1989.**
Chapter 1 covers Newton's absolute space and time and the Leibniz critique; subsequent chapters trace the debate through general relativity. The most rigorous philosophical treatment of the substantivalist-relationist debate. Demanding but essential for advanced study.

**Janiak, Andrew, ed. *Isaac Newton: Philosophical Writings*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2004.**
The best accessible collection of Newton's philosophical writings in English, including selections from the *Principia*, *Opticks*, correspondence, and unpublished manuscripts. Janiak's introductions are clear and philosophically informed.

**Hume, David. *A Treatise of Human Nature* I.ii.3–5. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2000.**
The primary source for Hume's analysis of time. The relevant sections are short (perhaps 10 pages total) but dense. Reading them alongside a commentary (Stroud's *Hume* is recommended) is advisable.

**Stroud, Barry. *Hume*. London: Routledge, 1977.**
Still the best introductory treatment of Hume's philosophy, combining philosophical precision with historical sensitivity. Chapter 2 on the theory of ideas and Chapter 3 on the nature of things are directly relevant to the time material.

**Mach, Ernst. *The Science of Mechanics*. Open Court, 1919.**
Chapter 2, section 6 contains Mach's critique of Newton's bucket argument and his formulation of the principle that bears his name. The passage is relatively brief and can be read profitably without the rest of the book.

**Price, Huw. *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1996.**
The most important contemporary treatment of temporal asymmetry and the asymmetry of causation. Price develops a view that builds on Hume's skepticism about necessary connection while extending it to the question of why time has a direction. Highly readable and philosophically stimulating.

**Garrett, Don. *Cognition and Commitment in Hume's Philosophy*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1997.**
A rigorous and careful treatment of Hume's epistemology and metaphysics. Chapters on ideas, impressions, and the theory of relations are directly relevant to the time material. Best for students who want to go deeper into Hume.

**Berkeley, George. *Principles* §§97–99. In *Works*, vol. 2. Thomas Nelson, 1948.**
Berkeley's direct treatment of the philosophy of time, including his critique of absolute time. Short and worth reading alongside the other empiricist material.
