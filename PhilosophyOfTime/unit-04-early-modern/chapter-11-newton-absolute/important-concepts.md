# Chapter 11: Important Concepts

---

**Absolute Time**
Newton's concept of time as "flowing equably without relation to anything external" — an independently existing temporal framework that would persist even in a universe with no events, matter, or processes. Absolute time is contrasted with relative time (the observable, measurable durations of physical processes) as the true temporal standard against which all relative times are measured. Newton holds that absolute time is required for the mechanics of his *Principia*.

**Relative (Common) Time**
The observable, measurable durations of physical processes, used as practical standards for temporal measurement (the rotation of the earth, the swing of a pendulum, the oscillation of a clock). Newton does not deny the existence of relative time; he insists that it is an approximation to absolute time and that the two can in principle diverge.

**Universal Synchrony**
The property of Newtonian absolute time by which all events in the universe can be unambiguously ordered as simultaneous or non-simultaneous, regardless of the motion of observers. Two events are objectively simultaneous if and only if they occur at the same moment of absolute time. Special relativity showed that universal synchrony is not physically correct: simultaneity is frame-dependent, and there is no absolute fact about whether spatially separated events are simultaneous.

**The Bucket Argument**
Newton's thought experiment designed to show that rotation is not merely relative to surrounding matter but has an absolute character — measured against absolute space. The concavity of the water surface in a spinning bucket is present when the water rotates in absolute space and absent when it does not, regardless of the relative rotation between bucket and water. This argument was used to support the necessity of absolute space and, by extension, absolute time.

**Mach's Principle**
The thesis, attributed to Ernst Mach, that the inertial properties of a body are determined by its relationship to all the other matter in the universe. If true, this would support relational accounts of space and time: inertia is relational, not absolute. Einstein was influenced by Mach's Principle in developing general relativity, though general relativity does not fully implement the principle.

**The Empiricist Principle (Hume's Fork)**
Hume's principle that all genuine ideas are copies of impressions (sensory or introspective experiences). Ideas that cannot be traced to a corresponding impression are either confused composites or pseudo-ideas — words without genuine content. Applied to time, this principle challenges the idea of absolute time: if we have no impression of time flowing independently of all succession, then the concept of absolute time is philosophically empty.

**Time from Succession (Locke's Account)**
Locke's claim that our idea of duration derives from the experienced succession of ideas in the mind. We get the idea of "any fixed interval" by observing the succession of our own mental states. This is an empiricist account of temporal knowledge that grounds our temporal concepts in inner experience rather than in any external, independent temporal framework.

**Abstract Ideas (Berkeley's Critique)**
Berkeley's argument that abstract general ideas — ideas supposedly obtained by abstracting away from all particular features — are philosophical fictions. Applied to time, this means that "abstract absolute time" (time stripped of all reference to particular mental or physical events) is not a genuine idea at all. Our concept of time is always the concept of some particular temporal sequence, not of time "in itself."

**The Temporal Succession of Impressions (Hume)**
Hume's account that time is "the manner in which impressions appear to the mind" — not a separate impression at all but the sequential order of our impressions. Time is experienced through variation (the change from one impression to another); a perfectly unchanging experience would have no temporal quality.

**Time Without Variation**
Hume's claim that we cannot form an idea of time without variation — without something changing from state to state. A perfectly static, changeless situation provides no experience of temporal passage and thus no basis for the idea of time. This argument challenges Newton's absolute time, which is supposed to flow even when nothing is changing.

**Temporal Priority of Causation**
The component of Hume's definition of causation that requires causes to temporally precede their effects. This is listed by Hume as one of the three necessary features of causation (alongside spatial contiguity and necessary connection). Whether temporal priority is conceptually required for causation, or whether simultaneous or backward causation is possible, is a live philosophical debate.

**Backward Causation**
The hypothetical scenario in which an effect precedes its cause in time — in which causation operates from future to past. Hume's analysis of causation includes temporal priority (cause before effect) as a conceptual requirement, which would make backward causation definitionally impossible. Contemporary philosophers debate whether backward causation is logically or conceptually coherent, particularly in the context of quantum mechanics.

**Haecceitism (about Time)**
The view that there are primitive facts about numerical temporal identity — facts about *which* particular temporal moments events occupy — that are not reducible to any qualitative facts. Clarke's implicit response to Leibniz's Identity of Indiscernibles argument invokes haecceitism: two worlds differing only in absolute temporal location are numerically distinct even though qualitatively identical.

**Special Relativity and Simultaneity**
Einstein's 1905 theory of special relativity showed that simultaneity is relative to reference frame: two events that are simultaneous in one inertial frame are not simultaneous in another moving relative to it. This directly undermined Newton's absolute time, which requires a single objective simultaneity relation holding independently of reference frames. Special relativity replaced Newtonian absolute time with a four-dimensional spacetime in which temporal order is partly relative to reference frame.
