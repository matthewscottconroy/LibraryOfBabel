# Chapter 11: Important Researchers

---

**Isaac Newton** (1643–1727)
English mathematician, physicist, and natural philosopher; widely regarded as one of the most important scientists in history. His *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687) established classical mechanics and defined the framework for physics for two centuries. The Scholium to the Definitions, in which Newton distinguishes absolute from relative time, space, and motion, is his central contribution to the philosophy of time. Newton was also deeply interested in theology and biblical chronology; his theological views shaped his commitment to absolute space and time as expressions of God's omnipresence.

**Samuel Clarke** (1675–1729)
English philosopher and theologian; Newton's closest philosophical ally. Clarke's contributions to the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence (1715–16) represent the most developed Newtonian philosophical position. His *Demonstration of the Being and Attributes of God* (1705) and his Newton-influenced natural theology were widely influential. Clarke defended absolute space and time against Leibniz's relational arguments.

**John Locke** (1632–1704)
English philosopher and founder of British empiricism in its systematic form. His *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) argues that all ideas derive from experience and applies this principle to time: our idea of duration derives from the succession of ideas in the mind. Though Locke does not directly attack Newton's absolute time, his empiricist account provides the framework within which Berkeley and Hume mounted their critiques.

**George Berkeley** (1685–1753)
Anglo-Irish philosopher and bishop. Berkeley's idealist philosophy holds that to be is to be perceived; material objects exist only as ideas in minds. His critique of abstract ideas — developed in *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710) — includes a critique of absolute time: abstract absolute time is a pseudo-concept, generated by the misuse of general terms.

**David Hume** (1711–1776)
Scottish philosopher, widely regarded as the most important British philosopher of the eighteenth century. His *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40) and *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) systematized empiricism and provided the analysis of causation that Kant later responded to. His analysis of time as the manner of succession of impressions, and his argument that time cannot be experienced without variation, are central to the empiricist critique of absolute time.

**Ernst Mach** (1838–1916)
Austrian physicist and philosopher. His *The Science of Mechanics* (1883) contains the most important nineteenth-century critique of Newton's absolute space and time. Mach's Principle — the thesis that inertia is determined by the distribution of matter in the universe — was enormously influential on Einstein and remains important in philosophy of physics. Mach's broader positivism, which held that physical concepts must be grounded in observable phenomena, influenced logical positivism.

**Robert DiSalle** (b. 1960)
Canadian philosopher of physics at the University of Western Ontario. His *Understanding Space-Time: The Philosophical Development of Physics from Newton to Einstein* (Cambridge University Press, 2006) is the best philosophical history of the development of the concepts of space and time in classical and relativistic mechanics. DiSalle is particularly good on what Newton's Scholium was actually trying to accomplish and how it relates to the later development of the concepts.

**Andrew Janiak**
American philosopher of science at Duke University. His edited volume *Isaac Newton: Philosophical Writings* (Cambridge University Press, 2004) is the most accessible collection of Newton's philosophical writings in English. Janiak's introduction and notes provide essential context for understanding Newton's philosophical views on space, time, and God.

**Huw Price** (b. 1953)
Australian philosopher of physics at Cambridge University. His *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point* (Oxford University Press, 1996) is the most important contemporary treatment of the asymmetry of time, including the asymmetry of causation (why causes precede effects). Price develops the case for what he calls the "Archimedean" view — seeing time symmetrically from a perspective outside the temporal flow — as the appropriate stance for philosophical analysis of time.
