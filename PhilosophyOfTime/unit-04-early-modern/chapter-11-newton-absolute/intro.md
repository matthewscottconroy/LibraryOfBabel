# Chapter 11: Newton and Absolute Time

---

## Chapter Introduction

Isaac Newton's *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687) is one of the greatest scientific works ever written, and its opening pages contain one of the most significant philosophical statements about time in modern history. Before presenting his laws of motion, Newton offers a set of definitional and conceptual clarifications — the "Scholium to the Definitions" — in which he carefully distinguishes absolute from relative concepts of space, time, and motion. His account of absolute time — time that "flows equably without relation to anything external" — defined the default framework for scientific thinking about time for two centuries, and it shaped the philosophical debates that Leibniz, Hume, Kant, and Mach all responded to.

But Newton's absolute time was philosophically contested from the start. The empiricists — Locke, Berkeley, and Hume — attacked it from the direction of the theory of ideas: if we have no experience of time-in-itself, independent of all processes, then the concept of absolute time is empty. Berkeley mounted a particularly sharp attack: abstract absolute time, like abstract matter or absolute space, is a philosophical fiction generated by the misuse of general terms. Hume, systematizing the empiricist critique, argued that our idea of time derives entirely from our experience of successive perceptions, and that the concept of time with no variation is simply incoherent.

Chapter 11 proceeds through four sections. Section 11.1 examines Newton's Scholium and its account of absolute time. Section 11.2 examines the bucket argument and Mach's response. Section 11.3 surveys the empiricist critiques from Locke and Berkeley. Section 11.4 examines Hume's analysis of causation and temporal priority.

What is at stake in this chapter is not just a historical dispute but a foundational question in the philosophy of time: is there a fact of the matter about the "real" rate of time's passage independent of all physical processes? Is there an objective present, independent of any observer's perspective? Newton believed yes; the empiricists and Leibniz believed no; and the question remains contested in contemporary philosophy of physics.
