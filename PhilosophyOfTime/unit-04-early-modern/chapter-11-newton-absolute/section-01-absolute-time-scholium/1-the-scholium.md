# 11.1.1 — Newton's Scholium: Absolute and Relative Time

---

## The Text

The relevant passage from Newton's *Principia* (1687), the "Scholium to the Definitions," reads:

> "Absolute, true, and mathematical time, in itself and of its own nature, without relation to anything external, flows uniformly, and by another name is called duration. Relative, apparent, and common time is any sensible and external measure of duration by means of motion, whether accurate or non-uniform, such as an hour, a day, a month, a year." (Newton, *Principia Mathematica*, Scholium to Definitions, trans. Cohen and Whitman 1999: 408)

This passage contains a fourfold description of the contrast: absolute/relative, true/apparent, mathematical/common, and a distinction between time "in itself" and the "sensible external measure" of time by motion. Each of these pairs carries philosophical weight.

**Absolute/Relative**: Newton's absolute time exists independently of anything else — it is not defined relative to any particular motion, observer, or process. Relative time, by contrast, is always measured by reference to some particular process (the sun's revolution, the swing of a pendulum). Newton does not deny the existence or utility of relative time; he insists on distinguishing it from absolute time.

**True/Apparent**: This is a normative distinction: absolute time is the *real* time, while relative time is only an approximation to it. Our clocks measure relative time and may diverge from absolute time if the processes used to measure it are not perfectly uniform. The ideal clock would track absolute time; real clocks are at best good approximations.

**Mathematical/Common**: Absolute time is the time of mathematical physics; common time is the time of ordinary life and conversation. Mathematical physics needs a precise framework; ordinary conversation is satisfied with rough measures.

**"Without Relation to Anything External"**: This is the key phrase. Absolute time is not defined in relation to any physical process or event; it flows on its own, independently of whether anything is happening. Time would pass even in a universe with no events, no motion, no change. This is what Leibniz found philosophically objectionable.

## The Flow Metaphor

Newton describes absolute time as "flowing uniformly." This is a metaphor — time flows like a river, smoothly and continuously, at a fixed rate. The metaphor is evocative but philosophically problematic: rivers flow at a rate measured against time (meters per second), so what does it mean for time itself to "flow"? At what rate does time flow? One second per second? That is tautologous, not informative.

Newton seems to be gesturing at the idea that absolute time is perfectly regular — it does not speed up or slow down, is not interrupted or uneven. But making this precise without presupposing a temporal framework is difficult. The "flow" metaphor may be inescapable for intuitive communication but is not philosophically adequate as a literal description.

## The Theological Background

Newton's absolute time has a theological dimension that is explicitly acknowledged in Newton's unpublished manuscripts and in his *Opticks*. In the *Opticks* (Query 31, 3rd ed., 1717), Newton writes that God's infinite duration is what absolute time represents: "He endures for ever, and is every where present; and by existing always and every where, he constitutes duration and space. Since every particle of space is always, and every indivisible moment of duration is every where, certainly the Maker and Lord of all things cannot be never and no where" (Newton, *Opticks*, Query 31, in Janiak 2004: 92–93).

This passage suggests that for Newton, absolute time is closely connected to God's infinite duration and omnipresence: space and time are, in some sense, expressions of God's eternal presence throughout creation. This is not quite the view that space and time are God's "sensorium" (sense-organ) that Leibniz criticized — but it is theologically motivated in a way that pure physics alone would not require.
