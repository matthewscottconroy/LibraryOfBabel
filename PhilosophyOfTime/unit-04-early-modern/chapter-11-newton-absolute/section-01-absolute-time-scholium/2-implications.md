# 11.1.2 — What Absolute Time Implies

---

## Universal Synchrony

Absolute time implies universal synchrony: for any two events occurring anywhere in the universe, there is a fact about whether they are simultaneous or not, and this fact is absolute rather than relative to any frame of reference. Two events are simultaneous if and only if they occur at the same absolute time. This simultaneity relation is universal — it applies across the entire universe, regardless of the motions of observers or reference frames.

This was one of the features of Newtonian physics that Einstein's special theory of relativity (1905) challenged most directly. Special relativity showed that simultaneity is relative to the reference frame of the observer: two events that are simultaneous in one reference frame may not be simultaneous in another. There is no absolute fact about whether two spatially separated events are simultaneous; simultaneity is frame-dependent. Newton's absolute time, with its universal synchrony, was thereby shown to be physically incorrect.

This is one of the most important results in the history of physics with respect to the philosophy of time: it demonstrated that Newton's absolute time was not just philosophically problematic (as Leibniz had argued) but empirically incorrect. Absolute simultaneity does not exist, at least not in the straightforward Newtonian sense.

## A Preferred Temporal Frame

Absolute time implies a preferred temporal framework: there is one objective temporal structure that correctly represents the true order and rate of temporal succession. Relative times — the times measured by different clocks in different states of motion — are all approximations to this one true time. They may diverge from the true time (if the clocks are inaccurate or the motion is non-uniform) but the standard against which they are judged is the absolute time.

In Newtonian mechanics, this preferred frame is implicitly provided by the fixed stars. Newton treats the center of mass of the solar system as approximately at rest in absolute space, and the time measured by the rotation of the earth (adjusted for irregularities) as approximately absolute time. The stars are so distant that their apparent motion can serve as the inertial reference frame against which all other motions are measured.

## Independence from All Processes

The most philosophically distinctive implication of Newtonian absolute time is its independence from all physical processes. Time passes whether or not anything is happening. In a universe with no matter — pure empty space — absolute time would still flow at the same uniform rate. In a universe where all matter was suddenly frozen in place — no motion, no change — absolute time would continue to pass, even though nothing would be happening.

This is the feature that Leibniz found most objectionable. If absolute time flows independently of all processes, there can be no sufficient reason for any particular event's absolute temporal location: the same event could, in principle, have occurred one second earlier or later in absolute time, with no intrinsic difference. Leibniz concluded that absolute time was a metaphysical fiction.

## Why Newton Thought It Was Necessary

Newton's motivation for positing absolute time was partly philosophical (connected to his theological views about God's omnipresence through time) and partly scientific (connected to the demands of mechanics). The scientific motivation was the more pressing: Newton's laws of motion require a reference framework within which to define concepts like velocity, acceleration, and force. If all motion is purely relative — if there is no fact about whether a body is "really" accelerating or merely accelerating relative to some chosen reference — then the second law of motion (F = ma) has no definite application.

Newton showed this with the bucket argument (Section 11.2.1): the behavior of spinning water cannot be fully accounted for by reference to the relative motion of the bucket and the surrounding matter. There seems to be a fact about "true" rotation that does not reduce to any particular relative motion. This fact requires a reference framework — absolute space — to be defined. And a fixed absolute spatial framework requires an absolute temporal framework to be coherent (Earman 1989: chap. 1; DiSalle 2006: 23–28).
