# 11.2.1 — Newton's Bucket Argument

---

## The Experiment

Newton describes the following scenario in the Scholium to the Definitions of the *Principia*: take a bucket filled with water and hang it by a long cord. Twist the cord many times by rotating the bucket, and then hold the bucket still until the water is at rest. Now release the bucket. The cord unwinds, the bucket spins, and the following sequence of events occurs:

**Stage 1**: The bucket begins to spin, but the water has not yet begun to move. At this stage, the water surface is flat. The bucket rotates rapidly relative to the water.

**Stage 2**: The bucket continues to spin and gradually communicates its rotation to the water through friction. The water begins to spin with the bucket, and the water surface becomes concave — it rises at the sides and depresses at the center. The bucket rotates slowly relative to the water (they are now approximately rotating together).

**Stage 3**: When the bucket is suddenly stopped, the water continues to spin for a while (from inertia). The water surface remains concave. Now the water rotates relative to the bucket — but the bucket is stationary.

Newton draws the following conclusion: the water surface is flat when the water is at rest in absolute space (Stage 1, and when no rotation at all), and concave when the water is rotating in absolute space (Stage 2 and Stage 3). The concavity is not related to the relative motion between the water and the bucket — in Stage 1, the water and bucket have relative motion but the surface is flat; in Stage 3, the water and bucket have relative motion in the opposite sense but the surface is still concave. What matters is not relative rotation but rotation relative to absolute space (Newton, *Principia*, Scholium, trans. Cohen and Whitman 1999: 412–413).

## The Argument

Newton's argument from the bucket is an argument against the view that all motion is merely relative — that there is no such thing as "true" or "absolute" motion, only motion relative to some chosen reference:

1. The concavity of the water surface (the centrifugal effect) is a measurable physical phenomenon.
2. This phenomenon is not correlated with the relative rotation between the water and the bucket.
3. The only candidate for what it *is* correlated with is the rotation of the water relative to absolute space.
4. Therefore, absolute space is real, and rotation relative to it is a genuine physical fact.

The argument generalizes to absolute time: if absolute space is real, then absolute rest and absolute motion are defined relative to it, and the temporal framework within which these motions occur must also be absolute.

## What the Argument Shows

The bucket argument is designed to show that not all motion is relative — that there is a privileged class of "true" motions (inertial motions, and deviations from them) that cannot be accounted for purely in terms of relations between material bodies. If this is right, then a purely relational account of space and time is inadequate: we need to appeal to some framework — absolute space and time — to define the relevant motions.

This is a genuine challenge to relationalism, and it was not adequately answered until Mach (Section 11.2.2) proposed that the relevant framework might be the totality of matter in the universe rather than absolute space, and until Einstein's general theory of relativity provided a more sophisticated relational framework for inertia.

For the philosophy of time specifically, the bucket argument establishes the following: if the inertial properties of bodies — their tendency to resist acceleration and to maintain uniform motion — require an absolute spatial and temporal framework, then absolute time is a scientific necessity, not merely a theological or metaphysical prejudice.
