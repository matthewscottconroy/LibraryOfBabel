# 11.2.2 — Mach's Critique and Mach's Principle

---

## Mach's Response

Ernst Mach's *The Science of Mechanics* (1883) contains the most important nineteenth-century critique of Newton's bucket argument. Mach's response is as elegant as it is challenging: he denies Newton's conclusion not by denying the phenomenon (water does become concave when rotating) but by proposing an alternative explanation.

Mach's claim is that the concavity of the rotating water is not evidence for rotation relative to absolute space but for rotation relative to the *total mass of the universe*. The fixed stars, the distant galaxies, the entire distribution of matter in the cosmos — these, not an invisible absolute space, are what determine the inertial properties of bodies. When the water in the bucket rotates and becomes concave, it is not rotating relative to Newton's absolute space; it is rotating relative to the totality of the matter distributed throughout the universe.

Mach's specific argument against Newton is a thought experiment of his own: "Try to fix Newton's bucket and rotate the heaven of fixed stars, and then prove the absence of centrifugal forces" (Mach 1919: 284). In other words: if you could hold the bucket still and rotate the entire universe around it, would you see the same concavity in the water? Mach says yes — and if so, then the concavity is due to relative rotation between the bucket and the cosmic matter, not to rotation relative to absolute space. The two scenarios (bucket rotating in a fixed universe, and universe rotating around a fixed bucket) would be physically equivalent.

## Mach's Principle

This leads to what Einstein later called "Mach's Principle": the inertial properties of a body (its resistance to acceleration, the centrifugal and Coriolis forces it experiences when rotating) are determined by its distribution of matter relative to all the rest of the matter in the universe. There is no "intrinsic" inertia; inertia is a relational property, determined by the cosmic matter distribution.

If Mach's Principle is correct, then Newton's bucket argument does not show what Newton thought it did. The rotating water is concave not because of absolute space but because of the relational structure of matter in the universe. Rotation is still physically real — it is not merely a matter of convention — but what makes it real is not its relationship to an absolute spatial framework but its relationship to the totality of cosmic matter (Mach 1919: 280–297; Barbour 1999: 82–97).

## Limitations of Mach's Response

Mach's Principle is suggestive but not fully worked out. It has several significant limitations:

**Lack of a mechanism**: Mach does not explain *how* distant matter determines local inertia. He asserts the dependence but provides no quantitative account of how the influence of distant galaxies on local inertial properties is transmitted.

**Empirical complications**: General relativity, the most successful physical theory incorporating Mach-type ideas, does not fully implement Mach's Principle. Some solutions to Einstein's field equations — including the Gödel universe (a rotating universe) and the de Sitter solution (an empty universe) — allow for well-defined inertial frames in universes with no matter or with uniformly distributed matter, contrary to Mach's expectations.

**The Leibniz problem remains**: Even if Mach's Principle is correct, it does not fully vindicate Leibniz's purely relational account. Mach replaces absolute space with the total matter distribution as the reference for inertia — but the total matter distribution is itself a physical entity with a determinate structure. Whether this counts as a "relational" account or a sophisticated form of quasi-absolutism is disputed.

## Why the Bucket Argument Matters for the Philosophy of Time

The bucket argument and Mach's response are not just about rotation and absolute space. They are about the nature of temporal frames as well. If inertia requires a reference framework, and if that framework must be either absolute space-time or the total matter distribution of the universe, then the question of which kind of temporal framework is "real" — absolute, Machian, or purely relational — is forced upon us by the physics itself.

The most important lesson may be this: the philosophy of time cannot be done in isolation from physics. Whatever account of time we favor must be compatible with the physical facts about inertia, rotation, and the propagation of forces. This is not a constraint that either Leibniz or Newton fully appreciated, and it is a lesson that has been repeatedly reinforced by the history of physics from Mach through Einstein to quantum gravity.
