# 11.3.1 — Locke and Berkeley on Time

---

## Locke's Empiricist Account

John Locke (1632–1704) is the founder of the British empiricist tradition in its systematic form. His *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) argues that all our ideas derive either from sensation (outer experience) or from reflection (inner experience of our own mental operations). There are no innate ideas; the mind begins as a blank slate, and everything in it has come through experience.

For time, Locke's key claim is that our idea of duration comes from the experience of the succession of ideas in our own minds: "From the constant change of ideas — for ideas, as far as perception is concerned, come and go in succession — we get the idea of duration, i.e. *any fixed interval* of that succession" (*Essay* II.14.3, trans. Nidditch 1975: 181). The source of our temporal concept is the internal succession of mental states — memory, perception, thought — not any external, independent temporal framework.

This account has an Augustinian flavor: the soul's succession of experiences is the ground of our temporal knowledge. But Locke draws a more deflationary conclusion: our idea of time is derived from experience of succession, and that is all there is to it. There is no further "absolute time" accessible to experience; our temporal concepts are exactly as robust as our experience of succession and no more.

Locke does not directly attack Newton's absolute time, but his empiricist account makes it hard to see what our supposed idea of absolute time would consist in, since it cannot derive from any experience.

## Berkeley's Critique

George Berkeley (1685–1753) is the most forthright empiricist critic of absolute time. In his *Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710) and other works, Berkeley argues that abstract general ideas — ideas supposedly derived by abstracting away from all particular, concrete features — are philosophical fictions. We cannot form a genuine idea of abstract time, any more than we can form a genuine idea of abstract matter or abstract space.

The relevant passage is *Principles* §98: "For my own part, whenever I attempt to frame a simple idea of time, abstracted from the succession of ideas in my mind, which flows uniformly and is participated by all beings, I am lost and embrangled in inextricable difficulties. I have no notion of it at all, only I hear others say it is infinitely divisible, and speak of it in such a manner as leads me to entertain odd thoughts of my existence: since that doctrine lays one under an absolute necessity of thinking, either that he passes away innumerable ages without a thought, or else that he is annihilated every moment of his life, both which seem equally absurd" (Berkeley, *Principles* §98, in Luce and Jessop 1948: vol. 2, 82–83).

Berkeley's point is that the concept of absolute time — time flowing independently of all mental states, at a uniform rate, whether anything is happening or not — has no genuine content. When we try to form this idea, we either smuggle in the mental succession that gives us our original idea of time, or we find ourselves with nothing at all. The "abstract absolute time" of Newton is a pseudo-concept, generated by the misuse of general terms.

## Berkeley's Positive Account

Berkeley's positive account of time, like Locke's, derives from the succession of ideas in the mind. Unlike Locke, Berkeley denies the existence of a mind-independent physical world: for Berkeley, to be is to be perceived (*esse est percipi*). Material objects exist only as ideas in minds; there is no external world independent of minds. Time, accordingly, is the order and succession of ideas — not the order of external physical events but the order of mental events in the minds of perceiving subjects.

This is a more radical idealism than anything in Locke, and it has the implication that there is no "cosmic" time independent of minds. Different minds with different rates of idea-succession would experience "time" differently, and there would be no absolute framework to adjudicate between them. This is Berkeley's reductio of Newtonian absolute time: the concept is not only empirically unwarranted but metaphysically incoherent given the dependence of all existence on minds.
