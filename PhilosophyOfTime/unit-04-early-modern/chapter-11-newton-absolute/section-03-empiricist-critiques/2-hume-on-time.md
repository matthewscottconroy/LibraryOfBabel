# 11.3.2 — Hume on Time

---

## Hume's Analysis of Time

David Hume (1711–1776) carried the empiricist critique of abstract ideas to its most systematic form. In the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), Book I, Part ii, sections 3–5, Hume analyzes the idea of time — and what he finds is both philosophically acute and philosophically challenging.

Hume's starting point is his principle that all genuine ideas are copies of impressions (sensory experiences or introspective experiences). Any idea that cannot be traced to a corresponding impression is either a confused composite of genuine ideas or a pseudo-idea — words without genuine content. This principle is Hume's master instrument of philosophical criticism.

Applied to time: "the idea of time is not derived from a particular impression mixed up with others, and plainly distinguishable from them; but arises altogether from the manner, in which impressions appear to the mind, without making one of the number" (*Treatise* I.ii.3, SBN 36, in Norton and Norton 2000: 34). Time, for Hume, is not a distinct impression at all: we have no impression of time per se. What we have are impressions of objects and their successions, and time is the "manner" in which these impressions appear — the sequential order of their appearance.

## The Succession of Perceptions

This is a subtle claim. Hume is not saying that we have no experience related to time; he is saying that the experience we have is always of something *in* time — a sequence of impressions, a before-and-after of perceptions — rather than of time itself. The "time" is the order of the succession; it is not an additional impression that accompanies the sequence.

The analogy Hume draws is illuminating: "As is the case with the ideas of space, the ideas we get of it are not the ideas of an abstract 'space' but of certain figures and colors; so in this case, we have no abstract idea of time, but that idea is based altogether on particular appearances which we see and hear change" (*Treatise* I.ii.3, SBN 35–36, in Norton and Norton 2000: 33–34, adapted).

Just as we do not have an impression of space itself — only of particular spatial configurations of colored patches — we do not have an impression of time itself, only of the sequential order of our impressions. Time is the form of succession, not a separately experienced entity.

## The Impossibility of Time Without Variation

One of Hume's most important arguments about time is that "time cannot make its appearance to the mind, either alone, or attended with a steady unchangeable object, but is always discovered by some perceivable succession of changeable objects" (*Treatise* I.ii.3, SBN 35, in Norton and Norton 2000: 33).

The argument is this: suppose you were presented with a single object undergoing no change — a perfectly static situation. Could you have any experience of time passing in this situation? Hume says no: without variation — without something changing from state to state — there is no experience of time, and therefore no idea of time. The idea of time requires the idea of succession, and succession requires variation.

This argument cuts directly against Newton's absolute time. Newton says time flows even when nothing is changing. Hume says that time, as an idea, cannot be formed from an experience of changeless stasis. If we have no impression of a changeless, motionless passage of time, then we have no genuine idea of it — the Newtonian notion of absolute time as flowing "without relation to anything external" is a philosophical fiction.

## Hume's Account as a Proto-Relationism

Hume's account is not exactly a relational theory of time in the Leibnizian sense — he is making an epistemological point (about the conditions for having the idea of time) rather than a metaphysical point (about what time is). But the epistemological point has metaphysical implications. If our idea of time is entirely constituted by our experience of succession, and if there is no impression of time-in-itself, then any claim about "absolute time" independent of all succession is making a claim that outstrips our conceptual resources. We simply cannot form the idea; the words are empty.

This anticipates Kant's diagnosis, in the next chapter, that time is a form of our intuition — a structure of our experience — rather than a feature of things as they are in themselves. Hume's empiricist analysis of the idea of time is one of the inputs to Kant's transcendental account.
