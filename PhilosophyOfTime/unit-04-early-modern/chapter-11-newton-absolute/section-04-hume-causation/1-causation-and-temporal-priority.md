# 11.4.1 — Causation and Temporal Priority

---

## Hume's Analysis of Causation

Hume's analysis of causation in *Treatise* I.iii.2 identifies three necessary components in our concept of causation:

1. **Spatial contiguity**: cause and effect must be spatially adjacent (or connected through a chain of adjacent intermediaries)
2. **Temporal priority of the cause**: the cause must precede the effect in time
3. **Necessary connection**: there must be some "necessary connection" between cause and effect

Hume famously argues that the third component — necessary connection — cannot be found in experience: we never observe the necessary connection between cause and effect, only their constant conjunction. Our idea of necessary connection derives from the mental habit formed by observing constant conjunction: after seeing many cases of A followed by B, the mind is determined to expect B upon observing A.

For our purposes, the second component — temporal priority — is most interesting. Hume states: "We may define a cause to be an object precedent and contiguous to another, and where all objects resembling the former are placed in like relations of precedency and contiguity to those objects that resemble the latter" (*Treatise* I.iii.14, SBN 170, in Norton and Norton 2000: 109). The reference to "precedency" (temporal priority) is built into the definition of cause.

## Is Temporal Priority Conceptually Required for Causation?

Hume treats temporal priority as conceptually required for causation — it is part of what we *mean* by "cause." This raises several questions.

**First question**: Is this a conceptual claim (about our concept of causation) or a metaphysical claim (about what causation in the world actually involves)? Hume, as an empiricist, tends to conflate these: the concept of causation is derived from experience of constant conjunction with temporal priority, so temporal priority is part of the concept. But one might hold that our concept includes temporal priority while the metaphysical structure of causation does not require it.

**Second question**: Could causes and effects be simultaneous? Kant argued explicitly, in the Second Analogy of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, that cause and effect can be simultaneous: a heavy ball resting in a cushion and the depression in the cushion are simultaneous, but the ball is the cause of the depression. Kant's point is that temporal priority is not conceptually required for causation, even if it is typically present (Kant, *Critique* A202–203/B247–248). This directly challenges Hume's inclusion of temporal priority in the definition.

**Third question**: Could effects precede their causes — backward causation? If temporal priority is merely a typical feature of causal relationships rather than a conceptual requirement, then "backward causation" (causation from future to past) is at least conceptually possible. This is debated in contemporary philosophy and physics (especially in quantum mechanics, where retrocausal interpretations of quantum phenomena have been proposed).

## Why Causes Precede Effects

Even if temporal priority is not conceptually required for causation, there is an empirical fact that demands explanation: in our experience, causes precede effects. Why? What grounds this asymmetry?

Several explanations have been proposed:

**The causal theory of time**: Some philosophers hold that the temporal order is partly *constituted* by the causal order — that A is earlier than B partly *because* A can cause B (or contributes to B's causal history). On this view, the temporal priority of causes is analytic: causes are by definition earlier, because "earlier than" is partly defined in terms of possible causation (Reichenbach 1956: §§21–23). This view makes the temporal priority of causes trivially true but at the cost of circularity if we want to explain both temporal order and causal order.

**The thermodynamic arrow**: Another explanation connects the priority of causes to the thermodynamic arrow of time — the fact that entropy increases in one temporal direction (the direction we call "future"). The asymmetry of causation may reflect the thermodynamic asymmetry of physical processes. Causes produce effects that increase entropy; effects do not "produce" causes in the reverse direction because that would require entropy decrease (Price 1996: chap. 7).

**The interventionist account**: James Woodward's interventionist account of causation (2003) connects causation to the possibility of manipulation: A causes B if intervening on A (changing A while holding other things constant) would change B. This account has a structural asymmetry built in but does not directly address the temporal direction of the asymmetry.

## Kant's Discussion

Kant's Second Analogy of Experience (*Critique* A189–A211/B232–B256) addresses the temporal priority of causes within his broader account of objective temporal experience. Kant argues that our ability to distinguish objective temporal sequences (event A followed by event B, as opposed to B followed by A, or A and B simultaneously) depends on the concept of causation: we judge that event A objectively preceded event B because A satisfies the condition of being a sufficient condition for B, while B is not a sufficient condition for A. The temporal order of events is constituted, for Kant, by the causal order — not the other way around.

This Kantian inversion of the Humean priority — where causation explains temporal order rather than temporal order explaining causation — is philosophically significant. If Kant is right, the temporal priority of causes is not a brute empirical fact but a consequence of the role that causal concepts play in constituting our experience of objective time.
