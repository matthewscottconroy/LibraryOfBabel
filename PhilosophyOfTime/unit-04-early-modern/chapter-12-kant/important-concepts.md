# Chapter 12: Important Concepts

---

**Transcendental Aesthetic**
The opening section of Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (A19/B33–A49/B73), devoted to the a priori forms of sensible intuition. The Transcendental Aesthetic argues that space and time are not features derived from experience but the framework that makes experience possible in the first place. It is the foundation of Kant's entire critical philosophy, and his account of time begins here.

**Pure (A Priori) Intuition**
An intuition is a direct, singular, non-conceptual representation of an object. A *pure* intuition is one that is given independently of experience — prior to all empirical content. Kant argues that time is a pure intuition: it is not abstracted from experience but is the very form within which all experience occurs. This distinguishes Kant's account sharply from both rationalist and empiricist predecessors.

**Intuition vs. Concept**
One of Kant's most fundamental distinctions. Concepts are general representations applied to many objects; intuitions are singular and immediately related to objects. Time, for Kant, is an intuition because it is singular (there is only one time, and all times are parts of it), not a concept under which many instances fall. The distinction explains why Kant denies that time is a mere abstraction from experience of temporal things.

**Form of Inner Sense**
Kant's characterization of time as the structural framework through which we represent our own mental states. When we attend to our thoughts, feelings, and experiences, they present themselves as temporally ordered — as earlier, later, or simultaneous. This ordering is not derived from the contents of the mental states themselves; it is the form that inner experience already has. All outer experience, mediated through the mind, is also temporal in this sense.

**Empirical Reality of Time**
Kant's claim that time is *objectively valid* for all objects of possible experience. For anything that can appear within human experience — any event, process, or state — temporal relations hold necessarily and universally. Time is not a subjective illusion; empirical science proceeds correctly in treating temporal relations as real features of the world as we encounter it. This is one half of Kant's dual thesis about time's ontological status.

**Transcendental Ideality of Time**
Kant's claim that time is not a property of things as they are in themselves, independently of human sensibility. Time belongs to the form of our intuition — to the structure of the way things appear to us — not to the intrinsic nature of reality apart from minds like ours. This is the second half of Kant's dual thesis, and it is what distinguishes his position from Newton's absolute realism about time.

**Synthetic A Priori Knowledge**
Knowledge that is both informative (not merely definitional or analytic) and knowable without appeal to experience (a priori). Arithmetic and geometry are Kant's paradigm examples; claims about time (e.g., that all changes are in time, that time is one-dimensional and has direction) are also synthetic a priori for Kant. The existence of such knowledge drives the core question of the *Critique*: "How are synthetic a priori judgments possible?"

**Transcendental Idealism**
Kant's overarching philosophical framework, according to which the objects of experience are "appearances" (phenomena) whose temporal and spatial structure is contributed by the cognizing subject, while "things in themselves" (noumena) remain unknowable. Transcendental idealism is neither subjective idealism (things exist only as mental contents) nor naive realism (things have exactly the properties they appear to have); it occupies a third position that Kant spent much of the *Critique* defending.

**Thing-in-Itself (Ding an sich)**
The object as it exists independently of any human cognitive contribution — stripped of the forms of intuition (space and time) and the categories of understanding. Kant holds that we cannot know things in themselves, only things as they appear under our cognitive conditions. Time, as a form of intuition, does not belong to things in themselves. This doctrine generates one of the deepest problems in Kant scholarship: if things in themselves are outside our cognitive reach, how can Kant even say anything about them?

**Schematism**
Kant's doctrine, developed in the chapter of that name (A137/B176–A147/B187), that the pure concepts of the understanding (categories) must be mediated by *temporal schemata* before they can be applied to experience. Since the categories are purely intellectual and experience is temporal, there must be an intermediary that is both intellectual and temporal. The schema of a category is its temporal determination — the rule for applying the category to objects in time.

**Temporal Schema**
The specific temporal character that a category receives through schematization, enabling it to apply to appearances. The schema of *substance* is *permanence through time* (something that persists while all else changes). The schema of *causality* is *necessary succession according to a rule* (the cause always precedes the effect under a law). The schema of *community* is *simultaneity according to a rule* (substances in mutual causal interaction exist at the same time). The schemata bind the abstract categories to the temporal structure of experience.

**Analogies of Experience**
Three synthetic a priori principles derived from the relational categories and their schemata (*Critique* A176–218/B218–265). The First Analogy states that substance is permanent through all change. The Second Analogy states that all changes have a cause — that every event is necessarily preceded by a cause according to a rule. The Third Analogy states that all simultaneously existing substances are in thoroughgoing causal interaction. Together, the three Analogies establish the conditions for objective temporal experience: persistence, succession, and simultaneity.

**Second Analogy of Experience**
Kant's argument (*Critique* A189/B232–A211/B256) that the objective temporal order of events — the fact that A objectively preceded B — is grounded in causal connection, not perceived directly. Since the sequence of our perceptions is always subjectively reversible (we can look at the parts of a house in any order), objective succession requires a rule that fixes the order as necessary: A causes B. This is Kant's transcendental response to Hume's analysis of causation, turning the tables by arguing that causation is what makes temporal order objective rather than deriving causation from observed temporal order.

**First Antinomy**
The first of four antinomies in Kant's Transcendental Dialectic (*Critique* A426/B454–A433/B461). The Thesis argues that the world must have had a beginning in time; the Antithesis argues that the world can have had no beginning. Kant claims both arguments are valid, which would be a contradiction if the world as a whole were an object of possible experience. Since it is not — since "the world as a totality" is a transcendental illusion — both arguments misfire by treating an unknowable whole as a knowable object.

**Transcendental Illusion**
The unavoidable tendency of reason to overstep the boundaries of possible experience and treat ideas (soul, world-totality, God) as if they referred to knowable objects. Unlike empirical illusion (which can be corrected), transcendental illusion persists even after it is diagnosed: we cannot help but think beyond experience, even though such thinking cannot yield knowledge. The antinomies are the diagnostic proof that this illusion leads to contradiction.

**Regulative vs. Constitutive Use of Ideas**
Ideas of reason (including the idea of the world as a temporal totality) can be used either constitutively (as if they referred to actual objects of knowledge) or regulatively (as guiding principles for the systematic extension of inquiry). Kant insists that cosmological ideas like "the beginning of the world" can only be used regulatively — as ideals that guide scientific investigation — not constitutively, as if we could know the world's temporal extent directly.

**Temporal Determination**
The application of a temporal predicate — before, after, simultaneous, permanent, successive — to an object of experience. For Kant, temporal determinations are among the most fundamental features of empirical objects: to be an object of experience at all is to stand in determinate temporal relations. The Analogies of Experience are the principles that make such determinations possible and objective.

**Post-Kantian Idealism**
The philosophical movement initiated by Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel in response to Kant. Where Kant treats the forms of intuition and the categories as fixed features of the human cognitive constitution, the post-Kantians "radicalize" transcendental idealism by making time a product of the self-positing activity of Spirit or the dialectical unfolding of Reason. Time is no longer a static framework but a dynamic achievement — the medium of spirit's self-realization. This trajectory runs from Kant through Hegel to Heidegger's existential analytics.

**Empiricist Idealism vs. Transcendental Idealism**
Kant carefully distinguishes his transcendental idealism from Berkeley's empiricist idealism. For Berkeley, time (and space) are contents of individual minds — ideas in particular perceiving subjects. For Kant, time is the universal form of intuition — a feature of human sensibility in general, not reducible to any individual's mental contents. Kant's empirical realism (the objectivity of temporal relations for all possible experience) is precisely what Berkeley's idealism cannot deliver.

**A/B Theory and Kant**
Contemporary debate between A-theorists (who hold that temporal becoming, the moving "now," and the distinction between past, present, and future are real features of time) and B-theorists (who hold that only the earlier-than/later-than/simultaneous-with relations are fundamental). Kant's account has affinities with the B-theory, since transcendental ideality deflates the ontological significance of the "moving now." But Kant's insistence on empirical reality also supports something like A-theory validity at the phenomenal level. The debate about which reading is correct remains alive in contemporary Kant scholarship.
