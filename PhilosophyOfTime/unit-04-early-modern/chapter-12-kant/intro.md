# Chapter 12: Kant

---

## Chapter Introduction

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) is, by many accounts, the most important philosopher of the last three centuries. His *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781, revised 1787) is the pivotal work in modern philosophy: it synthesized the rationalist and empiricist traditions, diagnosed where both went wrong, and proposed a revolutionary account of the relationship between the mind and the world. For the philosophy of time, the *Critique* contains Kant's most important contribution: the account of time as the *form of inner sense* — neither a feature of things as they are in themselves nor a mere fiction of the imagination, but the a priori structure through which all experience is organized.

Kant's position is genuinely difficult, and it is easy to misread. He is not saying that time is "in the mind" in the sense of being a mere mental fiction or an illusion. He is making a more precise and more interesting claim: time is a form of *intuition* — a structural feature of how the mind is equipped to receive experience — that is a priori (not derived from experience) and yet empirically real (genuinely applicable to all objects of possible experience). This combination of a priori structure with empirical applicability is the central achievement of Kant's transcendental philosophy.

The chapter proceeds through five sections. Section 12.1 examines the "Transcendental Aesthetic" of the *Critique* — the analysis of space and time as forms of pure intuition. Section 12.2 examines Kant's distinction between the empirical reality and the transcendental ideality of time, and the objections it faces. Section 12.3 turns to the Schematism and the Analogies of Experience — Kant's account of how time mediates between pure categories (like causality) and empirical experience. Section 12.4 examines the First Antinomy — the apparent contradiction between the thesis that the world had a beginning in time and the antithesis that it did not. Section 12.5 briefly examines post-Kantian developments: Fichte, Hegel, and the radical temporalization of idealism.

Kant requires patience. He invented much of his own technical vocabulary, and it takes time to learn what the terms mean. But the investment is justified: Kant's account of time is more comprehensive and more carefully argued than any previous treatment, and it defined the terms of discussion for all subsequent philosophy of time. Understanding Kant is not optional for serious students of the philosophy of time.
