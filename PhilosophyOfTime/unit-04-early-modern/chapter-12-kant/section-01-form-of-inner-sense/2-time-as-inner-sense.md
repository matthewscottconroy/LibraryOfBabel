# 12.1.2 — Time as the Form of Inner Sense

---

## Inner and Outer Sense

Kant distinguishes between outer sense and inner sense. *Outer sense* is the faculty through which we represent objects outside us — the spatial world of physical things. *Inner sense* is the faculty through which we represent our own inner states — our thoughts, feelings, memories, and experiences.

Space is the form of outer sense: we can only represent outer objects in spatial terms (as having location, extension, shape). Time is the form of inner sense: we can only represent our inner states as having temporal order — occurring earlier or later, lasting longer or shorter, following or preceding each other.

But Kant makes a stronger and more surprising claim: time is not only the form of inner sense but also the condition of all outer experience. Even when we represent outer objects — tables, trees, mountains — we must represent them in time: we represent them as persisting through time, as occurring at times, as changing over time. Space is only the form of outer objects; time is the form of all objects whatsoever, including outer ones. "Time is the formal condition a priori of all appearances whatsoever" (Kant, *Critique* A34/B50, trans. Kemp Smith 1929: 75).

## Why Time Has Priority Over Space

The reason for time's priority is that all experience — whether of outer objects or of inner states — occurs through representations, and representations are themselves inner states. My representation of the tree outside my window is itself an inner event — a mental occurrence. As an inner event, it occurs in time. Therefore, all outer experience (which is mediated through inner representations) must be temporal.

This is not the claim that outer objects are themselves temporal features of the mind. The tree is not in my mind. But my *representation* of the tree is in my mind, and it occurs in time. Since all access to outer objects is through inner representations, and inner representations are temporal, time conditions all outer experience.

## The Consequences for Mathematics

Kant holds that arithmetic is synthetic a priori because it deals with the pure form of temporal succession. When we count — 1, 2, 3, ... — we are enumerating units in a sequence, and sequence is temporal. The necessity of arithmetic truths (5 + 7 = 12 is necessarily true) reflects the a priori structure of temporal succession, not any merely empirical regularity.

This connection between arithmetic and time is one of the more controversial aspects of Kant's position. Contemporary philosophers of mathematics have largely rejected the idea that arithmetic is grounded in time. But the general point — that the structure of our mathematical knowledge reflects a priori structural features of our cognition — has been enormously influential.

## Inner Sense and Self-Knowledge

There is an important complication in Kant's account of inner sense. Time is the form of inner sense — the form through which we represent our own inner states. But Kant insists that inner sense gives us access to ourselves only as we *appear* to ourselves, not as we are in ourselves. Just as outer sense gives us access to objects as they appear (in space, in time) rather than as they are in themselves (the *Ding an sich*), inner sense gives us access to our own mental states as they appear in time rather than as they are in themselves.

This means that even our introspective access to our own minds is conditioned by temporal form: we represent our thoughts as earlier and later, as passing in succession, as lasting for durations. Whether our mental lives "in themselves" — from the perspective of the thing-in-itself — have a temporal structure is something we cannot know. This is a radical limitation on self-knowledge that follows from Kant's general framework.
