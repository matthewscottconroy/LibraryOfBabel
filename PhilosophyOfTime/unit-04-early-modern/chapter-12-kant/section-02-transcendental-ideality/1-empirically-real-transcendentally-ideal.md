# 12.2.1 — Empirically Real and Transcendentally Ideal

---

## The Twofold Claim

Kant states the twofold character of time at *Critique* A35–36/B52:

> "Time is therefore given a priori. In time alone is actuality of appearances possible at all. Appearances may, one and all, vanish; but time itself (as the universal condition of their possibility) cannot itself be removed... Our assertions in regard to time are therefore empirically valid, that is, are valid in regard to all objects of experience. But, since we have to do only with appearances, we deny that time has absolute reality, that is, we deny that it belongs to things, abstracted from all conditions of sensibility. What, then, is left? This, that time is a pure form of sensible intuition. Time, to be sure, is valid only for objects of sensible intuition, whether subjective or objective, whether we adopt one view or the other; but it is not valid absolutely, that is to say, in regard to things in general, abstracted from our mode of intuiting them."

**Empirically real**: Every object of possible experience is temporal. There are no exceptions — no empirical objects that are outside time. Any claim that appears to appeal to temporal concepts ("this happened before that," "this process lasts longer than that") can be verified in principle by experience. Time is not a mere convention or a useful fiction; it is the genuine structure of the phenomenal world.

**Transcendentally ideal**: Time is a feature of objects as they appear to us — as phenomena — not a feature of objects as they are independently of our cognition — things-in-themselves (the *Ding an sich*). If we could somehow "step outside" our own cognitive apparatus and examine things as they are without any contribution from our sensible forms, time would not appear as a feature of them. Time is, in this sense, "ideal" — it is contributed by the cognizing subject rather than simply found in the independent world.

## What This Does Not Mean

Two common misreadings should be avoided.

**It does not mean time is illusory.** The phenomenal world is the real world for us — it is the world of experience, of science, of practical life. Kant is not saying that temporal claims are false; he is saying that their truth pertains to the phenomenal world, which is the only world we have access to. The empirical reality of time is genuine and uncompromised.

**It does not mean time is "merely subjective" in the sense of being person-relative.** Kant's forms of intuition are universal: all human beings share the same spatial and temporal forms of sensibility. What is contributed by the mind is not anything personal or idiosyncratic but the universal structure of human sensibility. Time is subjective only in the sense that it is contributed by the subject — the cognizing mind — not in the sense that it varies from person to person.

## The Contrast with Newton and Berkeley

Kant's position is carefully distinguished from both Newton and Berkeley.

**Against Newton**: Newton holds that time is absolutely real — it is a feature of things as they are independently of any mind's contribution. Kant denies this: time is empirically real but transcendentally ideal. We cannot know that time belongs to things-in-themselves.

**Against Berkeley**: Berkeley holds that time is a feature of minds — of the succession of ideas in mental life. This makes time dependent on particular mental processes and raises the question of what time is when no minds are active. Kant's time is contributed by the transcendental subject — the universal conditions of cognition — not by any particular mental process. It is also "objective" in the sense of being universally applicable: not depending on the contingent mental history of any particular mind.

Kant's position is thus a "transcendental idealism" about time: time is ideal (contributed by the subject) in the transcendental sense (contributed by the universal structure of cognition) but real (genuinely applicable to all possible experience) in the empirical sense.
