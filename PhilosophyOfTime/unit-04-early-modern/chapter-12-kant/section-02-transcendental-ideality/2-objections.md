# 12.2.2 — Objections to Transcendental Ideality

---

## The Objection from Illusion

The most common objection to Kant's transcendental ideality is that it makes time a kind of illusion — a feature we project onto the world rather than find in it. If time is contributed by the subject and is not a feature of things-in-themselves, then the temporal world we experience is not the "real" world but a mind-dependent representation of it. This seems to make time as illusory as the colors in our perception (which we now know are not properties of surfaces themselves but results of the interaction between surfaces and light and the visual system).

Kant's response distinguishes between empirical and transcendental levels of analysis. At the empirical level — the level at which we judge, act, and conduct science — temporal claims are fully objective and real. The tree really did begin to grow before I was born; the meeting really will start in ten minutes. These are not illusions; they are genuine features of the phenomenal world, confirmed by experience.

What Kant denies is not the temporal reality of the phenomenal world but the claim that this temporal reality is also the reality of things-in-themselves. The transcendental ideality of time does not make time illusory at the empirical level; it locates the ground of time in the form of our sensibility rather than in the independent world. This is a claim about the *source* of time's structure, not about its *reality* within experience (Allison 2004: 112–121).

## The Objection from Kantian Idealism vs. Berkeley's Idealism

A related objection is that Kant's position collapses into Berkeley's idealism: if time is contributed by the subject, then it is "in the mind," and if it is in the mind, it is subjective, and if it is subjective, then the objective temporal world is an illusion — which is Berkeley's position.

Kant addresses this directly: "Our theory of time, as against the empirical theory, by which we maintain that time is not an empirical concept, differs fundamentally from the theory of Berkeley, which regards time as a succession of perceptions having their reality solely in the mind" (*Critique*, General Remarks on the Transcendental Aesthetic, A35/B52; trans. Kemp Smith 1929, 76–77, paraphrased). The difference is this: Berkeley's time is subjective in the sense of depending on the contingent, particular mental history of individual minds. Kant's time is "objective" in the sense of being universally necessary — it is the form of all possible experience for all possible experiencers (within the framework of human sensibility). The universal necessity of time as a form of intuition distinguishes Kant's position from Berkeley's empirical idealism.

## The Thing-in-Itself and Temporal Applicability

A deeper objection concerns the concept of the "thing-in-itself" (*Ding an sich*). If things-in-themselves are outside time, how can they causally interact with our sensibility to produce representations? Causation, as Kant himself analyzes it, is a temporal concept. If the thing-in-itself caused my representation of the tree, this causal relation would have to be temporal — but things-in-themselves are outside time.

This is the "affection" problem in Kant's philosophy, and it is recognized as one of the most serious difficulties in his system. Various responses have been proposed: some interpreters (notably Allison) argue that "things-in-themselves" is not a reference to a separate realm of entities but a way of talking about the objects of experience when we abstract from the conditions of our cognition — a logical abstraction rather than a metaphysical entity. Others (notably Strawson) have argued that Kant's system is inconsistent at this point.

## What the Transcendental Ideality of Time Gets Right

Despite these difficulties, Kant's account captures something important. The claim that time as experienced — the temporal structure of experience — is not simply "read off" from an independently existing temporal world but involves a contribution from the structure of cognition is philosophically defensible. Contemporary neuroscience and cognitive science support the view that our experience of temporal order, duration, and the "specious present" involves active constructive processes by the nervous system and brain, not passive reception of pre-structured temporal information (Eagleman 2011: 58–72). In this sense, Kant's general thesis — that temporal experience involves contributions from the cognizing subject — is confirmed by empirical research, even if the specific Kantian account of a priori forms of intuition requires modification.
