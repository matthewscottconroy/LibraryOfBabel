# Section 12.2: Empirical Reality and Transcendental Ideality

---

## Section Introduction

Kant's account of time involves a distinction that is initially confusing but crucial: time is *empirically real* and *transcendentally ideal*. These two claims are not in contradiction; they operate at different levels of analysis. Understanding the distinction is the key to understanding Kant's position and appreciating how it differs from both Newton's absolute time and Berkeley's idealist time.

Briefly: empirical reality means that time is genuinely applicable to all objects of possible experience — it is not a fiction, not an illusion, not a merely subjective quirk. Transcendental ideality means that time is a feature of things-as-they-appear-to-us (phenomena), not a feature of things-in-themselves (noumena). The phenomenal world is genuinely temporal; the noumenal world (things as they are independently of our experience) is neither temporal nor non-temporal — temporal categories simply do not apply to it.

Section 12.2.1 examines the twofold claim in detail. Section 12.2.2 examines the major objections to transcendental ideality and Kant's responses.
