# 12.3.1 — The Schematism of the Pure Concepts

---

## The Problem

Kant identifies a fundamental heterogeneity between the pure categories of the understanding and empirical intuitions. Categories are purely intellectual — they are concepts derived from the structure of logical functions. Intuitions are sensory — they are presentations in space and time. How can concepts apply to intuitions?

The problem is not unique to Kant's system; it is a version of the general problem of how abstract thought applies to concrete experience. But Kant's formulation is precise: a pure category like "causality" is a logical function (the schema of hypothetical judgment: if X then Y) that has no temporal or spatial content at all. A particular causal judgment — "the fire caused the water to boil" — applies this pure function to temporal events. The mediating element is the *temporal schema*: the time-determination that gives the pure function its empirical content.

Kant states: "The schema is, properly, only the phenomenon, or sensible concept, of an object in agreement with the category... The schemata of the pure concepts of understanding are thus nothing but time-determinations a priori according to rules, and these rules relate to the time-series, the time-content, the time-order, and lastly the scope of time in respect of all possible objects" (Kant, *Critique* A145–146/B185–186, trans. Kemp Smith 1929: 184).

## The Temporal Schemata of the Categories

Kant provides temporal schemata for each category in the table of categories. The most important for the philosophy of time are:

**Substance** → **Permanence in time**: The schema of substance is the permanent, the persistent substrate that underlies change. We apply the concept of substance to experience through the temporal schema of permanence: substance is what remains the same while its properties change. "Substance is that which can only be presented in time as a substrate — that is, as something permanent, as long as other determinations succeed one another" (Kant, *Critique* A144/B183).

**Causality** → **Necessary temporal succession (rule-governed)**: The schema of causality is the rule-governed succession of states — the schema of "something following according to a rule." We apply the concept of causality to experience through the temporal schema of necessary succession: A causes B means that whenever A occurs, B follows according to a rule. The *necessary* character of the succession — as opposed to merely habitual succession — is what distinguishes genuine causation from mere temporal correlation (Kant, *Critique* A144/B183–184).

**Community (reciprocal causation)** → **Simultaneous determination**: The schema of community is the simultaneous mutual interaction of substances — the schema of substances that coexist by determining each other's states. We apply the concept of community through the temporal schema of simultaneity: A and B are in community if their states are mutually and simultaneously determining (Kant, *Critique* A145/B184).

## What the Schematism Achieves

The Schematism performs two functions simultaneously. **First**, it explains how pure categories can apply to experience: categories have empirical content through their temporal schemata. **Second**, it shows that the application of categories to experience is always temporal: we only judge things to be substances, causes, or in community through temporal determinations — permanence, necessary succession, simultaneity.

This second point is significant for the philosophy of time: it means that the basic conceptual structure of experience — the categories through which we organize experience into objects, events, and causal processes — is inherently temporal. The objective structure of experience is a temporal structure. Kant is thus establishing a very strong connection between rationality, objectivity, and time: we can only think about objects as objects if we think about them as subject to temporal determinations.
