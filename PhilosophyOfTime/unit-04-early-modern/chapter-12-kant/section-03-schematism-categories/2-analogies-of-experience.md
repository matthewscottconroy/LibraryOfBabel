# 12.3.2 — The Analogies of Experience

---

## The Framework

The Analogies of Experience (*Critique* A176–218/B218–265) are three principles concerning the temporal structure of experience. They are "analogies" in Kant's technical sense: they are not constitutive principles (that directly determine what happens) but regulative principles (that specify the conditions under which objective temporal experience is possible). They assert the necessary temporal conditions for objective experience: that there must be permanent substance through time, causal succession, and simultaneous community.

Each Analogy corresponds to a temporal relation (permanence, succession, simultaneity) and a category (substance, causality, community). The three together constitute what Kant calls "the formal conditions of objective experience."

## The First Analogy: Permanence of Substance

The First Analogy states: "In all change of appearances substance is permanent, and its quantum in nature is neither increased nor diminished" (Kant, *Critique* B224, trans. Kemp Smith 1929: 212). Substance persists through all temporal change; what changes are its properties, not the substance itself.

Kant's argument for this principle is that objective experience requires a temporal framework — a way of objectively determining when events occur and how long they last. The only way to establish objective temporal positions and durations is by reference to something permanent: if everything changed simultaneously and completely, there would be no fixed reference point against which to measure change. Permanent substance is the necessary condition for the possibility of objective temporal determination (Kant, *Critique* A182–183/B225–226).

This argument has an interesting connection to the relationism debate: Kant seems to be saying that we need something permanent — analogous to Leibniz's "identities" or Newton's absolute time — to serve as the reference for measuring change. But for Kant, this permanent reference is provided by the form of our experience (the concept of permanent substance as a schema), not by any independently existing entity.

## The Second Analogy: Causal Succession

The Second Analogy states: "All alterations take place in conformity with the law of the connection of cause and effect" (Kant, *Critique* B232, trans. Kemp Smith 1929: 218). Every event has a sufficient cause — every change in the states of substance follows from a prior state according to a causal rule.

Kant's argument for this principle is that objective temporal order requires causation. Without causation, the temporal order of events would be indeterminate: I could not tell whether event A occurred before or after event B, since both are "presented" in succession in my experience, and the order of my presentations might not match the objective order of the events. The objective temporal order of events is determined by their causal connections — causation is what makes temporal succession objective rather than merely the order of my perceptions (Kant, *Critique* A192/B237).

This is a significant response to Hume. Hume had argued that the idea of causation derives from observed temporal succession — we observe A followed by B and infer causal connection. Kant reverses this: the *objective* temporal succession of events is what requires causal connection, not the other way around. Without causation, we could not distinguish the objective order of events from the mere order of our perceptions.

## The Third Analogy: Simultaneous Community

The Third Analogy states: "All substances, in so far as they can be perceived to coexist in space, are in thoroughgoing reciprocal interaction" (Kant, *Critique* B256, trans. Kemp Smith 1929: 233). Substances that exist simultaneously stand in causal interaction with each other.

Kant's argument is that simultaneity — the perception that two states coexist — requires that each substance be acting on the other: if A and B were causally independent, I could have no basis for judging that they are simultaneous rather than successive. It is through causal interaction that the concept of simultaneity is given objective content.

## The Significance of the Analogies

The three Analogies together constitute Kant's account of the temporal structure of objective experience. They show that:

1. Objective temporal duration requires permanent substance (First Analogy)
2. Objective temporal succession requires causation (Second Analogy)
3. Objective temporal simultaneity requires community/interaction (Third Analogy)

This is a comprehensive account of how temporal concepts get their application to experience. It responds to both Hume's skepticism about causation and Leibniz's relational theory of time. For Kant, causal structure is not derived from temporal order (against Hume) but is rather the condition for determinate temporal order. And the temporal framework is not derived from event sequences alone (against Leibniz) but requires the categories of the understanding to be determined.
