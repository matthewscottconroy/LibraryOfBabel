# 12.4.1 — The First Antinomy: The World's Beginning in Time

---

## The Thesis and Antithesis

The First Antinomy appears at *Critique* A426/B454–A433/B461. Both sides are presented and argued with apparent validity.

**Thesis**: "The world has a beginning in time, and is also limited as regards space."

Kant's argument for the Thesis: "If we assume that the world has no beginning in time, then up to every given moment an eternity has elapsed, and there has passed away in the world an infinite series of successive states of things. Now the infinity of a series consists in the fact that it can never be completed through successive synthesis. It therefore follows that it is impossible for an infinite world-series to have passed away, and that a beginning of the world is therefore a necessary condition of the world's existence" (Kant, *Critique* A426/B454, trans. Kemp Smith 1929: 396–397).

**Antithesis**: "The world has no beginning, and no limits in space; it is infinite as regards both time and space."

Kant's argument for the Antithesis: "For let us assume that it has a beginning. Since the beginning is an existence which is preceded by a time in which the thing is not, there must have been a preceding time in which the world was not, i.e. an empty time. Now no coming to be of a thing is possible in an empty time, because no part of such a time possesses, as compared with any other, a distinguishing condition of existence rather than non-existence; and this applies whether the thing is supposed to arise of itself or through some other cause. In the world many series of things can, indeed, begin; but the world itself cannot have a beginning, and is therefore infinite in respect of past time" (Kant, *Critique* A427/B455, trans. Kemp Smith 1929: 397).

## Analyzing the Arguments

Both arguments have genuine force, and Kant intends them as genuinely valid arguments for contradictory conclusions.

The Thesis argument is a version of an argument that was also pressed in medieval philosophy (Augustine's argument that an infinite past could never have been traversed). If the past is infinite, then before any given moment, an infinite series of moments has elapsed. But an actually infinite series cannot be "completed" — you cannot traverse an infinite series and arrive at its end. Therefore, the past cannot be infinite: there must be a first moment.

The Antithesis argument is a version of Augustine's own point, directed in the other direction: if the world had a beginning, there was a time before the beginning when the world did not exist — an empty time. But in an empty time, there is nothing to distinguish one moment from another: no sufficient reason for the world to begin *now* rather than at any other time. So the beginning would be arbitrary — a violation of the PSR. Therefore, the world cannot have had a beginning.

## Kant's Resolution

Kant's resolution is to argue that both sides are making a common error: they both presuppose that the universe as a whole — the "world" taken as a totality — is an object that can be characterized as having or lacking a temporal beginning. But for Kant, the universe as a whole is not an object of possible experience. We can only experience finite portions of the universe at a time; the universe as a totality is an idea of reason, not an object of intuition.

Once we recognize that "the universe as a whole" is not an object of possible experience, the question of whether it had a beginning or is eternal can have no determinate answer. Both the Thesis and the Antithesis try to answer a question that cannot be answered — because it is not a question about any possible object of experience. The antinomy is dissolved by recognizing that it arises from a "transcendental illusion" — the illusion that reason can extend its principles to things beyond all possible experience (Kant, *Critique* A500/B528–A509/B537).
