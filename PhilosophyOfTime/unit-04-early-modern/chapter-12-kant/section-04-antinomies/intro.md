# Section 12.4: The First Antinomy

---

## Section Introduction

The Antinomies of Pure Reason are one of the most dramatic and philosophically original sections of the *Critique*. An "antinomy" for Kant is a genuine contradiction that pure reason generates when it applies its principles beyond the limits of possible experience. Reason (in Kant's sense) has a natural drive to complete its explanations — to ask "why?" indefinitely until it reaches some ultimate, unconditioned ground. But this drive, when applied to questions about the universe as a whole, generates both apparently valid proofs of opposite conclusions.

The First Antinomy concerns the beginning of the world in time. The Thesis argues that the world must have had a beginning in time; the Antithesis argues that it could not have had a beginning — that time must be infinite. Both are argued with apparent philosophical validity. Kant's resolution is that both the Thesis and the Antithesis rest on a false presupposition: that the question admits of a determinate answer from the perspective of things-in-themselves. Since the phenomenal world is all we can experience and know, the question of whether the universe as a whole had a temporal beginning is not something we can settle.

Section 12.4.1 examines the First Antinomy. Section 12.4.2 considers its contemporary relevance to cosmological debates.
