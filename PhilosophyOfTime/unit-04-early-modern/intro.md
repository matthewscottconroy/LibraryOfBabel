# Unit IV: Early Modern Philosophy of Time

---

## Unit Introduction

The seventeenth century produced one of the most dramatic transformations in intellectual history: the Scientific Revolution, which replaced the Aristotelian picture of the cosmos with the mechanical philosophy of Descartes, Galileo, Newton, and their successors. This transformation had profound consequences for the philosophy of time. Where medieval thinkers had worked within a framework shaped by Aristotle's *Physics* and supplemented by theological concerns about eternity and creation, early modern thinkers faced new questions: What is the relationship between the time described by mathematical physics and the time experienced by conscious beings? Is time an absolute container — a fixed background against which all events occur — or is it merely the order of events in relation to each other? Can the mathematics of mechanics tell us what time fundamentally is?

These questions were not merely academic. They had implications for theology, for the foundations of mechanics, and for the emerging conception of nature as a closed system governed by mathematical laws. The answers proposed by the major early modern thinkers — Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz, Newton, Locke, Berkeley, and Hume — defined the terms of the philosophy of time that Kant then synthesized and transformed in the greatest system of the period.

The unit is organized around three chapters. Chapter 10 covers the rationalist tradition: Descartes's account of duration as a mode of substance and the doctrine of continuous creation; Spinoza's remarkable claim that time, as ordinarily conceived, is a product of the imagination rather than of adequate knowledge; and Leibniz's relational theory of time, developed in opposition to Newton's absolute time and culminating in the famous Leibniz-Clarke correspondence. Chapter 11 turns to Newton's own account of absolute time and examines both its philosophical foundations and the major empiricist critiques — from Locke's account of our idea of time to Berkeley's skepticism about absolute temporal frameworks to Hume's analysis of time as the manner of the appearance of impressions. Chapter 12, the climax of the unit and arguably of the entire book to this point, examines Kant's revolutionary account of time as the form of inner sense — neither a feature of things in themselves nor a mere mental fiction but the a priori condition of all experience.

Several themes recur throughout the unit. One is the opposition between absolute and relational accounts of time: does time exist independently of events and processes (Newton), or is it merely the order of events (Leibniz, the Epicurean tradition, Mach)? A second is the question of whether time depends on mind: is there time in a universe with no conscious beings (Newton, Descartes) or is time constituted by or dependent on mental activity (Spinoza, Hume, Kant)? A third is the question of the relationship between physical time and phenomenological time: is the time of mathematical physics the same as the time of experience, or do we need separate accounts for each?

These themes were not invented in the early modern period; they are continuous with the ancient and medieval discussions of Units II and III. What changed in the seventeenth century was the urgency and the precision with which they were pressed, driven by the demands of a new physics that needed a clear account of what time is in order to know what its equations were about.
