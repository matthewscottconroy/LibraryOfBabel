# Chapter 13 Exercises: McTaggart and the Unreality of Time

---

## Comprehension and Analysis

**1.** State the A/B distinction in your own words without using McTaggart's terminology. Then return to his terminology and check: did your formulation capture the essential difference? What did you get right? What, if anything, did you miss?

**2.** McTaggart claims that the B-series alone cannot account for change. Construct the strongest possible objection to this claim from the perspective of a B-theorist. Then construct the strongest reply to that objection from a McTaggart-sympathizer. Which side do you find more convincing, and why?

**3.** The regress argument turns on the claim that relativizing A-properties to times merely pushes the problem up a level. Is this claim correct? Identify exactly where the regress gets its grip, and try to formulate a response that genuinely blocks it rather than merely delaying it.

**4.** Prior argues that A-properties are not genuine properties but indexical expressions of a speaker's temporal perspective. Is this response available to someone who holds that A-series facts are *objective* — i.e., not dependent on any speaker or observer? Explain your answer carefully.

**5.** Compare McTaggart's distinction between A-series and B-series facts with the grammatical distinction between tensed and tenseless sentences. Are these the same distinction, or do they come apart? Give an example of a tensed sentence that expresses a B-series fact, if such a thing is possible.

---

## Synthetic and Argumentative

**6.** Dummett argues that the regress shows not the incoherence of A-theory but the irreducibility of tensed concepts. Write a one-page response from McTaggart's perspective: would he regard Dummett's reconstruction as a refutation of his argument, an endorsement of it, or a misreading of it?

**7.** Suppose you accept McTaggart's regress argument. Does his specific positive conclusion — that the C-series is real and time is an illusion — follow from the argument, or is it an additional commitment? What other conclusions might one draw from the same argument?

**8.** "The A-series is the only way to capture what is meant by the passage of time; but the concept of passage is itself confused." Evaluate this claim. Does admitting that the passage of time is a central temporal concept commit one to the reality of A-series properties?

---

## Lab and Observational Exercises

**9. Lab — Temporal Vocabulary Audit.** For a period of one hour during an ordinary day, keep a running note every time you or someone near you uses an A-series expression (words like "now," "just," "soon," "already," "still," "not yet") versus a B-series expression (words like "before," "after," "earlier," "later," "at the same time as"). Tally the results. What does the distribution suggest about whether ordinary temporal thought is primarily A-theoretic or B-theoretic? Write a short reflection (200–300 words) on what you found.

**10. Lab — The "Now" Experience.** Sit quietly for five minutes with a timer. Every thirty seconds, focus your attention directly on the experience of "now" — not on what is in the present, but on *present-ness* itself. Can you isolate it? Does it feel like a property, a perspective, a boundary, or something else? After the five minutes, write a brief phenomenological report (150–200 words). Does your experience bear on the metaphysical dispute about whether A-properties are real?

**11. Lab — McTaggart's B-Series Calendar.** Take any significant personal event from your past (a graduation, a birthday, a first day of school). Write two descriptions of it: one using only B-series language (no tenses, only "before/after/simultaneous with" relations to other events), and one using only A-series language. Which description feels more *informative* to you personally? Which feels more accurate? Does the difference between your reactions track a philosophical point, or is it merely psychological?

---

## Reflection

**12.** McTaggart concludes that time is unreal. Without yet knowing the major alternatives (B-theory, presentism, etc.), write a half-page reflection: does the conclusion strike you as absurd, possible, or somewhere in between? What would have to be true for you to take the claim seriously? What cannot you give up in your conception of time, even at philosophical gunpoint?
