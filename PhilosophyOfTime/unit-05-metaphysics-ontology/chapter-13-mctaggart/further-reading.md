## Annotated Entries

**McTaggart, J. M. E. 1908. "The Unreality of Time." *Mind* 17 (68): 457–474.**
The original source and the unavoidable starting point. McTaggart's prose is clear, unhurried, and often witty. Read it at least twice: once for the overview, once tracking the exact moves. Pay special attention to pages 468–469, where the regress argument is set out. Do not let the conclusion bias you against the quality of the argument.

**Prior, Arthur N. 1967. *Past, Present and Future*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.**
The foundational text of tense logic, and the most important response to McTaggart's regress from an A-theoretic direction. Prior's technical apparatus is genuinely illuminating, but the first two chapters are accessible without formal logic training. Prior writes with verve and genuine philosophical passion.

**Prior, Arthur N. 1959. "Thank Goodness That's Over." *Philosophy* 34 (128): 12–17.**
A short, beautifully written paper arguing that tensed facts are irreducible because of their existential and emotional significance. When you feel relieved that a painful experience is *over* — not just that it stands in a certain B-series relation to you — you are, Prior argues, responding to an A-series fact. Essential reading.

**Mellor, D. H. 1998. *Real Time II*. London: Routledge.**
The definitive contemporary B-theoretic response to McTaggart and to A-theory in general. Mellor's arguments are technically sharp and philosophically careful. He distinguishes the meaning of tensed claims from their truth conditions in a way that allows him to take tensed language seriously while denying the existence of A-properties. Chapter 1 is the best short statement of the B-theory available.

**Dummett, Michael. 1960. "A Defence of McTaggart's Proof of the Unreality of Time." *Philosophical Review* 69 (4): 497–504.**
A short but philosophically dense reinterpretation of McTaggart's argument that shifts the emphasis from contradiction to the irreducibility of tensed concepts. Dummett's reconstruction is controversial — many think it does not do what McTaggart wanted — but it opens up important questions about the relationship between metaphysics and meaning.

**Lowe, E. J. 1998. *The Possibility of Metaphysics: Substance, Identity, and Time*. Oxford: Clarendon Press. Chapters 4–5.**
One of the most careful and technically proficient defenses of A-theory available. Lowe argues that McTaggart's regress is benign rather than vicious and that A-series facts are genuinely irreducible. His account of temporal properties is embedded within a broader metaphysics of substance and property that rewards attention.

**Le Poidevin, Robin, and Murray MacBeath, eds. 1993. *The Philosophy of Time*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
An essential anthology containing most of the key papers, including McTaggart's original and selected responses. The editors' introduction is one of the clearest overviews of the debate available. Start here if you want a map of the terrain before diving into individual papers.

**Broad, C. D. 1938. *An Examination of McTaggart's Philosophy*. Vol. II. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. Chapter 38.**
The most thorough and technically precise engagement with McTaggart's arguments from any period. Broad is a careful, sympathetic critic: he finds much to admire and much to reject. His analysis of the regress argument is more detailed than anything else in the literature of its period.

**Oaklander, L. Nathan. 2012. "A- and B-Theories of Time." In *The Oxford Handbook of Philosophy of Time*, edited by Craig Callender, 41–76. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
An excellent up-to-date overview of the debate, with careful attention to both historical and contemporary positions. Oaklander is a committed B-theorist, so read with that in mind, but his presentation of A-theory positions is fair and thorough.

**Le Poidevin, Robin. 2007. *The Images of Time: An Essay on Temporal Representation*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
A wide-ranging exploration of how we represent time, bridging philosophy of mind, metaphysics, and empirical psychology. Le Poidevin examines what it means to perceive time, how temporal concepts are formed, and how representations of time can be both accurate and misleading. An ideal companion to the more purely metaphysical discussions.

**Dyke, Heather. 2002. "Tensed Meaning: A Tenseless Account." *Journal of Philosophical Research* 27: 67–83.**
An important paper arguing that tensed sentences can be given truth conditions in purely tenseless terms, without losing any genuine semantic content. Dyke is one of the clearest defenders of the B-theory position on language and meaning, and this paper is a good entry point into the technical debate about tensed versus tenseless semantics.
