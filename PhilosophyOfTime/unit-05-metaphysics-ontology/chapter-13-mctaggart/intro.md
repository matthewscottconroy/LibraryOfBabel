# Chapter 13: McTaggart and the Unreality of Time

---

## Chapter Introduction

John McTaggart Ellis McTaggart published "The Unreality of Time" in *Mind* in 1908, and it remains, over a century later, one of the most influential and most argued-over papers in the entire history of analytic philosophy. Its central claim — that time is not real — strikes most readers on first encounter as obviously wrong, probably confused, and certainly avoidable. What sustains its influence is the discovery, upon closer examination, that the argument is neither obviously wrong, nor confused, nor avoidable except at significant cost.

McTaggart's gift to philosophy was not primarily his conclusion. It was his conceptual framework. Before McTaggart, philosophers who discussed time often talked past each other because they were working with different, tacit conceptions of what temporal relations fundamentally are. McTaggart made those conceptions explicit. He distinguished two fundamentally different ways of ordering temporal positions: the A-series, which orders events as past, present, or future (and as more or less past, more or less future), and the B-series, which orders events as earlier than, simultaneous with, or later than one another. These look similar but are, McTaggart argued, profoundly different in character — and determining which is more fundamental has consequences for everything else in the philosophy of time.

This chapter proceeds through four stations. We begin with the A/B distinction itself, getting it sharp enough to work with. We then examine McTaggart's argument that the A-series is fundamental — that the B-series alone cannot capture what is distinctive about time, namely, that it involves genuine change. We then turn to his regress argument: the claim that the A-series, despite being necessary, is also contradictory, and that attempts to resolve the contradiction only generate a vicious infinite regress. Finally, we examine his conclusion: that time is unreal, and that what we experience as temporal change is an appearance generated by an underlying atemporal reality.

Each of these moves deserves careful scrutiny. Some philosophers find the regress argument decisive; others find it dissoluble. What no one seriously disputes is that engaging with McTaggart's framework is the unavoidable starting point for contemporary work on the metaphysics of time.
