# 13.1.1 — McTaggart's Distinction: A-Series, B-Series, and C-Series

---

In 1908, J. M. E. McTaggart opened "The Unreality of Time" with a deceptively clean observation: positions in time can be distinguished in two fundamentally different ways, and conflating these two ways has been the source of considerable confusion in the philosophy of time. Let us follow his lead and distinguish them carefully.

## The B-Series

The **B-series** is the ordering of temporal positions — events, moments, or states of affairs — by the relations of *earlier than*, *later than*, and *simultaneous with*. These relations are, McTaggart stressed, permanent and tenseless. The Battle of Hastings is earlier than the French Revolution. That is a B-series fact, and it does not change. It was true before either event occurred (in the sense that those events were always going to stand in that relation), it is true now, and it will always be true. The B-series relation holds timelessly between positions in time: it holds, or it doesn't; it does not become true, or cease to be true, or fluctuate. If A is earlier than B, it is earlier than B simpliciter — not earlier than B *at t₁* but later at *t₂*.

Notice what the B-series gives you. It gives you a fully determinate ordering of all temporal positions. You can say that event E₁ comes before E₂, E₂ before E₃, and so on. You can assign a direction — from earlier to later — and you can place events within a structured sequence. What you cannot do, within the B-series alone, is identify which of these events is *present*, which is *past*, and which is *future*. The B-series gives you order without tense.

This is crucial. The B-series facts are sometimes called "tenseless" facts or "eternal" facts because they do not shift with the perspective of the observer. That E₁ is earlier than E₂ is not a fact about when *you* are. It is a fact about the intrinsic relational structure of the temporal manifold, independent of any viewpoint (McTaggart 1908: 458).

## The A-Series

The **A-series** is the ordering of temporal positions according to their properties of being *past*, *present*, or *future* — and of being *more past* or *less past*, *more future* or *less future*, relative to the present moment. Unlike B-series relations, A-series properties are constantly changing. An event that is presently future becomes present, then past, then ever more remotely past. What is now distant future will become near future, then present, then near past, then distant past. The A-series is inherently dynamic: its characterization of events changes as time passes.

Notice that this is a different kind of change from what the B-series records. The B-series ordering of events does not change: E₁ is always earlier than E₂. What changes, in the A-series, is which events count as present — and therefore which events are past (earlier than the present) and which are future (later than the present). The A-series is, in effect, a B-series seen from a moving vantage point — the vantage point of *now* (McTaggart 1908: 458–459).

The contrast can be put sharply: B-series positions are identified by their relations to each other; A-series positions are identified by their relation to *the present*. "Earlier than the First World War" is a B-series characterization; "a hundred years in the past" is an A-series characterization that changes in content as the present moves.

McTaggart's language for this distinction is that B-series relations are "permanent" whereas A-series properties involve the "continual realization and extinction" of temporal properties as moments pass from future to present to past. The A-series captures something the B-series cannot: the sense in which time is *passing*, not merely ordered.

## The C-Series

McTaggart also introduces a third notion, the **C-series**, which receives less attention in contemporary discussions but is important for understanding his overall argument. The C-series is the ordering of temporal positions *without* temporal direction — a bare serial ordering, like the arrangement of points on a line before we decide which direction to call "positive." The C-series gives you the positions in an order, but it does not, by itself, tell you which direction is earlier and which is later.

The C-series becomes relevant in McTaggart's conclusion. He argues that time as ordinarily conceived — with both B-series order and A-series dynamism — is unreal. What is real, for McTaggart, is the C-series: the atemporal underlying order that generates the *appearance* of temporal direction and flow (McTaggart 1927, Vol. II: §§347–350). The A-series and B-series are, in his view, features of how we experience or represent the C-series, not features of the C-series itself.

## Logical Structure of the Distinction

Let us be as precise as possible about the logical form of each series:

**B-series facts** are two-place relational facts of the form: *E₁ is earlier than E₂*, *E₁ is simultaneous with E₂*, *E₁ is later than E₂*. These are timeless, or tenseless: they do not change their truth value over time.

**A-series facts** are one-place predications of the form: *E is past*, *E is present*, *E is future*. These are genuinely tensed: they change their truth value over time. What is future becomes present; what is present becomes past. The A-properties are time-indexed in the sense that which events have which properties shifts as the present moment shifts.

One immediate consequence: the two series are not inter-definable without remainder. You cannot construct the A-series from B-series facts alone, because B-series facts do not tell you where *now* is. And you cannot, McTaggart argues, construct the B-series from A-series facts alone, because the A-series gives you tense without giving you a permanent ordering independent of *now*.

Whether each series requires the other — whether one is more fundamental — is precisely what is at issue in the next section. But the distinction itself is clear, and it provides the essential vocabulary for contemporary philosophy of time. The debate between "A-theorists" and "B-theorists" that dominates the literature is a direct descendant of McTaggart's 1908 paper.
