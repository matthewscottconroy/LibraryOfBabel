# 13.1.2 — Examples and Analysis: Making the Distinction Vivid

---

Philosophical distinctions only become useful when they connect to concrete cases. The A/B distinction is no exception. Let us work through some examples with enough care that the distinction becomes genuinely operational — something you can apply on your own, not just a pair of labels you can recite.

## The Battle of Hastings and the Moon Landings

Take two events: the Battle of Hastings (1066 CE) and the Apollo 11 Moon landing (1969 CE). Now consider two different kinds of statement about them.

**Statement 1:** The Battle of Hastings is earlier than the Moon landing.

This is a B-series statement. It records a permanent relational fact between two temporal positions. It was true in 1200 CE, it is true in 2026 CE, and it will be true in 3000 CE — if anything is true in 3000 CE. The relation "earlier than" between these two events does not change over time. It holds timelessly, or, if you prefer, it holds at every time. Notice that this statement does not mention any viewpoint, any observer, any "now." It is a purely objective, perspective-independent characterization of the temporal structure.

**Statement 2:** The Battle of Hastings is now past.

This is an A-series statement. It characterizes the Battle of Hastings with an A-property: being past. And this property *did* change — not in the sense that the Battle itself changed, but in the sense that its A-series characterization changed over time. During the battle, it was present. Just before it, it was future. In the centuries since, it has been past — and increasingly remotely past as the present moment recedes from it. The property of being past is not a permanent, intrinsic feature of the event; it is a relational feature involving the position of the event relative to the present.

Now here is a subtlety worth sitting with: Statement 2, as uttered *now*, expresses a truth. But the same sentence uttered in 1050 CE would have expressed a falsehood. The sentence "The Battle of Hastings is now past" shifts its truth value depending on *when* it is uttered. That is the mark of a genuinely tensed, A-series statement: its truth value is not fixed, but depends on temporal context.

Compare this with Statement 1. "The Battle of Hastings is earlier than the Moon landing," uttered in any century whatsoever, expresses the same truth. The B-series statement has a fixed truth value; the A-series statement does not.

## The Structural Difference

This example reveals the structural difference McTaggart had in mind. The B-series is a *relational* ordering among events: it tells you how events stand to each other. The A-series is an *intrinsic* characterization of events in relation to *now*: it tells you how events stand to the present moment.

Or, from a different angle: the B-series is like a map. A map shows you where things are relative to each other — this city is north of that city, this river flows into that sea — but it does not tell you where *you* are. The A-series is like a map with a "You Are Here" marker. It locates you within the temporal structure and describes everything else relative to that location.

The analogy is imperfect — we will see why when we discuss the A-theorist's claim that "now" is *not* merely indexical — but it is useful for getting the structural point in view.

## Why the Distinction Matters

You might wonder whether this is just two ways of saying the same thing. After all, an event's being in the past is equivalent to its being earlier than the present moment — which sounds like a B-series statement. Can we not translate every A-series statement into a B-series statement?

McTaggart's answer is: no, and here is why. The A-series and B-series cannot be reduced to each other because they are answering different kinds of questions. The B-series answers questions of the form: "What is the ordering of events?" The A-series answers questions of the form: "Which events are happening *now*?" That second question has no answer within the B-series alone. The B-series gives you the structure; the A-series locates the present within it.

This matters enormously for the question of *change*. Consider what it means for something to change. A poker changes from cold to hot. A student changes from ignorant to knowledgeable. A leaf changes from green to yellow. All these involve something possessing a property and then *not* possessing it. But notice: in the B-series, there is no change in this sense. The poker is cold at t₁ and hot at t₂, and those are permanent facts. The B-series does not record the poker *becoming* hot; it records the co-existence of (poker-is-cold-at-t₁) and (poker-is-hot-at-t₂) as timeless facts about a four-dimensional manifold. Is that change? Or is it just variation across the manifold?

McTaggart argued it is not genuine change — and that genuine change requires the A-series. We will examine that argument in detail in Section 13.2. But you can already see the shape of the problem: if the A-series is necessary for real change, and the A-series turns out to be incoherent (as McTaggart argues in Section 13.3), then change — and therefore time itself — is in trouble.

## A Second Example: The Lecture and the Exam

Consider a more immediate case. Right now, as you read this page, you might have a lecture scheduled for tomorrow and an exam scheduled three weeks from now. These are your A-series facts: the lecture is (relatively) near future; the exam is (relatively) far future.

Now, from a B-series perspective, it is true that this page-reading event is earlier than the lecture event, which is earlier than the exam event. That ordering is fixed. But the A-series characterizations — near future, far future — are changing as you live through the days between now and those events. The lecture will become present (when it happens), then past (the morning after). The exam will become less future, then present, then past. This progressive "movement" of events through the A-series — from future to present to past — is exactly what it *feels like* to live in time.

Here is the crucial observation: that feeling has no counterpart in the B-series. The B-series is static. The map showing this-page-reading before the lecture before the exam does not "move" or change. All those facts are just there, fixed, eternal. If you want to capture what it is like to *experience* time — to live through the approach of the exam, to feel the lecture recede into the past — you need the A-series. The B-series alone gives you temporal structure without temporal life.

This is why McTaggart's distinction has proved so durable. It isolates exactly what is philosophically distinctive about time, as opposed to merely spatial or atemporal orderings. The philosophical question of whether the A-series is real, or fundamental, or coherent, is precisely the question of whether time has that distinctive character — or whether the appearance of "flowing" time is just that: an appearance.
