# 13.2.1 — Why the A-Series Is Fundamental: Change and the Limits of B-Series Time

---

McTaggart's argument for the primacy of the A-series over the B-series rests on a deceptively simple claim: the B-series alone cannot account for change, and change is essential to time. If time without change is not genuinely time — or at least not the time we care about — then the B-series alone is insufficient.

This argument moves quickly in McTaggart's original presentation (1908: 459–460), and it requires a careful unpacking to assess its force. Let us reconstruct it step by step.

## What Change Requires

What does it mean for something to change? The intuitive answer is that something possesses a property at one time and lacks it at another. The sky changes from cloudy to clear; the temperature changes from low to high; a person changes from young to old. Change, on this picture, involves the acquisition and loss of properties over time.

Now consider how the B-series represents this. On a B-series picture, the facts are: *the sky is cloudy at t₁* and *the sky is clear at t₂*, where t₁ is earlier than t₂. These are two permanent, tenseless facts about the temporal manifold. They represent variation across time, in the same way that a spatial cross-section of a mountain represents variation across space. But — and this is McTaggart's point — *do they represent change*?

Consider the spatial analogy. A mountain is high in the middle and low at the edges. That is variation, but it is not change — the mountain is not *becoming* higher or *becoming* lower; it simply has different heights at different spatial positions. McTaggart's worry is that the B-series represents time in exactly this way: as a dimension along which properties vary, without anything genuinely *happening*. The B-series manifold contains (sky-at-t₁-is-cloudy) and (sky-at-t₂-is-clear) as co-existing, eternal facts — but it contains no *transition* from cloudy to clear, no moment of becoming, no event of changing. The "change" has been replaced by a static pattern of variation (McTaggart 1908: 460).

This objection is sometimes called the objection to "Cambridge change" or the "pseudo-change" problem. D.H. Mellor acknowledges a version of it when he distinguishes between change and mere variation, though he thinks B-theory can handle genuine change (Mellor 1998: 7–10). McTaggart's position is that it cannot, at least not without supplementation by the A-series.

## The Argument Reconstructed

Here is the argument in explicit form:

1. Time is essentially characterized by change. (A world with no change whatsoever would not be a temporal world in the relevant sense.)
2. Real change involves something genuinely *becoming* different — not merely being different at different times as a matter of eternal fact.
3. The B-series, which consists of permanent, tenseless relational facts, can represent variation across time but not genuine becoming.
4. Therefore, the B-series alone is insufficient to constitute time.
5. The A-series captures the element of becoming: things move from future to present to past, and it is this movement that constitutes the passage of time.
6. Therefore, the A-series is more fundamental than the B-series.

The contentious premises are (2) and (3). Let us examine the main objection to each.

## The B-Theorist's Response: The Adverbial Strategy

The most common B-theorist response to premise (2) is to deny that "genuine becoming" is anything over and above what the B-series captures. When we say that the sky "became" clear, we mean nothing more than that the sky is clear at t₂ and cloudy at t₁, with t₁ earlier than t₂. There is no additional "becoming" over and above this variation across time. The appeal to becoming is either a confused way of talking about B-series variation, or it is a grip of phenomenology (the *experience* of change) being mistaken for a metaphysical requirement on change.

D.H. Mellor makes this response explicitly: the indexical analysis of "now" and "then" is sufficient to account for everything true in tensed language without positing genuinely changing A-properties (Mellor 1998: Ch. 2). The B-theorist can say: the experience of change is real, but its metaphysical basis is just the B-series structure. Saying "the sky is changing" is a tensed way of reporting a B-series fact, and that is all there is to it.

McTaggart anticipated this move and rejected it. His counter is that the B-theorist is not giving an account of change; they are denying that there is anything to account for beyond B-series variation. But the very question at issue is whether B-series variation is sufficient for time as we know it — and to simply assert that it is, is to beg the question. The burden of proof lies with those who claim variation is enough.

## The Problem of Genuine Passage

Here is a way of sharpening McTaggart's intuition. Consider two scenarios:

**Scenario A:** The universe consists of a four-dimensional block of events, all equally real, ordered by B-series relations. Nothing is happening — in the sense that all events simply *are*, timelessly, at their positions in the manifold. The "passage" of time is merely our experience of this static structure as our consciousness moves along it (or as our temporal stages succeed one another).

**Scenario B:** The universe is genuinely dynamic. Events genuinely come to be and pass away. The present is not just one more position in a static manifold; it is the site of actual occurring, the threshold of becoming.

McTaggart's claim is that Scenario A is not really a temporal world — or not a world with time in the sense that matters philosophically and phenomenologically. Scenario B is what we mean by time, and capturing Scenario B requires the A-series.

The B-theorist will say this is an illusion — that Scenario A, properly understood, is all there is. But then the burden is on the B-theorist to explain why the world *seems* to have the character of Scenario B, and why that seeming does not track a real distinction. The argument is by no means settled.

## Assessment

McTaggart's primacy argument is best understood as a challenge: it places the burden of proof on the B-theorist to show that genuine temporal passage — the "becoming" aspect of time — can be accounted for without the A-series. Contemporary B-theorists have sophisticated responses (see Chapter 14), but they have not silenced the worry. Authors as recent as A.N. Prior (1959), E.J. Lowe (1998: Ch. 4), and Nathan Oaklander (2012) have argued that the B-series genuinely fails to capture what is distinctive about time, and that McTaggart's primacy claim is correct even if his regress argument proves his undoing.

What the argument does, at minimum, is establish why the A-theory is not obviously absurd or regressive. A-theorists are not simply confused about time; they are insisting on a feature of time — its passage — that the B-series provably cannot represent. That insistence has real philosophical weight.
