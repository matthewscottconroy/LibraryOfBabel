# Section 13.2: The Primacy of the A-Series

---

## Section Introduction

Having distinguished the A-series from the B-series, McTaggart goes on to argue that they are not equally fundamental. The A-series, he claims, is more basic than the B-series, because the B-series alone cannot account for the most essential feature of time: *change*. This section examines that argument carefully.

The claim of A-series primacy is controversial, and the controversy matters. If McTaggart is right that the B-series is insufficient for genuine change, then any theory of time that dispenses with A-series properties — as B-theorists and eternalists attempt to do — faces a deep problem. It is not enough to show that we can *represent* temporal facts in tenseless language; one must show that such a representation is *adequate* to what time fundamentally is. McTaggart's argument presses precisely on that question of adequacy.

The argument in this section is sometimes underappreciated relative to the regress argument that follows, but it deserves equal attention. It has, in various forms, been endorsed by philosophers as different as C.D. Broad, Arthur Prior, and more recently E. J. Lowe and Timothy Williamson. The section presents McTaggart's argument, reconstructs it charitably, and assesses the strongest objections.
