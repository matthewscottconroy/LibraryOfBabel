# 13.3.1 — The Regress Argument: A-Properties and Contradiction

---

The McTaggart regress is one of the most elegant and troublesome arguments in the philosophy of time. It moves with remarkable economy from a simple observation about A-series properties to the conclusion that the A-series is incoherent — and therefore that time is unreal. Let us follow the argument carefully.

## The Initial Contradiction

Here is the setup: the A-series assigns every temporal position one of three properties — *past*, *present*, or *future*. But here is the problem: every event must, over the course of its existence, have *all three* of these properties. Every event was once future, became present, and then became past. The Battle of Hastings was once future, was for a brief time present, and has been past ever since. The same is true of every event: they all traverse the full range of A-properties.

But past, present, and future are mutually incompatible properties. An event cannot be both past and present, both present and future, or both past and future. These are not merely different properties; they are contradictory. Just as a thing cannot be both entirely red and entirely blue simultaneously, a thing cannot be both past and future.

Yet every event must have all three. So every event instantiates a set of mutually incompatible properties. Contradiction (McTaggart 1908: 468).

## The Obvious Reply and Why It Fails

The obvious reply is: of course every event has all three properties, but not *at the same time*. The Battle of Hastings was future at some times, present at one time, and past at all subsequent times. There is no contradiction because the incompatible properties are distributed across different times. Past-ness, present-ness, and future-ness are time-indexed: E is past *at t*, present *at t'*, future *at t''*, where t, t', t'' are different times.

This is exactly the reply a careful philosopher would make, and McTaggart was ready for it. His response: to say that E is past *at t*, present *at t'*, and future *at t''* is to characterize the times t, t', and t'' using A-series properties. The time t at which E is past is itself a past time — or a present time, or a future time. We have not eliminated A-properties; we have pushed them up a level. We are now characterizing *times* as past, present, or future, rather than events directly. But the same problem arises for these times: each of the times t, t', t'' was once future, became present, and became past. So we need to say: t is past-at-t₁, present-at-t₂, future-at-t₃, where t₁, t₂, t₃ are themselves times with A-properties. And so on without end (McTaggart 1908: 468–469).

The regress is vicious, not merely infinite. An infinite regress is only a problem if the earlier stages of the regress are *presupposed* by the later ones — if the whole structure is needed to make the original claim coherent. Here, the regress is vicious precisely because we cannot complete it: we cannot arrive at a base-level characterization of A-properties that does not itself invoke further A-properties. Every attempt to characterize A-properties without contradiction requires characterizing further times, which itself requires A-series characterization, and so on. We never arrive at a coherent foundation.

## The Formal Structure of the Regress

Let us state this more precisely. Suppose we have:

- Level 0: Event E is (past, present, and future). This is contradictory.
- Reply: E is past-at-t₁, present-at-t₂, future-at-t₃ (where t₁ < t₂ < t₃).
- Level 1: But t₁ is (past, present, and future) itself. Contradiction at the level of times.
- Reply: t₁ is past-at-t₁₁, present-at-t₁₂, future-at-t₁₃ (where t₁₁ < t₁₂ < t₁₃).
- Level 2: But t₁₁, t₁₂, t₁₃ are themselves (past, present, and future)...

And the pattern repeats indefinitely. At every level, we introduce new times to relativize A-properties, and these new times themselves require A-properties, and the same contradiction reappears. We can never finish the regress in a way that makes the original claim coherent. The A-series is, McTaggart concludes, irredeemably contradictory (McTaggart 1908: 469).

## What the Argument Actually Shows

It is important to be precise about what the regress argument establishes, if it succeeds. It does not show that the A-series vocabulary is meaningless or that we cannot use tensed language. It shows that the A-series, understood as a genuine metaphysical structure involving objective, mind-independent properties of being past, present, and future, cannot be made coherent. Every attempt to remove the contradiction by relativizing A-properties to times simply pushes the contradiction to a higher level.

The argument's target is specifically the *realist* interpretation of A-properties: the view that pastness, presentness, and futurity are genuine, mind-independent features of events or times. The question is whether any realist treatment of A-properties can escape the regress.

As McTaggart puts it: "If we try to avoid this difficulty by saying that [the contradictory A-properties] are all false together, we are saying that nothing is in time at all. And thus our first conclusion is that time is unreal" (1908: 469). The choice is between contradiction and the unreality of time. McTaggart opts for unreality.
