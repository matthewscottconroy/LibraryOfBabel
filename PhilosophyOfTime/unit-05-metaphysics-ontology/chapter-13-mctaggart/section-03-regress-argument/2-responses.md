# 13.3.2 — Responses to the Regress

---

McTaggart's regress argument has attracted more responses than perhaps any other argument in the philosophy of time. It has been called decisive, refuted, confused, circular, and illuminating — sometimes by the same philosopher in successive publications. This section surveys the most important responses and assesses their success.

## Prior's Indexical Response

The most influential response comes from Arthur Prior, the founder of tense logic. Prior's strategy is to deny that A-properties are genuine properties at all, in the metaphysically robust sense McTaggart assumes. On Prior's view, tensed statements — "The battle is past," "It is now raining" — do not report the possession of an objective property by an event or time. They express something more like an indexical perspective: they are ways of locating the speaker within a temporal structure, analogous to "I am here" (Prior 1967: 6–13).

On this view, "The battle is past" is not attributing the property of *pastness* to the battle; it is expressing something like "The battle has occurred" — a statement that is true just in case the battle occurs before the time of utterance. There is no freestanding property of being past that events possess; there are only tensed facts that are perspectivally expressed. Because there are no objective A-properties floating around waiting to contradict each other, the McTaggart contradiction cannot get started.

This response has attracted both admirers and critics. The admirers note that it deflates the regress cleanly: if there are no A-properties, there is no contradiction among them, and no regress. The critics ask: has Prior dissolved the problem or merely redescribed it? Specifically: what is the *ground* for the truth of tensed statements? When I say "The battle is past," something makes that true. If Prior's answer involves the battle occurring before the present moment, he needs to say what the present moment is — and whether *that* is characterized by an A-property. The worry is that Prior has moved the problem rather than dissolved it (Mellor 1998: 45–47).

## Lowe's Defense of A-Theory

E.J. Lowe has argued that McTaggart's regress is not vicious because the infinite levels it generates are ontologically harmless (Lowe 1998: Ch. 4). Lowe's strategy is to accept the infinite regress of A-series characterizations but deny that it is vicious. A vicious regress is one in which each level is *presupposed* or *required* by the previous level as a condition of its making sense. Lowe argues that, in the case of A-series properties, the higher levels of the regress are not presupposed by the lower levels; they are merely available as further characterizations that one can give if pressed. The first level — E is past-at-t₁, present-at-t₂, future-at-t₃ — is genuinely explanatory of how E can have incompatible A-properties without contradiction. The further levels do not need to be completed for this explanation to succeed.

Whether this response succeeds depends on whether one accepts Lowe's distinction between benign and vicious regresses, and on whether the higher-level characterizations are really independent of the lower-level ones. McTaggart's opponents are skeptical: each level invokes A-properties, and if A-properties are problematic at the first level, they are equally problematic at every subsequent level. Lowe owes us an account of what grounds A-properties at *some* level without reintroducing the original contradiction.

## Dummett's Reconstruction

Michael Dummett offered an important reconstruction of McTaggart's argument that shifts the emphasis from contradiction to a conceptual point about what it means to employ A-properties (Dummett 1960). Dummett argues that McTaggart's regress shows not that A-properties are contradictory, but that they cannot be fully characterized in tenseless terms. Any attempt to say, in tenseless language, what "the present" or "being past" amounts to generates a regress because tenseless language lacks the resources to capture what is distinctive about tensed characterization.

On Dummett's reconstruction, the moral is not the unreality of time but the irreducibility of tensed discourse: tensed concepts cannot be analyzed in tenseless terms. This is actually a *pro*-A-theory conclusion, not a destructive one. If Dummett is right, then the regress shows that A-theorists are dealing with genuinely irreducible concepts — which is a reason to take A-theory seriously, not a reason to abandon it.

## The B-Theorist's Escape: Deny A-Properties Altogether

B-theorists typically respond to the regress by accepting its force against A-theory and using it as motivation for B-theory. If the A-series cannot be made coherent, and the B-series is sufficient for temporal order, variation, and the truth of tensed statements (as the token-reflexive account maintains), then we should dispense with A-properties altogether. The regress is not a problem for B-theory because B-theory does not posit A-properties in the first place (Mellor 1998; Sider 2001: Ch. 2).

This response is coherent, but it comes at a price: it requires showing that the B-series is genuinely sufficient for everything that time involves — including the phenomenology of passage, the significance of the present, and the logic of tensed discourse. Chapter 14 examines whether that price has been paid.

## Does Anyone Escape?

The honest assessment is: no response is entirely satisfying, and none commands consensus.

Prior's indexical response deflates the ontological pressure but does not eliminate the need for some account of what grounds tensed truths. Lowe's response requires a controversial account of benign versus vicious regress. Dummett's reconstruction reframes rather than resolves the issue. The B-theorist's response works only if one already accepts that B-theory is adequate — which is precisely what the argument was meant to challenge.

The McTaggart regress is best understood not as a knock-down proof but as a diagnostic: it reveals a deep structural tension in A-theory, a tension that any A-theorist must manage. Some manage it better than others, and the best contemporary A-theories (presentism, growing block, moving spotlight) all have sophisticated strategies for doing so. But the regress remains a live philosophical challenge, not a settled refutation.
