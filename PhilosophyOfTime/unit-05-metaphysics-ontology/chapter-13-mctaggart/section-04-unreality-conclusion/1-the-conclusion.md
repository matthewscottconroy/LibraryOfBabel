# 13.4.1 — The Conclusion: Time is Unreal

---

When McTaggart concludes that time is unreal, he does not mean that nothing exists, or that what we call "temporal" events are a complete fiction. He means something more precise, though no less radical: the appearance of temporal order, temporal direction, and temporal passage is a systematic misrepresentation of an underlying atemporal reality. The universe, at its deepest level, has a structure — the C-series — but that structure is not temporal. It is time's pale shadow.

## What "Unreality" Means

McTaggart is working within an Idealist metaphysical tradition (associated with his teacher Francis Herbert Bradley and with Hegel's broader influence) in which the distinction between *appearance* and *reality* is fundamental. Something is real if it is what it appears to be, or more precisely, if it can be given a fully coherent account without contradiction. Something is unreal if the only way to describe it generates contradictions — if the very concepts through which it presents itself are internally inconsistent. By this criterion, time is unreal: the A-series, which is the concept through which time presents itself as dynamic and directed, generates the contradiction and regress we have examined. Therefore, time, as it presents itself to us, is not what is ultimately real (McTaggart 1908: 470–474; McTaggart 1927, Vol. II: Ch. 33).

This does not mean that *nothing* corresponds to our temporal experience. It means that what corresponds to it is something different from what the experience presents. Just as a perceptual illusion points to a real stimulus — the Müller-Lyer arrows really are lines on a page, even though they are not unequal in length — temporal experience points to a real underlying order that is not genuinely temporal.

## The C-Series as the Underlying Reality

The positive account involves the C-series. As introduced in Section 13.1, the C-series is a bare ordering of "permanent existents" (McTaggart's term for the fundamental entities of his ontology) that does not by itself involve direction or passage. The C-series has the structure of an ordered sequence — position A, position B, position C — but without the B-series's "earlier than" relation (which would assign direction) and without the A-series's "present" marker (which would assign the appearance of flow).

The question McTaggart must answer is: if the C-series is the fundamental reality, how does the *appearance* of temporal direction and temporal passage arise? His answer involves the spiritual nature of reality. For McTaggart, reality is constituted by spiritual substances — what he calls "selves" — and the C-series positions correspond to stages of these selves as they are known through spiritual perception. The appearance of temporal flow arises from the *misperception* of this spiritual order as a temporal one. Our experience of time is a confused and distorted apprehension of the C-series (McTaggart 1927, Vol. II: §347–350).

This positive account is widely regarded as the least convincing part of McTaggart's system. It depends on a heavily idealist metaphysics that most contemporary philosophers do not share. But McTaggart is careful to note that the negative argument — the unreality of time — does not depend on the specific character of his positive account. Even if one rejects the C-series and the spiritual metaphysics, the argument that A-properties are contradictory and generate a vicious regress can stand or fall on its own terms.

## The Legacy: What McTaggart Actually Established

Even philosophers who find McTaggart's conclusion unacceptable almost universally credit him with having established several important points:

**First**, the A/B distinction is real and philosophically significant. The contemporary philosophy of time is structured around this distinction; it has proved extraordinarily productive.

**Second**, the question of which series is more fundamental is not trivially answered. McTaggart's argument that the B-series alone cannot capture genuine change has not been definitively refuted; it has been variously accommodated, defused, and disputed.

**Third**, A-series properties cannot be taken for granted. A-theorists who posit objective, mind-independent A-properties must explain how events can have all three properties (past, present, future) without contradiction, and without generating a vicious regress. This is a genuine obligation, and not all contemporary A-theories discharge it equally well.

**Fourth**, the positive project matters. McTaggart is not just a demolition expert; he is trying to give a full account of what is real. Philosophy of time is not just the project of identifying what is wrong with rival views; it is the project of giving the best positive account of what time is. McTaggart's method — pushing every view until it reaches its contradictions, then asking what remains — is the right method, even if his specific conclusion is wrong.

## A Thought Experiment

Here is a way to feel the force of McTaggart's position. Imagine that a complete physical description of the universe were given to you — every event, its B-series location, its complete causal history. You have, in effect, the universe's four-dimensional manifold laid out before you. Now ask: does that description include the fact that *today* is today? That this very moment is present? Or is "now" something extra, something that must be added to the physical description from the outside?

If you say "now" must be added — that the physical description alone cannot settle what is presently happening — then you are agreeing with McTaggart that the A-series is not reducible to the B-series. And you face the question: what kind of thing is this additional fact, and can it be made coherent?

If you say the physical description is complete, and "now" is just a perspective within that description, then you are agreeing with the B-theorist — but you owe us an account of what that perspective amounts to and why it feels so decisive.

McTaggart's great contribution is to have shown that neither answer is comfortable. That discomfort is the beginning of genuine philosophy of time.
