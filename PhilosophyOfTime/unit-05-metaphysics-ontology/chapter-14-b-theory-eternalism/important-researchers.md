# Chapter 14 Important Researchers

---

**J. J. C. Smart (1920–2012)**
Jack Smart was an Australian philosopher at the Australian National University who was one of the earliest and most influential defenders of the B-theory of time in the analytic tradition. His 1949 paper "The River of Time" argued that the passage of time is a metaphysical illusion and that all temporal facts are B-series facts. Smart was also a prominent defender of physicalism in the philosophy of mind and of utilitarianism in ethics, and his work consistently exemplifies the virtues of analytic clarity and theoretical parsimony.

**D. H. Mellor (1938–2020)**
Hugh Mellor was a British philosopher at Cambridge whose *Real Time* (1981) and *Real Time II* (1998) are the most sustained and technically rigorous defenses of B-theory in the philosophical literature. Mellor developed the token-reflexive analysis of tensed language in careful detail, argued that the existence of causation is compatible with a B-series ontology, and engaged with A-theoretic positions from a position of genuine respect for their motivations. His work is a model of how to argue for a controversial metaphysical position with rigor and fairness.

**Ted Sider (born 1966)**
Ted Sider is an American metaphysician whose *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001) is the standard contemporary reference work for worm theory and B-theoretic metaphysics. Sider argues comprehensively for perdurantism over endurantism (the view that objects persist by being wholly present at each time), defends the block universe against A-theoretic objections, and connects the debate about persistence to broader questions in ontology. His later *Writing the Book of the World* (2011) develops a metaontological framework in which B-series relations are among the most natural and fundamental features of reality.

**David Lewis (1941–2001)**
David Lewis was arguably the most influential analytic metaphysician of the twentieth century. His worm theory of persistence, developed in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), is one of the most elegant and comprehensive developments of the four-dimensionalist picture. Lewis also made foundational contributions to the analysis of counterfactual conditionals, possible worlds semantics, and the philosophy of mind. His work on the asymmetry of time and the "past hypothesis" is discussed in Chapter 18.

**Katherine Hawley (born 1970)**
Katherine Hawley is a Scottish philosopher whose *How Things Persist* (2001) is one of the most careful and balanced examinations of the persistence debate. Hawley defends a version of stage theory while engaging seriously with the alternative positions, and her work is distinguished by its attention to what the various theories actually predict about ordinary cases. She is also known for work on knowledge and evidence, and her philosophical writing exemplifies clarity and precision.

**Hilary Putnam (1926–2016)**
Hilary Putnam was one of the most versatile and influential philosophers of the twentieth century, making major contributions to philosophy of language, philosophy of mind, mathematics, and metaphysics. His 1967 paper "Time and Physical Geometry" argued for the block universe on the basis of the special theory of relativity, developing what is now called the Rietdijk-Putnam argument. Putnam's later career involved a significant revision of many of his earlier positions, but his work on time and relativity remains highly influential in the philosophy of physics.

**C. W. A. Rietdijk (1932–2009)**
Cornelis Wietze Rietdijk was a Dutch physicist and philosopher who, independently of Putnam and in the same year, published the argument from special relativity to the reality of all times. His paper "A Rigorous Proof of Determinism Derived from the Special Theory of Relativity" (1966) argued that special relativity entails determinism and the equal reality of all events. Though less well known than Putnam, his formulation of the argument was clear and influential.

**Hans Reichenbach (1891–1953)**
Hans Reichenbach was a German-American philosopher of science who made foundational contributions to the philosophy of time, space, and probability. His token-reflexive analysis of tensed language in *Elements of Symbolic Logic* (1947) anticipated the full B-theoretic semantics developed by later philosophers. His work on the conventionality of simultaneity (discussed in Chapter 16) and on the direction of time (discussed in Chapter 18) established the terms of debate for subsequent generations. Reichenbach was a pioneer of logical empiricism and a rigorous analyst of the philosophical implications of modern physics.
