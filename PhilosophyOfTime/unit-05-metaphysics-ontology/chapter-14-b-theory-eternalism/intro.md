# Chapter 14: B-Theory and Eternalism — The Block Universe

---

## Chapter Introduction

Suppose McTaggart is right that the A-series is incoherent. Where does that leave us? One natural response is to accept the consequence: since the A-series is what distinguishes genuinely tensed time from a static ordering, and since the A-series cannot be coherently maintained, time as ordinarily conceived is unreal, and what remains is the B-series — a four-dimensional manifold of events related by the permanent, tenseless relations of "earlier than" and "later than."

But most philosophers who accept the B-series do not accept this as the conclusion of a reluctant argument; they accept it as a positive and attractive picture of reality. The B-theory, or eternalism, or the block universe view — the names vary in emphasis — holds that all times and all events are equally real. The past is not gone; the future is not absent; the present is not special. The universe is a four-dimensional block of events, related by B-series ordering relations, and "now" is simply the temporal location of whoever is speaking, just as "here" is simply the spatial location of whoever is speaking.

This is one of the most striking metaphysical positions in all of philosophy. It implies that your future self already exists — there is a temporal stage of you ten years hence, sitting somewhere else in the four-dimensional manifold, as real as you are right now. It implies that dinosaurs still exist — not in the sense that they are lurking about somewhere spatial, but in the sense that there are temporal positions at which they exist, positions as real as the one you currently occupy. The past is not nothing; it is just elsewhere in the temporal dimension.

This chapter develops the block universe in careful detail. Section 14.1 presents the core thesis and examines what it means for "now" to be indexical. Section 14.2 surveys the strongest arguments for eternalism: the argument from relativity, the truthmaker argument, and the argument from parsimony. Section 14.3 examines the two major theories of how ordinary objects persist through time on an eternalist picture — worm theory and stage theory. Section 14.4 considers the major objections, including the passage problem and the challenge from phenomenology. Section 14.5 addresses the question that most people raise first: does the block universe eliminate free will?
