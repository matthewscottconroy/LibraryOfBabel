# 14.1.2 — "Now" as Indexical: The Token-Reflexive Analysis

---

One of the most important components of the B-theory is its account of how "now" functions in language. The B-theorist holds that "now," like "here" and "I," is an *indexical* expression: its reference shifts depending on the context of utterance. Understanding this claim — and understanding what distinguishes the B-theorist's indexical treatment from the A-theorist's claim that the present is metaphysically special — is crucial to understanding the entire debate.

## What Indexicals Are

An **indexical** is an expression whose reference is systematically determined by features of the context in which it is used. "I" refers to whoever is speaking; "here" refers to the location of speech; "now" — on the B-theory — refers to the time of utterance. Indexicals shift their reference with their context. The sentence "I am hungry" expresses a different proposition depending on who says it. The sentence "There is a fire here" expresses a different proposition depending on where it is said.

The crucial feature of indexicals is that they do not pick out a metaphysically privileged object. When you say "here," you are not attributing a special metaphysical property of "hereness" to your location; you are simply locating your utterance spatially and describing what is at that spatial location. The word "here" is egocentric — it is about the speaker's location — but it does not imply that the speaker's location is cosmically special.

The B-theorist claims that "now" works in exactly the same way. When you say "It is now Tuesday," you are not attributing a special metaphysical property of *present-ness* to Tuesday; you are locating your utterance temporally and describing what is at that temporal location. "Now" picks out the time of utterance, just as "here" picks out the place of utterance. The present is not an objective feature of the world; it is a feature of the speaker's temporal context.

## The Token-Reflexive Analysis

The B-theorist's standard analysis of tensed statements uses what philosophers call the **token-reflexive** approach. On this analysis, the truth conditions of a tensed statement make reference to the token utterance (or the token statement) itself.

The analysis works as follows:
- "It is raining now" (said at time t) = "It is raining at t."
- "The battle was past" (said at time t) = "The battle is earlier than t."
- "The exam is future" (said at time t) = "The exam is later than t."

In each case, the tensed statement is analyzed in terms of a B-series relation between the event described and the time of the token utterance. The word "now" in the first sentence is replaced by the B-series characterization "at t." The past tense in the second sentence is replaced by the B-series relation "earlier than t." Future tense in the third is replaced by "later than t."

Notice what this analysis achieves: it gives truth conditions for tensed statements in purely B-series terms. It does not invoke A-properties — no mention of objective pastness, presentness, or futurity as mind-independent features of events. Everything is cashed out in terms of the speaker's temporal location and B-series ordering relations.

This approach has its origins in Hans Reichenbach's analysis of tense in *Elements of Symbolic Logic* (1947: §51) and was developed into a comprehensive B-theoretic semantics by Mellor (1998: Ch. 2) and others.

## What the Analysis Implies for Metaphysics

The token-reflexive analysis has direct metaphysical implications. If "now" is a perspectival indexical rather than a metaphysically loaded term, then there is no fact about what is *objectively present* over and above facts about what is occurring at various temporal positions. There is no "metaphysical present" — a time that is present not relative to any observer but absolutely, in its own right.

Compare this to "here." We do not think that there is a special metaphysical property of "hereness" that uniquely belongs to one spatial location while all other locations lack it. Every location is "here" for someone located there. Similarly, on the B-theory, every time is "now" for someone located at that time. The present is an observer-relative concept, not a feature of objective reality.

This implication has an interesting consequence: it means that the famous sense in which we are "in" the present — the sense in which our experience is always of *this moment*, not some other — is fully accounted for by the fact that our experiences are temporally located where they are. When you are at time t, all your experiences are experiences at t, so they are all "now" experiences. There is nothing more to being present than being temporally located where you are. The sense of being "in" the present is the sense of being a temporal stage within the manifold — which every temporal stage is, relative to itself.

## The A-Theorist's Objection

The A-theorist resists the indexical analysis. The objection runs roughly as follows: "here" and "now" are not genuinely analogous, because no one thinks that any particular spatial location is metaphysically special in a way that makes the others "absent." But we do ordinarily think that the present is metaphysically special — that what is happening *now* is real in a way that what is past and future is not. The A-theorist insists that this intuition tracks a real ontological distinction. To treat "now" as a mere indexical is to beg the question against the intuition, not to refute it.

This disagreement is fundamental and is not resolved by pointing to the analogy with "here." The question is whether the analogy holds. If it holds — if time and space are genuinely symmetric in the relevant respects — then "now" is indexical and the B-theory is correct. If it does not hold — if the present really is objectively special in a way that "here" is not — then A-theory has the advantage. Chapters 15 and 16 develop the A-theorist's position in detail.
