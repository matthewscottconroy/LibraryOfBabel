# 14.2.3 — The Argument from Parsimony

---

The third argument for the block universe is methodological rather than directly metaphysical: B-theory is simpler than A-theory, and simplicity is a theoretical virtue. All else being equal, the theory that posits fewer fundamental categories, fewer primitive notions, and fewer basic facts is to be preferred.

## What the B-Theory Requires

The B-theory requires, as its fundamental temporal ontology, just one thing: the B-series ordering relations of "earlier than," "later than," and "simultaneous with." These are the temporal facts. Every other temporal claim — including claims that are grammatically tensed — can be given truth conditions in terms of these relations plus the temporal location of the utterance.

B-theory does not require:
- Objective A-properties (presentness, pastness, futurity) as mind-independent features of events
- A primitive "now" that singles out one time as metaphysically special
- A "flow" or "passage" of time as a further fact about the world
- Any distinction in the *existence* of past, present, and future events

The ontology is lean: events arranged in a four-dimensional manifold by B-series relations.

## What the A-Theory Requires

The A-theory requires everything the B-theory requires (it needs ordering relations) plus additional primitive notions: the A-properties of pastness, presentness, and futurity as objective, mind-independent features of temporal positions; a fact about which time is *now*; and, on most versions, some account of why this fact changes over time (the passage of time). On the presentist version, it also requires a fundamental ontological distinction between present things (which exist) and past and future things (which do not). On the moving spotlight version, it requires an additional primitive feature of reality — the spotlight of the present — over and above the four-dimensional manifold.

In each case, the A-theory posits more than the B-theory. It posits the B-series structure plus additional ontological furniture: A-properties, a privileged present, temporal passage.

## The Parsimony Point

Occam's Razor — the principle that entities and fundamental categories should not be multiplied beyond necessity — is one of the most robust methodological principles in philosophy and science. All else being equal, a theory that gets the same explanatory work done with fewer fundamental posits is to be preferred.

The B-theorist argues: A-theory posits all the B-theory's fundamental structure, plus more. If B-theory can account for all the temporal facts — including those expressed in tensed language — then A-theory's additional posits are unnecessary. They are not required by any phenomenon that B-theory cannot accommodate; they are simply added on top. By the principle of parsimony, A-theory's extra ontology should be rejected.

Ted Sider makes a related point in terms of *naturalness*: the fundamental ontology of B-theory involves natural, scientifically fundamental relations (the relations that figure in spacetime physics), whereas A-properties would have to be sui generis features of reality with no clear connection to scientific categories (Sider 2001: 41–42).

## The Limits of Parsimony

The parsimony argument is not decisive. Parsimony is a theoretical virtue, but it is not the only one. If B-theory systematically fails to account for something real — if there is some genuine feature of temporal reality that requires A-properties — then parsimony does not justify B-theory's omission. You cannot achieve parsimony by eliminating what is real; you can only achieve it by not positing what is not real.

The A-theorist's response is that temporal passage, objective presentness, and the genuine flow of time are *not* eliminable from an adequate account of reality. They are real features of the world, not merely features of how we represent the world. If that is correct, then B-theory achieves its parsimony by the illegitimate route of denying real features of the world, and it is no achievement at all.

The parsimony argument and this response are, in the end, about what the *data* requires — what phenomena a theory of time must account for. That question is the deep issue behind the entire A/B debate.
