# 14.3.1 — Worm Theory: Perdurantism and Temporal Extension

---

The most natural development of the block universe view for the metaphysics of objects is **perdurantism**, commonly called worm theory. The view holds that objects persist through time by having distinct temporal parts, or **temporal stages**, at each moment of their existence — just as they have distinct spatial parts at each region of their spatial extent.

## The Core Idea

When you look at a spatial object — a table, say — you see it as spatially extended: it has a top, legs, corners, and edges, all of which are spatial parts of the table. On the worm theory, objects are extended through *time* in the same way. You, right now, are a **temporal stage** of a larger, four-dimensional entity — your whole life history, from birth to death — that is extended through time the way the table is extended through space. Your entire life history is the "temporal worm"; your current self is one temporal slice of that worm.

David Lewis, in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986, Ch. 4), put the point vividly: "Persons persisting through time are like highways or rivers or other things that extend through space. A highway doesn't wholly exist at a single place; it has different parts existing at different places" (Lewis 1986: 202). The worm theory makes the same point about time: you don't wholly exist at a single time; you have different temporal parts existing at different times.

## How Worm Theory Handles Change

The worm theory offers a natural account of change. When we say "the leaf changed from green to yellow," the worm theorist says: the leaf-at-time-t₁ (a temporal stage) is green; the leaf-at-time-t₂ (a later temporal stage) is yellow. There is no contradiction, just as there is no contradiction in saying "the top of the table is flat and the underside is not flat" — those are different spatial parts of the table. Similarly, different temporal stages of the leaf have different colors, and the "change" from green to yellow is the succession of these differently propertied stages.

This is an elegant solution to the so-called "problem of temporary intrinsics" — the problem that one and the same thing seems to instantiate incompatible properties at different times (Lewis 1986: 204). The worm theory dissolves this problem by noting that it is never the same stage that has both properties; it is different stages.

Sider's *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001, Chs. 3–4) provides the most technically comprehensive development of perdurantism, arguing that the four-dimensionalist picture is supported both by ordinary reasoning about persistence and by the structure of modern physics, which treats spacetime as a four-dimensional manifold.

## The Self as Temporal Worm

The worm theory has striking implications for personal identity. You are not a three-dimensional entity that endures through time while remaining wholly present at each moment; you are a four-dimensional entity, temporally extended from birth to death. "You" in the most comprehensive sense refers to your whole temporal worm. "You right now" refers to your current temporal stage.

This picture changes the phenomenology of personal identity. When you think about yourself in the future — anticipating the exam, worrying about retirement — you are thinking about later stages of your temporal worm, which are, in a meaningful sense, as real as your current stage. The future-you is not a hypothetical entity; it is a real entity in the four-dimensional manifold, just not a stage you currently overlap.

This has implications for how we think about prudential concern. Should you care equally about all your temporal stages? Some philosophers have argued that the worm theory, combined with psychological continuity views of personal identity, yields a picture on which prudential concern is rational if it tracks the degree of psychological continuity between stages (Parfit 1984: 281–287).

## Objections

The main objection to worm theory is that it seems to mislocate properties. When we say "the leaf is green," we seem to be ascribing a property to the leaf — not to the leaf-at-time-t₁. The leaf *is* green, not a temporal stage of the leaf. The worm theorist's response is that this is just a loose way of talking: strictly speaking, it is temporal stages that have properties, and ordinary object-talk involves implicit relativization to times.

A related objection concerns our ordinary way of counting objects. If the leaf is a temporal worm extending over years, how many leaves are there? One worm, or a worm plus all its stages (which are also "the leaf" at various times)? Worm theorists typically say there is one leaf (one temporal worm) and many temporal stages of it — but deciding which entities to count requires some argument.
