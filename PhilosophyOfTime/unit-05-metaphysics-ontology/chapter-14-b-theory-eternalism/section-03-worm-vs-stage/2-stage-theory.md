# 14.3.2 — Stage Theory: Persistence via Temporal Counterparts

---

Stage theory is a variant of four-dimensionalism that differs from worm theory in a subtle but philosophically significant way. Where worm theory holds that ordinary objects are four-dimensional temporal worms, stage theory holds that ordinary objects are **instantaneous (or very brief) temporal stages** — and that what we call "persistence" is not a matter of one entity being extended across time, but of a succession of distinct stages being related by a **temporal counterpart** relation.

## The Core Distinction

The key disagreement between worm theory and stage theory is about what we pick out when we use ordinary object terms like "the leaf," "you," or "this table."

**Worm theory:** "The leaf" refers to the four-dimensional temporal worm — the entire extended history of the leaf through time.

**Stage theory:** "The leaf" refers to the current temporal stage — the instantaneous leaf, as it exists right now. When we say "the same leaf was green last autumn and is yellow now," we are using temporal counterpart relations to link the current stage (yellow) to an earlier stage (green) that is its temporal counterpart.

Ted Sider (2001, Ch. 5) and Katherine Hawley (2001: Ch. 2) are the primary developers of stage theory. Sider calls the view "temporal counterpart theory" and argues that it handles certain problems that worm theory cannot.

## How Temporal Counterpart Relations Work

The temporal counterpart relation is modeled on David Lewis's modal counterpart relation for possible worlds (Lewis 1968). Just as objects in different possible worlds are related by counterpart relations rather than by identity — the person who would have become a pianist in another world is not *identical* to you but is your *counterpart* — temporal stages at different times are related by counterpart relations rather than by strict identity.

What grounds the temporal counterpart relation? Roughly: psychological continuity, physical continuity, causal ancestry, and whatever other criteria we use to track objects through time. The current-stage-of-the-leaf and the earlier-stage-of-the-leaf are temporal counterparts because they are causally connected, physically continuous, and share relevant properties in an appropriate way.

When we say "the leaf was green and is now yellow," stage theory translates this as: "the present temporal stage of the leaf is yellow, and an earlier temporal counterpart of this stage was green." The leaf — the current stage — is yellow; what was green was a different entity, a temporal counterpart, not the leaf itself.

## Advantages of Stage Theory

Stage theory has several advantages over worm theory.

**The persistence of properties:** On stage theory, when we say "the leaf is green," we are attributing a property directly to an instantaneous stage, without any need for temporal qualification. The leaf-stage *is* green, simpliciter. This preserves the intuitive grammar of property attribution better than worm theory, which must say the leaf-stage is green while the worm is green-at-t₁.

**The transient problem:** Certain cases of coincident objects — where two things occupy the same space at the same time — are problematic for worm theory but handled more naturally by stage theory. Stage theory allows two objects to share a temporal stage (or to be co-located temporal counterparts) without requiring a puzzling "two worms occupying the same position."

**Ordinary temporal reference:** When you point to a leaf and say "this leaf is green," you are naturally pointing to something present — an instantaneous or brief stage. Stage theory takes this seriously; worm theory must insist that your pointing picks out an extended four-dimensional object.

## The Cost: Counterpart Theory

The main cost of stage theory is the reliance on counterpart relations rather than identity for persistence. On the worm theory, the leaf *is* identical to the leaf that was green last year — they are stages of the same worm. On stage theory, the current leaf and the green leaf are *counterparts*, not strictly the same entity.

This has a counterintuitive consequence. When you learn that the leaf "you are looking at" was green last autumn, you learn something about a *different entity* — a temporal counterpart — not about the leaf itself. Similarly, when you anticipate what *you* will do tomorrow, stage theory says you are anticipating the actions of a temporal counterpart, not of yourself. This strikes many people as odd: how can I prudentially care about someone who is not, strictly speaking, me?

Sider argues that these concerns can be handled by noting that the counterpart relation is genuinely the right relation for grounding persistence claims — not identity but something close enough to identity for practical purposes (Sider 2001: 188–199). Hawley (2001: Ch. 5) provides additional defenses. The debate between worm theory and stage theory is not settled, but both represent sophisticated developments of the four-dimensionalist picture.
