# 14.4.1 — The Passage Problem

---

The most frequently voiced objection to the block universe is that it cannot account for the passage of time. If all times are equally real — if past, present, and future all exist with the same ontological status — then in what sense does time *pass*? The block seems, by definition, to be static. Nothing is happening; everything is just there, four-dimensionally arranged.

## The Objection Stated

Donald Williams put the objection in classic form in 1951. On the block universe picture, he noted, time is symmetric with space in a particular way: spatial extension and temporal extension are simply two ways in which a four-dimensional manifold can be extended. The apparent "motion" of the present through time is, on this view, an illusion — or at best, a feature of our experience of traversing the manifold, not a feature of the manifold itself. But then there is a sense in which "time does not pass" on the block universe view: the four-dimensional manifold does not change, events do not come to be or cease to be, and the experience of flow is just the successive activation of temporal stages, which is itself a feature of the manifold (Williams 1951: 462–464).

William Lane Craig (2000: 145–147) has argued more recently that the block universe cannot accommodate what he calls the "true flow of time" — the feature of time by which future events are "not yet" real and genuinely become real as time advances. On the block universe view, this is not a feature of reality; it is a feature of our indexical perspective on a static manifold. Craig takes this as a reductio: if the block universe view entails that time does not really flow, and time does really flow, then the block universe view is false.

## The B-Theorist's Response

B-theorists have offered several responses to the passage problem.

**The redundancy response** holds that the "passage" of time that the objection appeals to is simply the B-series ordering — the fact that time is asymmetrically ordered from earlier to later. On this view, there is a sense in which time "passes": earlier events give way to later ones, and the manifold has a well-defined direction. This is all that "passage" amounts to, properly understood. The A-theorist who insists on more — on genuine becoming — is insisting on something over and above the B-series structure, but it is unclear what that additional feature is or why we should believe in it (Mellor 1998: 71–75).

**The experiential response** holds that the feeling of temporal passage is fully explained by the B-series structure, together with the fact that we are temporally located entities with memories of earlier stages. When you "experience" time passing, what is happening is that your current temporal stage has memories of earlier stages and anticipations of later ones, creating the experiential sense of being located in an ongoing flow. There is no additional "passage" beyond this; the experience is fully accounted for without positing a dynamic A-series.

**The 'no movement needed' response** holds that the very idea of time "moving" is confused in the way McTaggart argued. Movement requires a rate — meters per second, miles per hour. What would the "rate" of temporal passage be? One second per second — but that is trivially true and tells us nothing. If the concept of temporal passage is incoherent, then the failure to account for it is not a deficiency. The block universe view correctly abandons a confused notion (Smart 1949: 487).

## Assessment

The passage problem is genuinely uncomfortable for B-theory. The experiential response and the redundancy response are plausible, but they require the B-theorist to explain what is actually happening when we have the vivid experience of time flowing — and this explanation must be good enough to make the phenomenological objection (Section 14.4.2) tractable as well. The "no movement needed" response risks seeming dismissive: it tells us that what we think we experience is incoherent, rather than explaining why we have that experience.

Critics of B-theory like Craig argue that these responses show, at most, that B-theory can *describe* the temporal manifold, but cannot explain why the manifold is experienced as dynamic. The explanatory gap between the static block and the dynamic experience remains.
