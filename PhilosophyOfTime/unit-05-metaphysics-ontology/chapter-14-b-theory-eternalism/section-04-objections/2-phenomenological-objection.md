# 14.4.2 — The Phenomenological Objection

---

The phenomenological objection to B-theory is distinct from, but related to, the passage problem. The passage problem asks about a feature of the world: does time, as a feature of objective reality, pass? The phenomenological objection asks about a feature of experience: does our experience of temporal flow represent anything real, or is it a systematic illusion? And if it is an illusion, how can the B-theorist explain why the illusion is so vivid and so universal?

## The Basic Datum

The datum is this: when you are conscious, your experience has a characteristic temporal structure. You experience events as happening *now*, as fading into the immediate *just past*, and as coming from the *immediate future*. The present moment does not feel like a durationless mathematical point; it feels like an interval of several seconds with a direction — a sense of moving forward through it. The philosopher William James called this the "specious present" (1890: 609–610; see Chapter 16), and it is a cross-cultural, near-universal feature of conscious experience.

Moreover, the experience is not merely of the present as one moment among others; it is of the present as uniquely *actual*, uniquely *happening*. When you remember the lecture you attended yesterday, the memory has a specific phenomenal quality — it is a representation of something past, something no longer happening. Your perception of the words on this page has a different phenomenal quality — it is a perception of something happening now. This phenomenal distinction between remembering and perceiving is, intuitively, tracking a real distinction between past and present.

## The Objection Against B-Theory

The B-theorist is committed to saying that the phenomenal distinction between past and present — the sense of the present as uniquely actual — does not track any metaphysical distinction between past and present. For the B-theorist, past and present events are equally real; there is no metaphysical "presence" over and above the indexical fact that your temporal stage is located at a particular time. The phenomenal quality of the present — the sense of immediacy, of actuality, of *happening* — is, on the B-theory, not tracking a real difference in the world; it is a feature of your experience that requires a different kind of explanation.

The objection is that this is too costly. If an experience systematically represents something as having a feature that it does not have, that is an illusion — and an important one. But our experience of the present is not a peripheral or occasional experience; it is the most fundamental structure of all conscious experience. To say that this most basic datum of experience is illusory is a bold claim, and it requires not just an acknowledgment but an explanation: *why* do we have this illusion? What generates it? How does the B-theorist's picture explain the systematic misrepresentation?

## B-Theoretic Responses

**The reduction response** holds that the phenomenal distinction between present and past is explained by psychological and physical facts available to the B-theorist. The sense of the present as "happening" is explained by the fact that current perceptual processes have access to information about events at the current time, while memory processes have access only to stored representations of past times. The phenomenal difference between perception and memory is real and is explained by the different mechanisms involved — but it does not require positing an objective metaphysical present. (See Dainton 2000: Ch. 2 for a sophisticated development of this view.)

**The 'no illusion' response** holds that the B-theorist should not say the experience is illusory at all. The experience of the present as actual is accurate: what is happening at your temporal stage is *actually* happening at your temporal stage. The experience correctly represents the B-series structure of temporal reality from the perspective of your temporal location. There is nothing illusory here — only a perspectival representation of a real structure (Sider 2011: 18–19).

**The residue response** concedes that B-theory cannot fully account for the phenomenology and argues that phenomenology is not the right evidence base for metaphysics. Our experience represents time as flowing, but our experience also represents the Earth as flat, colors as intrinsic properties of objects, and the sky as a dome. Experience is a poor guide to metaphysical structure. What we need is not a phenomenologically adequate metaphysics but a metaphysics adequate to science and argument.

## Assessment

The phenomenological objection cuts deep. The residue response is weak: while experience is indeed not infallible, the experience of temporal flow is not like perceiving the sky as a dome; it is more like the experience of logical consistency, which we take very seriously. The reduction response is promising but requires an account of why the reduction works — why the phenomenal quality of present-ness maps onto nothing in the world. The 'no illusion' response is perhaps the most defensible, but it requires a careful account of what perspectival representation involves.

This objection is one of the main motivations for A-theory, particularly presentism and the moving spotlight view (see Chapter 15). If you find the phenomenological objection compelling, you are in good company.
