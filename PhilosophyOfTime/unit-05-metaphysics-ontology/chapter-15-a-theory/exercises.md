# Chapter 15 Exercises: A-Theory and Its Variants

---

## Comprehension and Analysis

**1.** State the three A-theoretic positions — presentism, growing block, moving spotlight — in your own words, identifying exactly what exists on each view. Make a table comparing them on four dimensions: (a) do past events exist?, (b) do present events exist?, (c) do future events exist?, (d) is the present metaphysically special?

**2.** What is the truthmaker problem for presentism? Identify the three main presentist responses and explain why none is entirely satisfying. Which do you find most promising, and why?

**3.** The growing block view faces an epistemological problem: how do you know you are at the edge of the block? Why is this a problem specifically for the growing block view and not equally for presentism? Does presentism avoid the problem, or merely sidestep it?

**4.** The moving spotlight theory holds that the present is an objective, non-relational property of one time. Reconstruct the "rate problem" as a formal argument. Then assess Skow's response that the movement is primitive and requires no further analysis.

**5.** Reconstruct Aristotle's sea battle argument. Identify the exact premise that leads to fatalism. Does supervaluationism successfully block the fatalistic inference? What does it cost?

---

## Synthetic and Argumentative

**6.** Among presentism, growing block, and moving spotlight, which is most compatible with special relativity? Rank the three views in terms of how serious the relativity challenge is for each, and explain your ranking.

**7.** The truthmaker problem seems to favor eternalism over all forms of A-theory. But the A-theorist might argue that eternalism has its own "truthmaker problem" — for propositions about what is not the case. Develop this counter-argument as carefully as you can, then evaluate it.

**8.** "Presentism matches common sense better than any other theory of temporal ontology, but common sense is a poor guide to metaphysics." Evaluate this claim, distinguishing between (a) the methodological point about common sense, (b) whether presentism actually matches common sense, and (c) whether 'matching common sense' is a genuine virtue in metaphysics.

## Lab and Observational Exercises

**9. Lab — The Growing Block Diary.** For three days, keep a brief daily diary. At the end of each day, write: "What now exists, that did not exist this morning?" Think about the growing block view: new events have occurred, and they have been added to the real. How does it feel to think of the day's events as *permanent additions* to the block of the real, rather than as "gone"? Write a half-page reflection at the end of the three days.

**10. Lab — The Open Future Exercise.** Make a list of five things you plan to do tomorrow. Now, for each item, consider: is the proposition "I will do X tomorrow" true or false right now? Try to hold in mind the experience of genuine uncertainty — not just epistemic uncertainty about what will happen, but *ontological* openness, the sense that what will happen is genuinely not yet real. Does this experience feel different from the experience of not knowing what has already happened? Write 150–200 words on what you notice.

**11. Lab — News as Ontology.** Listen to a news broadcast and pay attention to how reports use tense. Pick five past events reported in the news. For each, ask: on presentism, what makes this report true? On the growing block, what makes it true? On the block universe, what makes it true? Which account feels most natural for each type of report? Do different types of past-directed claim (recent vs. distant past, physical vs. historical) call for different ontological treatments?

## Reflection

**12.** Consider the phenomenological experience of the future as "open" — the way in which upcoming decisions feel genuinely undetermined, as if the future genuinely awaits constitution. Does this phenomenological datum provide strong evidence for A-theory over B-theory? Or can it be explained within a B-theoretic framework? Write a half-page reflection on whether phenomenology is the right kind of evidence for metaphysical claims about temporal ontology.
