# 15.1.1 — Presentism: The Thesis and Its Motivations

---

Presentism is defined by a single, stark claim: only present things exist. The past has ceased to exist — not merely ceased to be present, but ceased to exist altogether. The future does not yet exist — not merely the future is not present, but the future is literally nothing. Reality is exhausted by what is happening now.

## Formulating the View

A precise formulation is important, because presentism is easily confused with weaker claims. It is not the view that the present is the most important or salient time — that is a claim about value, not ontology. It is not the view that we have more knowledge of the present than of the past or future — that is a claim about epistemology. It is the claim that only present *entities* — present events, present objects, present states of affairs — genuinely *exist*. The unrestricted existential quantifier, in a correct ontology, ranges only over present things.

Dean Zimmerman gives a clean formulation: presentism is the view that "everything is present" — or equivalently, that there are no non-present things (Zimmerman 2008: 211). Compare this with eternalism (everything — past, present, and future — exists) and with the growing block view (past and present things exist, future things do not).

## Arguments for Presentism

Several considerations motivate presentism.

**The commonsense argument.** Presentism is what the pre-theoretic conception of time actually says. When we talk about "what exists," we mean what exists *now*, not what existed in the past or will exist in the future. If someone asks "does the dodo exist?," the correct answer is "no" — even though dodos once existed. The dodo no longer exists because it is no longer present. Presentism simply takes this commonsense view seriously (Markosian 2004: 48–49).

**The flow of time argument.** If time genuinely flows — if the present is the dynamic site of what is actually happening — then it is natural to think that only present things are real in the fullest sense. The past is what *has* happened: it is over, no longer happening, no longer real. The future is what *will* happen: it has not yet arrived, is not yet real. Presentism captures the intuitive sense in which the present is uniquely actual.

**The causal relevance argument.** What causes what happens now? Causal influence flows from the present and the past into the present. The future does not cause anything that is happening now; the future has no causal power over present events. This causal asymmetry suggests a corresponding ontological asymmetry: the causally efficacious is real; what has no causal power has at best a secondary or derived existence. But the future has no causal power over the present. Hence the future does not exist.

**The epistemological argument.** We have direct access — through perception — to what is present. We have indirect, inferential access to what is past (through memory, records, traces). We have only predictive, uncertain access to what is future. This epistemological asymmetry tracks an ontological asymmetry: what we are directly acquainted with is real; what we must infer from traces is gone.

None of these arguments is decisive on its own, and each has been challenged. But together they constitute a genuinely attractive case for taking presentism seriously.

## The Presentist's Distinctive Picture

The presentist picture is dramatically different from the block universe. Think of reality as a spotlight that illuminates a single time — the present — against a background of complete darkness. The past is not "in storage" somewhere; it does not exist. The future is not "waiting in the wings"; it does not exist. There is only what is present.

This means that presentism is committed to a dynamic, non-static conception of reality. Reality is not the complete four-dimensional manifold; it is the momentary cross-section that constitutes the present. As time passes, this cross-section is replaced by another — not added to, not revealing what was already there, but genuinely new. The universe grows, event by event, not in the sense of accumulating into an ever-larger block, but in the sense of constituting itself moment by moment.

There is something deeply appealing about this picture. It captures the sense that what is *happening* — what is *going on right now* — is real in a way that what has happened and what will happen are not. It preserves what may be the deepest A-series intuition: that reality has a present, and the present is where life is being lived.
