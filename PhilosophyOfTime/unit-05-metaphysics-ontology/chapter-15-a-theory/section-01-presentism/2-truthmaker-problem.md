# 15.1.2 — The Truthmaker Problem for Presentism

---

The most persistent difficulty for presentism is the truthmaker problem. True statements about the past seem to require truthmakers — something in reality that makes them true. But if the past no longer exists, there is nothing left to be the truthmaker. The presentist faces the challenge of explaining how past-directed truths are grounded without appealing to past entities.

## The Problem Stated

Consider the proposition: "The Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event occurred approximately 66 million years ago."

This proposition is true. It is true now, and it was true before anyone thought about it, and it will be true long after anyone cares. What makes it true? What is its truthmaker?

The natural answer: the extinction event itself. The event occurred; its having occurred makes propositions about it true. But the presentist says the event no longer exists. It is not a past entity lurking in some temporal attic; it has simply ceased to be. If it no longer exists, how can it make anything true now?

John Bigelow noted that this problem has deep roots: the challenge of grounding truths about the past without appeals to past entities was recognized, in different forms, by Lucretius and other ancient philosophers (Bigelow 1996: 35–36). In its modern form, it is one of the main objections to presentism.

## Proposed Solutions

Presentists have offered several strategies for dealing with the truthmaker problem.

**Haecceitism / Ersatzism about the past.** One strategy posits *abstract representations* of past events — "ersatz" past events that exist in the present as abstract entities or as theoretical posits. The present reality includes not just concrete objects and events but also abstract representations of past states of affairs. The proposition "the extinction event occurred" is made true by a present abstract fact to the effect that a past extinction event is represented in the abstract record of reality.

This strategy faces the objection that it multiplies ontology uncomfortably (we now need abstract entities corresponding to every past event) and that it is unclear what grounds the abstract facts themselves.

**Lucretianism.** Another strategy holds that the universe contains certain features that depend on past events — dispositional properties, for instance, that encode a causal history. The present-day universe has properties that it would not have had if the extinction event had not occurred, and these properties serve as truthmakers. "The extinction event occurred" is made true by the present world's having these trace-properties.

This strategy faces the "Cambridge change" worry: the properties serving as truthmakers are relational, not intrinsic, and it seems odd to say that a proposition about a specific historical event is made true by the contemporary world's having certain dispositional or trace-properties. The truthmaker seems too weak or too remote from what the proposition is about.

**Presentist haecceities.** A third strategy, developed in various forms, posits *individual essences* or *haecceities* of past objects as abstract entities that persist into the present. The haecceity of Julius Caesar — the property of being-Caesar — exists now, even though Caesar does not. Propositions about Caesar are made true by this haecceity together with facts about what that haecceity was "like" in the past.

This is clever, but critics argue it amounts to a covert form of the growing block view: if haecceities of past objects persist into the present, then something associated with past objects exists now — and one might reasonably ask what distinguishes this from simply saying that past objects themselves exist in some (perhaps diminished) form.

**Primitivism.** Some presentists bite the bullet: they deny the truthmaker principle, at least in its unrestricted form. It is a primitive, ungrounded fact that certain propositions about the past are true. There is no deeper explanation — no truthmaker — for why "the extinction event occurred" is true. It just is. This is a philosophically uncomfortable response, but it is not obviously wrong; the truthmaker principle is a theoretical commitment, not a self-evident truth (Cameron 2008: 327–342).

## Assessment

No response to the truthmaker problem fully satisfies. The haecceity strategy and the abstract-entity strategies multiply ontology without obvious benefit. The primitivism response gives up a plausible theoretical principle. The Lucretian strategy seems to misidentify the truthmaker.

The challenge is this: the more robust the past-directed truth — the more specific and concrete the claim about what happened — the less plausible it is that anything present can serve as its truthmaker. "There was a time when no humans existed" seems groundable in present evolutionary and geological facts. "Julius Caesar's last words were 'Tu quoque, Brute?'" seems not groundable in anything presently existing.

This is a serious difficulty for presentism, and it motivates both the growing block view (which preserves the existence of past events) and the block universe view (which preserves the existence of all events). Presentists are aware of the difficulty and continue to develop responses, but a fully satisfying resolution has not yet appeared.
