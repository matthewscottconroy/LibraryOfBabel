# 15.2.1 — The Growing Block: Broad's Formulation

---

C.D. Broad introduced the growing block view in *Scientific Thought* (1923), in the context of a general examination of the nature of time. His formulation is simple but pregnant with philosophical consequence: "Nothing has happened to the present by becoming past except that fresh slices of existence have been added to the total history of the world" (Broad 1923: 59).

## The View Stated

On the growing block view, the ontological inventory of the universe at any given time includes:

- All *past* events (everything that has occurred up to and including the present)
- All *present* events (whatever is occurring now)
- No *future* events (whatever has not yet occurred does not exist)

The universe is, in this sense, a growing block: it has a fixed "bottom" (the earliest events, whatever they are) and a constantly advancing "top" (the present), and the block grows as new events occur. Past events do not cease to exist when they are superseded by newer events; they remain in the block, their positions simply receding from the leading edge.

## Advantages over Presentism

The growing block view solves the truthmaker problem that bedevils presentism. Because past events genuinely exist — they are real parts of the block — they can serve as truthmakers for past-directed propositions. "The extinction event occurred 66 million years ago" is made true by the extinction event itself, which exists at its position in the growing block. The growing block theorist does not need to posit abstract representations, haecceities, or trace-properties; they can appeal directly to the events themselves.

The growing block also preserves one of the presentist's most important intuitions: the future is not yet real. This means that the universe has a genuinely open future — an ontological openness that the block universe denies. On the growing block view, there is no block corresponding to future events, because future events do not yet exist. What will happen is not yet settled in the sense of being already real.

## The Phenomenological Appeal

There is something phenomenologically satisfying about the growing block view. It captures the way in which the past feels permanent and the future feels open. When you remember yesterday's events, they feel genuinely past — not just "earlier in the manifold" but *actually gone, not happening any more*. When you anticipate tomorrow, it feels genuinely open — not "later in the manifold, already there," but *genuinely undetermined, not yet real*.

The growing block formalizes these intuitions. The past has happened and is preserved in the block; it is real, fixed, and not subject to change. The future has not happened; it is not yet real, and therefore genuinely open. This seems to match the ordinary experience of time better than either presentism (which eliminates the past) or eternalism (which eliminates the ontological distinction between past, present, and future).

## The Direction of Growth

One feature of the growing block that requires comment is the direction of growth. Why does the block grow "upward" — from the past toward the future — rather than "downward"? This is not a trivial question. If the block is real and growing, there must be something about reality that explains why it grows in the direction from earlier to later rather than the reverse.

Broad's answer is that the direction of growth *is* what the future direction of time consists in. Time's direction is the direction of ontological accretion — the direction in which new events come to be. This gives the growing block view a natural account of the direction of time that neither presentism nor eternalism possesses: for the growing block, the future direction is the direction in which reality is expanding.

This connects to the broader debate about the arrow of time, examined in Chapter 18. Some philosophers have argued that the growing block provides the most natural ontological ground for the asymmetry of time.
