# 15.2.2 — The Epistemological Problem for the Growing Block

---

The growing block view has the resources to handle the truthmaker problem and preserves an ontologically open future. But it faces a distinctive and deeply unsettling epistemological problem, first articulated clearly by Craig Bourne: how do you know you are at the edge of the block?

## The Problem Stated

On the growing block view, the present is the leading edge of reality — the most recently formed layer of the block. Past events exist in the block, but they are not the present; the present is specifically the newest, most recently real events.

Now here is the problem. From the inside — from the perspective of a conscious agent embedded in the block — the experience of being at the "leading edge" of the growing block would be indistinguishable from the experience of being embedded somewhere in the *middle* of a completed block.

Consider: if you are a temporal stage located in the year 2026, your experience consists of perceptions of your immediate environment, memories of past events, and anticipations of future events. This experiential profile — memory of the past, anticipation of the future — is exactly what you would have if you were:

(a) A person at the leading edge of the growing block in 2026, with the real future genuinely open.
(b) A person embedded in a completed block universe in 2026, with all times equally real.
(c) A person in a growing block in 2027, looking back at a "past" that includes a 2026-temporal-stage with no genuine future.

You cannot distinguish these scenarios from the inside. Your experience in each case is exactly the same: you are conscious, you have memories, you anticipate a future. But in scenario (c), the "you" in 2026 is not at the leading edge of the block; you are already in the past of the 2027-observer, a fixed and completed portion of the block. Your sense that you are "at the edge" is illusory.

Bourne formulates the challenge precisely: "If the growing block theory is true, how do any of us know that we are located at the edge of the block rather than embedded somewhere in its interior?" (Bourne 2006: 19–21).

## The Implications

The problem is more serious than it might initially appear. The growing block view is supposed to capture the intuition that we are genuinely at the "living edge" of reality — that we are experiencing real becoming, that the future is genuinely open to us. But if we cannot know that we are at the edge, then for all we know the future is already real (we are in the middle of a completed block), and the "openness" of the future is illusory.

This is a strange epistemic predicament. The growing block view is motivated partly by the phenomenological sense that we are at the edge of reality, that the future is open, that becoming is real. But the view itself cannot guarantee that this phenomenology is accurate. It allows for the possibility — indeed, it cannot rule out the possibility — that our sense of being at the "living edge" is an illusion produced by being embedded in the middle of an already-completed block.

## Responses

**The indexical response.** Some growing block theorists argue that "I am present" is like "I am here" — it is automatically true of anyone who asserts it, wherever (or whenever) they are located. If I am a temporal stage located at time t, then I am present *relative to t*, whatever the ontological status of later times. This response makes "being present" perspectival rather than objective — but then it seems to collapse into a form of B-theory.

**The primitive present response.** Others argue that the present is a primitive, mind-independent feature of reality — not merely the "leading edge" in a geometrical sense but something genuinely metaphysically distinguished. If the present is objectively marked as such — not just as the latest time in the block but as having a distinctive metaphysical property — then an agent at the leading edge would have *non-inferential access* to the fact that they are present. The problem with this response is that it seems to require positing A-properties of exactly the kind that McTaggart's regress targets.

**The acceptance response.** Perhaps the most honest response is to accept that the growing block view, unlike presentism, does not give us epistemic access to the openness of the future. Whether we are at the edge or in the middle is not something we can determine from the inside. The growing block theorist may simply accept this and argue that the view is nonetheless the best metaphysical account of temporal ontology — whatever our epistemic limitations.

## Assessment

The epistemological problem is a genuine difficulty for the growing block. It reveals that the view's phenomenological motivation — the sense of being at the living edge of reality — cannot be secured from within the view itself. A thoughtful growing block theorist must either introduce additional metaphysical machinery (a primitive present, an irreducible A-property) or accept the epistemological cost.
