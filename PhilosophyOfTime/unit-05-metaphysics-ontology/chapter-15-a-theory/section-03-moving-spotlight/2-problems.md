# 15.3.2 — Problems for the Moving Spotlight

---

The moving spotlight theory faces several serious difficulties, two of which are especially penetrating: the problem of the rate of change, and the regress problem. Both suggest that the view's attempt to combine the block universe with a dynamic present is unstable.

## The Rate Problem

If the spotlight moves — if the present advances through the temporal manifold — then it moves at some rate. But what rate? The natural answer is "one second per second" — the present advances one second for every second that passes. But this is trivially true and profoundly uninformative. It is like saying a car moves one kilometer per kilometer.

The problem is that measuring a rate requires two distinct variables: the quantity changing (position) and the parameter against which it changes (time). The present's "movement" through the temporal manifold would need to be measured against something. Movement in space is change of spatial position over time. But movement of the present through time would be change of temporal position over *what*? Over a second-order time?

This is the regress worry: if the present moves through first-order time at some rate, then it takes some amount of second-order time for the first-order present to advance. But then the second-order time needs a present of its own, and the second-order present would need to move through third-order time, and so on without end. The moving spotlight view seems to require a hierarchy of times, which is difficult to make sense of without generating a vicious regress (see Prosser 2012 for a careful development of this objection).

Skow's response in *Objective Becoming* (2015: Ch. 6) is to argue that the rate problem does not generate a vicious regress, because the "rate" of the spotlight's movement is not a real rate in the problematic sense. Rather, the movement of the present is a brute, primitive feature of temporal reality that does not admit of a further analysis in terms of a rate. Just as we do not ask at what rate space extends through some "meta-space," we should not ask at what rate the present moves through some meta-time. The movement is primitive.

This response may be coherent, but it is not entirely satisfying. The accusation that "one second per second" is trivial is precisely that it provides no information about the nature of the movement. If the movement is primitive and unanalyzable, what distinguishes a world with a moving present from a world with a static present? What does the "movement" amount to, if not a change that can be characterized in terms of some parameter?

## The Extra Structure Problem

A related difficulty is that the moving spotlight view postulates a feature of reality — the objective present — that makes no difference to the physical description of the world. The four-dimensional block manifold of events, with all its B-series relations, remains exactly the same whether or not there is a moving spotlight. The spotlight adds no physical structure; it does not affect the causal relations between events or the distribution of matter and energy.

This means the moving spotlight view posits something physically epiphenomenal: the objective present is real, but it makes no testable difference. Critics argue that such a posit violates the principle of parsimony (see Chapter 14.2.3) and raises the suspicion that the "objective present" is a metaphysical addition with no genuine explanatory work to do.

Moving spotlight theorists reply that the objective present is metaphysically required even if it is physically epiphenomenal: it grounds the truth of A-series statements, it explains why the present feels genuinely special, and it vindicates the intuitions that motivated A-theory in the first place. Parsimony in physics does not equal parsimony in metaphysics; the metaphysics may require features that the physics does not describe.

## The Regress Problem

The most serious objection is a regress that parallels McTaggart's original argument. If the present is an objective, non-relational property that one time has and all others lack, and if this present "moves" — so that yesterday's present is today's past — then we need to account for this change. The present moves from time t₁ to time t₂ to time t₃... but how are we to characterize this movement without invoking a second-order temporal structure? And doesn't that second-order structure require its own moving present?

Skow's response is to deny that the movement of the spotlight requires characterization in terms of a higher-order temporal structure. The movement is simply the fact that at one time t₁ the property of being present belongs to t₁, and at another time t₂ it belongs to t₂. This is a perfectly well-defined fact about a four-dimensional manifold with an additional primitive property, and no second-order time is required.

Whether this response is adequate depends on whether one accepts that a property can "move" through a manifold without that movement being analyzable in terms of some further structure. The intuition that motivates the regress worry is that any genuine change requires a parameter against which it is measured. The moving spotlight theorist says: not so — some changes are primitive. This is a defensible position, but it requires accepting a degree of primitiveness in the account of temporal change that many philosophers find unsatisfying.

## Assessment

The moving spotlight theory is the most ambitious A-theory, but it purchases its ambitions at a high philosophical cost. The rate problem, the extra structure problem, and the regress problem together suggest that the combination of block universe ontology and a dynamic present is not as stable as it initially appears. The view has defenders — Skow's treatment is the most serious — but the problems are genuine.

If you are drawn to the moving spotlight view, the key question to answer is: what does the movement of the present consist in, if not a rate measurable against some second-order time? If you cannot answer that question satisfactorily, you may be pushed toward either the pure block universe (which eliminates the objective present) or toward presentism or the growing block (which makes the present ontologically, not merely formally, distinctive).
