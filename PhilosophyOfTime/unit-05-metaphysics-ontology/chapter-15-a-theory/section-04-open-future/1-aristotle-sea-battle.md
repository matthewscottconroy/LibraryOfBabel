# 15.4.1 — Aristotle's Sea Battle: Bivalence and the Open Future

---

In *De Interpretatione* chapter 9, Aristotle raises what is perhaps the earliest philosophical puzzle about the metaphysics of the future. The problem is known as the *sea battle argument*, and its core is a collision between two principles that both seem compelling:

1. **The principle of bivalence:** Every proposition is either true or false; there is no third option.
2. **The principle of the open future:** Future contingent events — events that could go either way — are not yet determined; the future is genuinely open.

Aristotle argues that if both principles hold, an absurdity follows.

## The Argument

Consider the proposition: "There will be a sea battle tomorrow."

By the principle of bivalence, this proposition is either true or false — now, before tomorrow arrives. Suppose it is true. Then it is already settled that a sea battle will occur tomorrow. But if it is already settled, then the sea battle is necessary — it cannot fail to happen. And if the sea battle's occurrence is necessary, then there seems to be nothing anyone can do to prevent it. The generals' plans, the captains' decisions, the individual sailors' choices are all irrelevant: the battle will happen.

Suppose instead that the proposition is false. Then it is already settled that no sea battle will occur tomorrow. In that case, again, the outcome is necessary: the non-battle is inevitable.

Either way, if bivalence holds for future contingent propositions, the future is already fixed — not just known by some omniscient being, but *logically* fixed by the truth of present propositions about future events. This is the fatalistic conclusion: the future is as settled as the past (Aristotle, *De Interpretatione* 9, 18a28–18b25).

## The Threat of Fatalism

The worry here is not exactly determinism. Determinism is the thesis that the laws of nature and the current state of the universe necessitate every future state — a physical/metaphysical thesis. Fatalism is stronger: it holds that the future is fixed *logically*, independently of causal structure. Even in an indeterministic universe, if bivalence holds for future contingent propositions, fatalism follows: the truth of the proposition fixes the outcome independently of causal processes.

A.N. Prior (1967: 113–127) was particularly attentive to this distinction and argued that the threat of logical fatalism is a genuine problem, not dissolved by appeal to indeterminism. In a tense logic framework, the question is whether future tensed propositions have determinate truth values, and this is independent of whether the processes that determine future states are deterministic.

## Aristotle's Response: Rejecting Bivalence for Future Contingents

Aristotle's own response is radical: he denies that bivalence applies to future contingent propositions. "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" is neither true nor false right now. It is, we might say, *indeterminate*: it will become either true or false as the future unfolds, but it does not currently have a truth value. Future contingents occupy a middle status: they are not yet determined.

This position is logically controversial. It requires restricting bivalence — a principle that many logicians regard as a fundamental theorem of classical logic. How can a proposition be neither true nor false? Aristotle seems to think it can: the proposition is neither fixed-as-true nor fixed-as-false, because the underlying facts that would fix it do not yet exist.

## Supervaluationism

A more technically sophisticated version of Aristotle's response is **supervaluationism**, developed in the philosophy of language by Bas van Fraassen and applied to the logic of time by Kit Fine and others. Supervaluationism holds that future contingent propositions are *indeterminate* — their truth value is not yet fixed — but this indeterminacy is compatible with the validity of the law of excluded middle ("P or not-P") while blocking the inference to a fixed truth value for P alone.

The idea is this: a proposition P is *supertrue* if it is true in all admissible precisifications (all ways of making the indeterminate future determinate), *superfalse* if it is false in all, and indeterminate if it is true in some and false in others. "There will or won't be a sea battle tomorrow" is supertrue (it is true in every precisification), but "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is indeterminate (true in some and false in others). This preserves excluded middle while blocking fatalism (Fine 1975: 281–293).

Supervaluationism is technically elegant but philosophically controversial. It requires accepting a three-valued (or more) logic for future-directed propositions, which has consequences for many standard inferences.

## The Connection to A-Theory

The open future debate connects directly to A-theory. If the future is genuinely open — if future contingent propositions lack determinate truth values — then the future is not "already there" in the block. The open future view is naturally allied with presentism and the growing block: if future events do not yet exist, then there are no facts about them that could fix truth values of future-directed propositions.

The block universe view, by contrast, straightforwardly supports bivalence for future contingents: there is a fact about whether a sea battle occurs tomorrow, because tomorrow's events exist in the four-dimensional manifold. The B-theorist is comfortable with a fixed future precisely because all times are equally real.

## Assessment

Aristotle's sea battle argument remains philosophically productive because it isolates the tension between two deep commitments: our conviction that the future is open and our conviction that meaningful propositions have truth values. Any theory of temporal ontology must take a stance on this tension.

If you accept bivalence, you are pushed toward either accepting fatalism (and working out what that means) or explaining how bivalence is compatible with genuine openness (and the block universe offers one such explanation). If you reject bivalence for future contingents, you face the technical challenge of developing a logic that handles the resulting indeterminacy.

The debate has not been resolved, but it has been clarified: we now understand much better what the options are and what each costs.
