## Annotated Entries

**James, William. 1890. *The Principles of Psychology*. Vol. 1. Chapter 15: "The Perception of Time."**
The original source for the specious present. James writes with exceptional clarity and vividness, moving between philosophical analysis and empirical observation. Chapter 15 is among the most enjoyable pieces of philosophical psychology in the language, and its arguments remain worth engaging even after 130 years of subsequent research.

**Husserl, Edmund. 1991. *On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time*. Translated by J. B. Brough. Dordrecht: Kluwer.**
Husserl's foundational phenomenological analysis of temporal experience. Dense, technically demanding, and rewarding. The key sections are §§1–16 (the critique of Brentano's theory) and §§17–36 (the analysis of retention, primal impression, and protention). Brough's translation is the standard English version and includes a helpful introduction.

**Dainton, Barry. 2000. *Stream of Consciousness*. London: Routledge. Chapters 1–6.**
The best contemporary analytic treatment of temporal experience, defending extensionalism with care and rigor. Chapters 1–3 introduce the specious present and the debate between extensionalism and retentionalism. Chapters 4–6 engage with Husserl's retentionalism and develop Dainton's own positive account. Accessible to readers with philosophy backgrounds.

**Reichenbach, Hans. 1958. *The Philosophy of Space and Time*. Translated by M. Reichenbach and J. Freund. New York: Dover.**
Reichenbach's comprehensive treatment of the philosophy of spacetime physics. Chapter 2 develops the analysis of simultaneity, including the epsilon parameter and the conventionality thesis. The book is the founding text of logical empiricist philosophy of physics and remains essential reading for understanding the implications of special relativity.

**Perry, John. 1979. "The Problem of the Essential Indexical." *Noûs* 13 (1): 3–21.**
A short, beautifully argued paper that introduces the concept of the essential indexical and shows why certain first-person, here-and-now indexical facts are cognitively irreducible. Perry's examples are from practical reasoning, but the implications for philosophy of time are direct. One of the most influential papers in the philosophy of language.

**Maudlin, Tim. 2012. *Philosophy of Physics: Space and Time*. Princeton: Princeton University Press. Chapters 3–4.**
The clearest and most philosophically sophisticated treatment of the philosophy of Minkowski spacetime available at the introductory level. Maudlin's discussion of the conventionality of simultaneity debate (Chapter 4) provides an important corrective to the Reichenbach orthodoxy and connects the technical issues to broader metaphysical questions about the present.

**Prosser, Simon. 2012. "Why Does Time Seem to Pass?" *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 85 (1): 92–116.**
An important contemporary paper arguing that the phenomenological appearance of temporal passage cannot be straightforwardly taken as evidence for A-theoretic metaphysics. Prosser develops a sophisticated analysis of what the experience of passage involves and what it does and does not establish metaphysically.

**Varela, Francisco J. 1999. "The Specious Present." In *Naturalizing Phenomenology*, 266–314. Stanford: Stanford University Press.**
A technically rich essay that brings Husserlian phenomenology into dialogue with contemporary neuroscience. Varela's account of the "three-scale" structure of the temporal present — involving sub-second neural dynamics, the specious present, and narrative time — is one of the most sophisticated attempts to naturalize temporal phenomenology. Demanding but rewarding.

**Stein, Howard. 1991. "On Relativity Theory and Openness of the Future." *Philosophy of Science* 58 (2): 147–167.**
An important paper arguing against the strong conventionality of simultaneity thesis. Stein shows that in Minkowski spacetime, the "becoming" relation is well-defined and non-conventional — though its character differs dramatically from presentist expectations. Essential for understanding what relativistic spacetime actually allows in the way of objective temporal distinctions.
