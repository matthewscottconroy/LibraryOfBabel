# Chapter 16 Important Researchers

---

**William James (1842–1910)**
William James was an American philosopher and psychologist who founded the school of pragmatism and wrote *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), one of the founding texts of scientific psychology. His chapter on "The Perception of Time" introduced the concept of the specious present and provided the most influential early account of temporal experience in the empirical tradition. James's approach — combining philosophical precision with psychological observation — established the terms of debate for all subsequent discussions of temporal phenomenology.

**Edmund Husserl (1859–1938)**
Edmund Husserl was the founder of phenomenology, the philosophical movement that takes the structure of experience as its primary subject matter. His *On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* (lectures from 1904–1917, published in 1928) is the founding text of phenomenological approaches to temporal experience. Husserl's analysis of retention, primal impression, and protention as the three-part structure of the "living present" has influenced not only philosophy but cognitive science and neuroscience. His approach to time is notably different from the analytic tradition's: he starts from the structure of experience and works outward, rather than starting from physics and working inward.

**Hans Reichenbach (1891–1953)**
Reichenbach's work on the conventionality of simultaneity, presented in *The Philosophy of Space and Time* (1928/1958), shaped the philosophy of space and time for the rest of the twentieth century. Reichenbach was a rigorous philosopher of science who engaged seriously with the technical details of physics and drew out their philosophical implications. His claim that simultaneity involves a conventional element has been contested but not refuted, and the debate he initiated remains active.

**John Perry (born 1943)**
John Perry is an American philosopher of language who introduced the concept of the "essential indexical" in a 1979 paper of that name. His analysis of why indexical expressions like "I," "here," and "now" cannot be replaced by non-indexical equivalents without loss of cognitive content has been widely influential. Perry's framework clarifies the philosophical significance of temporal indexicals and raises the question of whether "now" is essentially indexical in a way that supports the A-theorist's claim that tensed knowledge is irreducible.

**Barry Dainton (born 1963)**
Barry Dainton is a British philosopher at the University of Liverpool whose *Stream of Consciousness* (2000) is the most thorough and philosophically sophisticated examination of temporal experience available in the analytic tradition. Dainton defends extensionalism — the view that the specious present is genuinely temporally extended — and engages seriously with the retentionalist alternative. His work bridges analytic philosophy, phenomenology, and empirical psychology in an unusually productive way.

**Adolf Grünbaum (1923–2018)**
Adolf Grünbaum was a German-American philosopher of science at the University of Pittsburgh who extended Reichenbach's analysis of simultaneity and developed a comprehensive account of the philosophical implications of the special and general theories of relativity. His *Philosophical Problems of Space and Time* (1963) is a major work that takes the physics seriously and draws its philosophical consequences rigorously. Grünbaum argued forcefully for the conventionality of simultaneity and against objective A-series properties.

**Tim Maudlin (born 1958)**
Tim Maudlin is an American philosopher of physics who has engaged closely with both the technical content of spacetime physics and its philosophical implications. His *Philosophy of Physics: Space and Time* (2012) is the best introductory treatment of the philosophy of relativistic spacetime for students with philosophical backgrounds. Maudlin's engagement with the conventionality of simultaneity debate — where he defends a non-conventionalist reading — provides an important corrective to the Reichenbach-Grünbaum position.

**Francisco Varela (1946–2001)**
Francisco Varela was a Chilean biologist and neuroscientist who co-developed the theory of enactivism — the view that mind and cognition arise from the dynamic interaction between organism and environment. His work on the "specious present" from a neurophenomenological perspective, combining Husserlian phenomenology with neuroscience, represents an important bridge between philosophical and empirical approaches to temporal experience. His 1999 essay "The Specious Present" remains one of the most sophisticated treatments of the neuroscience of the temporal present.
