# 16.1.1 — Nowness: Objective or Perspectival?

---

One of the most persistent questions in the philosophy of the present is whether "nowness" — the property of being present, the distinctive character of *this* moment as opposed to all others — is an objective feature of reality or a perspectival one.

## Two Positions

The **objective view** holds that there is a fact about which time is present that is independent of any observer's perspective. Just as there is a fact about how many electrons there are in the universe, independent of any observer, there is a fact about which moment is *now* — a fact that holds regardless of whether anyone is observing it, thinking about it, or located at that moment. On this view, if the universe existed with no conscious beings in it, there would still be a present moment — the metaphysical present would still be advancing through the manifold (or one moment would still possess objective presentness).

This is the view of most A-theorists, and especially of moving spotlight theorists. It is what makes A-theory distinctively A-theoretic: the claim that A-series properties are mind-independent, not mere artifacts of a cognitive or linguistic perspective.

The **perspectival view** holds that "nowness" is like "hereness" — a feature that belongs to a time only in relation to an observer. Just as there is no observer-independent "here," there is no observer-independent "now." Every time is "now" for someone located at that time; no time is "now" independently of any observer. On this view, the apparent specialness of the present is real but not metaphysically deep: it reflects the trivial fact that our experiences are always at our temporal location, just as our spatial perceptions are always from our spatial location.

This is the view of B-theorists and of philosophers who think the indexical analysis of "now" is correct.

## The Problem of Epistemic Presence

There is a puzzle about the present that cuts across the objective/perspectival distinction: why do we always seem to know that we are in the present? Wherever and whenever you are — whatever time you are located at — you know that that time is "now." You cannot imagine being in the present and not knowing it. This is sometimes called the *epistemic presence* problem.

On the B-theory's perspectival view, this is trivially explained: "now" refers to the time of the speaker's utterance, so of course you always know that you are "now" — it is a trivially self-referential claim. You know that *this very moment* is *this very moment* in the same way you know that *you* are *you*. It is not a substantive metaphysical discovery; it is a logical truth about self-reference.

But on the objective view, the epistemic presence is less trivially explained. If there is an objective present — a time that has the mind-independent property of presentness — and if your being at the present time is not guaranteed (you might be at a past or future time that lacks presentness), then your knowledge that you are at the present requires explanation. How do you know you are at the objectively present time and not at a past or future time?

The A-theorist's natural response is that you are at the present time *because* conscious experience is always present — that consciousness is inherently present-directed. But this seems to invoke an extra connection between consciousness and presentness that requires further argument.

## The "Essential Indexical" and Its Consequences

John Perry's work on essential indexicals is relevant here (Perry 1979: 3–7). An "essential indexical" is an indexical expression whose use cannot be replaced by a non-indexical expression without loss of content. "I need to clean up the mess I am making" has an essential use of "I": if you replace "I" with your name, the sentence no longer has the same cognitive role — you might not know that you are the person making the mess. Similarly, "the meeting is happening now" has an essential use of "now": if you replace "now" with a clock time, the sentence loses the urgent action-guiding quality that makes "now" practically significant.

If "now" is an essential indexical — if its practical and cognitive role cannot be captured by any non-indexical replacement — then this supports the A-theorist's claim that tensed knowledge is irreducible. But it also fits the B-theorist's indexical analysis: it is precisely because "now" is indexical that it cannot be replaced by "at 3:00 PM" without loss of practical content.

## Living in the Present

Here is the phenomenological datum that both sides need to account for. When you are conscious, your experience is always experience of *this moment* — not of some abstract moment specified by a clock or a B-series relation, but of *now*. The presentness of experience is not a theoretical conclusion; it is the most immediate fact of conscious life.

The B-theorist says: this is just what it is like to be a temporal stage. Of course your experiences are at your temporal location; they could not be elsewhere. The A-theorist says: yes, but the *quality* of that location — the sense of it as the *actual*, *happening* present — cannot be reduced to a B-series fact. It is the A-series that makes the present feel like the present.

This disagreement may be deep enough that it cannot be resolved by argument alone; it may depend on whether you find the phenomenological datum of "epistemic presence" genuinely explanatorily demanding or not. What is clear is that the datum is real and that any adequate philosophy of the present must account for it.
