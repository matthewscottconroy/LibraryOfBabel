# 16.2.2 — Extensionalism versus Retentionalism

---

The specious present raises a fundamental question about the structure of temporal experience: is the "extended now" of experience genuinely extended — does the experience itself occupy an interval of time — or is the sense of extension produced by a different mechanism, one in which the experience is really instantaneous but incorporates representations of the just-past?

This question divides philosophers and psychologists into two broad camps: **extensionalists** and **retentionalists**.

## Extensionalism

Extensionalism holds that the specious present is genuinely temporally extended. When you hear a melody over three seconds, your experience of hearing the melody genuinely occupies three seconds: the experience itself has temporal breadth. The experiencing of the first note and the experiencing of the third note are both parts of a single, extended experience that spans the interval.

Barry Dainton is the most prominent contemporary defender of extensionalism (*Stream of Consciousness*, 2000). Dainton argues that temporal experiences are "extensionally connected" — they overlap and interpenetrate in ways that create the characteristic flow of consciousness. The specious present is not a compressed snapshot of temporal information; it is a genuinely temporally thick episode of awareness.

The key claim is that the experience of temporal passage is not produced by memory or retention of earlier states; it is a matter of directly experiencing an interval. Just as spatial perception can encompass a region of space — not a point — temporal perception can encompass an interval of time — not a moment.

**The advantage:** Extensionalism takes seriously the phenomenological datum that we *genuinely experience* temporal sequence — not just recall it or anticipate it. The experienced melody is a single, extended experience of sequence, not a memory of notes combined with a current hearing.

**The difficulty:** If the experience of a three-second melody genuinely occupies three seconds, then we have an experience that is extended in the very dimension it is about. This creates a puzzle about how the experience is unified: what makes the experience of note-1 and the experience of note-3 parts of the *same* experience? If they are temporally separated by three seconds, they need something to bind them into a single experience.

## Retentionalism

Retentionalism holds that the specious present is not genuinely extended but is produced by a retention mechanism: the just-past is represented in the present experience as "just past" — it is not remembered in the full sense, but it is held in a kind of immediate consciousness that Edmund Husserl called *retention*. The current experience incorporates retentions of the immediate past and *protentions* (anticipations) of the immediate future, creating the experiential sense of temporal flow without requiring the experience itself to be temporally extended.

Husserl developed this view in *On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* (1928). On his account, the "living present" is always already structured by retentions and protentions. When you experience note-3 of a melody, your experience of note-3 is accompanied by a retention of note-2, which is accompanied by a retention of note-1. The experience is present-located but temporally structured by these retained representations of the immediate past.

**The advantage:** Retentionalism preserves the idea that each moment of experience is genuinely present — occurring at a durationless instant — while explaining how temporal sequence is represented. The experience of temporal flow is real, but it does not require the experience to be temporally thick.

**The difficulty:** Critics (especially Dainton 2000: Ch. 6) argue that retentionalism cannot adequately explain the *genuine flow* of experience — the sense in which one is experiencing a sequence as it happens, not merely remembering it. Retention seems too much like memory, too much like a representation of the past rather than a direct experience of sequence.

## The Stakes

This debate has implications beyond cognitive psychology. If extensionalism is right, then the experienced present is genuinely extended, and the mathematical model of the present as a durationless instant is profoundly misrepresenting the temporal structure of experience. If retentionalism is right, then the extensionally rich experience of temporal flow is produced by a different mechanism — and the "thickness" of the present is a feature of representation rather than of reality.

For the metaphysics of the present, the extensionalist/retentionalist debate raises the question: should we model the metaphysical present on the mathematical instant (as physics does) or on the specious present (as phenomenology suggests)? Can physics and phenomenology be reconciled on this point, or is there an irreducible gap between the "now" of the physicist's equations and the "now" of conscious experience?
