# 16.3.1 — Relativity of Simultaneity and the Objective Present

---

Einstein's 1905 special theory of relativity includes among its consequences the relativity of simultaneity: two events that are simultaneous in one inertial reference frame are not, in general, simultaneous in all inertial reference frames. This is not a matter of measurement error or epistemic limitation; it is a structural feature of spacetime.

## What Relativity of Simultaneity Means

In Newtonian mechanics, there is an absolute simultaneity: two events either happen at the same time or they do not, and this is an observer-independent fact. The time variable t in Newton's equations is the same for all observers; there is a universal "now" that synchronizes all events in a single moment.

Special relativity replaces this with a spacetime structure (Minkowski spacetime) in which there is no universal t. Different inertial observers — observers in uniform motion relative to each other — decompose spacetime into time and space differently. Their "planes of simultaneity" tilt relative to each other in spacetime, so that events simultaneous on one observer's plane are not simultaneous on another's.

The physical basis for this is the constancy of the speed of light. Einstein showed that if the speed of light is the same for all inertial observers (regardless of their motion relative to the light source), then simultaneity must be relative. The celebrated "train and lightning" thought experiment illustrates the point: lightning strikes the front and back of a moving train simultaneously for an observer on the platform but not simultaneously for an observer on the train.

## Implications for the Concept of the Present

The present moment, as ordinarily understood, is the set of all events simultaneous with the observer's "now." If simultaneity is relative, then the present moment is also relative: what is "now" for one observer is not "now" for another observer in relative motion.

For an observer at rest on Earth in 2026, their "present" consists of all events simultaneous with their current temporal stage according to their simultaneity convention. For an observer moving at a significant fraction of the speed of light relative to Earth, their "present" tilts relative to the Earth observer's — it includes different events at distant locations.

This has a striking consequence. Suppose there is a civilization one billion light-years away. For an Earth observer in 2026, the "present" events of that civilization — on the Earth observer's simultaneity slice — might be events corresponding to the civilization's year 2026. But for an observer on Earth who has just taken a fast walk to the right, their simultaneity slice tilts slightly, and the "present" events of the distant civilization might correspond to the civilization's year 2016, or 2036, depending on the direction of the walk. A slight change in the observer's velocity shifts the "present" of distant regions dramatically (because the simultaneity slices in Minkowski spacetime are tilted, and small tilts at zero distance become large temporal differences at cosmological distances).

## Does This Eliminate the Objective Present?

The immediate philosophical implication seems to be: if the present is determined by simultaneity, and simultaneity is observer-relative, then the present is observer-relative. There is no observer-independent, objective present — no global "now" that holds for all observers.

This is the premise from which the Rietdijk-Putnam argument (Chapter 14.2.1) derives the conclusion that all events are equally real. But the relativity of simultaneity also bears on a different question: whether there is an objective present at all, as opposed to merely an observer-relative present.

For the A-theorist committed to an objective present, this is genuinely threatening. If the present is what is simultaneous with the observer, and simultaneity is relative, then the present is not objective. The moving spotlight theorist who posits an objectively moving present must identify the "frame" in which the spotlight moves — but special relativity says there is no privileged frame.

For the B-theorist, relativity of simultaneity is not threatening in the same way: the B-theorist did not believe in an objective present to begin with. The relativity of simultaneity simply confirms what B-theorists already thought: "now" is observer-relative and perspectival.
