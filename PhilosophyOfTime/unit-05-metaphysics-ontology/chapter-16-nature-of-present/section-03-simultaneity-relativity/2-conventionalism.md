# 16.3.2 — Conventionalism about Simultaneity: Reichenbach and Grünbaum

---

Hans Reichenbach introduced a further complication into the philosophy of the present: even within a given reference frame, the choice of simultaneity relation involves a conventional element. This is the thesis of the **conventionality of simultaneity**, and it has direct implications for the metaphysics of "now."

## Reichenbach's Epsilon

In *The Philosophy of Space and Time* (1958, originally published in German in 1928), Reichenbach argued that the synchronization of distant clocks — and hence the determination of which events at distant locations are simultaneous with a given event here — requires a *convention* about the one-way speed of light. The two-way speed of light (a beam emitted, reflected, and returned) is measurable without any convention. But the one-way speed — the speed of light traveling *from* here *to* there — is not directly measurable; it depends on the synchronization of the clocks at the two ends, which is precisely what we are trying to determine.

Reichenbach introduced a parameter ε (epsilon) to represent this conventional choice. If ε = 1/2, we assume the one-way speed of light is the same in both directions (the standard Einstein synchrony convention). But ε can in principle take any value between 0 and 1, giving different simultaneity conventions, all consistent with the same observable facts.

Different values of ε give different "planes of simultaneity" — different determinations of which distant events are simultaneous with a given local event. The observable predictions of special relativity are the same for all values of ε (because the two-way speed of light is invariant). Only the simultaneity relation — and hence the "present" — changes.

## Implications for the Present

The conventionality of simultaneity has a striking implication: even setting aside the relativity of simultaneity between reference frames, there is a conventional element in determining what is "simultaneous with now" at distant locations. Different synchrony conventions give different "presents."

Adolf Grünbaum extended Reichenbach's analysis and argued that the conventionality of simultaneity shows that there is no "natural" or "objectively privileged" present, even within a reference frame (Grünbaum 1963: 347–358). The choice of which events at distant locations are "now" is partly conventional — a matter of how we choose to use our clocks and coordinate systems.

For the metaphysics of the present, this adds a further layer of difficulty for the A-theorist. Not only is the present observer-relative (due to special relativity), it is also convention-relative within a reference frame (due to the conventionality of simultaneity). For the B-theorist, this further supports the indexical view of "now": "what is present" turns out to be partly a matter of convention, not a metaphysical discovery.

## How Much Conventionality?

Not everyone accepts the Reichenbach-Grünbaum conventionality thesis. Some philosophers of physics — notably Howard Stein (1991) and Tim Maudlin (2012: 156–162) — have argued that while ε is technically free to vary, there is a physically privileged choice (ε = 1/2) that corresponds to the geometrically natural synchrony convention in Minkowski spacetime. On this view, the convention is not wholly arbitrary; it is the convention that reflects the geometric structure of spacetime most cleanly.

This does not fully restore an objective present, but it suggests that the conventionality is less radical than Reichenbach supposed. The "natural" synchrony convention gives a "natural" simultaneity relation within each frame — even if that relation is still frame-relative.

## What Remains

After Reichenbach and Grünbaum, the situation is this: the concept of "the present moment" at distant locations is both observer-relative (frame-dependent) and convention-laden (synchrony-convention-dependent). For any local event, the determination of what is "simultaneous with it" at distant locations depends on both the observer's reference frame and the synchrony convention adopted.

What is *not* conventional, and not frame-relative, is the local present: the temporal stage of the observer at a given moment, which is present for that observer simpliciter. The conventionality and relativity concern the *extension* of the local present to distant events. The present, as a local phenomenon, is as objective as any physical fact.

This suggests a more modest A-theory: an A-theory that claims objectivity for the local present — the fact that there is a genuine "now" for each observer at each point in their history — while accepting that the extension of "now" to distant events involves conventional or frame-relative elements. Whether this is a substantive A-theory or a collapse into B-theory depends on how much one requires the present to be global.
