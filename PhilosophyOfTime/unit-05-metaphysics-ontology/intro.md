# Unit V: The Metaphysics of Temporal Ontology

---

## Unit Introduction

You have probably spent most of your philosophical life — and your ordinary life — assuming that the past is gone, the present is real, and the future has not arrived yet. This seems not just obvious but unavoidable. What else could "the past" mean, if not what has ceased to exist? What else could "the present" mean, if not what is actually happening right now, as opposed to what merely was or will be?

These assumptions are so deeply woven into everyday thought that questioning them feels like a form of perversity. And yet the moment you subject them to even minimal philosophical pressure, they begin to strain. What does it mean to say that something "has ceased to exist"? Ceased to exist *for whom*? According to what standard? If you say the Battle of Hastings happened in 1066, you are implying that it happened — that there is a fact about it, that it was real. But if the past is literally nothing, if it no longer exists in any sense, what is this fact a fact *about*? What makes your statement true?

These are not merely verbal puzzles. They cut to the heart of what it means for anything to exist at all, and to exist *in time*.

This unit confronts those questions directly. We will examine the foundational framework established by J. M. E. McTaggart at the turn of the twentieth century — a framework that continues to organize analytical philosophy of time — and then follow the major positions that have developed in response to it. McTaggart's distinction between the A-series and the B-series provides the essential conceptual vocabulary, and his argument for the unreality of time provides the essential provocation. From that provocation, two broad camps emerge: the B-theorists, who hold that the universe is a four-dimensional block in which all times exist equally, and the A-theorists, who insist that time is genuinely dynamic, that there is a real difference between what is past, present, and future, and that this difference is not merely a matter of perspective.

Chapter 13 gives McTaggart his full due — not as a curiosity or a cautionary tale, but as a thinker whose argument is more rigorous and more difficult to escape than most people suppose. Chapter 14 develops B-theory and eternalism in their strongest contemporary forms, examining the implications of the block universe for identity, persistence, and free will. Chapter 15 surveys the A-theoretic alternatives: presentism, the growing block, and the moving spotlight theory. Chapter 16 zooms in on what may be the single most contested object in the philosophy of time: the present itself — what it is, whether it is an objective feature of reality, and what our experience of it actually shows.

By the end of this unit, you will not simply know the names of positions and their standard objections. You will understand why intelligent people are driven to each of them, what the real costs are of accepting each one, and what questions remain genuinely open. The metaphysics of temporal ontology is not a solved subject. It is a living argument, and you are invited to enter it.
