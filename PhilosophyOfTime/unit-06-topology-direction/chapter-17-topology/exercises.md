# Chapter 17 Exercises: The Topology of Time

---

## Comprehension and Analysis

**1.** What are the five defining properties of a strict total order? Which of these properties does circular time violate? Does branching time violate any of them, and if so, which?

**2.** Explain the difference between discrete, dense, and continuous time with an example of a mathematical structure that exemplifies each. What physical or philosophical evidence might bear on which structure actually applies to time?

**3.** What is a closed timelike curve (CTC)? How does Gödel's solution demonstrate that CTCs are consistent with general relativity? What philosophical conclusion did Gödel himself draw from this, and do you agree with his reasoning?

**4.** Explain Kant's antinomy about the beginning of time. Which premise of each argument (thesis and antithesis) is most vulnerable, and why? Does modern cosmology resolve the antinomy, or merely transform it?

**5.** What is the Hartle-Hawking "no-boundary" proposal? In what sense does it imply that time had no beginning? In what sense does it still allow for a boundary of classical spacetime?

---

## Synthetic and Argumentative

**6.** Nietzsche's eternal return is often presented as implying a circular time topology, but the argument can be reconstructed in a linear time with Poincaré recurrence. Distinguish carefully between: (a) the topological thesis (time is circular), (b) the metaphysical thesis (the universe will eventually recur), and (c) the existential thesis (you should live as if your life will recur). Which of these three theses is most defensible?

**7.** Consider the possibility that time is discrete at the Planck scale. What would this imply for (a) Zeno's paradoxes, (b) the mathematical model of instantaneous velocity, (c) the question of whether the present is a durationless instant? Are these implications welcome or problematic?

**8.** David Lewis argues that time travel to the past is possible but that paradoxes (like the grandfather paradox) are resolved by the consistency principle: you cannot change the past, only revisit it. Is this reply satisfying? What does it imply about the causal structure of time-travel scenarios?

## Lab and Observational Exercises

**9. Lab — Mapping Time's Topology.** Take a piece of paper and draw the topology of time as you ordinarily assume it to be. Then draw what circular time would look like. Then draw branching time. Now compare: can you draw a topology that is both circular and branching? What would that mean? Bring your diagrams to class and compare with your classmates' drawings — are there differences that reveal different background assumptions about time's topology?

**10. Lab — The Eternal Return as Thought Experiment.** Nietzsche asks: if you had to live your life again, exactly as it has been, infinite times, would you will it? Spend twenty minutes writing a response. This is not a physics question; it is an ethical and existential one. What does your response reveal about what you value most and least about your current life? Does the thought experiment have philosophical value independent of whether eternal recurrence is metaphysically true?

**11. Lab — Observing Temporal Order.** Spend an hour observing the regularities of the world around you that depend on the linearity of time (effects following causes, things aging in one direction, sequences having a fixed order). Now try to imagine — as vividly as possible — what it would be like to observe these regularities if time were circular. Would the observations look any different? What would give away the circularity? Write a 200-word reflection.

---

## Reflection

**12.** "If time had a beginning, then there was literally nothing before it — no space, no time, no cause, no reason. The universe simply began." Do you find this coherent? Disturbing? Acceptable? Does it feel different from the claim that the universe has always existed? Write a half-page reflection exploring your intuitions about these two possibilities.
