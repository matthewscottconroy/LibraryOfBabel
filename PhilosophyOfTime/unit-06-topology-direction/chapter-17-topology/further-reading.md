## Annotated Entries

**Newton-Smith, W. H. 1980. *The Structure of Time*. London: Routledge.**
The most comprehensive philosophical treatment of temporal topology available. Newton-Smith carefully separates empirical from conceptual questions about time's structure, examines discrete, dense, and continuous time with logical precision, and engages seriously with the physics. Still the standard reference for rigorous work on temporal topology.

**Gödel, Kurt. 1949. "A Remark about the Relationship Between Relativity Theory and Idealistic Philosophy." In *Albert Einstein: Philosopher-Scientist*, 555–562. La Salle: Open Court.**
Short (eight pages), dense, and extraordinary. Gödel presents his rotating universe solution and draws the philosophical conclusion that time is "ideal" in the sense of McTaggart and Kant. Essential reading for anyone interested in the intersection of physics, logic, and philosophy of time. Read it alongside the physics papers for the full picture.

**Lewis, David. 1976. "The Paradoxes of Time Travel." *American Philosophical Quarterly* 13 (2): 145–152.**
The classic philosophical treatment of time travel paradoxes, arguing that they are resolved by the consistency principle. Lewis's analysis is characteristically clear and rigorous, and his distinction between "personal time" and "external time" is essential for thinking about what time travel would involve.

**Prior, Arthur N. 1967. *Past, Present and Future*. Oxford: Clarendon Press. Chapters 2 and 7.**
Chapter 2 develops tense logic for linear time; Chapter 7 extends it to branching time. Prior's treatment is the foundational formal work on the logic of branching time, and his philosophical motivation for the formal developments is always explicit. Essential for anyone interested in the formal aspects of temporal topology.

**Belnap, Nuel, Michael Perloff, and Ming Xu. 2001. *Facing the Future: Agents and Choices in Our Indeterminist World*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
A rigorous and comprehensive formal treatment of branching time, connecting temporal structure to indeterminism, agency, and causal structure. The first half of the book develops the formal framework; the second applies it to questions about agency and free will. Demanding, but the clearest thing available on branching space-times.

**Hawking, Stephen. 1988. *A Brief History of Time*. New York: Bantam.**
The most widely read popular treatment of cosmology in the twentieth century. Chapters 8–9 on the origin and fate of the universe and Chapter 10 on the arrow of time are directly relevant. Hawking writes with accessibility and wit, and his discussion of the no-boundary proposal (Chapter 8) is essential context. Read critically: some of Hawking's philosophical conclusions are more tentative than his confident prose suggests.

**Salmon, Wesley C. 2001. *Zeno's Paradoxes*. Indianapolis: Hackett.**
An anthology of important papers on Zeno, including selections from ancient sources, modern mathematical treatments, and philosophical analyses. The editor's introduction provides a useful map of the debate. Salmon's own "A Contemporary Look at Zeno's Paradoxes" is an excellent starting point for engaging with the mathematical and philosophical dimensions.

**Rovelli, Carlo. 2018. *The Order of Time*. New York: Riverhead Books.**
A beautifully written popular treatment of the physics of time by one of the founders of loop quantum gravity. Rovelli argues that many of our commonsense features of time — the present, the arrow, the flow — are emergent and perspectival rather than fundamental. An ideal companion to more technical treatments; the book is philosophically sophisticated despite its accessible style.

**Nietzsche, Friedrich. 1974. *The Gay Science*. Translated by Walter Kaufmann. New York: Vintage. §341.**
The original statement of the eternal return thought experiment, in the form of a parable about a demon who tells you that you will live your life again, exactly, infinitely. Read §341 slowly and carefully; then read *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* III ("The Vision and the Riddle" and "The Convalescent") for the more dramatic treatment. Understanding the existential dimension of the doctrine requires both texts.
