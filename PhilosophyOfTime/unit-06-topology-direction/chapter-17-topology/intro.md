# Chapter 17: The Topology of Time

---

## Chapter Introduction

Topology is the branch of mathematics that studies the properties of spaces that are preserved under continuous deformation — stretching, bending, twisting — but not tearing or gluing. A coffee cup and a donut have the same topology (both have one hole); a sphere and a donut do not. Topology is concerned not with distances or angles but with the global shape and connectivity of a space.

Applied to time, topological questions ask about the *shape* of the temporal dimension in the most fundamental sense: is it linear or circular? Does it have endpoints? Does it branch into multiple futures? Is it a continuum, or might it be discrete? These questions are prior to — and independent of — the questions examined in Units IV and V about the content of temporal positions. Whatever exists at temporal positions (events, facts, experiences), the question of how those positions are arranged topologically is a separate question.

Some of these questions can be settled by physics. The topology of spacetime is, in principle, an empirical matter: we can ask what models of spacetime are consistent with our best physical theories and what evidence we have for or against each. Other questions are more purely philosophical: what would it even mean for time to be circular, and what consequences would that have for identity, causation, and free will? What does it mean for time to have a "beginning" in the cosmological sense, and does that differ from having a beginning in the philosophical sense?

This chapter moves through four sections. Section 17.1 examines the three main topological alternatives for temporal ordering: linear, circular, and branching. Section 17.2 examines the mathematical structure of temporal order: discrete, dense, and continuous. Section 17.3 examines temporal boundaries — whether time has a beginning or an end — and what modern cosmology says about this. Section 17.4 examines Gödel's universe: a solution to Einstein's equations that allows time travel and reveals the extent to which temporal topology is a contingent physical matter.
