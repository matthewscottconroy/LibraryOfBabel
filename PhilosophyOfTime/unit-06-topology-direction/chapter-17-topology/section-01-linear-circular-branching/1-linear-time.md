# 17.1.1 — Linear Time

---

The linear model is so deeply embedded in Western thought about time that most discussions of temporal topology do not bother to state it explicitly. It is simply assumed: time is like a line, with a before-and-after structure, no loops, extending in at least one direction toward infinity. Let us take this assumption apart.

## What Linearity Means

The **linear model** holds that time is isomorphic to the real number line, or to some subset of it (such as the positive reals, if time has a beginning). This implies:

**Total ordering:** For any two distinct temporal positions t₁ and t₂, either t₁ is before t₂ or t₂ is before t₁. There are no two times that are "incomparable" — neither before nor after each other.

**Transitivity:** If t₁ is before t₂ and t₂ is before t₃, then t₁ is before t₃.

**Irreflexivity:** No time is before itself.

**Asymmetry:** If t₁ is before t₂, then t₂ is not before t₁.

**No loops:** There is no sequence of times t₁, t₂, ..., tₙ such that each is before the next and tₙ is before (or is) t₁.

**Unboundedness (optionally):** Time extends without end in both the past direction and the future direction. (This is optional: a linear time can have a beginning, an end, or both.)

These conditions are jointly sufficient for what mathematicians call a *strict total order* — the simplest and most familiar ordering structure. The real number line, with its familiar ordering of numbers by size, is the paradigm case.

## Philosophical Consequences of Linearity

Linear time has several philosophical consequences that are worth making explicit.

**Unique temporal positions.** On the linear model, each event occupies a unique temporal position: it happened once, at one time, and that time is distinct from all others. Events do not recur; they occur at most once. This supports the uniqueness of history: there is one sequence of events, with a determinate before-and-after structure.

**The past is fixed.** Because the linear ordering is asymmetric and irreflexible, the past is permanently behind us. There is no going back — not because of physical limitations, but because of the topology: "backward" is a well-defined direction, and the events in that direction are in the past and stay there.

**Counterfactual reasoning.** The linear model makes counterfactual reasoning well-defined: "what would have happened if..." refers to a non-actual temporal sequence that branches from the actual sequence at some earlier point. On branching time, counterfactuals might be taken more literally (as referring to actual alternative branches). On circular time, counterfactuals become paradoxical. The linear model makes the most natural sense of counterfactual reasoning.

**The open question of infinity.** Linear time is compatible with being infinite in both directions, semi-infinite (having a beginning but no end, or an end but no beginning), or even finite in both directions. Whether time is infinite is an empirical question, not settled by linearity alone.

## Built-In Assumptions

The linear model builds in several assumptions that are not forced on us by experience alone.

**Totality:** The assumption that any two times are comparable — that there are no "incomparable" times — is not forced by experience. In relativistic spacetime, spacelike-separated events are not temporally comparable in a frame-independent way. The linear model is an idealization appropriate for a single causal trajectory (a "worldline") but not for relativistic spacetime as a whole.

**Continuity:** The standard linear model takes time to be a continuum — like the real numbers, with no gaps. But this is an assumption. Time might be dense (like the rationals, with no gaps in the density sense but with "holes" in the completeness sense) or discrete (like the integers, with no times between adjacent times). We will examine this in Section 17.2.

**Uniqueness of the future:** The linear model says there is one future — a single continuation of the present temporal sequence. This is exactly what branching time denies.

## Assessment

The linear model is not merely a default assumption; it is a substantive metaphysical and physical commitment. It works well for many purposes — for reasoning about history, causation, and personal identity — and it is supported by classical physics. But as we examine its competitors, we will see that linearity is neither the only coherent possibility nor necessarily the correct description of temporal reality.
