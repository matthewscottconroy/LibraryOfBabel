# 17.1.2 — Circular Time

---

Circular time is the topology in which the temporal order wraps around on itself, so that the sequence of events eventually returns to its own beginning. It is the temporal analog of a circle rather than a line: if you travel far enough in the future direction, you arrive at the past. Every event is, in principle, both before and after every other event — just as every point on a circle is both "clockwise from" and "counterclockwise from" every other point, depending on which way you traverse the circle.

## The Mathematical Structure

In circular time, the temporal dimension is topologically a circle (in the mathematical sense: the 1-sphere S¹). There is a cyclic ordering of events: event A can be "before" B and B before C and C before A, with no contradiction — because "before" is being read as a local relation along the circle, not a global one.

This means that on circular time, there is no global "earlier than" relation that works the way the linear model requires. You can define a local ordering — in the region of the circle around a given point, there is a clear before-and-after — but this local ordering cannot be extended to a global one without contradiction. There is no time that is globally "first" or globally "last": every time has events before and after it (in the local sense), and every time has every other event in both its past and its future (in the global sense).

## Nietzsche's Eternal Return

Circular time is famously associated with Nietzsche's doctrine of the **Eternal Return** (ewige Wiederkehr), announced in *The Gay Science* (1882) and dramatized in *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (1883–1891). Nietzsche presented the eternal return as his most fundamental metaphysical thought: that the universe, having a finite amount of matter and energy in infinite time, must eventually repeat every configuration it has ever been in, infinitely many times. Every moment of your life, with every detail intact, will recur, has recurred, infinitely.

It is important to distinguish Nietzsche's **metaphysical** claim (the universe actually does repeat) from the **topological** claim (time is circular). Nietzsche's metaphysical claim does not require circular time in the topological sense; it requires only that the physical states of the universe recur in a linear time — which is what Boltzmann's Poincaré recurrence theorem establishes (see Chapter 18). Topologically circular time is a different, stronger thesis.

But Nietzsche's primary interest in the eternal return was not metaphysical but **ethical and existential**: the thought experiment "Would you be willing to live this life again, exactly as it is, infinitely?" is meant as a test of affirmation. Does your life have enough meaning and value that you would wish it to recur? This ethical use of the eternal return does not depend on the metaphysical claim being true.

## Philosophical Paradoxes of Circular Time

Circular time generates several philosophical puzzles.

**The impossibility of global "before":** As noted, on circular time, every event is both before and after every other event in the global sense. This seems to violate the transitivity of "earlier than" in a way that makes tense logic and causal reasoning very difficult to formulate. Prior (1967: 65–67) showed that circular time generates paradoxes in standard tense logic.

**Personal identity:** If time is circular and you eventually "return" to your earlier moments, what does this mean for personal identity? Are you the numerically same person who existed at that earlier moment, or a qualitatively identical person? If numerically the same, then there is a sense in which you have always already lived your life — the distinction between first and second occurrence collapses.

**Change and recurrence:** On a perfectly circular time, every state of the universe eventually recurs exactly. But if exactly the same state recurs, has there been any genuine change? Or is the "recurrence" merely the same moment approached again — in which case, is there really any multiplicity of moments, or just one moment?

**The direction of time:** Perhaps the most striking consequence: on perfectly circular time, the distinction between past and future collapses globally. Every event is in the past (if you go far enough "back") and in the future (if you go far enough "forward"). This eliminates any global arrow of time, though local asymmetries may be preserved.

## Does Physics Support Circular Time?

In standard general relativity, spacetime can have *closed timelike curves* — trajectories in spacetime that are locally future-directed but globally loop back to their starting point. Gödel's solution (Section 17.4) is the most famous example. These are not exactly "circular time" in the simple sense, but they share the key feature: temporal loops. General relativity does not prohibit this topology; it merely requires that the spacetime metric satisfy Einstein's field equations.

There is, however, no direct evidence that our universe has a circular time topology.
