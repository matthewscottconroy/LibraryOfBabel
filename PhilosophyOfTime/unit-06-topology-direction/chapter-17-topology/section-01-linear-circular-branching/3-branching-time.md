# 17.1.3 — Branching Time

---

Branching time is the model in which time branches into multiple futures, while the past remains unique. At any given time, there may be multiple possible future continuations — different branches of the temporal structure — and the actual world develops along one of them. The topology is that of a tree: one root, branching repeatedly into multiple limbs.

## The Mathematical Structure

On a branching time model, temporal positions form a *tree structure* (a partially ordered set with a unique predecessor for every element). The tree has:

- A unique past for any temporal position: looking back from any time, there is a single chain of past events leading to the "root" of the tree.
- Multiple future continuations: from any time, there may be two or more branches leading to different future histories.

The branching structure can be finite (only finitely many branches) or infinite (branching into a continuous infinity of future possibilities). In the most common philosophical applications, branching represents genuine indeterminism: different branches represent different ways the world could go from a given time.

## Prior and Tense Logic for Branching Time

Arthur Prior (1967: Ch. 7) developed tense logic for branching time structures. The key semantic tool is the distinction between **settled truth** and **historical truth** at a branching time.

A proposition is **historically true** at a time t (on branch b) if it is true at t, but only relative to the branch b. If the world branches at some future time, then propositions about post-branching events are historically true on some branches and false on others.

A proposition is **settled true** at t if it is true at t on all branches passing through t — including all future branches. Settled truth is the appropriate notion for what is "fixed" at a given time: what is true regardless of how the future develops.

This distinction allows a natural semantic treatment of future contingents (see Chapter 15.4). "There will be a sea battle tomorrow" is historically true on some branches (those where the battle occurs) and historically false on others (those where it does not). It is settled true only if the battle occurs on every branch — which is the case only if the battle is inevitable.

## Connection to Indeterminism

Branching time is naturally connected to objective indeterminism — the view that the world is not deterministic and that there are genuinely multiple possible futures from any given moment. If quantum mechanics is indeterministic (as the standard Copenhagen interpretation holds), then branching time provides a natural model: the branching points correspond to quantum measurement events, and the branches correspond to the different outcomes.

It is important not to confuse branching time with the Everett/many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. On the many-worlds interpretation, *all* branches are real — the world "splits" at each quantum measurement and all outcomes occur in different branches of a real physical branching structure. On the philosophical branching time model discussed here, only one branch is the "actual" history; the other branches are unactualized possibilities. The many-worlds interpretation endorses a much stronger ontological commitment.

Nuel Belnap and collaborators (2001) developed the most rigorous formal treatment of branching time, in the framework of "branching space-times." Their treatment is notable for connecting temporal branching to causal indeterminism and for providing a sophisticated logical framework for reasoning about what is settled, what is possible, and what is inevitable at branching temporal structures.

## Philosophical Implications

Branching time has several important philosophical consequences.

**The open future:** Branching time provides a natural model for the openness of the future (Chapter 15.4). The future is "open" because there are genuinely multiple future branches, and the world has not yet "selected" which branch to follow. This is a richer account of openness than the mere denial of determinism: it provides a topological structure that represents the openness.

**Free will:** Branching time is sometimes used to model libertarian free will. At each moment of choice, there are genuinely multiple future branches — and the agent's choice determines which branch is actualized. The choice is not determined by prior causes; it is the act of "selecting" a branch.

**Historical truth and truth about the past:** On branching time, the past is settled and unique. Propositions about past events are settled true or settled false at any time — there is no branching in the backwards direction. This is an asymmetry between past and future that is built into the topology.

**Haecceity of branches:** If time branches, then events that "didn't happen" — events on non-actual branches — have a kind of ontological status. Are they real, possible, or merely counterfactual? Different philosophers answer this differently, with implications for the relationship between branching time and modal realism.
