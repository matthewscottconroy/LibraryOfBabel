# 17.2.1 — Three Mathematical Structures for Time

---

The question of the local structure of time — whether time is discrete, dense, or continuous — is a question about the mathematical type of the ordering of temporal positions. Each type has a precise mathematical definition and distinct philosophical implications.

## Discrete Time

A **discrete** ordering is one in which there are "adjacent" elements — elements with nothing between them. The integers are the paradigm: between 3 and 4 there is no integer. A discrete time would consist of a sequence of temporal "atoms" — minimal temporal units — with nothing between adjacent atoms. Time would be like a sequence of beads on a string: individual, separated, countable.

Discrete time implies:
- There is a smallest unit of time (the temporal atom, sometimes called a "chronon").
- Every temporal position has an immediate successor and (unless it is the first moment) an immediate predecessor.
- There is no time "between" adjacent temporal atoms.
- Motion and change must be analyzed in terms of jumps between adjacent temporal atoms, not continuous transitions.

Discrete time has occasionally been defended in philosophy (notably by the atomists, in various ancient traditions) and appears in certain approaches to quantum gravity (see below). It is a coherent mathematical possibility, and some of its apparent counterintuitive features (no continuous motion, no infinitely divisible intervals) may be features rather than bugs.

## Dense Time

A **dense** ordering is one in which between any two distinct elements there is always a third. The rational numbers are the paradigm: between any two rationals, there is another rational. Dense time has no adjacent moments: between any two times, there is always another time. Time would be infinitely divisible without being continuous — or rather, it would be divisible without end, but it might still have "gaps" (positions in the ordering that are not occupied by any rational).

Dense time implies:
- There is no smallest temporal unit: every interval of time can be further subdivided.
- There are no adjacent moments; between any two moments, there is always another.
- Unlike continuous time, dense time does not require completeness: there can be "holes" where the Cauchy sequences converge to limits that are not in the ordering.

## Continuous Time

A **continuous** ordering is a dense ordering that is also *complete* in the sense that every bounded Cauchy sequence converges. The real numbers are the paradigm. Continuous time is dense — between any two times there is always another — but it also "fills in" all the gaps that dense time might leave. Every sequence of times that converges toward a limit actually arrives at a limit that is also in the time.

Continuous time implies everything dense time implies, plus completeness. It is the structure assumed in classical physics, in which time is modeled by the real number line.

## Philosophical Consequences

Each structure has philosophical consequences.

**Discrete time and change:** On discrete time, what does it mean for something to change? If there are adjacent moments t₁ and t₂ with no moment between them, then the change from the object's being in state S₁ at t₁ to being in state S₂ at t₂ is a "jump" — there is no continuous transition between the states. Some philosophers find this attractive (it gives a clear account of what an instant of change consists in), others find it puzzling (change without a process of changing).

**Dense but not continuous:** Is there a coherent possibility of dense-but-not-continuous time? Mathematically, yes (the rationals). But physically, it is hard to see what feature of nature would leave the "gaps" unfilled. Dense but not continuous time would mean there are limits of sequences of times that are not themselves times — which seems physically unmotivated.

**Continuous time and Zeno:** The most famous implication of the continuous/dense structure for philosophy is its interaction with Zeno's paradoxes (see below). The infinite divisibility of time in both dense and continuous models seems to generate Zeno's puzzle about how a finite journey can require infinitely many steps.
