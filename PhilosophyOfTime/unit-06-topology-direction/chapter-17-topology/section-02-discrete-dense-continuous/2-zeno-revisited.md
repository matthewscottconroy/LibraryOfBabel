# 17.2.2 — Zeno Revisited: Discrete Time and Quantum Gravity

---

Zeno of Elea (c. 490–430 BCE) constructed a series of paradoxes designed to show that motion, change, and plurality are impossible — or at least that our ordinary beliefs about them generate contradictions. Two of his paradoxes connect most directly to the topology of time: the **Dichotomy** and the **Arrow**.

## The Dichotomy

Zeno's Dichotomy argues that motion is impossible because to traverse any distance, you must first traverse half of it, then half of the remaining half, and so on without end. The journey consists of an infinite number of stages, and completing an infinite number of stages seems impossible.

On a continuous time model, the standard resolution is mathematical: the infinite series 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... converges to a finite sum (1), so the infinite number of stages can be completed in finite time. This is the mathematical solution, and it is widely accepted as adequate (Salmon 2001: 23–47).

But the mathematical solution does not fully satisfy everyone. The worry is not just that an infinite sum can converge, but that each stage requires a *positive* (non-zero) duration — and how can infinitely many positive durations sum to a finite total? The answer involves careful attention to the sizes of the intervals: because the durations form a convergent geometric series, the sum is finite.

**Does discrete time dissolve the paradox?** On a discrete time model, the Dichotomy dissolves: to traverse the distance from A to B, you take a finite number of discrete temporal steps. There is no infinite series of sub-journeys; the journey consists of a finite number of atomic temporal stages. The paradox loses its grip.

This is sometimes cited as an advantage of discrete time. But the "dissolution" comes at a cost: we must accept that motion consists of a series of discrete jumps, with no continuous trajectory between the positions occupied at adjacent temporal atoms. The object is at one position at t₁ and at another position at t₂, with no continuous path between them. This is strange, but it may be what quantum mechanics actually suggests: quantum particles do not have continuous trajectories in the classical sense.

## The Arrow

Zeno's Arrow paradox argues that an arrow in flight is, at each instant, at rest — it occupies a specific position and does not move in that instant. But if it is at rest at every instant, how can it be in motion at all? Motion seems to vanish into a succession of static instants.

On a dense or continuous time model, the standard response is that instantaneous rest and instantaneous velocity are distinct: a particle can be at a specific position at an instant while having a non-zero velocity, which is defined as the limit of average velocities over intervals approaching that instant. The velocity is not a property of the instant in isolation but a property of the particle's trajectory in the neighborhood of the instant.

**Does discrete time dissolve the Arrow?** On discrete time, the Arrow also dissolves in a different way: at each temporal atom, the object occupies a specific position. "Velocity" is not an instantaneous property but an average property: the object moves from position x₁ at t₁ to position x₂ at t₂, and its velocity is the ratio of the spatial displacement to the temporal displacement. There is no instantaneous velocity, but there is motion between adjacent atoms. The paradox cannot get started because there are no durationless instants.

## Quantum Gravity and the Planck Time

The most provocative recent development in this area is the suggestion from quantum gravity that time may actually be discrete at a fundamental level. The **Planck time** — approximately 5.39 × 10⁻⁴⁴ seconds — is the smallest temporal interval at which our current physical theories (combining quantum mechanics and general relativity) give any coherent predictions. Below the Planck scale, spacetime itself may not be well-defined in the classical sense.

Several approaches to quantum gravity — including loop quantum gravity and causal dynamical triangulations — suggest that spacetime is fundamentally granular: composed of discrete "atoms" of spacetime at the Planck scale. If this is correct, then the continuous time of classical physics is an emergent phenomenon — an approximation valid at macroscopic scales — and the fundamental structure of time is discrete.

The philosophical implications are significant. If time is fundamentally discrete:

- Zeno's paradoxes are genuinely dissolved, not merely defused.
- The Leibniz-Newton debate about continuous vs. relational spacetime takes a new turn.
- The concept of an "instant of time" — a durationless point — ceases to have fundamental physical meaning.
- Change and motion are re-analyzed as sequences of discrete jumps between adjacent temporal atoms.

None of this is established — quantum gravity theories are still speculative — but it represents a genuine live possibility for the fundamental structure of time. Philosophy should be prepared to engage with it.
