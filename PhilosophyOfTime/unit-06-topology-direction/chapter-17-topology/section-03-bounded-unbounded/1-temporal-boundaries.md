# 17.3.1 — Does Time Have a Beginning? Kantian Antinomy and Modern Cosmology

---

The question of whether time has a beginning is one of the oldest in philosophy, and the debate has not been resolved — it has been transformed. Let us trace the transformation.

## Kant's Antinomy

In the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781), Kant argued that the question "Did the world have a beginning in time?" is an antinomy: both the thesis (the world had a beginning) and the antithesis (the world had no beginning) can be supported by seemingly valid arguments, and both lead to contradictions if taken as claims about things-in-themselves.

The **thesis** argument: if the world has no beginning, then an infinite amount of time must have elapsed before any given moment. But an actually infinite temporal series cannot be completed (to "reach" the present, one must have traversed infinitely many prior moments). Therefore, the world must have had a beginning.

The **antithesis** argument: if the world had a beginning, then there was a "time before" the beginning when the world did not exist. But a "time before" the beginning of time is incoherent — time itself requires events or states to be ordered; there cannot be "empty" time before any events. Therefore, the world cannot have had a beginning.

Kant's resolution is that both arguments presuppose that space and time are features of things-in-themselves, when they are actually only forms of human intuition. The question of whether time had a beginning is not a question about reality as it is in itself but about how reality appears to us through the forms of spatiotemporal intuition (Kant 1781/1929: A426–A433/B454–B461).

Contemporary philosophers and physicists do not typically accept Kant's Idealist resolution, but the antinomy itself remains instructive: it shows that the concepts of "beginning of time" and "infinite past" both generate difficulties, and that naive deployment of either leads to puzzles.

## The Big Bang as a Boundary of Classical Spacetime

Modern cosmology, built on general relativity, provides a specific model for the early universe: the Big Bang. In standard cosmological models (the Friedmann-Robertson-Walker models), spacetime has a boundary in the past — a singularity — at which the metric tensor breaks down and the classical spacetime description ceases to apply. The age of the universe is approximately 13.8 billion years.

Does the Big Bang singularity represent a "beginning of time" in the philosophical sense?

This is not straightforward. The Big Bang singularity is not a "moment before which there was no time" — it is a point at which the mathematical description of spacetime breaks down. It is a boundary of the classical spacetime model, not necessarily a boundary of time itself. As Stephen Hawking (1988: 49) put it, asking what happened "before" the Big Bang is like asking what is north of the North Pole: it is a question that does not have a well-formed meaning within the framework of classical spacetime.

This point is philosophically important. The Big Bang is best described not as "the first moment of time" but as "the earliest moment of *classical* spacetime." Whether there is something beyond that boundary — a quantum gravitational regime, a different phase of the universe — is an open question.

## What "Beginning" Means

The Kantian puzzle illuminates a distinction between two senses of "beginning."

**Beginning as a first element:** In this sense, time has a beginning if there is a first moment — an earliest temporal position with nothing before it. This is the standard sense, and it is what the Big Bang singularity represents (within the classical framework).

**Beginning in the relational sense:** In this sense, time does not require a "before" the beginning; the beginning is simply the earliest point, and the question "what was there before?" does not arise. Augustine (in a different context) made a similar point: God did not create the world "in time" but created time together with the world (*Confessions* XI.13.16).

These two senses need to be kept distinct. The first is the cosmological question: does classical spacetime have a first moment? The second is the philosophical question: if there is a first moment, does that require a "pre-temporal" existence? The answer to the second is no: a beginning in the relational sense is perfectly coherent.
