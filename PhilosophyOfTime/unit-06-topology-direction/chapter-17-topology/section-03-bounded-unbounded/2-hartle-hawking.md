# 17.3.2 — The Hartle-Hawking No-Boundary Proposal

---

In 1983, James Hartle and Stephen Hawking proposed a cosmological model in which the Big Bang singularity is replaced by a smooth, non-singular quantum geometry. Their "no-boundary" proposal is one of the most philosophically provocative contributions of modern physics to the question of whether time had a beginning.

## The Proposal

Standard general relativistic cosmology describes the universe as emerging from a singularity — a point where curvature and density become infinite and the equations break down. The Big Bang singularity represents a boundary of the classical spacetime description.

Hartle and Hawking asked: what happens if we apply the methods of quantum mechanics to the geometry of spacetime itself? In their approach (quantum cosmology), the universe is described by a wavefunction over all possible spacetime geometries, and the "history" of the universe is a sum over all such geometries weighted by probability amplitudes.

The key move is the use of **imaginary time**: by rotating the time variable from real to imaginary values (multiplying by i = √-1), the Lorentzian metric signature (+,-,-,-) becomes a Euclidean signature (+,+,+,+). In this Euclidean regime, there is no clear distinction between time and space. The four-dimensional geometry is smooth, closed, and has no boundary — no singularity, no edge, no point where the description breaks down.

The initial conditions of the universe are determined by this no-boundary prescription: the universe "begins" not with a singularity but with a smooth, rounded-off quantum geometry, analogous to the South Pole of a sphere. The South Pole is not a singularity; it is just a point on a smooth sphere. "What is south of the South Pole?" is a question without a meaningful answer — and similarly, "what was before the no-boundary initial state?" is a question without a meaningful answer.

## Philosophical Implications

The Hartle-Hawking proposal raises several philosophical questions.

**Does time have a beginning?** Hawking's own answer is nuanced. In imaginary time (the Euclidean regime), there is no boundary — the geometry is smooth and closed, without a beginning. In real time (the Lorentzian regime), the universe appears to have a beginning (the South Pole of the smooth geometry appears as the Big Bang when translated back to real time). Which description is "real"? Hawking suggested that the imaginary time description is the "deep" one, and the beginning is an artifact of the coordinate description.

This suggests a philosophical revisionism about the concept of a beginning: perhaps "did time have a beginning?" is not a question that has a determinate yes-or-no answer — or rather, the answer depends on which description one uses, and neither description is more "real" than the other.

**Is imaginary time "real" time?** The use of imaginary time is a mathematical technique (a Wick rotation) that simplifies certain calculations. Whether it represents a real physical dimension — a genuinely timeless direction of extension — or is merely a computational tool is a deep question that Hawking acknowledged but did not resolve (*A Brief History of Time*, 1988: 134–139).

**The question dissolves?** Hawking famously suggested that the no-boundary proposal makes the question "What happened before the Big Bang?" dissolve, because there was no "before" in any meaningful sense. The philosophical analogy is to Augustine's point: God created time with the world, so "before the creation" is not a well-formed temporal notion. For Hartle and Hawking, quantum cosmology creates "time" with the universe, and asking what preceded it is asking for a temporal reference point that does not exist within the theory.

**What this does not show:** It is important to note that the Hartle-Hawking proposal, even if correct, does not show that the universe had no cause, or that it "created itself." It shows, at most, that it had no temporal antecedent within the framework of the theory. Whether there is a deeper ontological question — why there is something rather than nothing — is not addressed.

## The Kalam Cosmological Argument

The debate about temporal boundaries is not purely academic. William Lane Craig's "Kalam Cosmological Argument" (1979) argues from the premise that "everything that begins to exist has a cause" to the conclusion that the universe (which began to exist) has a cause — which Craig identifies with God. The Hartle-Hawking proposal is sometimes deployed against the first premise: if the universe began to exist in the Hartle-Hawking sense, did it "begin" in the sense the Kalam requires? These exchanges between cosmology and theology illustrate how concrete philosophical issues about temporal topology connect to the broadest questions about the nature of reality.
