# 17.4.1 — Gödel's Rotating Universe and Closed Timelike Curves

---

Kurt Gödel, best known for his incompleteness theorems in mathematical logic, made a remarkable contribution to physics in 1949. He published a paper in a volume honoring Einstein's seventieth birthday, presenting a new exact solution to Einstein's field equations of general relativity — a solution describing a rotating universe in which time travel to the past is physically possible.

## The Gödel Solution

Gödel's universe is a homogeneous, rotating spacetime filled with a pressureless dust of matter. It is a cosmological model — an entire universe — though one very different from our own: it does not expand, and it contains a global rotation. Gödel showed that in this spacetime, there exist **closed timelike curves (CTCs)**: trajectories through spacetime that are:

- **Timelike:** The trajectory is always within the "future light cone" of each of its points — it is always a physically possible trajectory for a massive object moving slower than light.
- **Closed:** The trajectory returns to its own starting point — the same event in spacetime.

A closed timelike curve is, in effect, a time machine. A traveler on such a curve, always moving forward in "local time" (as experienced along the trajectory), eventually returns to the same spacetime event they started from. They travel into their own causal past.

Gödel proved that in his rotating universe, any event can be connected to any other event by a future-directed timelike curve — including events in the past. Every event in Gödel's universe is in the causal past of every other event. The universe has, as Gödel put it, "no objective lapse of time" (Gödel 1949: 560).

## Philosophical Implications

Gödel's solution has philosophical implications that go well beyond physics.

**The contingency of temporal topology.** Gödel demonstrated that the existence of CTCs is consistent with Einstein's field equations. Whether CTCs actually exist depends on the matter distribution of the universe — it is an empirical, contingent question. This shows that the topology of time is not a matter of logical or metaphysical necessity; it is a feature of the physical world that could have been otherwise.

**The objective lapse of time.** Gödel drew a philosophical conclusion from his solution that connects to the A/B debate. He argued that in a universe with CTCs, the concept of an "objective lapse of time" — the A-series concept of events becoming present and then past — is inapplicable. In a spacetime where every event is in the causal past of every other, there is no coherent global temporal order, and therefore no global sense in which events "come to pass." Gödel took this as support for the view that the passage of time is a subjective rather than an objective phenomenon — a conclusion in the spirit of McTaggart's B-series (Gödel 1949: 561).

**Fatalism and causal loops.** If CTCs exist, then causal loops are physically possible: an event can be (in part) the cause of its own past conditions. This generates the famous "grandfather paradox" and related puzzles: if you travel back in time and prevent the events that led to your time travel, do you travel back or not? Gödel's solution forces philosophy to engage with causal loops as genuine physical possibilities.

**Consistency conditions.** One response to the paradoxes of time travel is the **consistency principle**: all events in a spacetime with CTCs must be consistent with each other — no genuine contradictions are allowed. This was developed by philosophers like David Lewis (1976) and physicists like Kip Thorne. On this view, you can travel back in time, but you cannot change the past — because the "past you travel to" is already the past in which your time travel occurred. The consistency principle allows CTCs without logical contradiction.

## Does Our Universe Have CTCs?

Our universe does not appear to have the rotating structure of Gödel's solution, and there is no direct evidence for CTCs. The expansion of our universe and its apparent homogeneity and isotropy are incompatible with the Gödel solution's rotation.

However, general relativity admits other solutions with CTCs — notably, rotating black holes (Kerr metric) and cosmic strings — though the physical realizability of CTCs in these solutions is debated. Stephen Hawking proposed the **Chronology Protection Conjecture**: that the laws of physics prevent the formation of CTCs in any physically realistic spacetime, possibly through quantum gravitational effects that destroy the CTC before it can be completed (Hawking 1992). This conjecture remains unproven.

The philosophical legacy of Gödel's solution is that the topology of time — including the existence or non-existence of CTCs — is not fixed by logic or by the form of the laws, but is a contingent feature of the actual universe that requires empirical investigation. Temporal topology is physics, not pure philosophy.
