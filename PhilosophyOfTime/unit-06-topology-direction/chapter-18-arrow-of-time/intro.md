# Chapter 18: The Arrow of Time

---

## Chapter Introduction

Here is the most fundamental puzzle about time's direction: the basic laws of physics are (nearly) time-reversal symmetric. If you filmed a physical interaction at the molecular level — two gas molecules colliding, a photon being absorbed by an atom, a particle undergoing radioactive decay — and played the film backward, the reversed film would be physically valid: it would describe a process consistent with the laws. The laws do not distinguish past from future at the fundamental level.

And yet time has a direction. Everyone knows this. Eggs break but do not reassemble. Heat flows from hot to cold but not the reverse. You remember the past and anticipate the future, not vice versa. The universe expands; it does not contract (at least not currently). These asymmetries are dramatic, pervasive, and practically important. How can a fundamentally symmetric physics produce a macroscopic world with such pronounced temporal asymmetry?

This question — the question of the arrow of time — is among the deepest in all of philosophy and physics. It sits at the intersection of thermodynamics, statistical mechanics, cosmology, and the philosophy of time. And it is not resolved. Serious scientists and philosophers disagree about whether there is a genuine asymmetry in the fundamental laws, about what explains the thermodynamic arrow, and about whether the various arrows of time (thermodynamic, psychological, cosmological, causal) are independent or derive from a single source.

This chapter works through the major aspects of the problem. Section 18.1 presents the basic puzzle: time-symmetric laws but time-asymmetric experience. Section 18.2 develops the thermodynamic arrow in detail. Section 18.3 examines David Albert's "Past Hypothesis" as an explanation of the arrow. Section 18.4 confronts the Boltzmann Brain worry as a consequence of certain explanatory approaches. Section 18.5 examines the causal theory of the arrow, and Section 18.6 addresses the psychological arrow — why we remember the past and anticipate the future.
