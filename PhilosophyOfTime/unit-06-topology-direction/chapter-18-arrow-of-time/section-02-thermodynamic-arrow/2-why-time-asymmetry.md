# 18.2.2 — Why Temporal Asymmetry? Loschmidt's Objection

---

The Reversibility Objection, formulated by Josef Loschmidt in 1876, goes to the heart of the problem of the thermodynamic arrow. If the underlying mechanics is time-reversal symmetric, then for every entropy-increasing evolution there is a time-reversed entropy-decreasing evolution that is equally consistent with the laws. Where, then, does the asymmetry come from?

## The Objection Stated

Loschmidt's argument is simple. Consider any physical system evolving from a low-entropy state to a high-entropy state, in accordance with the laws of mechanics. Now time-reverse the trajectory: reverse all velocities and run the dynamics backward. By the time-reversal symmetry of the laws, this time-reversed trajectory is also a lawful evolution of the system. But the time-reversed trajectory evolves from the high-entropy state *back to* the low-entropy state: it is an entropy-decreasing evolution.

If both the forward (entropy-increasing) and backward (entropy-decreasing) trajectories are lawful, then the Second Law — which says entropy never decreases in isolated systems — cannot be derived from the laws alone. The laws are silent about which direction entropy evolves. Something else must explain why we see entropy increasing rather than decreasing.

Boltzmann's response was to appeal to probabilities: the entropy-increasing trajectories are overwhelmingly more probable than the entropy-decreasing ones, because high-entropy macrostates correspond to far more microstates. But Loschmidt's objection cuts deeper: the symmetry argument shows that this probabilistic argument, applied equally in both time directions, gives the wrong answer about the past. If we are currently in a state of moderate entropy, the probabilistic argument says the *past* state was also of higher entropy (since most trajectories leading to the current state came from higher-entropy states). But we know the past was lower-entropy. What explains this asymmetry?

## The Asymmetry of Time in Statistical Reasoning

Here is the crux of the problem. Statistical mechanical reasoning typically involves *conditional probabilities*: given the current state, what is the most probable past or future state? If the dynamics are time-symmetric, then the conditional probability of a lower-entropy past, given the current state, should be the same as the conditional probability of a lower-entropy future — which is very small.

But we are confident that the past was lower in entropy than the present. How? The answer, Albert argues, is that we must be importing an asymmetric assumption about the initial conditions. We assume, in effect, that the past was special — that the universe began in a very low-entropy state — and that this assumption breaks the symmetry between past and future inferences.

Without this assumption, the time-symmetric dynamics gives the wrong answer about the past: it predicts that the past was higher-entropy, that our memories and records are unreliable, that the apparent regularity of history is a fluctuation. These are absurd conclusions. The statistical mechanics must be supplemented by an initial-condition assumption.

## The Role of the Initial Condition

Boltzmann eventually recognized that the Second Law requires an assumption about the initial state of the universe, not just an appeal to statistical probabilities. The universe began in a very low-entropy state — a highly "special" initial condition — and has been evolving toward higher entropy ever since. This initial condition breaks the time-symmetry: it explains why we see entropy increasing toward the future, not toward the past.

But this explanation shifts the problem rather than dissolving it: now we need to explain why the initial state was so special — so low in entropy. Was it just a brute fact? Was it cosmologically necessary? This is what Albert's "Past Hypothesis" (Section 18.3) addresses.

The philosophical lesson of Loschmidt's objection is that the thermodynamic arrow of time cannot be explained by the laws of dynamics alone. It requires a boundary condition — a statement about the special nature of the universe's initial state. The direction of time is not in the laws; it is in the history.
