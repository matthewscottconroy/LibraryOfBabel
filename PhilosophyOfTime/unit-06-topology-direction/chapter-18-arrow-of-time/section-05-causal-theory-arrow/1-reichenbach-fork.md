# 18.5.1 — Reichenbach's Conjunctive Fork and Lewis's Asymmetry of Overdetermination

---

## Reichenbach's Conjunctive Fork

Hans Reichenbach proposed that the direction of time is grounded in the asymmetric structure of causal processes — specifically, in the "conjunctive fork" (*The Direction of Time*, 1956).

A **conjunctive fork** occurs when two separated events A and B are correlated, and the correlation is explained by a common cause C in the past, rather than by a common effect in the future. For example: when two people at opposite ends of a city both develop the same unusual illness, we explain their correlation by a common cause (they both ate at the same restaurant, say), not by a common future effect.

Reichenbach argued that the fork structure is asymmetric: correlations between separated events are explained by common causes in the *past*, not by common effects in the *future*. Open forks — correlations that require a common cause explanation — are open toward the future (the common cause is behind them, not ahead). This asymmetry in causal structure constitutes, Reichenbach proposed, the direction of time: the past is the direction of common causes; the future is the direction of common effects (Reichenbach 1956: 161–163).

The fork structure connects to the thermodynamic arrow: the reason common causes explain correlations better than common effects is that low-entropy initial conditions create coherent correlations that are then "erased" by the approach to equilibrium. The thermodynamic asymmetry grounds the fork asymmetry.

## Lewis's Asymmetry of Overdetermination

David Lewis (1979) developed a related but distinct approach. His starting point is an asymmetry in how events are *overdetermined* by later versus earlier events.

Consider any macroscopic event — say, the explosion of a supernova. This event creates an enormous number of "traces" in its future: shock waves, radiation, chemical reactions, records of human observation. These traces are collectively vastly more informative about the explosion than the antecedent conditions that caused it. The explosion is "overdetermined" by its future effects — there are many, many independent traces of it, each sufficient to establish that it occurred.

In contrast, the antecedent causes of the explosion — the star's prior history of stellar evolution — are fewer and less directly informative. The explosion is not similarly "overdetermined" by its past.

Lewis argues that this asymmetry of overdetermination explains why we have *records* of the past but not of the future: the past is overdetermined by its effects (there are many traces), while the future is underdetermined by its causes (there are few antecedent indicators). The psychological arrow — we remember the past, anticipate the future — follows from this asymmetry: our memories are records, and records track the overdetermined past (Lewis 1979: 33–37).

The asymmetry of overdetermination is not a fundamental physical law; it derives from the thermodynamic asymmetry and ultimately from the Past Hypothesis. Lewis is explicit that the causal asymmetry is not primitive but derived.

## Huw Price's Objection

Huw Price argues that the causal theory of the arrow is circular (Price 1996: Ch. 6). The problem is that our ordinary notion of causation is already temporal: we think of causes as preceding effects, and we define the "past" partly in terms of what causes what. If we use causation to explain the direction of time, we are using a time-loaded concept to explain what time's direction is. The explanation is circular.

Price's alternative is to try to explain the arrow of time in terms that are genuinely independent of temporal notions — ultimately in terms of the thermodynamic asymmetry and the cosmological initial condition. Whether this project succeeds is a matter of ongoing debate.
