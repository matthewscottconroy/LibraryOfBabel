# 18.6.1 — Memory, Anticipation, and the Physical Arrow

---

You remember the past but anticipate the future. This is so fundamental to your cognitive life that it can seem like a logical truth — as if the very concept of memory implies a relation to the past, and the very concept of anticipation implies a relation to the future. But the psychological arrow is not a logical truth; it is a contingent fact about the physical and cognitive structure of our universe. A physically different universe — one with a different thermodynamic arrow — might contain beings who "remember" the future and "anticipate" the past.

## What the Asymmetry Consists In

The psychological arrow involves several distinct components:

**Records:** We have memories — stored representations of past events. These memories are, in many cases, accurate: they correctly represent what actually happened.

**Anticipations:** We have anticipations — representations of expected future events. These anticipations are often probabilistic and uncertain.

**The phenomenal difference:** Memories feel different from anticipations. Remembering a dental appointment that happened yesterday feels qualitatively different from anticipating a dental appointment that will happen tomorrow, even if both involve a representation of the same kind of event.

**The asymmetry of concern:** Many people — and this was noted by Arthur Prior (1959) — feel differently about past versus future suffering. A person told that they will undergo a painful operation tomorrow feels differently than a person told they underwent a painful operation yesterday while unconscious. The future suffering seems to demand response in a way the past suffering does not.

## Derivation from the Physical Arrow

Lewis (1979) argued that the asymmetry of records — having memories of the past but not of the future — is a consequence of the physical arrow of time, specifically the asymmetry of overdetermination.

Here is the derivation. Physical records are physical states whose characteristics are causally explained by the recorded events. A photograph of a person is a physical record because its specific features (the arrangement of silver halide crystals) are causally explained by the light that came from the scene at the time of taking the photograph. The photograph's features are "overdetermined" by the scene: many independent physical features of the photograph reliably track the scene.

For a physical record to exist, the recorded event must have left many independent physical traces. Lewis argues that this condition is massively satisfied for past events (because of the thermodynamic asymmetry: past low-entropy states leave many causal traces in higher-entropy future states) and is not satisfied for future events (which have not yet had the opportunity to create traces). This is why we have records of the past but not the future: the asymmetry is physical, not merely psychological.

The phenomenal asymmetry — the distinctive "pastness" of memories — may or may not be derivable from the physical asymmetry. Some philosophers (e.g., Price 1996: Ch. 7) argue that the phenomenology of memory is fully explained by the physical facts: it feels different to remember than to anticipate because memory involves a different physical process (retrieval of stored traces) than anticipation (extrapolation from current states using models). Others argue that the phenomenal character of temporal experience is not fully reducible to physical facts.

## Prior's Point about Past and Future Suffering

Arthur Prior's observation about the different attitudes we take to past versus future suffering raises a question about whether the psychological arrow is merely cognitive or involves something deeper (Prior 1959: 12–17).

Prior asked: why should I be relieved that a painful experience is *over* (in the past) rather than merely in a B-series position earlier than my current temporal stage? The relief seems to track the A-series fact that the event is *past* — genuinely gone, no longer happening — rather than the B-series fact that it is earlier than now. This suggests that the psychological asymmetry may track A-series distinctions that the B-theory claims do not exist.

Whether this is genuine evidence for A-theory or merely evidence for the psychological salience of A-series concepts (which might be explained in terms of the physical arrow without positing objective A-properties) is a live debate.
