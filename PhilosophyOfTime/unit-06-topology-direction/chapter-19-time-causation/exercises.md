# Chapter 19 Exercises: Time and Causation

---

## Comprehension and Analysis

**1.** Explain Hume's three-part analysis of causation. Which of the three conditions (constant conjunction, contiguity, temporal priority) is most contested philosophically? Give a concrete example that challenges whichever condition you identify as most vulnerable.

**2.** Lewis's counterfactual theory analyzes causation in terms of counterfactual dependence. Explain what it means for E to "counterfactually depend" on C in Lewis's framework. How does the asymmetry of counterfactual dependence explain the causal asymmetry?

**3.** Formulate the bilking argument against backward causation precisely. At which step is the argument most vulnerable? How might Dummett respond?

**4.** What is a causal set? What is the partial order relation, and what does it represent? How does the causal set program invert the ordinary relationship between time and causation?

**5.** Explain Price's circularity objection to the causal theory of temporal direction. Does the objection show that temporal direction cannot be grounded in causal direction, or only that the grounding must be non-circular?

---

## Synthetic and Argumentative

**6.** Lewis's counterfactual theory is challenged by cases of overdetermination, preemption, and trumping. Choose one of these problem cases and analyze it carefully. Does the problem challenge only Lewis's specific formulation, or does it challenge the counterfactual approach more generally?

**7.** "The Wheeler-Feynman absorber theory shows that backward causation is physically coherent; therefore, backward causation is possible." Is this argument valid? What is the relationship between physical coherence and metaphysical possibility in this context?

**8.** Causal set theory makes causal order more fundamental than temporal order. If this is correct, does it vindicateReichenbach's causal theory of temporal direction, or does it change the topic so significantly that Reichenbach's original program is no longer relevant?

## Lab and Observational Exercises

**9. Lab — Identifying the Causal Arrow.** Choose five causal claims from everyday life (e.g., "the sound of thunder was caused by the lightning"; "the match caused the fire"; "the missing key was caused by my carelessness"). For each, test whether Lewis's counterfactual analysis applies: would the effect have occurred if the cause had not? Then identify any cases where the counterfactual is false (overdetermination) or ambiguous (preemption). Write a 200-word reflection on whether the counterfactual analysis matches your ordinary causal intuitions.

**10. Lab — Tracing Causal Chains.** Pick a recent event in your life (a grade you received, a conversation you had, a decision you made). Try to trace the causal chain backward: what caused it? What caused those causes? Go back as far as you can and record the chain. Then ask: at what point does the causal chain trace back to something that feels like a "boundary condition" rather than a cause? Does this exercise illuminate the relationship between the causal arrow and the cosmological initial condition?

**11. Lab — Retrocausation in Everyday Language.** Collect five examples from ordinary language of apparent "backward" temporal reasoning — statements where future events seem to "explain" or "cause" present ones ("I'm nervous because tomorrow's exam will be hard"; "the goal saved them from embarrassment"; "the storm they were walking into had already begun"). Analyze each: are these genuine backward causal claims, or are they claims about present states caused by anticipation? Write a 200-word reflection on what these cases suggest about the temporal structure of causal language.

## Reflection

**12.** If causal set theory is correct and time is derivative from causal order, then time itself is not fundamental to reality. Would this change how you think about your own existence — about the significance of being at a particular moment in time? Does the grounding of temporal facts in causal facts preserve what matters about temporal experience, or does it eliminate something important? Write a half-page reflection.
