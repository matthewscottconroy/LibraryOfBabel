## Annotated Entries

**Hume, David. 1975. *Enquiries Concerning Human Understanding*. Edited by L. A. Selby-Bigge. 3rd ed. Oxford: Clarendon Press. Section VII.**
The foundational text for modern philosophy of causation. Section VII ("Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion") develops Hume's analysis with great care and wit. His challenge to "necessary connection" remains unrefuted in the sense that no one has given a fully convincing account of where necessity comes from. Essential primary source.

**Lewis, David. 1973. "Causation." *Journal of Philosophy* 70 (17): 556–567.**
The foundational paper of the counterfactual approach. Lewis develops the analysis, distinguishes direct from ancestral causation, and engages with the main objections. Short, clear, and philosophically rich. Should be read alongside Lewis (1979) for the full picture.

**Lewis, David. 1979. "Counterfactual Dependence and Time's Arrow." *Noûs* 13 (4): 455–476.**
One of the most important papers in the philosophy of causation and time. Lewis develops the asymmetry of overdetermination, explains why forward counterfactuals are asymmetric, and connects the causal asymmetry to the thermodynamic arrow. Essential for anyone working on causation and temporal direction.

**Price, Huw. 1996. *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. Chapters 6 and 8.**
Chapter 6 develops the circularity objection to causal theories of temporal direction; Chapter 8 examines retrocausality and backward causation. Price's approach is unusual and illuminating: his insistence on avoiding temporal presuppositions in the analysis of temporal asymmetry forces the debate onto genuinely foundational ground.

**Dummett, Michael. 1964. "Bringing About the Past." *Philosophical Review* 73 (3): 338–359.**
The classic defense of the coherence of backward causation. Dummett's argument is carefully constructed and his engagement with the bilking objection is instructive. The paper exemplifies Dummett's characteristic approach: taking a counter-intuitive philosophical position and defending it with meticulous logical care.

**Schaffer, Jonathan. 2000. "Trumping Preemption." *Journal of Philosophy* 97 (4): 165–181.**
The paper that introduced the "trumping" problem case for counterfactual theories of causation. Schaffer's cases are ingeniously constructed and the philosophical analysis is rigorous. An important contribution to the ongoing project of developing an adequate theory of causation.

**Reichenbach, Hans. 1956. *The Direction of Time*. Berkeley: University of California Press. Chapters 3–4.**
The classic statement of the causal theory of temporal direction. Chapters 3–4 develop the conjunctive fork analysis and its application to the direction of time. Reichenbach's engagement with both the philosophy and the physics of his era is instructive. Read alongside Price for a sense of the continuing debate.

**Bombelli, Luca, et al. 1987. "Space-Time as a Causal Set." *Physical Review Letters* 59 (5): 521–524.**
The foundational paper of the causal set program. Short and technically demanding, but the core idea — that the causal order determines the spacetime geometry — is accessible without deep technical background. Reading the paper alongside Sorkin's (2003) lectures provides a complete picture of the program.

**Henson, Joe. 2009. "The Causal Set Approach to Quantum Gravity." In *Approaches to Quantum Gravity*, edited by Daniele Oriti, 393–413. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.**
The clearest philosophical and technical introduction to the causal set program, written for an audience of philosophers and physicists. Henson explains the key ideas, surveys the main results, and discusses the philosophical implications carefully. An ideal entry point for philosophers interested in quantum gravity.

**Wheeler, John A., and Richard P. Feynman. 1945. "Interaction with the Absorber as the Mechanism of Radiation." *Reviews of Modern Physics* 17 (2–3): 157–181.**
Technically demanding but historically important. The absorber theory introduced a time-symmetric description of radiation and raised questions about backward causation in physics that have not been fully resolved. For philosophers interested in the physics of time, this paper is essential context for understanding what time-symmetric physical theories actually look like.
