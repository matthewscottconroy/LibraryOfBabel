# Chapter 19 Important Researchers

---

**David Hume (1711–1776)**
David Hume was a Scottish philosopher who developed one of the most influential analyses of causation in the history of philosophy. His *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739) and *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) argued that causation consists in constant conjunction, contiguity, and temporal priority, and that the impression of "necessary connection" between cause and effect is a projection of the mind rather than an objective feature of the world. Hume's analysis remains the starting point for virtually all contemporary work on causation.

**Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)**
Kant argued, against Hume, that causation is not derived from experience but is a category of understanding — a condition of the possibility of experience. His "Second Analogy" in the *Critique of Pure Reason* argues that the objective temporal sequence of events is constituted by the application of the causal concept. Kant's treatment of the relationship between temporal order and causal order is foundational, and his acknowledgment of simultaneous causation complicates the simple picture of causes always preceding effects.

**David Lewis (1941–2001)**
Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation, developed in papers from 1973 onward, is the most influential contemporary account. His integration of possible worlds semantics, counterfactual conditionals, and the analysis of causation has been enormously productive. His 1979 paper on the temporal asymmetry of counterfactual dependence connects the philosophy of causation directly to the arrow of time. Lewis's broader metaphysics — modal realism, worm theory, the analysis of laws — forms a unified system in which the philosophy of causation and the philosophy of time are deeply integrated.

**Michael Dummett (1925–2011)**
Dummett's 1964 paper "Bringing About the Past" is the classic philosophical defense of the coherence of backward causation. Working from first principles about the structure of causal reasoning, Dummett argued that if we had sufficiently strong evidence of a systematic correlation between a later event and an earlier one, it would be rational to infer a causal connection — even a backward one. Dummett's broader philosophical work on anti-realism and meaning has deep connections to the philosophy of time, and his essay on backward causation remains essential reading.

**Huw Price (born 1953)**
Price's *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point* (1996) contains important chapters on backward causation and retrocausality, as well as the circularity objection to causal theories of temporal direction. Price is unusual among philosophers of time in advocating for an "Archimedean" approach: thinking about time from a standpoint that does not presuppose any temporal direction, in order to identify genuine asymmetries rather than those smuggled in by our temporal intuitions.

**Rafael D. Sorkin (born 1945)**
Rafael Sorkin is a theoretical physicist at the Perimeter Institute who is one of the founders and principal developers of causal set theory. His 2003 lectures "Causal Sets: Discrete Gravity" provide the clearest introduction to the program. Sorkin's long-term commitment to causal set theory — maintaining it as a serious approach to quantum gravity in an era dominated by string theory and loop quantum gravity — reflects a deep philosophical commitment to the primacy of causal structure over spacetime geometry.

**Jonathan Schaffer (born 1974)**
Jonathan Schaffer is an American philosopher at Rutgers University who has made important contributions to the metaphysics of causation, including the introduction of "trumping" as a problem case for counterfactual theories. His broader work on "grounding" — the metaphysical relation of one fact making another fact obtain — connects to questions about the grounding relationship between causal and temporal facts. Schaffer is a sophisticated metaphysician with a detailed knowledge of the relevant physics.

**John Wheeler (1911–2008) and Richard Feynman (1918–1988)**
Wheeler and Feynman collaborated on the absorber theory of radiation (1945), which provides a time-symmetric description of electromagnetism using both retarded and advanced waves. Wheeler was a major figure in general relativity and quantum gravity who coined many of the most memorable phrases in modern physics ("black hole," "it from bit"). Feynman won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1965 for his development of quantum electrodynamics. Their 1945 paper remains a landmark in the physics of time-symmetric processes.
