# Chapter 19: Time and Causation

---

## Chapter Introduction

Time and causation are the two most fundamental structural features of the physical world as we ordinarily experience it. They are also deeply entangled. We think of causes as preceding their effects — causation runs from earlier to later. We think of time as having a direction — from past to future — and we are inclined to say that this direction is the direction of causation. But which comes first? Does time get its direction from causation, or does causation get its direction from time? Is causation even a real feature of the world, or is it something we project onto a world that contains only regularities?

These questions have been debated for three centuries, from Hume's skeptical analysis of causation to the contemporary causal set program in quantum gravity, which proposes that spacetime itself emerges from a more fundamental causal ordering.

This chapter traces the main positions. Section 19.1 begins with Hume's analysis of causation and its implications for the temporal priority of causes. Section 19.2 develops Lewis's counterfactual theory, the most influential contemporary account, and examines how it handles the temporal asymmetry of causation. Section 19.3 asks whether the direction of causation can ground the direction of time — a project associated with Reichenbach and challenged by Price. Section 19.4 examines backward causation — the coherent possibility that causes could be in the future of their effects — and what physics has to say about it. Section 19.5 presents the causal set program in quantum gravity, in which causal order is the fundamental ontological category from which spacetime emerges.
