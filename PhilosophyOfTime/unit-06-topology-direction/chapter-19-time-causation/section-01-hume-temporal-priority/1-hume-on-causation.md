# 19.1.1 — Hume on Causation and Temporal Priority

---

David Hume's analysis of causation in the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748, Section VII) and in the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739, Book I, Part III) is the foundational text for modern discussions of the topic. Hume asked: when we say that C causes E, what are we actually saying? His answer was deflationary and influential: causation consists in nothing more than constant conjunction, contiguity, and temporal priority.

## Hume's Analysis

Hume identified three components of the causal relation:

**Constant conjunction:** C is regularly followed by E; whenever events of type C occur, events of type E follow. The connection is a matter of observed regularity, not of any "necessary connection" between the events themselves. Hume argued that we never observe necessary connection directly — only regularities — and that the idea of causal necessity is a habit of the mind projected onto these regularities (*Enquiry*, Section VII, §50–51).

**Contiguity:** Cause and effect are contiguous in space and time. There is no action at a distance; causal influence is transmitted continuously through intervening events.

**Temporal priority:** The cause is *earlier than* the effect. C occurs before E; causation runs from earlier to later.

The temporal priority condition is what concerns us here. For Hume, it is a contingent but empirically universal feature of causal relations that causes precede their effects. He does not treat this as a conceptual truth — it is something we discover from experience, not something we know a priori.

## Is Temporal Priority Conceptually Necessary?

Hume's claim that temporal priority is an empirical rather than conceptual requirement leaves open the question: could causes and effects be simultaneous? Or could a cause occur *after* its effect — could there be backward causation?

Kant argued that causes *must* precede their effects — that temporal priority is not merely an observed regularity but a condition of the possibility of experience. In the *Critique of Pure Reason*, Kant argued that the sequence of perceptions in experience is not self-explanatory: it is made objective (not merely a succession of impressions) by the application of the category of causality, which requires that the cause be temporally prior to the effect (Kant 1781/1929: B233–B256, "Second Analogy").

Kant's argument also allows for *simultaneous* causation in certain cases — where cause and effect are co-occurring and cannot be separated temporally. His example is a lead ball resting on a cushion: the ball (cause) and the hollow it produces (effect) are simultaneous — there is no temporal interval between them. Kant takes this as showing that temporal priority, while typical, is not conceptually necessary for causation.

## Contemporary Implications

Whether temporal priority is conceptually necessary for causation is not merely a historical question. It bears directly on:

- **Backward causation** (Chapter 19.4): if temporal priority is merely contingent, then backward causation — causes occurring after their effects — is conceptually coherent, even if uncommon or physically unusual.
- **Simultaneous causation:** if temporal priority is not necessary, then there can be causal relations between events at the same time, which is relevant to the metaphysics of properties and constitution.
- **The direction of time:** if causation requires temporal priority, then causation cannot be used to *define* temporal direction without circularity — we need to know what direction time runs before we can apply the priority condition.

Hume's analysis leaves these questions open, which is part of what makes it foundational: it clears away metaphysical inflation (necessary connection) without settling the downstream philosophical questions.
