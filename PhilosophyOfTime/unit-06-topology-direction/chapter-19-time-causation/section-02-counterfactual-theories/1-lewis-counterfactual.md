# 19.2.1 — Lewis's Counterfactual Theory: Causation and Temporal Asymmetry

---

David Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation, developed in a series of papers beginning with "Causation" (1973), is one of the most influential positions in contemporary metaphysics. Lewis analyzes causation in terms of counterfactual dependence between events, using his possible worlds semantics to give truth conditions for counterfactual conditionals.

## The Theory

Lewis's basic analysis: event C causes event E if and only if E counterfactually depends on C — that is, if C had not occurred, E would not have occurred (*Journal of Philosophy*, 1973: 556–558). In cases of causal chains, C causes E if there is a chain of events C, D, E,... such that each depends counterfactually on its predecessor.

The truth conditions for counterfactuals are given by Lewis's possible worlds semantics (*Counterfactuals*, 1973): "If C had not occurred, E would not have occurred" is true iff in the closest possible worlds in which C does not occur, E does not occur. "Closeness" of possible worlds is measured by a similarity metric — how much the world would have to be different for C not to occur.

## The Temporal Asymmetry of Counterfactual Dependence

One of the most important features of Lewis's account is that it generates an asymmetry between past and future counterfactual dependence, which corresponds to the causal asymmetry.

Lewis argued in "Counterfactual Dependence and Time's Arrow" (1979) that our counterfactuals are systematically "forward-looking" rather than "backward-looking." When we evaluate "If C had not occurred, what would have been different?", we hold the past fixed and allow the future to vary. We do not hold the future fixed and allow the past to vary.

This is the asymmetry of counterfactual dependence. Compare:
- **Forward counterfactual:** "If the lightning had not struck, the barn would not have burned down." (We hold the past fixed and allow the future to vary.)
- **Backward counterfactual:** "If the barn had not burned down, the lightning would not have struck." (We would be allowing the past to vary — but we don't ordinarily reason this way.)

The forward counterfactual is typically true; the backward counterfactual is typically false (or at least not the way we would reason). We think of the past as fixed and the future as open to influence.

Lewis explains this asymmetry in terms of the overdetermination argument from 1979. The past is "overdetermined" by the future — any past event has many independent future traces — so small counterfactual changes to the past would require "miracles" (violations of laws) at many future points, which makes the closest possible worlds in which the past is changed very distant. Changes to the future, by contrast, require only one "miracle" at the relevant point, so forward counterfactuals involve closer possible worlds.

## The Temporal Asymmetry of Causation

The temporal asymmetry of counterfactual dependence grounds the temporal asymmetry of causation. Since we use forward-looking (not backward-looking) counterfactuals in causal reasoning, causation is asymmetric: C causes E (if C had not occurred, E would not have) but typically not vice versa (even if E had not occurred, C would still have occurred).

Lewis is explicit that the asymmetry of counterfactual dependence derives from the physical asymmetry of overdetermination — which in turn derives from the thermodynamic arrow and ultimately from the Past Hypothesis. The causal asymmetry is not a brute or primitive feature of causation; it is derived from the thermodynamic structure of the universe.
