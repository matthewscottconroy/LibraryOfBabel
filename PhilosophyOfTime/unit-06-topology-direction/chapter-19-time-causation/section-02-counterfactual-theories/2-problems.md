# 19.2.2 — Problems for Counterfactual Theories: Overdetermination, Preemption, and Trumping

---

Lewis's counterfactual theory of causation is elegant and has generated an extensive research program, but it faces persistent problem cases that challenge both the theory and the temporal asymmetry it purports to explain.

## Overdetermination

**Simultaneous overdetermination:** Two assassins, acting independently, both shoot a target at exactly the same time. Either shot alone would have killed the target. The target dies.

For Lewis's theory, neither shot is the cause of the death, because each counterfactual is false: even if assassin A had not shot, the target would still have died (assassin B would have killed them). And vice versa. But intuitively, both shots are causes of the death — or at least, one of them is.

Overdetermination poses a direct challenge to the counterfactual analysis. The theory cannot easily accommodate cases where multiple simultaneous causes are each sufficient for the effect.

## Preemption

**Early preemption:** Assassin A shoots first; the bullet from A kills the target before the bullet from B arrives. Assassin B's shot was en route but never completed its causal journey. Here, A's shot is clearly the cause; B's shot was preempted.

Lewis's theory handles this moderately well: A's shot counterfactually depends on the death (if A had not shot at that time, B's bullet would not have arrived in time either — wait, actually, B's bullet would still arrive, but later; the death at the actual time counterfactually depends on A's shot). In more complex variants ("late preemption"), where both causal processes run to completion and one preempts only at the last moment, the theory struggles.

## Trumping

**Trumping** (Schaffer 2000): A major and a sergeant both shout "Charge!" simultaneously to the troops, who advance. The major outranks the sergeant. The major's order was the cause of the advance; the sergeant's was not — even though they were simultaneous and each would have been sufficient.

If both shouts were simultaneous and each sufficient, Lewis's theory cannot distinguish them. Intuitively, there is a fact (the major's order caused the advance) that the counterfactual analysis cannot capture. Trumping suggests that priority relations — which cause is "dominant" when two simultaneous sufficient causes compete — cannot be fully analyzed in counterfactual terms.

## Implications for Temporal Asymmetry

These problem cases are not merely embarrassments for Lewis's specific analysis; they reveal a general tension in the counterfactual approach to causation and temporal asymmetry.

The temporal asymmetry of counterfactual dependence was supposed to explain why causes precede effects: it is because forward counterfactuals (from cause to effect) are typically true while backward counterfactuals (from effect to cause) are typically false. But in cases of overdetermination and preemption, even the forward counterfactuals break down. The "typical" case turns out to be the exception, not the rule.

Lewis responded to many of these objections in later work, developing a more sophisticated "influence" account of causation (2000). But the problem cases remain active areas of research, and no fully satisfactory counterfactual analysis of causation has emerged. The temporal asymmetry of causation, in particular, remains a topic of active philosophical discussion.
