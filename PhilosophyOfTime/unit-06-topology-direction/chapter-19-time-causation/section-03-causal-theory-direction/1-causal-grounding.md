# 19.3.1 — Grounding Temporal Direction in Causation: Reichenbach's Program and Price's Objection

---

Can the direction of time be grounded in the direction of causation? Reichenbach proposed this as a way of explaining why time has a direction without appealing to brute temporal asymmetry. The direction from past to future is the direction in which causal processes propagate — the direction "from cause to effect." This would give a reductive account of temporal direction: temporal order is constituted by causal order.

## Reichenbach's Program

Reichenbach's conjunctive fork analysis (see Chapter 18.5.1) identified a structural asymmetry in causal processes: correlations between separated events are explained by common causes in the past, not by common effects in the future. The fork is "open" toward the future: the correlated events branch off from a common cause in the past direction.

Reichenbach proposed that the temporal direction can be *defined* in terms of this structural asymmetry: the past is the direction of common causes; the future is the direction of common effects. If this definition is viable, then temporal direction is not primitive but is constituted by causal structure.

This has the philosophical attraction of reducing a mysterious temporal fact (why does time have a direction?) to a structural causal fact (forks are open toward the future). Temporal asymmetry is explained by causal asymmetry.

## Price's Circularity Objection

Huw Price argues that Reichenbach's program is circular (Price 1996: Ch. 6). The problem is that causation, as ordinarily understood, is an *intrinsically* temporal concept: causes are *defined* (at least in part) as events that precede their effects. If the causal asymmetry — causes before effects — is built into the concept of causation, then we cannot use the causal asymmetry to *explain* temporal direction without presupposing it.

More precisely: when Reichenbach says the fork opens "toward the future" and that common causes are "in the past," he is already using temporal language. The claim that forks open toward the future presupposes that we know which direction is the future — i.e., which direction time runs. The causal analysis does not tell us which direction is the future; it presupposes we already know.

Price's own alternative is to seek a non-circular explanation of temporal asymmetry in terms that are not themselves temporally loaded — ultimately in terms of the physical and statistical structure of the universe. He argues that this requires adopting an "Archimedean" standpoint outside time, from which one can see that the asymmetry of causal structure is a consequence of the special initial conditions, not an independent feature of causation.

## The Response

The standard response to Price's circularity objection is to argue that the circularity is benign. We are not trying to explain temporal direction from scratch, in a vocabulary that contains no temporal concepts whatsoever; we are trying to explain the direction of time in terms of causal structure, where our ordinary conception of causation is taken as a starting point. The resulting analysis is not circular in the vicious sense; it is a coherence constraint: temporal direction and causal direction must fit together consistently.

Whether this response is adequate depends on how demanding one's standard for explanation is. If we require that temporal direction be grounded in something entirely non-temporal, then Price's objection stands. If we allow that metaphysical grounding can be holistic — with temporal and causal concepts mutually supporting each other — then the response is satisfying.

This is an unresolved debate, and its resolution depends partly on broader questions about the methodology of metaphysics.
