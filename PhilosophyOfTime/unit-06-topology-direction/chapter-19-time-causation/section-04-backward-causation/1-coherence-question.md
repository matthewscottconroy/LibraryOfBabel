# 19.4.1 — Backward Causation: Coherence, the Bilking Argument, and Physics

---

**Backward causation** is the hypothetical phenomenon in which a later event causes an earlier one — a cause occurs after its effect in the ordinary temporal ordering. The question whether backward causation is coherent has been a persistent topic in the philosophy of time and causation.

## Dummett's Defense

Michael Dummett argued in "Bringing About the Past" (1964) that backward causation is conceptually coherent under certain conditions. His argument is based on the structure of our causal reasoning and does not require any specific physical theory.

Dummett's key observation: we accept "unusual" or "non-standard" causal reasoning when we have a sufficiently strong evidential case. Consider the case of a ritual: a tribe performs a dance today to ensure the success of a hunting party that departed yesterday. On Dummett's analysis, if the tribe consistently finds that the dance correlates with past hunting success — more specifically, if on days when they perform the dance, the hunters consistently return with more game — then a rational response might be to infer a causal connection. The dance (later event) causes the hunting success (earlier event).

Dummett does not claim that such backward causation actually occurs; he claims it would be *rational* to infer it given sufficiently strong and consistent correlations, and that therefore the concept of backward causation is coherent. The structure of evidential reasoning does not preclude backward causation in principle (Dummett 1964: 338–341).

## The Bilking Argument

The most influential objection to backward causation is the **bilking argument**. It runs as follows. Suppose E (the cause) occurs in the future and C (the effect) occurs in the past. If we know that E causes C, then:

1. We can observe whether C has occurred (it is in the past).
2. We can choose whether to bring about E (it is in our future).
3. We can deliberately *not* bring about E on those occasions when C has already occurred, and bring about E on those occasions when C has not occurred.

If we can do this — if we can "bilk" the causal relation by choosing E or not-E depending on whether C occurred — then the supposed causal connection between E and C would be broken. We could systematically decouple E from C, which is impossible if E causes C.

The bilking argument suggests that backward causation is not merely unusual but self-undermining: any alleged case of backward causation can be bilked, and when it is bilked, the causal connection is severed. This seems to show that backward causation cannot be a stable feature of the world.

Dummett's response is that the bilking argument succeeds only if the agent has access to both the cause and the effect at the right time — which is not always guaranteed. In some scenarios (e.g., quantum measurement), the conditions for bilking may not be satisfiable. The bilking argument shows that backward causation is constrained, not that it is impossible (Dummett 1964: 340–341).

## Wheeler-Feynman Absorber Theory

A physical theory that involves something like backward causation is the **Wheeler-Feynman absorber theory** (Wheeler and Feynman 1945). The theory provides an alternative interpretation of electromagnetism in which both retarded waves (propagating forward in time from the source, the standard picture) and advanced waves (propagating backward in time, from the future absorber toward the source) are used to describe radiation.

In the Wheeler-Feynman picture, when a charged particle accelerates, it emits radiation that is received by future absorbers. The absorbers, in turn, emit advanced waves that propagate backward in time to the source and contribute to the reaction force on the source. The full description of radiation requires both time-symmetric and advanced contributions.

The absorber theory is mathematically consistent and makes the same empirical predictions as standard electromagnetism. It suggests that a time-symmetric description of radiation, involving backward causation, is physically coherent. The fact that we observe only retarded waves in practice is explained by a special asymmetry in the distribution of absorbers (related to the thermodynamic arrow).

Whether this constitutes genuine backward causation in the philosophical sense is disputed. The advanced waves are "retrocausal" in the sense that the future influences the past, but whether this involves genuine causal priority of the future over the past, or merely a time-symmetric description of a symmetric interaction, is a matter of interpretation.

## Philosophical Assessment

Backward causation is conceptually coherent (Dummett's conclusion is widely accepted) but physically unusual. The bilking argument places real constraints on when backward causation can be instantiated: it requires that the causal mechanism be in some sense inaccessible to agents who might disrupt it. The Wheeler-Feynman absorber theory provides a case study in which something like backward causation appears in physics, though its interpretation is contested.

The broader lesson: the temporal priority of causes is a contingent feature of the actual world, not a conceptual necessity. Other arrangements are possible, and understanding what makes the actual arrangement the way it is — why causes precede effects in our universe — requires the thermodynamic and cosmological resources of Chapter 18.
