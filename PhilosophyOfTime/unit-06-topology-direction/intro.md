# Unit VI: The Topology and Direction of Time

---

## Unit Introduction

The debates of Unit V — about presentism and eternalism, the A-series and the B-series, the nature of the present — all assume that time has a definite structure: it extends in one direction (from past to future), it is probably infinite or semi-infinite, and one moment is followed by another in a linear sequence. These assumptions are so natural that questioning them feels disorienting. And yet, the moment you take them seriously as philosophical claims, you realize they deserve examination. Why should time be linear rather than circular? Why should there be a direction at all? Does time have to have a beginning, and if it does, does that beginning have to be "earlier" in any meaningful sense?

This unit strips away the assumptions we carry unreflectively about temporal structure and examines them from the ground up. We will ask what shapes time can in principle have, what physics and cosmology say about its actual shape, and what it means for time to have a "direction" — an asymmetry between past and future that seems obvious in daily experience but deeply puzzling in fundamental physics.

Chapter 17 surveys the topology of time: its global shape and local structure. We examine the standard linear model and its competitors — circular time, branching time — as well as the mathematical structure of temporal order: whether time is discrete, dense, or continuous. We examine whether time has boundaries (a beginning, an end) and what modern cosmology says about this. We culminate with Gödel's extraordinary solution to Einstein's field equations, which allows for "closed timelike curves" — trajectories in spacetime that loop back to their own past.

Chapter 18 turns to the arrow of time: the fact that time is not merely ordered but *directed* — that past and future are not symmetric. Entropy increases in one direction; causes precede effects; we remember the past and anticipate the future. Yet the fundamental laws of physics are (nearly) time-reversal symmetric. Where does the arrow come from? This is one of the deepest open questions in physics and philosophy.

Chapter 19 examines the relationship between time and causation. Is causation temporally prior to time — can we ground temporal direction in causal direction? Or is causation itself already temporal? And what about backward causation — causes that precede their effects in the future? These questions connect the metaphysics of time to the metaphysics of causation, and to some of the most exciting recent work in physics.

By the end of this unit, you will have a clear picture of what is known and unknown about the deep structure of time — its global topology, its arrow, and its relationship to the causal fabric of the universe.
