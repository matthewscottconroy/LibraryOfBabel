# Chapter 20 Exercises

## Conceptual and Analytical Exercises

**1. The Scholium as Philosophy**
Newton's Scholium distinguishes "absolute, true, and mathematical time" from "relative, apparent, and common time." (a) In your own words, explain what Newton means by each distinction. (b) Identify at least two metaphysical commitments that are built into Newton's notion of absolute time but that go beyond what his physics requires. (c) How does Newton's theological framework support his commitment to absolute time? Do you think physics can or should be insulated from such theological commitments?

**2. Non-Dynamical Time**
Explain what it means for time to be "non-dynamical" in Newtonian mechanics. Use the analogy of graph paper if helpful, but also try to explain it in purely physical terms. How does this contrast with the situation in general relativity? Why does the dynamical character of time in GR matter philosophically?

**3. T-Symmetry and Its Limits**
(a) Newton's second law is symmetric under time reversal. Demonstrate this by showing explicitly that F = m(d²x/dt²) is unchanged under t → -t. (b) What is the difference between the T-symmetry of the fundamental laws and the macroscopic irreversibility we observe? (c) Is a ball rolling to a stop (due to friction) an example of T-violation at the fundamental level, or something else? Explain carefully.

**4. The CP Violation Problem**
The weak nuclear force violates T-symmetry. Some students conclude: "Therefore physics explains the arrow of time — we know why time runs forward." Write a response to this student. What is true in the claim? What goes wrong? What additional considerations are needed to account for the macroscopic arrow of time?

**5. Mach's Challenge**
Reconstruct Mach's argument against absolute space using the rotating bucket thought experiment. (a) What is Newton's original argument for absolute rotation? (b) What is Mach's counterargument? (c) Can you think of a thought experiment that would (in principle) distinguish Mach's view from Newton's? Consider what would happen inside a bucket in a universe containing only a single particle.

**6. The Principle of Sufficient Reason and Time**
Leibniz argued that if the universe were shifted forward by one hour with respect to absolute time, there would be no observable difference — and therefore, by the Principle of Sufficient Reason, God would have no reason to create it shifted by one hour rather than not. Does this argument succeed? What would Newton's theological response be? Is Leibniz's relational view of time free of corresponding difficulties?

**7. Noether's Theorem and Energy**
Noether's theorem tells us that time-translation symmetry implies conservation of energy. (a) What does "time-translation symmetry" mean, precisely? (b) Why does energy fail to be globally conserved in an expanding universe? (c) Does this mean that physics is "wrong" about energy conservation, or that energy conservation has a scope that we had not previously appreciated? What are the philosophical implications?

**8. Barbour-Bertotti Relational Mechanics**
(a) What is the key difference between "extrinsic time" and "intrinsic time" as defined by Barbour and Bertotti? (b) What does their 1982 paper demonstrate about the necessity of absolute time in Newtonian mechanics? (c) Does demonstrating that absolute time is not *necessary* for reproducing the predictions of Newtonian mechanics show that it does not *exist*? What additional philosophical argument would be required?

## Comparative and Synthetic Exercises

**9. Newton vs. Leibniz**
The Newton-Leibniz debate about absolute vs. relational space and time, conducted through the Clarke-Leibniz correspondence (1715–1716), is one of the most important philosophical debates in early modern philosophy. Write a structured comparison of their positions on time, identifying (a) the metaphysical disagreements, (b) the theological or philosophical principles each appeals to, and (c) which considerations seem most compelling to you and why. Cite at least two specific passages from the correspondence (Alexander 1956).

**10. From Mach to Einstein**
Einstein stated that Mach's critique directly influenced his development of general relativity. (a) What features of general relativity seem to embody Mach's principle? (b) In what ways does GR fail to fully satisfy Mach's principle (consider: can GR have empty spacetime solutions with non-trivial inertial structure)? (c) Does the partial success of Mach's influence on Einstein support or undermine Mach's philosophical position?

## Observational and Laboratory Exercises

**11. Tracking Time Standards**
The definition of the second has changed repeatedly in the history of physics: from fractions of the solar day, to the ephemeris second (based on Earth's orbital period), to the cesium atomic second (1967). Research this history and write a 600-word account that addresses: (a) What motivated each change? (b) How does each standard relate to Mach's idea that time is abstracted from physical processes? (c) Is the atomic second a more "absolute" standard, a more "relational" standard, or neither?

**12. Foucault's Pendulum Observation**
Foucault's pendulum, which rotates its plane of oscillation over the course of a day, has been interpreted as evidence for absolute rotation (the pendulum's plane stays fixed relative to the "fixed stars," not relative to the rotating Earth). Visit a science museum or planetarium with a Foucault pendulum, or use a simulation. (a) Observe or simulate the rotation rate at different latitudes. (b) Write a 400-word analysis: is the Foucault pendulum evidence for Newtonian absolute space, for Machian rotation-relative-to-distant-matter, or is it agnostic between the two? What additional experiment could distinguish them?

**13. Conservation of Energy Lab**
Design and perform a simple experiment to test conservation of mechanical energy — for example, a ball rolling down an inclined plane, or a pendulum. (a) Measure the potential and kinetic energy at several points in the trajectory. (b) Account for energy losses due to friction, air resistance, etc. (c) Write a reflection: in what sense does this experiment test Noether's theorem? What assumptions about time-translation symmetry are implicit in the experimental design?
