## Annotated Entries

**Newton, Isaac. 1687/1999. *The Principia: Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy*. Berkeley: University of California Press.**
The Cohen-Whitman translation is the authoritative English edition of Newton's masterwork. For the philosophy of time, the key text is the "Scholium on Space, Time, Place, and Motion" that opens the work, and the "General Scholium" appended to Book III. The editors provide extensive historical commentary. Essential primary source for any serious study of Newtonian time.

**Alexander, H. G., ed. 1956. *The Leibniz-Clarke Correspondence*. Manchester: Manchester University Press.**
The collected letters exchanged between Leibniz and Samuel Clarke (Newton's spokesman) between 1715 and 1716. The debate covers absolute vs. relational space and time, the Principle of Sufficient Reason, and the theological implications of each position. Leibniz's letters are among the most penetrating critiques of Newtonian metaphysics ever written. With the editor's introduction, indispensable for understanding the philosophical stakes of the Newtonian framework.

**Mach, Ernst. 1883/1960. *The Science of Mechanics*. Translated by Thomas J. McCormack. LaSalle, IL: Open Court.**
The book that defined the relational program in mechanics for the twentieth century. Mach's critique of Newton's absolute space and time is found in the second and third chapters. He combines historical scholarship, experimental analysis, and philosophical argument in a way that remains a model of the genre. Chapter II, §6, "Newton's Views of Time, Space, and Motion," is the locus classicus for the relational position.

**Earman, John. 1989. *World Enough and Space-Time: Absolute versus Relational Theories of Space and Time*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.**
The definitive philosophical treatment of the absolute-relational debate. Earman distinguishes multiple versions of substantivalism and relationism and analyzes their logical relationships with precision. Technical but accessible to advanced undergraduates. Provides the best philosophical reconstruction of both Newton's and Mach's positions, and connects them to contemporary work in the philosophy of physics.

**Barbour, Julian B. 1999. *The End of Time: The Next Revolution in Physics*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
Barbour's popular account of his relational and timeless approach to mechanics and quantum gravity. Written for a general audience, it develops the idea of "Platonia" — the space of all possible configurations of the universe — and argues that time, at the fundamental level, does not exist. Provocative, beautifully written, and philosophically ambitious. Should be read alongside a more critical account.

**Pooley, Oliver. 2013. "Substantivalist and Relationalist Approaches to Spacetime." In *The Oxford Handbook of Philosophy of Physics*, 522–586. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
The best up-to-date survey of the debate between absolutists and relationalists about spacetime. Pooley is scrupulous about distinguishing the many different versions of both positions and the different arguments that bear on each. A graduate-level reference that rewards careful reading. Particularly useful for understanding the relationship between the classical debate and the contemporary philosophy of physics.

**Kosmann-Schwarzbach, Yvette. 2011. *The Noether Theorems*. New York: Springer.**
A comprehensive study of Noether's 1918 paper and its legacy in mathematics and physics. Includes an annotated translation of the original paper, detailed historical commentary, and analyses of the theorem's implications for conservation laws in general relativity, quantum field theory, and modern physics. The authoritative scholarly source on Noether's work.

**Price, Huw. 1996. *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point*. New York: Oxford University Press.**
A philosophical investigation of the arrow of time from a committed perspectivalist viewpoint. Price argues that many standard formulations of the time-asymmetry problem are confused because they adopt a temporally biased viewpoint. His analysis of T-symmetry, the thermodynamic arrow, and the special initial conditions of the universe is rigorous and provocative. Accessible to advanced undergraduates with some background in philosophy.

**Earman, John. 2002. "What Time Reversal Is and Why It Matters." *International Studies in the Philosophy of Science* 16 (3): 245–264.**
A careful, technical analysis of what the time-reversal operation means in classical and quantum mechanics. Earman distinguishes several different senses in which a theory might be "time-reversal symmetric" and argues that the standard treatment is frequently confused. Essential reading for anyone who wants to understand the philosophical significance of T-symmetry.

**Roberts, Bryan W. 2022. *Reversing the Arrow of Time*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.**
A recent, philosophically sophisticated treatment of time reversal and the arrow of time. Roberts argues for a particular account of what time reversal means and applies it to quantum field theory as well as classical mechanics. More accessible than some technical treatments and philosophically explicit about the implications for the metaphysics of time.

**Barbour, Julian B., and Bruno Bertotti. 1982. "Mach's Principle and the Structure of Dynamical Theories." *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London A* 382: 295–306.**
The original technical paper establishing the Barbour-Bertotti relational mechanics. Shows that Newtonian mechanics can be reformulated using only relational quantities between particles, without reference to absolute space or time. Requires some familiarity with Lagrangian mechanics, but the conceptual sections are accessible. A landmark in the relational mechanics program.
