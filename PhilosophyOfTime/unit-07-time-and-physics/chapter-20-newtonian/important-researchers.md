# Important Researchers: Chapter 20

**Isaac Newton (1643–1727)**
English mathematician and natural philosopher whose *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687) established classical mechanics and defined the framework of absolute space and time. Newton's philosophical commitments — particularly his theological grounding of absolute time as the *sensorium Dei* — were inseparable from his physics and shaped the metaphysical assumptions of Western science for two centuries. His *Opticks* (1704) and private manuscripts reveal a deeper engagement with theology and alchemy than his physics alone suggests. The Newton-Leibniz debate, conducted through Clarke, was the defining controversy in early modern philosophy of nature.

**Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)**
German mathematician and philosopher, and Newton's greatest philosophical antagonist on the question of space and time. Leibniz held a relational view: space is the order of coexistent things, and time is the order of successive things; neither exists independently of the objects and events that define them. His letters to Samuel Clarke (1715–1716) contain some of the most penetrating critiques of Newtonian absolutism ever written, deploying the Principle of Sufficient Reason and the Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles against Newton's position. Leibniz's relational view was largely ignored in his own time but was vindicated, in important respects, by the relational programs of Mach and Barbour.

**Ernst Mach (1838–1916)**
Austrian physicist and philosopher of science who developed the most influential critique of Newtonian absolute space and time in the nineteenth century. In *The Science of Mechanics* (1883), Mach argued that absolute space and time have no empirical content and should be replaced by purely relational, operationally defined concepts. His "Mach's Principle" — that inertia arises from the distribution of all matter in the universe — directly influenced Einstein. Though Mach never accepted atoms as real physical entities (a position that ultimately proved untenable), his empiricist critique of metaphysical absolutes in physics was historically and philosophically productive.

**Emmy Noether (1882–1935)**
German mathematician who proved the theorem bearing her name in 1915 (published 1918), establishing the fundamental connection between continuous symmetries and conserved quantities in physics. Despite extraordinary talent, she faced severe discrimination: she was initially denied the right to lecture at Göttingen (she lectured under Hilbert's name) and was expelled from Germany in 1933 by the Nazi regime. Einstein called her the most significant mathematical genius produced since the higher education of women began. Her theorem is now recognized as one of the pillars of theoretical physics, underlying our understanding of energy conservation, momentum conservation, and the structure of gauge theories.

**Julian Barbour (1937–)**
British physicist and philosopher of physics who has developed the relational program in mechanics over six decades of largely independent scholarship. His 1982 paper with Bruno Bertotti established a technically rigorous relational mechanics without absolute time; his 1999 book *The End of Time* extended the program to quantum gravity, arguing that time does not exist at the fundamental level and that our experience of time is an emergent illusion arising from the structure of the wavefunction over configuration space. Barbour is one of the few physicists who has developed a sustained, technically serious alternative to the standard treatment of time in physics.

**Samuel Clarke (1675–1729)**
English philosopher and theologian who served as Newton's spokesman in the famous correspondence with Leibniz. Clarke's letters defend Newton's absolute space and time on theological grounds: absolute space is infinite and omnipresent because God is infinite and omnipresent; absolute time reflects God's eternal existence. Clarke's theological arguments were central to the Newton-Leibniz debate and illuminate the metaphysical foundations that Newton's physics presupposed.

**James Cronin (1931–2016) and Val Fitch (1923–2015)**
American physicists who, together with their collaborators (Christenson and Turlay), discovered CP violation in neutral kaon decay in 1964. Their discovery established that the weak nuclear force violates the combined symmetry of charge conjugation and parity, which by the CPT theorem implies T-violation. They were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1980. Their finding was the first experimental proof that the fundamental laws of physics are not perfectly symmetric under time reversal.

**John Earman (1942–)**
American philosopher of physics at the University of Pittsburgh (emeritus), whose work on the philosophy of spacetime, the Hole Argument, and the foundations of classical and relativistic mechanics has been central to the discipline for five decades. His 1989 book *World Enough and Space-Time* provides the definitive philosophical treatment of the absolute-relational debate in the philosophy of physics. Earman is notable for bringing the precision of formal logic and philosophy of science to bear on questions that had previously been treated in looser terms.

**Andrei Sakharov (1921–1989)**
Soviet theoretical physicist and human rights activist who identified the three conditions necessary for the universe to contain more matter than antimatter — baryon number violation, C and CP violation, and departure from thermal equilibrium — in a landmark 1967 paper. These "Sakharov conditions" connect CP violation (and hence T-violation) to the large-scale structure of the universe, providing the only known cosmological context in which weak-force T-asymmetry may have played a significant role in the temporal development of the universe. Sakharov later became a prominent dissident and was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1975.
