# Chapter 20: Newtonian Time

When Isaac Newton published his *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica* in 1687, he did not simply invent a new branch of physics. He offered a comprehensive vision of how the universe is structured in space and time — a vision so successful, so mathematically elegant, and so internally coherent that it dominated natural philosophy for more than two centuries. At the heart of that vision lay a conception of time that Newton made explicit in the very first pages of the *Principia*: absolute, true, and mathematical time, flowing uniformly from the infinite past to the infinite future, indifferent to everything that transpires within it.

This conception of time may seem obvious to us now — it is, after all, the conception most of us grew up with. But it was a specific, philosophically contentious claim, and Newton knew it. He distinguished absolute time from what he called "common time" or "relative time" — the time we measure with clocks and observe in the movements of celestial bodies. Common time, he recognized, is always approximate, always potentially irregular. Absolute time is what lies beneath it: the true, independent, uniform flow against which all physical processes are ultimately measured.

This chapter examines Newtonian time from several angles. We begin with Newton's own foundational text — the famous Scholium, or interpretive note, that opens the *Principia* — and read it as the philosophical document it is, attending not only to Newton's physical claims but to his theological motivations and metaphysical commitments. We then examine what it means, technically, for time to function as a parameter rather than a dynamical variable in the equations of Newtonian mechanics, and why that feature proves so significant when we compare Newton's framework with Einstein's.

The chapter then considers the most powerful challenge to Newtonian absolute time from within the classical tradition: Ernst Mach's relational critique, which argued that absolute space and time are metaphysical fictions with no empirical content. Mach's critique was historically important — it directly influenced Einstein — and it raises questions about parsimony, empirical underdetermination, and the relationship between physics and metaphysics that remain live today. We close by examining what may be the deepest connection between time and physics in the Newtonian framework: Emmy Noether's theorem, which reveals that time-translation symmetry — the fact that the laws of physics are the same today as they were yesterday — is directly responsible for the conservation of energy. The relationship between time and energy, Noether shows us, is not merely contingent. It is structural.

By the end of this chapter, Newton's time should look like neither an obvious truth nor a naive error, but what it actually is: a sophisticated, philosophically rich proposal that earned its dominance through centuries of extraordinary predictive success, and that reveals its deepest tensions only under sustained critical pressure.
