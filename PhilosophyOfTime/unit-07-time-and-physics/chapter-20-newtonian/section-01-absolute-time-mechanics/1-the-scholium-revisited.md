# The Scholium Revisited

## Newton's Philosophical Manifesto

The *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica* opens with a series of definitions — of mass, motion, force — and then pauses. Before the laws of mechanics are stated, Newton inserts what he calls a "Scholium," a Latin term for an explanatory note or commentary. In eight dense paragraphs, he sets out his understanding of the most fundamental categories through which the science of mechanics is possible at all: space, time, place, and motion. The Scholium is not supplementary material. It is the philosophical heart of Newtonian physics.

"Absolute, true, and mathematical time," Newton writes, "of itself, and from its own nature, flows equably without relation to anything external, and by another name is called duration" (Newton 1687/1999, 408). The contrast Newton draws is with "relative, apparent, and common time," which is "some sensible and external (whether accurate or unequable) measure of duration by the means of motion." Common time is what we read off clocks. Absolute time is what lies beneath them.

This distinction might initially seem like pedantic philosophical housekeeping. On closer inspection, it reveals a deep and systematic metaphysical commitment. Newton is claiming that there is a fact of the matter about how much time passes between any two events — a fact that is independent of any measurement, any observer, and any physical process. Even in a universe with no clocks, no matter, and no motion, absolute time would continue to pass. Duration, for Newton, is not a relation between events but a feature of reality itself.

## Non-Dynamical: What It Means to Be a Background

The most important structural feature of Newtonian absolute time — the one that distinguishes it most sharply from Einsteinian time — is that it is *non-dynamical*. This term, familiar from physics, carries a precise meaning: absolute time does not interact with matter, does not affect physical processes, and is not affected by them. It provides the framework within which matter moves and events occur, but it is not itself part of the dynamics.

Think of it like the lines on a sheet of graph paper. The graph paper provides a coordinate system within which you can plot curves, but the paper itself is not a physical object interacting with the curves. Absolute time is, for Newton, the temporal coordinate grid of the universe — always there, always uniform, entirely indifferent to what is plotted on it.

This is in stark contrast with the time of general relativity, where time is part of the spacetime metric, and the metric is dynamical — it curves in response to the presence of matter and energy, and that curvature in turn affects how matter moves. In Einstein's universe, time and matter are in a genuine relationship of mutual influence. In Newton's universe, time simply *is*, prior to and independent of matter. Matter happens in time; but time does not happen because of matter.

The non-dynamical character of Newtonian time has a consequence that Newton's critics would later press hard: if absolute time has no observable effects, if every measurement we make is of "relative time" (the positions of clock hands, the swinging of pendulums), what grounds the claim that absolute time exists? This is Mach's question, and we will take it up in Section 3. For now, notice that Newton himself was aware of the challenge — which is why the Scholium is so careful to distinguish what is philosophically true from what is practically measurable.

## The Theological Dimension

Newton's conception of absolute time did not arise in a philosophical vacuum. It was embedded in a rich theological framework that shaped his entire natural philosophy, though this dimension of his thought was often suppressed or minimized by later interpreters who wished to claim Newton purely for secular science.

For Newton, absolute space and time were intimately connected with his understanding of God. In the *Principia*'s "General Scholium," added in the 1713 second edition, Newton writes that God "is not eternity and infinity, but eternal and infinite; he is not duration and space, but he endures and is present. He endures forever, and is everywhere present; and, by existing always and everywhere, he constitutes duration and space" (Newton 1713/1999, 941). In Newton's *Opticks* (1704) and in his private manuscripts, the point is made even more explicitly: absolute space is the *sensorium Dei*, God's sensory organ — the medium through which God is omnipresent in creation and through which God perceives all events.

Absolute time, on this view, is not just a metaphysical primitive. It is the temporal dimension of God's omnipresence. Because God exists at all times, there is an absolute fact about when events occur in relation to God's eternal awareness. Time is, in a sense, the structure of divine attention to the world.

This theological grounding was not incidental. It explains why Newton felt justified in positing absolute time as a physical reality despite its unobservability. For Newton, unobservable by humans does not mean not real — it means, if anything, more real, since the deepest realities (God, absolute space, absolute time) are precisely those that transcend human sensory access. Leibniz, who corresponded vigorously with Newton's champion Samuel Clarke between 1715 and 1716, rejected this picture entirely, arguing that a space or time with no observable differences from a space shifted by any amount or duration would violate the Principle of Sufficient Reason: there would be no reason for God to create the world *here* and *now* rather than there and then (Alexander 1956).

## Philosophical Implications

Reading the Scholium as a philosophical document illuminates several things. First, it shows us that the concept of absolute time is not forced on Newton by his physics — it is a metaphysical addition, a claim about what underlies the physics, and one that goes beyond anything the experiments and equations by themselves require. Second, it reveals the theological scaffolding on which the concept rests. When that scaffolding was dismantled — partly by the Enlightenment secularization of science, partly by the Leibnizian relational tradition, ultimately by Einstein's 1905 paper — absolute time became much harder to defend.

Third, and most importantly for our purposes, the Scholium models a pattern of thought that recurs throughout the philosophy of time: the attempt to distinguish the time we measure from the time we believe is real. Newton thought the real time was absolute and the measured time approximate. We will encounter versions of this distinction throughout this unit — in the difference between coordinate time and proper time in relativity, in the contrast between emergent and fundamental time in quantum gravity, in debates about whether the present moment we experience is genuinely metaphysically privileged. Newton was not the last to suspect that the time we live in is somehow a shadow of a deeper temporal reality. He was, however, the first to embed that suspicion systematically in the foundations of mathematical physics.
