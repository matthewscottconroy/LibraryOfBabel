# Time as Parameter

## The Equations of Motion

When you study Newtonian mechanics, you learn to write equations of motion: *F = ma*, the second law, or more generally, the Euler-Lagrange equations derived from an action principle. In every case, time appears in these equations in a specific and philosophically revealing way: as an independent variable against which everything else is measured. The position of a particle is *x(t)* — a function of time. Its velocity is *dx/dt*, the rate of change of position with respect to time. Its acceleration is *d²x/dt²*. Time *t* is the argument of all these functions; it is what changes, and everything else changes relative to it.

This is what physicists and philosophers of physics mean when they say that time is a *parameter* in Newtonian mechanics. A parameter, in this context, is an independent variable that is not determined by the dynamics of the system being described. The system evolves as a function of *t*, but the equations say nothing about what *t* is, where it comes from, or what determines its value. It is simply given — supplied from outside the system, as it were — and the system's job is to evolve with respect to it.

Compare this to the other variables in the equations. The position *x*, velocity *v*, and momentum *p* of a particle are all *dynamical variables* — they are determined by the equations of motion, given initial conditions. If I know *x(0)* and *v(0)*, I can calculate *x(t)* for any later time. But *t* itself is never calculated; it is always already there. You can think of Newtonian time as the coordinate along which the movie of the universe is played out, but the movie projector — time itself — is not part of the film.

## What "Non-Dynamical" Really Means

The word "non-dynamical" can sound like a purely technical term, but its philosophical significance runs deep. To say that time is non-dynamical in Newtonian mechanics is to say that time is not something the universe does or produces. It is not generated by the distribution of matter. It is not a consequence of motion or interaction. It simply *is* — prior to, and independent of, all the physical processes that unfold within it.

One way to feel the force of this is to ask: what would happen to time in a completely empty Newtonian universe? The answer, implicit in Newton's framework, is: nothing. Time would continue to flow, equably and uniformly, whether or not there was a single particle to mark its passage. There would be no clocks, no processes, no events — but there would still be durations. If God were to create a particle today, it would appear *now*, at a specific moment in the absolute temporal sequence, and all its subsequent motion would be measured against a temporal structure that was already there, waiting.

This is a genuine metaphysical commitment, and it is worth sitting with its strangeness. We are being asked to believe in a feature of the universe — the ongoing passage of time — that has no physical effects, that nothing interacts with, and that would exist even if the universe were empty. The only entity that could, in Newton's view, ground this claim was God — an eternal, omnipresent being for whom "time" is the structure of a creation always in God's awareness. As the theological foundations of physics eroded over the following centuries, this grounding became unavailable, and the non-dynamical character of Newtonian time became increasingly hard to defend on independent grounds.

## Contrast with Einstein

The contrast between Newtonian time as parameter and Einsteinian time as dynamical quantity is the single most important conceptual shift in the history of temporal physics. Understanding it clearly will pay dividends throughout the rest of this unit.

In special relativity (Chapter 21), time is still, in a sense, "external" to matter — the Minkowski metric of spacetime is fixed, not curved by matter. But even here, time is no longer absolute: what counts as a duration depends on the reference frame of the observer. Two observers in relative motion will measure different time intervals between the same pair of events. Time is no longer a single background against which all observers agree.

In general relativity (Chapter 22), the shift is even more radical. The metric of spacetime — which encodes both spatial distances and temporal durations — is a dynamical field, curved by the distribution of mass and energy according to Einstein's field equations. Here, time is genuinely part of the physics; it is determined by the equations of motion, just as the positions of particles are. In this sense, time in general relativity is as dynamical as any other physical field: it evolves, it interacts (gravitationally), and its structure depends on what matter is present.

This means that in general relativity, the question "how much time passes between two events?" does not have a frame-independent, matter-independent answer. The answer depends on the spacetime geometry, which depends on the matter distribution. Time, in this sense, is part of the story the universe tells about itself — not the externally-given medium in which the story is told.

## What It Means for Time to Be Non-Dynamical

Let us try to make this contrast as clear as possible with an analogy. Imagine the Newtonian universe as a stage play. The stage — space and time — is there before the performance begins; it is set up by the theater, indifferent to the drama about to unfold. The actors (particles, forces) move around on it, but their movements do not change the stage. Now imagine the Einsteinian universe as a different kind of performance: one where the movements of the actors *reshape* the stage as they go, causing it to flex and curve, and where the curvature of the stage in turn influences how the actors can move. In the first theater, time is furniture; in the second, time is a participant.

This shift from time as furniture to time as participant has profound philosophical consequences. If time is dynamical, then questions about the nature of time become questions about the nature of a physical field. It becomes possible, at least in principle, to have regions of the universe where time behaves very differently from regions we are familiar with — near black holes, near the Big Bang, or perhaps in quantum gravitational contexts where the very concept of a spacetime manifold breaks down. A dynamical time is a time that can, in principle, be created, destroyed, accelerated, and twisted. This is a far stranger entity than Newton's equable, divine flow — and it is where the physics of the last century has taken us.

The journey from Newton's non-dynamical parameter to Einstein's dynamical field is not just a story about physics becoming more mathematically sophisticated. It is a story about time becoming more fully part of the natural world — less like the mind of God and more like the fabric of matter. Whether that makes time more or less real, more or less philosophically tractable, is a question this unit will pursue at length.
