# Section 1: Absolute Time and Classical Mechanics

The opening pages of Newton's *Principia* contain a surprise. We expect a physics textbook to begin with equations or experiments. Instead, Newton begins with definitions and an extended philosophical commentary — the Scholium — in which he lays out his views on the nature of space, time, place, and motion. This was not an accident or a digression. Newton understood that mechanics required not just mathematical machinery but metaphysical foundations, and he took those foundations seriously.

The two sections of this chapter examine what Newton meant by absolute time and why it matters. The first section revisits the Scholium itself, reading it with both philosophical and historical attention, and traces the theological dimension that shaped Newton's thinking about temporal structure. The second section examines how time functions in the actual equations of Newtonian mechanics — as an external parameter, not a variable determined by the system — and draws out the contrast with Einstein's later, dynamical conception of time. The distinction between these two conceptions of time is not merely technical; it shapes what we think time is, whether it depends on matter, and whether a universe without matter would still have a temporal structure.
