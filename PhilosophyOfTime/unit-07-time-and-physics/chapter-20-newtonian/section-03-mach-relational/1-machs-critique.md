# Mach's Critique

## The Bucket and the Stars

Newton's most famous argument for absolute space involves a rotating bucket of water. If you fill a bucket with water and set it spinning, the water at first remains flat — it hasn't started rotating yet — but gradually, as the water is dragged into rotation by friction with the bucket walls, it begins to climb the walls of the bucket and form a concave surface. The concavity is evidence of centrifugal force, which Newton attributed to rotation relative to absolute space.

Now, Newton imagines asking: rotation relative to what? The water in the bucket is eventually rotating *with* the bucket, so the concavity cannot be due to rotation relative to the bucket. There is no other nearby object relative to which we could measure the rotation. Newton concludes that the rotation must be absolute — rotation relative to absolute space itself.

This is a powerful argument, but Mach, in *Die Mechanik in ihrer Entwicklung historisch-kritisch dargestellt* (The Science of Mechanics, 1883), challenged it on empirical grounds. Newton's bucket experiment, Mach points out, was conducted against the background of all the matter in the universe — the fixed stars, the galaxies (though Mach did not use that word), the entire cosmic mass distribution. We have never performed the experiment in genuinely empty space. And there is a crucial question Newton never asked: how do we know that the concavity of the water would appear if the bucket were rotating but all the matter of the universe were absent?

Mach's contention was that there is no empirical difference between:
1. The bucket rotating relative to all the matter in the universe
2. All the matter in the universe rotating relative to a stationary bucket

Both descriptions, he argued, should produce the same physical effects. If that is right, then "rotation relative to absolute space" is not a distinct physical state from "rotation relative to the distribution of all matter" — they are two descriptions of the same physical situation. Absolute space, as a distinct entity that matter can rotate relative to, drops out as empirically redundant.

## Mach's Principle

This argument generalizes into what Einstein later called "Mach's Principle": the inertial properties of a body should be determined entirely by the distribution of matter in the universe. Inertia — the tendency of a body to resist acceleration — should not be a relation between the body and absolute space or absolute time, but a relation between the body and all the other matter in the universe.

The philosophical force of Mach's principle depends on a commitment to what is sometimes called *empiricist parsimony*: entities whose existence makes no empirical difference should not be posited. This is related to Ockham's razor (prefer simpler theories) but goes further: it is not just about simplicity but about empirical content. Mach was influenced by the positivist tradition in philosophy of science, which held that meaningful scientific claims must be cashed out in terms of possible observations. Absolute space and absolute time, on this view, are not meaningfully distinct from "rotation relative to everything else" and "duration relative to all physical processes" — because the two descriptions make the same empirical predictions.

## The Relational Critique of Absolute Time

Mach's critique of absolute space is the more famous, but his critique of absolute time is just as pointed. If absolute time flows uniformly regardless of physical processes, how do we detect this uniformity? We compare clocks — pendulums, spring mechanisms, atomic transitions. But these comparisons are themselves physical processes. We have no access to absolute time except through physical clocks, and physical clocks might all be running at a rate that is systematically drifting relative to absolute time without our ever being able to tell.

More pointedly: what is the empirical content of the claim that one second today is the "same duration" as one second a billion years ago? We cannot compare them directly. If the laws of nature had changed their rate over cosmological time — if processes had slowed down uniformly — we would have no way of detecting it. The claim that absolute time flows uniformly is, on Mach's view, not a fact about the world but a methodological choice: we define duration in terms of physical processes, and then we adopt the norm of uniform duration as a convention that simplifies our description.

## Time from Processes

Mach's positive proposal is that time should be understood not as an independent entity but as abstracted from the totality of physical changes. He writes:

> "It is utterly beyond our power to measure the changes of things by time. Quite the contrary, time is an abstraction, at which we arrive by means of the changes of things" (Mach 1883/1960, 273).

This is a relational, process-based view of time: time is not the container in which processes occur but the abstraction we derive from processes themselves. The standard of time is set not by an absolute external flow but by the overall rate of change of all physical processes taken together. This is what Barbour would later develop into the concept of "ephemeris time" — a notion of time derived not from any single clock but from the overall dynamical state of the physical system under consideration.

## Influence on Einstein

Mach's relational program had a direct and acknowledged influence on Einstein. In a 1913 letter to Mach, Einstein wrote: "Your happy investigations on the foundations of mechanics... were what first led me to find out that Mach's criticism of Newton was not merely a negative step, but contains the germ of a positive theory of relativity" (quoted in Misner, Thorne, and Wheeler 1973, 543). Einstein intended general relativity to embody Mach's principle — to build in the idea that inertia arises from the distribution of matter. Whether general relativity actually satisfies Mach's principle is, interestingly, debated. Some solutions to Einstein's field equations (particularly empty or nearly-empty spacetimes) seem to allow inertial structure without matter, which Mach would have rejected.

The legacy of Mach's critique is not a finished theory but a methodological orientation: a demand that physical concepts earn their place by having empirical content. Applied to time, this demand has been productive and disruptive in equal measure, motivating not only the relational program in classical mechanics but the more radical proposals in quantum gravity that suggest time itself may be derivative rather than fundamental.
