# Barbour and Bertotti: Relational Mechanics

## The Relational Program

Julian Barbour is one of the most persistent and philosophically serious advocates for the relational approach to space and time in modern physics. An independent scholar who spent decades working outside academia, Barbour has developed a research program that takes Mach's critique with full seriousness and asks: can we construct a formulation of mechanics that makes no reference to absolute space or absolute time, using only relational quantities between particles?

The 1982 paper by Barbour and Bruno Bertotti, "Mach's Principle and the Structure of Dynamical Theories," published in *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London*, is the most technically polished realization of this program in the classical context. It is worth understanding what they achieved and where the approach runs into difficulties.

## Relational Quantities

The key move in the Barbour-Bertotti approach is to restrict the fundamental ontology to *relational* quantities: the distances and angles between particles, and the rates at which those distances and angles change. Nothing in the theory refers to the absolute position of any particle in space, nor to the rate of change of any particle's position relative to absolute space, nor to the absolute rate of time's passage.

In the Newtonian framework, the state of a system of *N* particles is specified by *3N* coordinates (three for each particle) plus *3N* momenta, giving a *6N*-dimensional phase space. The Barbour-Bertotti approach instead works with the *relational configuration space* — the space of all possible sets of inter-particle distances and angles, modulo overall rotation, translation, and scaling. This space is called "shape space," and the fundamental claim is that it, not the Newtonian phase space, is where physics really lives.

## Intrinsic Time vs. Extrinsic Time

One of the central conceptual contributions of the Barbour-Bertotti program is the distinction between *intrinsic time* and *extrinsic time*. This distinction is philosophically important and will recur in our discussions of quantum gravity.

*Extrinsic time* is time as Newton understood it: a parameter supplied from outside the physical system, measuring the duration of processes against an absolute, external standard. This is the *t* that appears in *x(t)*, *v(t)*, and *a(t)*. It is external to the system in the sense that the system's dynamics do not determine *t*; *t* is given independently.

*Intrinsic time* is time as read from within the system itself, derived from the system's own dynamical state. Barbour and Bertotti propose to replace Newtonian absolute time with an intrinsic time defined as follows: the best possible measure of duration, consistent with the system's equations of motion, is the one that makes the dynamics as simple as possible. This is a variational principle — you choose the time parameterization that minimizes the departure from Newtonian dynamics, as measured in terms of relational quantities only.

This approach is closely related to the idea of *ephemeris time* used in astronomy: rather than relying on a single clock, astronomers define a time standard by requiring that it make the dynamics of the solar system as predictable as possible (Clemence 1952). The Barbour-Bertotti approach generalizes this to any system of particles.

## What the Program Achieves

The Barbour-Bertotti paper demonstrated that, for a system of particles interacting via certain forces (specifically, those derivable from homogeneous potential functions), a relational formulation can reproduce the predictions of Newtonian mechanics exactly. The theory makes the same empirical predictions as Newton's, but without invoking absolute space or absolute time.

This is a significant result. It shows that the empirical content of Newtonian mechanics can be fully captured by a theory that uses only observable, relational quantities. Absolute time, in Mach's sense, is not needed to do the physics. The appearances saved by absolute time can be equally well saved by intrinsic time derived from the relational dynamics.

Moreover, the relational framework gives a natural account of time's measurability: time is what is measured by clocks, and clocks are physical subsystems that undergo regular periodic processes. The "tick" of a clock is not a comparison to absolute time but a phase in the overall relational dynamics of the universe. This is exactly the kind of operational, process-based account of time that Mach was calling for.

## Where the Program Faces Challenges

The Barbour-Bertotti program does not succeed in all respects, and the difficulties are philosophically instructive.

First, the program works cleanly only for certain force laws. Gravity, as described by Newtonian mechanics, does not obviously fit into the Barbour-Bertotti framework without modification. Barbour has continued to develop the framework over subsequent decades, culminating in his proposal (Barbour 1994; 2003) of "best matching" — a variational principle that determines the relational dynamics by minimizing a certain functional over the configuration space. This is technically impressive but involves non-trivial choices about what counts as the "best" match between successive configurations.

Second, the extension to field theories (electromagnetism, quantum mechanics) is not straightforward. The relational program works most naturally for particle systems; incorporating fields introduces additional complications about what constitutes the fundamental relational structure.

Third, and most fundamentally, the program has difficulty with the *direction* of time. Intrinsic time, defined variationally from relational dynamics, gives a parameterization of trajectories but does not by itself distinguish the forward from the backward direction. The arrow of time must still be explained by appeal to boundary conditions and thermodynamics. Relational mechanics is no better positioned than Newtonian mechanics to explain why time runs in one direction rather than the other.

## Philosophical Significance

Despite these difficulties, the Barbour-Bertotti program makes an important philosophical point: the existence of absolute time is not necessary for physics to work. The Newtonian framework includes absolute time as an element whose removal does not change the empirical predictions of the theory. This is exactly what Mach predicted, and it is a philosophically significant finding.

Whether this means that absolute time does not *exist* is a further question — one that requires philosophical argument about the relationship between theoretical structure and ontological commitment. An instrumentalist might say that absolute time is mere theoretical machinery that we can dispense with. A realist might argue that the best explanation of why the relational formulation works is that there genuinely is an intrinsic temporal structure to the universe, which the relational framework is tracking in a roundabout way. The debate between these positions is a microcosm of larger debates about scientific realism and the relationship between theory and reality.

What is not in doubt is that the Barbour-Bertotti program, and the broader Machian tradition, forced physics to take seriously the question of what is genuinely necessary in our fundamental temporal concepts — a question that becomes even more pressing in the context of quantum gravity, where the program's direct descendant, Barbour's "timeless" approach to quantum cosmology, has become one of the most provocative proposals in contemporary foundations of physics.
