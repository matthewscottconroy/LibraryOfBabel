# Section 3: Mach's Relational Critique

Ernst Mach occupies a peculiar position in the history of physics: he was a scientist who never accepted atoms, a philosopher who profoundly influenced Einstein, and a critic of Newton whose ideas shaped the development of the theory that superseded Newton's. His critique of absolute space and absolute time, developed in *The Science of Mechanics* (1883), is a model of the kind of critical analysis that exposes the empirical and metaphysical assumptions hiding in apparently neutral scientific concepts.

Mach's central question was disarmingly simple: what is the empirical content of Newton's claim that space and time are absolute? If absolute space and time have no observable effects that distinguish them from their relational competitors, do they deserve a place in our scientific ontology? The answer Mach gave — no — launched a relational research program in mechanics that continues to this day.

This section follows Mach's critique and then examines one of its most technically developed descendants: the program of Barbour and Bertotti (1982), who constructed a formulation of mechanics that uses only relational quantities, without absolute time, and explored how well it could reproduce the predictions of Newtonian mechanics. Their work raises deep questions about what is genuinely necessary in our fundamental physics.
