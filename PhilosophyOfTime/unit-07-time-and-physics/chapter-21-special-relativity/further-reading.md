## Annotated Entries

**Einstein, Albert. 1905/1923. "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies." In *The Principle of Relativity*. London: Methuen.**
The original paper, translated into English. Remarkable for its accessibility relative to its depth: Einstein's derivation of the Lorentz transformations and the discussion of simultaneity in the opening sections are models of clear physical reasoning. Reading the original is essential for understanding both the physics and the philosophical significance. The Perrett-Jeffery translation is widely available and reliable.

**Einstein, Albert. 1916/1920. *Relativity: The Special and General Theory*. Translated by Robert W. Lawson.**
Einstein's own popular account of both special and general relativity. The train-and-platform thought experiment is developed here in accessible terms. Though not a substitute for the technical papers, it provides invaluable access to Einstein's thinking about the philosophical significance of his theory. Essential reading for anyone approaching special relativity with philosophical interests.

**Mermin, N. David. 2005. *It's About Time: Understanding Einstein's Relativity*. Princeton: Princeton University Press.**
The best introduction to special relativity for non-physicists, written by one of the most gifted physics expositors of the twentieth century. Mermin takes care to distinguish what is physically real from what is frame-dependent, and his treatment of simultaneity is especially clear. Strongly recommended as a supplement to this chapter for readers without a physics background.

**Taylor, Edwin F., and John Archibald Wheeler. 1992. *Spacetime Physics*. 2nd ed. New York: W. H. Freeman.**
The classic undergraduate physics textbook on special relativity, notable for its emphasis on the spacetime interval and proper time as the central concepts. The "interval conservation" approach to teaching relativity is adopted throughout. Requires basic algebra and some physics background, but the conceptual discussions are excellent and philosophically useful.

**Sklar, Lawrence. 1974. *Space, Time, and Spacetime*. Berkeley: University of California Press.**
A comprehensive and philosophically careful treatment of special and general relativity, the conventionality of simultaneity, and the metaphysics of spacetime. Still one of the best philosophical introductions to the subject. Sklar is scrupulous about distinguishing physical content from interpretive and metaphysical claims.

**Reichenbach, Hans. 1928/1958. *The Philosophy of Space and Time*. New York: Dover.**
Reichenbach's major work on the philosophical interpretation of relativity theory, including the famous treatment of the ε-convention for synchronization. Philosophically important as an early attempt to extract the philosophical content of special relativity by a thinker deeply familiar with both the physics and the positivist philosophy of science tradition. Some of the logical positivist framework is dated, but the core analysis of conventionalism remains influential.

**Maudlin, Tim. 2002. *Quantum Non-Locality and Relativity*. 2nd ed. Oxford: Blackwell.**
A demanding but rewarding treatment of the relationship between quantum mechanics and special relativity, with an extended defense of a neo-Lorentzian interpretation. Maudlin argues for the importance of preferred frames in understanding quantum non-locality and presents the case for a dynamical, non-conventional approach to simultaneity. An important counterweight to more standard philosophical treatments.

**Putnam, Hilary. 1967. "Time and Physical Geometry." *Journal of Philosophy* 64: 240–247.**
The philosophical paper that, along with Rietdijk (1966), crystallized the argument from special relativity to the block universe. Short, accessible, and forceful. Putnam formulates the argument in terms of what he calls "soft facts" and "hard facts" about the world, and argues that consistency requires all events to be equally real. Essential reading for anyone interested in the metaphysics of time.

**Stein, Howard. 1991. "On Relativity Theory and Openness of the Future." *Philosophy of Science* 58: 147–167.**
Stein's developed response to Putnam, arguing that a relativistically appropriate notion of "becoming" can be defined using the causal structure of Minkowski spacetime. One of the most technically careful papers in the philosophy of time. Requires some familiarity with special relativity, but the central arguments are accessible with effort.

**Malament, David. 1977. "Causal Theories of Time and the Conventionality of Simultaneity." *Noûs* 11: 293–300.**
The paper that established that Einstein synchrony is the unique simultaneity relation definable from the causal structure of Minkowski spacetime. An important result for evaluating the significance of Reichenbach's ε-convention. Technical but essential for a full understanding of conventionalism about simultaneity.
