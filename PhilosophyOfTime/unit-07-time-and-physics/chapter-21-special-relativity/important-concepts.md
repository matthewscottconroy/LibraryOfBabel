# Important Concepts: Chapter 21

**Principle of Relativity**
The first postulate of special relativity: the laws of physics are the same in all inertial reference frames. No physical experiment can distinguish between two frames in uniform relative motion; there is no such thing as "absolute rest." This principle was recognized in a form by Galileo for mechanics; Einstein extended it to all laws of physics, including electromagnetism.

**Constancy of the Speed of Light**
The second postulate of special relativity: the speed of light in vacuum is the same for all inertial observers, regardless of the motion of the source or observer. This postulate, combined with the principle of relativity, entails the relativity of simultaneity, time dilation, and length contraction. It eliminates the need for the luminiferous aether.

**Inertial Reference Frame**
A reference frame in which an object subject to no forces moves in a straight line at constant velocity (Newton's first law holds). Inertial frames are in uniform relative motion — neither accelerating nor rotating with respect to each other. Special relativity is formulated for inertial frames; non-inertial (accelerating) frames require additional analysis.

**Lorentz Transformation**
The coordinate transformation relating the spacetime coordinates of two inertial reference frames in relative motion. Replaces the Galilean transformation of Newtonian mechanics. The Lorentz transformation encodes the relativity of simultaneity, time dilation, and length contraction, and ensures that the speed of light is the same in all frames.

**Lorentz Factor (γ)**
The quantity γ = 1/√(1 - v²/c²) that appears in the Lorentz transformation. It equals 1 when v = 0 and increases without bound as v approaches c. The Lorentz factor measures the degree of time dilation and length contraction for a body moving at speed v relative to a reference frame.

**Relativity of Simultaneity**
The fact that whether two spatially separated events are simultaneous is not an absolute, frame-independent fact but depends on the reference frame of the observer. Observers in relative motion will disagree about which events are simultaneous. This is a physical consequence of the Lorentz transformation and is not merely a measurement artifact.

**Time Dilation**
The slowing of moving clocks: a clock moving at speed v relative to a reference frame runs slow by a factor of γ relative to clocks at rest in that frame. The effect is real and symmetric (each observer says the other's clock runs slow) while both are in inertial motion, but becomes asymmetric when one observer accelerates, as in the twin paradox.

**Length Contraction**
An object moving at speed v relative to a reference frame is measured to be shorter in the direction of motion by a factor of 1/γ compared to its proper length (length in the object's rest frame). Like time dilation, this is a real physical effect rather than a measurement artifact.

**Proper Time**
The time measured by a clock that is carried along with a physical system — the time elapsed as measured in the system's own rest frame, or equivalently, the length of the system's worldline in the Minkowski metric. Proper time is an invariant: all observers agree on how much proper time has elapsed along a given worldline. It is the fundamental temporal concept in special relativity.

**Coordinate Time**
The time coordinate assigned to events in a particular reference frame. Coordinate time is frame-dependent and is not the same as proper time for an object moving in the frame. The distinction between coordinate time and proper time is essential for understanding time dilation and the twin paradox.

**Spacetime Interval**
The invariant quantity Δs² = -c²Δt² + Δx² + Δy² + Δz² that measures the "distance" between two events in Minkowski spacetime. Unlike spatial distances or time intervals alone, the spacetime interval is the same for all inertial observers. It is the central geometric object of special relativity.

**Minkowski Spacetime**
The four-dimensional geometric structure of special relativity, with three spatial dimensions and one time dimension, and the Minkowski (pseudo-Riemannian) metric. The Lorentz transformations are the isometries (symmetry transformations) of Minkowski spacetime. The structure unifies space and time into a single geometric entity.

**Light Cone**
The set of all events connected to a given event E by lightlike (null) intervals: events that can be reached from E by a light signal (future light cone) or from which a light signal could reach E (past light cone). The light cone structure defines the causal relations in special relativity and provides a covariant criterion for temporal order.

**Worldline**
The path traced by a physical object through spacetime — the set of all events at which the object is present. For an object in uniform motion, the worldline is a straight line in Minkowski spacetime. Proper time is the length of the worldline, measured using the Minkowski metric.

**Block Universe (Eternalism)**
The metaphysical view that all events — past, present, and future — are equally real, forming a four-dimensional spacetime manifold. Special relativity's lack of absolute simultaneity is often cited in support of the block universe, most notably by the Rietdijk-Putnam argument.

**Rietdijk-Putnam Argument**
An argument from special relativity to the block universe, developed independently by C. W. Rietdijk (1966) and Hilary Putnam (1967). The argument uses the relativity of simultaneity to show that if events are real when they are "present" for some observer, and if reality is frame-independent, then all events are equally real.

**Neo-Lorentzian Interpretation**
An interpretation of special relativity that posits a metaphysically preferred reference frame (analogous to Newton's absolute space) relative to which simultaneity is absolute, while granting that this preferred frame is physically undetectable. Motivated by the desire to preserve presentism in the face of the Rietdijk-Putnam argument.

**ε-Convention (Reichenbach)**
Hans Reichenbach's observation that the synchronization of spatially separated clocks within a reference frame involves a conventional choice, parameterized by ε (0 < ε < 1). The standard Einstein synchrony corresponds to ε = 1/2, which assumes light travels at the same speed in both directions. Malament (1977) showed that Einstein synchrony is the unique choice definable from the causal structure of Minkowski spacetime.
