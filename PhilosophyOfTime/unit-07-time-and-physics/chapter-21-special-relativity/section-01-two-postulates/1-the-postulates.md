# The Postulates of Special Relativity

## The Theoretical Background

By the late nineteenth century, two of the greatest theories in physics were in tension with each other. Newtonian mechanics, which governed the motion of massive objects, was thoroughly understood and extraordinarily successful. Maxwell's equations for electromagnetism, published in 1865, described electric and magnetic fields and correctly predicted that light was an electromagnetic wave — one of the triumphs of Victorian physics. But the two theories made different predictions about what would happen when you asked a simple question: does the speed of light depend on the motion of the observer?

Newtonian mechanics said yes. If you are moving toward a source of light at speed *v*, the light approaches you at *c + v*, where *c* is the speed of light relative to its medium. This is the standard composition of velocities — the same logic that tells you a ball thrown forward from a moving train arrives faster than a ball thrown from a stationary train.

Maxwell's equations, however, gave a fixed speed for light — approximately 3 × 10⁸ meters per second — without any mention of an observer's velocity. The prevailing interpretation was that Maxwell's equations described the behavior of light relative to a medium called the "luminiferous aether," a pervasive but undetectable substance that filled all space and served as the medium for electromagnetic waves. Light moved at *c* relative to the aether; observers moving relative to the aether would measure different speeds for light.

This was the theoretical landscape that Albert Michelson and Edward Morley set out to probe in 1887.

## The Michelson-Morley Experiment

Michelson and Morley designed an interferometer — a device that splits a beam of light, sends the two halves in perpendicular directions, reflects them back, and recombines them. If the Earth is moving through the aether (as it must be, since it orbits the Sun), the two beams should travel different effective distances relative to the aether and arrive slightly out of phase, producing a detectable interference pattern.

Michelson and Morley observed no fringe shift — no evidence of any aether drift — at a level of precision far better than was needed to detect the expected effect (Michelson and Morley 1887). This was one of the most consequential null results in the history of science. The aether hypothesis — the medium relative to which light was supposed to propagate at speed *c* — received no experimental support.

Various attempts were made to save the aether picture: Lorentz and FitzGerald proposed that objects moving through the aether would contract in the direction of motion by exactly the amount needed to cancel the expected fringe shift. This *ad hoc* proposal accounted for the null result, but it was deeply unsatisfying — it explained the experimental outcome by positing a mysterious contraction with no independent motivation.

Einstein's 1905 paper took a different approach entirely: accept the experimental result at face value, abandon the aether, and build a new mechanics that takes the constancy of the speed of light as a fundamental postulate.

## The Two Postulates

Einstein's paper opens by noting an asymmetry in the Maxwell equations: when a magnet moves near a conductor, the induced current depends only on the relative motion between them, not on whether it is the magnet or the conductor that is "really" moving. This observation, Einstein writes, "suggests that the phenomena of electrodynamics as well as of mechanics possess no properties corresponding to the idea of absolute rest" (Einstein 1905/1923, 37). He then states his two postulates:

**Postulate 1 — The Principle of Relativity:** The laws of physics (including the laws of electrodynamics) are the same in all inertial reference frames.

**Postulate 2 — The Constancy of the Speed of Light:** Light in vacuum propagates with a definite velocity *c*, which is independent of the state of motion of the emitting body.

An inertial reference frame is one in which a body subject to no forces moves in a straight line at constant velocity (or remains at rest). The set of all inertial frames are those in uniform relative motion — not accelerating. The principle of relativity says that no physical experiment can distinguish between two inertial frames. There is no such thing as "truly at rest" versus "truly moving at constant velocity" — these are equally valid descriptions of the same physical situation.

## Why the Postulates Are Revolutionary

Postulate 1 alone is not new. Galileo had argued for something similar: if you are below deck on a smoothly sailing ship, no experiment with falling objects, bouncing balls, or flies buzzing around will tell you whether the ship is moving or at rest. This is why Newton's mechanics is invariant under Galilean transformations (adding a constant velocity to all objects in a closed system changes nothing observable).

The novelty is Postulate 2. Einstein insists that this applies to *all* laws of physics, including Maxwell's equations — and therefore to the speed of light. If two observers are in uniform relative motion, they must each measure the speed of light as the same constant *c*, regardless of their motion relative to each other or relative to the source of the light.

This seems to contradict everyday intuition immediately. Suppose Alice is standing still and Bob is running toward a light source at half the speed of light. Common sense says Bob should measure the light approaching at *c + (c/2) = 1.5c*. But Postulate 2 says Bob measures exactly *c*. How can this be?

The answer is that something must be wrong with the common-sense assumption. The common-sense velocity addition formula assumes that time passes at the same rate for all observers, and that lengths are the same for all observers. Einstein's insight was that *these are the assumptions that must be abandoned*. If the speed of light is truly constant for all inertial observers, then the way we measure time and space must be different from what Newton assumed — and different for different observers. This is why special relativity implies the relativity of simultaneity, time dilation, and length contraction.

## Why These Postulates?

One might ask: why should the laws of physics be the same in all inertial frames? Is this not just an aesthetic principle? And why should the speed of light specifically be constant?

On the first postulate, the answer involves both theoretical elegance and experimental motivation. Centuries of experiment have confirmed that no experiment conducted in a closed, uniformly moving laboratory has ever detected the laboratory's absolute velocity. The principle of relativity is an empirical generalization, not an a priori truth.

On the second postulate, the motivation is the conflict between Maxwell's equations and Newtonian mechanics. Maxwell's equations as written do not transform nicely under Galilean velocity boosts — they are not Galilean-invariant. Either Maxwell's equations are only approximately right (and the true equations are Galilean-invariant), or Newton's mechanics is only approximately right (and the true mechanics respects a different symmetry). The Michelson-Morley result strongly suggested the second option. Einstein chose to take Maxwell's equations seriously and re-derive mechanics from there.

There is also a deep theoretical reason: quantum field theory, which is our best current description of elementary particles, requires that physics be Lorentz-invariant — invariant under the transformations that special relativity specifies. The successes of quantum field theory, which is the most precisely tested theory in the history of science, provide overwhelming indirect confirmation of special relativity's postulates.
