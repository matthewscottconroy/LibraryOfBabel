# Section 1: The Two Postulates

Einstein's special theory of relativity is built on two postulates that are individually plausible but jointly revolutionary. The first, the principle of relativity, is in some ways a continuation of an idea that goes back to Galileo: the laws of physics should look the same to any observer in uniform motion. The second postulate — that the speed of light is the same for all inertial observers, regardless of the motion of the source — is the radical one. Together, they entail that Newton's absolute time must be abandoned.

This section develops both postulates in detail. The first subsection explains what the postulates say, why they are motivated by experiment and theoretical reflection, and what makes the second postulate so surprising. It places them in the context of the Michelson-Morley experiment of 1887, which found no evidence of the aether — the medium through which light was supposed to propagate — and which created a deep theoretical crisis for classical electromagnetism. The second subsection shows how the Lorentz transformations follow from the two postulates, and derives the key consequences: the relativity of simultaneity, time dilation, and length contraction. These derivations are presented at a conceptual level accessible to readers without advanced mathematical training, while remaining physically precise.
