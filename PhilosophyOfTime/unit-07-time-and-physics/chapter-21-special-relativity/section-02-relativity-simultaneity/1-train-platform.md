# The Train and Platform Thought Experiment

## Einstein's Setup

One of the most effective thought experiments in the history of physics comes from Einstein himself, elaborated in his popular book *Relativity: The Special and General Theory* (1916/1920). The scenario is elegantly simple: a long train is moving at high speed relative to a railway embankment. At the moment the midpoint of the train passes a particular point on the embankment, two lightning bolts strike simultaneously — one at the front of the train and one at the rear. Is this simultaneous?

To an observer standing on the embankment at the midpoint between the two strike points, yes. The light from both strikes arrives at the same instant, and since both strikes are equidistant from the observer, they must have occurred simultaneously. The embankment observer concludes: the two lightning bolts struck at the same time.

Now consider an observer riding at the midpoint of the train. For this observer, the two strike points are also equidistant — they are the front and rear of the train. But the train is moving *toward* the location of the front strike. By the time the light from the strikes reaches the train observer, the light from the front strike arrives *first*, because the train observer has moved toward it during the transit time of the light.

If Newtonian mechanics were correct, we would say: the train observer sees the front strike first because she moved toward the light from that strike. There is an objective fact (both strikes were simultaneous) and an observer-dependent appearance (the train observer sees them at different times). The apparent discrepancy is explained by the motion of the observer relative to the absolute reference frame.

But Einstein's Postulate 2 changes everything. The speed of light is the same for both the embankment observer and the train observer, regardless of their relative motion. The train observer cannot explain her observation by saying "the light from the front reached me first because I moved toward it while it was in transit" — that reasoning involves the light traveling at a speed relative to her that is different from *c*, which violates Postulate 2.

Instead, the train observer must conclude: the front lightning bolt struck *before* the rear lightning bolt. In her reference frame, the two events are not simultaneous.

## What the Thought Experiment Shows

The thought experiment establishes precisely that what the embankment observer calls "simultaneous" is not what the train observer calls "simultaneous." Both observers are correct in their respective frames. There is no privileged frame in which we can find out "which one is really right."

This is philosophically striking. Newton would have said that one of them must be wrong about the simultaneity, or that simultaneity in an absolute sense can only be defined relative to the aether frame. Einstein denies this entirely: there is no absolute frame, and therefore no absolute simultaneity. The question "are the lightning bolts really simultaneous?" has no frame-independent answer. Simultaneity is, in Einstein's precise sense, *relative*.

It is worth distinguishing three positions one might take:

1. **Absolute simultaneity with epistemic limitation:** The lightning bolts are absolutely simultaneous or not, but we cannot always tell which because of measurement limitations. This is the Newtonian view, which special relativity decisively refutes (given the postulates).

2. **No simultaneity at all:** The concept of simultaneity is simply meaningless. This is too strong — simultaneity remains well-defined within any given frame; it just fails to be frame-independent.

3. **Frame-relative simultaneity:** Simultaneity is a well-defined but frame-relative relation. Two events are simultaneous *relative to a frame F* if they receive equal time coordinates in *F*. No frame is privileged. This is Einstein's position, and the correct one given special relativity.

## The Philosophical Significance

The philosophical significance of frame-relative simultaneity cannot be overstated, but it also should not be exaggerated. Let us be precise about what it does and does not establish.

It establishes: that there is no objective, frame-independent present. Any two spatially separated events that are "simultaneous" in one frame are not simultaneous in some other frame. The set of events that are "happening now" is frame-relative.

It does not establish: that there is no fact about the temporal order of events in general. For *timelike*-separated events — events close enough in space and far enough apart in time that information could in principle pass from one to the other — temporal order is frame-independent. If event A is in the past light cone of event B (meaning a light signal from A could reach B), then every inertial observer agrees that A happened before B. Only for *spacelike*-separated events — events so far apart in space that no signal could connect them — is temporal order frame-dependent.

This is a crucial qualification. The frame-dependence of simultaneity applies only to events that have no possible causal connection — events that are so far apart in space that light, traveling at the maximum speed, could not have reached one from the other within the available time. For causally connected events, temporal order is absolute.

The deep question this raises for the metaphysics of time is: what is the status of spacelike-separated events? If simultaneity is frame-relative for them, and if "existing now" is defined in terms of simultaneity, then what exists in the present depends on which frame you are in. This is the starting point for the Rietdijk-Putnam argument.

## A Careful Observation

Before moving to Rietdijk and Putnam, it is worth noting a subtlety that is often overlooked. The Einstein train-platform thought experiment, as typically presented, involves light signals as the mechanism for determining simultaneity. One might wonder: is the relativity of simultaneity perhaps just an artifact of using light signals to synchronize clocks? Maybe "true" simultaneity could be defined differently, and light signals just give a distorted picture?

This is the question addressed by Reichenbach's ε-convention (Section 5 of this chapter): within any inertial frame, there is a conventional element in the synchronization of spatially separated clocks, and this convention affects what we call "simultaneous." But even granting this conventionality, the relativity of simultaneity *across frames* is not conventional — it is a physical consequence of the postulates. The philosophical debate about conventionalism in simultaneity concerns what is conventional within a single frame, not whether the Lorentz transformations correctly describe the relationship between frames.
