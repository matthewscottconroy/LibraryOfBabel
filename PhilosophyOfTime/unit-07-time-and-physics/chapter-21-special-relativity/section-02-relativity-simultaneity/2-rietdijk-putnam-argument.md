# The Rietdijk-Putnam Argument for the Block Universe

## The Argument

In the late 1960s, two philosophers independently developed an argument from special relativity to the conclusion that all events — past, present, and future — are equally real. C. W. Rietdijk published "A Rigorous Proof of Determinism Derived from the Special Theory of Relativity" in 1966, and Hilary Putnam published "Time and Physical Geometry" in 1967. The argument, despite their differences in emphasis, is essentially the same, and it has been one of the most discussed arguments in the philosophy of time ever since.

The argument can be stated as follows:

1. Let event E₁ be the event of you reading this sentence — your "here, now."

2. Let event E₂ be some distant event that is simultaneous with E₁ in *your* reference frame — say, something happening on a planet a hundred light-years away, right now.

3. Now consider another observer, moving relative to you at high speed past the same "here." Call this observer the Traveler.

4. Because of the relativity of simultaneity, E₂ may be in the Traveler's *past* — not simultaneous with your E₁, but already occurred, from the Traveler's perspective.

5. Putnam's key premise: if something is "real" at all, it must be real from *every* inertial reference frame. (He calls this the requirement that "what is real is independent of what frame we use to describe it.")

6. Therefore: since E₂ is in the Traveler's past, E₂ is "real" from the Traveler's perspective — it has already happened, is part of the fixed past. By premise 5, E₂ is therefore real from every perspective, including yours.

7. But by the same argument applied to a Traveler moving in the other direction, E₂ might be in the *future* for some observer — not yet happened.

8. If E₂ is real regardless of whether it is in someone's past or future, then the present/past/future distinction does not correspond to any real difference in the ontological status of events.

9. Conclusion: all events are equally real, whether they are in our past, present, or future. The universe is a four-dimensional block in which all events exist, and temporal distinctions are merely perspectival.

## The Argument's Power

The Rietdijk-Putnam argument is genuinely powerful. Its premises are: (a) the empirically confirmed relativity of simultaneity from special relativity, and (b) a plausible-sounding principle about the frame-independence of reality. If you accept both, the block universe follows.

The argument is not obviously fallacious. It is not a simple confusion between coordinate time and proper time, nor a misunderstanding of special relativity. It is a principled attempt to draw metaphysical conclusions from physical theory, and the conclusions are consequential: if correct, the argument shows that presentism — the view that only the present moment is real — is false, and that the block universe is the correct metaphysics of time.

## Response 1: Stein's Argument

Howard Stein (1968, 1991) offered a sophisticated response that accepts the framework of special relativity but resists the block universe conclusion. Stein argues that the Rietdijk-Putnam argument misidentifies what "real" or "determinate" should mean in special relativity.

Stein's proposal: the correct relativistic notion of "what is real for an observer at spacetime point P" is not what is simultaneous with P in some inertial frame, but what is in the *past light cone* of P — the set of all events from which a causal signal could in principle reach P. This set is fully covariant: every observer at P agrees on what is in P's past light cone, because whether an interval is timelike or spacelike is frame-independent.

On Stein's view, "real" for an observer at P means "in or on P's past light cone." The future is genuinely open and not yet real; the past is fixed and real; but "the past" is defined causally, not by some arbitrary choice of simultaneity surface.

Stein's proposal is technically elegant and avoids the frame-dependence that creates the Rietdijk-Putnam problem. But critics have objected that it is ontologically strange: on Stein's view, two observers at the same spacetime point but moving at different velocities have the same ontological reality (same past light cone), while two observers at the same spacetime point with the same velocity but at different locations may have different realities. The view is coherent but departs significantly from our ordinary notion of "what exists now."

## Response 2: Neo-Lorentzian Strategy

A second response is to resist the relativity principle itself — not to deny the experimental results of special relativity, but to insist that there is a *metaphysically preferred* frame (like Newton's absolute space), which happens to be undetectable by any physical experiment. This is the neo-Lorentzian strategy, associated with various philosophers including William Lane Craig and, in a more sophisticated form, Tim Maudlin.

The idea is that special relativity is empirically correct but metaphysically incomplete: the Lorentz transformations correctly describe how measurements transform between frames, but there is nevertheless a fact of the matter about which frame is "truly at rest." In this preferred frame, simultaneity is absolute. Events that are simultaneous in the preferred frame are genuinely simultaneous.

The philosophical appeal is that it preserves absolutism about simultaneity and hence makes presentism coherent. The cost is enormous: you posit a structure (the preferred frame) that has no observable effects and that nothing in our physics requires. This seems to violate the empiricist parsimony that motivates good philosophy of science.

## Response 3: Quantum Gravity Escape

A third response, associated with Dean Zimmerman (2008) and others, looks to future physics — specifically quantum gravity — for an escape route. The argument is: special relativity may not be the final word on spacetime structure. Various approaches to quantum gravity (particularly canonical quantum gravity and loop quantum gravity) involve a preferred foliation of spacetime into spacelike hypersurfaces. If quantum gravity does require such a structure, then at the fundamental level, there may be an objective present after all — just one that is not accessible at the level of special relativity.

This is a conditional defense of presentism: *if* quantum gravity requires a preferred foliation, *then* the physical barriers to presentism may dissolve. The defense is speculative, but it identifies a genuine logical gap in the Rietdijk-Putnam argument: special relativity may not be a complete theory of spacetime structure.

## Philosophical Assessment

The Rietdijk-Putnam argument places serious pressure on presentism. The neo-Lorentzian strategy preserves presentism at the cost of undetectable metaphysical structure; Stein's proposal offers a frame-independent notion of the past but changes the meaning of "real"; the quantum gravity escape is speculative. None of these responses is clearly satisfactory, which is why the argument continues to be debated.

The most honest assessment may be this: special relativity, taken at face value, strongly suggests the block universe. But "taken at face value" involves philosophical interpretive choices — about the status of the simultaneity convention, about how to interpret frame-relative properties, about whether special relativity is a complete description of temporal structure. There is room for defensible dissent from the block universe conclusion, but that dissent requires accepting non-trivial philosophical costs.

What is clear is that the pre-relativistic picture of an absolute present, shared by all observers and metaphysically privileged, cannot survive special relativity intact.
