# Time Dilation

## The Formula

From the Lorentz transformations, the time dilation formula follows directly. If a clock is at rest in reference frame S' moving at velocity *v* relative to frame S, and if it registers a time interval *Δt'* between two ticks, then the time interval *Δt* between the same two ticks as measured in frame S is:

*Δt = γ Δt'*

where *γ = 1/√(1 - v²/c²)* is the Lorentz factor. Since *γ ≥ 1* (with equality only when *v = 0*), we have *Δt ≥ Δt'*: the time interval measured in the frame where the clock is moving is longer than the time interval measured by the clock itself. The moving clock appears to run *slow* relative to the stationary frame.

The time *Δt'* registered by a clock that is carried along with a physical system — what the clock in motion actually measures — is called the *proper time* of that system. Proper time is invariant: every observer agrees on how much proper time a clock accumulates along a given worldline, even if they disagree on the coordinate time intervals. This makes proper time a more fundamental temporal concept than coordinate time.

At ordinary speeds, *γ* is so close to 1 that time dilation is completely negligible. For an airplane traveling at 900 km/h, *γ - 1* is approximately 3 × 10⁻¹³: a second is lengthened by three-tenths of a picosecond. For a particle at 90% of the speed of light, *γ ≈ 2.3*: a proper second corresponds to 2.3 coordinate seconds in the lab frame. At 99.9% of the speed of light, *γ ≈ 22*: a second becomes twenty-two seconds. Near *c*, time dilation can be extreme.

## Is Time Dilation "Real"?

A natural worry: is this genuine slowing of time, or is it merely an artifact of how we define time intervals in relativity?

The answer is that time dilation is unambiguously real in any physically meaningful sense. The proper time accumulated by a clock along a given worldline is an invariant — agreed upon by all observers. If a twin travels at high speed and returns to find they are younger than their stay-at-home sibling, that age difference is real and measurable: we can count the twins' heartbeats, observe the changes in their cells, check the reading on any watch they carry. The traveling twin has experienced less elapsed time, by any physical measure.

What is not "real" in the sense of being frame-independent is the *rate* at which a moving clock runs, as measured from a given reference frame. From the stay-at-home twin's frame, the traveling twin's clock runs slow. From the traveling twin's frame (while in inertial motion), the stay-at-home twin's clock runs slow. Both descriptions are correct within their respective frames. The invariant fact is the proper time accumulated along each worldline, which depends on the worldline's geometry through spacetime.

## Experimental Evidence

The reality of time dilation is not theoretical speculation. It has been directly confirmed by multiple independent experiments.

**Muon lifetime extension.** Muons are unstable subatomic particles produced when cosmic rays strike the upper atmosphere, roughly 10–15 km above the Earth's surface. Muons decay with a proper lifetime of approximately 2.2 microseconds — in the rest frame of a muon, it survives an average of about 2.2 μs before decaying. Traveling at roughly 0.98*c*, a muon should travel only about 0.65 km before decaying (at the non-relativistic rate), and virtually none should reach the Earth's surface. In fact, a substantial flux of muons is detected at sea level. The explanation: from the Earth's frame, the muons' internal "clocks" (the process governing their decay) are running slow by a factor of *γ ≈ 5*. Their effective lifetime in the lab frame is about 11 μs, long enough to travel the 10–15 km to the surface. This was confirmed in careful experiments by Rossi and Hall (1941) and later by Frisch and Smith (1963).

**Hafele-Keating experiment.** In 1971, J. C. Hafele and R. E. Keating flew cesium-beam atomic clocks on commercial airliners around the world in both directions, then compared the flying clocks with reference clocks left on the ground (Hafele and Keating 1972a, 1972b). The experiment tested *both* special relativistic time dilation (due to the aircraft's speed) and gravitational time dilation (due to the aircraft's altitude — general relativistic effect). The results agreed with the theoretical predictions to within the experimental uncertainties. Moving clocks really do run slow.

**GPS satellites.** The Global Positioning System provides an important practical application. GPS satellites orbit at about 20,200 km altitude and move at approximately 14,000 km/h relative to Earth's surface. They experience both special relativistic time dilation (slowing their clocks by about 7 microseconds/day due to speed) and gravitational time dilation (speeding their clocks up by about 45 microseconds/day due to altitude, a general relativistic effect). The net effect is a clock gain of about 38 microseconds per day. If this were not corrected, GPS position errors would accumulate at roughly 10 km per day — rendering the system useless. GPS is a practical technology that depends on special and general relativistic time dilation for its accuracy.

**Atomic clock comparisons.** Increasingly precise atomic clock experiments have confirmed time dilation at lower speeds. In 2010, Chou et al. used aluminum-ion optical clocks to measure relativistic time dilation at speeds as low as 10 m/s — speed achievable by a walking person (Chou et al. 2010). Time dilation is not exotic; it is occurring around you at every moment.

## Proper Time as the Fundamental Temporal Concept

These experimental confirmations converge on a conceptual point: the physically fundamental quantity is *proper time* — the time recorded by a clock that travels along a given worldline through spacetime. Coordinate time is a useful bookkeeping device, but proper time is what is physically real and invariant.

This has implications for the metaphysics of time. The proper time elapsed along a worldline depends on the worldline's shape through spacetime — on how much the worldline deviates from a geodesic (the relativistic equivalent of a straight line). Worldlines that "take the long way around" through spacetime, deviating from geodesics, accumulate *less* proper time than worldlines that follow geodesics. (This is analogous to the fact that the straight line is the *shortest* distance between two points in space; in spacetime, the geodesic is the *longest* proper time between two timelike-separated events.)

This means that the age of a physical system — measured in proper time — is not a question about coordinate time at all. It is a geometric question about the shape of the system's worldline through spacetime. Time, in this framework, has become genuinely geometric.
