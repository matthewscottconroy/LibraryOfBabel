# Conventionalism Revisited: Reichenbach's ε-Convention

## The Synchronization Problem

Within a single inertial reference frame, consider two spatially separated clocks — clock A at location *x_A* and clock B at location *x_B*. How do we synchronize them? How do we ensure that clock A reads "noon" at the same time as clock B reads "noon"?

Einstein's prescription, used in his 1905 paper, is this: send a light signal from A to B at time *t₁* (as read on A's clock), let it be reflected immediately at B, and let it return to A at time *t₂*. Then set B's clock so that when the reflection occurred, it read the time *t₁ + (t₂ - t₁)/2* — the midpoint between the emission and return times. This procedure assumes that the speed of light is the same in both directions: from A to B, and from B to A.

This is Einstein's convention for clock synchronization. It defines what "simultaneous" means within the frame, and it is the convention implicit in the Lorentz transformation.

## Reichenbach's Challenge

Hans Reichenbach, in *The Philosophy of Space and Time* (1928), pointed out that Einstein's synchronization convention involves a choice that is not forced by the physics. Reichenbach proposed a more general synchronization procedure: rather than setting B's clock to *t₁ + (t₂ - t₁)/2*, set it to *t₁ + ε(t₂ - t₁)* for any *ε* with *0 < ε < 1*. The parameter ε encodes an assumption about the one-way speed of light: if ε = 1/2 (Einstein's choice), we assume the speed of light is the same in both directions; if ε ≠ 1/2, we are assuming the light travels faster in one direction than the other.

The key observation: *the two-way speed of light* (the round-trip average) is measurable and is always *c*. But *the one-way speed of light* depends on the synchronization convention: it is *c* in both directions only if we choose ε = 1/2. For any other choice of ε, the one-way speed in one direction is greater than *c* and in the other direction is less than *c*, but in such a way that the round-trip average is always *c*.

Reichenbach argued that the one-way speed of light — and hence simultaneity within a reference frame — is a matter of convention. There is no experiment that can determine the one-way speed of light independently of a synchronization convention. Therefore, different choices of ε give empirically equivalent but conceptually different accounts of simultaneity.

## Implications for Metaphysics

If simultaneity within a frame is partly conventional, what are the implications for metaphysical claims about simultaneous existence?

The most obvious implication is deflationary: if "simultaneous" is partly a conventional label rather than a description of an objective feature of the world, then claims like "event A exists at the same time as event B" may be partly conventions as well. The metaphysician who wants to make substantive claims about which events exist at the same time needs to be careful that they are not just reporting a convention.

However, it is important not to overstate the conventionality. Several points bear emphasis:

First, the ε-conventionality concerns the synchronization of clocks *within a single frame*. It does not affect the Lorentz transformation between frames: the relativity of simultaneity *across* frames is not a matter of convention. Whatever choice of ε we make within each frame, we will still find that observers in relative motion disagree about the temporal ordering of spacelike-separated events.

Second, the conventional element identified by Reichenbach is genuinely thin. The choice of ε affects the *labeling* of events with time coordinates, but it does not affect any observable quantity. It is analogous to choosing between different coordinate systems in ordinary geometry — a matter of labeling, not of physics. The physical predictions (measured time intervals between events that can be compared locally, proper times along worldlines) are ε-independent.

Third, and most importantly, many philosophers have argued that the ε-conventionalism does not undermine the core metaphysical content of special relativity. Even if there is a conventional element in how we assign time labels, the fact remains that no frame-independent, ε-independent fact distinguishes "the present" from "the future" for spacelike-separated events. The conventionality affects the labels, not the underlying causal structure — and it is the causal structure that grounds the deepest metaphysical questions about temporal order.

## Malament's Result

An important constraint on the significance of the ε-convention was established by David Malament (1977). Malament proved that if we require a synchronization relation to be (1) definable from the causal structure of Minkowski spacetime (lightlike relations) and (2) non-trivial (not trivially collapsing everything onto the same simultaneity class), then Einstein's standard synchrony (ε = 1/2) is the *unique* synchronization relation satisfying these conditions.

Malament's result suggests that the standard simultaneity is not merely a convenient convention among many equally good ones, but the only natural choice that is fully grounded in the causal structure of spacetime. If we want our notion of simultaneity to be derived from something physically fundamental — the causal structure — rather than arbitrarily stipulated, we are committed to Einstein synchrony.

The debate over the significance of Malament's result continues, but it provides a strong reason to think that the ε-convention, while technically available, is not philosophically on a par with Einstein synchrony.

## Summary

The conventional element in simultaneity within a reference frame is real but limited. It does not undermine the relativity of simultaneity across frames, which is a genuine physical result. It does not make the block universe optional or presentism easily defensible. What it does do is remind us that some of the structure we assign to spacetime — including the specific time labels we assign to events — involves conventional choices, and that we should be careful not to draw strong metaphysical conclusions from features of the physics that are artifacts of convention rather than reflections of objective structure.
