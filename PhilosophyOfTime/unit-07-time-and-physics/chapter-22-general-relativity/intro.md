# Chapter 22: General Relativity

Ten years after special relativity, Einstein completed his general theory of relativity — a work of extraordinary intellectual ambition that extended the relativity principle to accelerating observers and, in doing so, reimagined the nature of gravity, space, and time altogether. Where special relativity had shown that time is frame-relative and that space and time must be unified into spacetime, general relativity went further: it made spacetime itself a dynamic participant in physics, curved and warped by the presence of mass and energy.

The consequences for our understanding of time are profound. In Newtonian mechanics, time was an absolute, uniform background. In special relativity, time was still a fixed structure — Minkowski spacetime is flat and unchanging. In general relativity, the structure of spacetime — including the rate at which time passes — varies from place to place and changes over time. Near a massive object like the Earth, time runs slightly slower than far from it. Near a black hole, this effect becomes extreme: at the event horizon, time (from the perspective of a distant observer) appears to stop entirely. And in the early universe, the geometry of spacetime was very different from what it is today.

This chapter explores the major implications of general relativity for the philosophy of time. We begin with the equivalence principle, Einstein's crucial insight that freely falling frames are locally inertial, and its implication of gravitational time dilation. We then examine singularity theorems — the Penrose and Hawking results that show, under general conditions, GR predicts the existence of spacetime singularities, including the Big Bang. This raises the question of whether time has a beginning.

The chapter then turns to the exotic structures permitted by general relativity: closed timelike curves, which would allow time travel to the past, and the Gödel universe, which Einstein's own friend Kurt Gödel constructed as a philosophical argument about the nature of time. We examine the interior of black holes, where the roles of space and time in some sense exchange, and close with the Hole Argument of Earman and Norton — a deep puzzle about the nature of spacetime that has driven some of the most sophisticated recent work in the philosophy of physics.
