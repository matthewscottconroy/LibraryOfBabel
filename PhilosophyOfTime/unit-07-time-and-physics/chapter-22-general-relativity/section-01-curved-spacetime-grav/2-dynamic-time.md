# Dynamic Time: Spacetime as a Physical Field

## Spacetime as a Participant

In Newtonian mechanics, spacetime is the stage on which physics plays out: fixed, absolute, indifferent to the drama of matter and motion. In special relativity, the Minkowski spacetime structure is still fixed — flat, uniform, unchanging — even though temporal and spatial measurements become frame-relative. Einstein called the fixed Minkowski background of special relativity a "rigid scaffolding" that participated in all physical processes without being affected by them, and he found this philosophically unsatisfying.

General relativity dissolves the scaffolding. The key equation of the theory, Einstein's field equation, is:

*G_μν = (8πG/c⁴) T_μν*

On the left side is the Einstein tensor *G_μν*, which encodes the curvature of spacetime — specifically, how the geometry of spacetime departs from flatness at each point. On the right side is the stress-energy tensor *T_μν*, which encodes the distribution of matter, energy, momentum, and stress at each point. The equation states that the curvature of spacetime is determined by the distribution of matter and energy, and conversely, the distribution of matter and energy determines the curvature of spacetime.

The famous summary, due to John Wheeler, captures the mutual determination: "Spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve."

## Time as Dynamical

The implication for time is direct and radical. The spacetime metric *g_μν* — the object that encodes how distances and time intervals are measured at each point of spacetime — is not fixed and given in advance. It is a dynamical field, determined by and evolving with the distribution of matter and energy. This means:

1. **The rate of time passage varies from place to place.** As we saw in the equivalence principle discussion, clocks in strong gravitational fields run slower than clocks in weak fields. More precisely: the component of the metric that governs time intervals (the g₀₀ component) varies with position, and this variation is the gravitational time dilation.

2. **The structure of time can change over time.** As matter moves and redistributes, the spacetime curvature changes, and with it the temporal structure. In the early universe, the geometry of spacetime was very different from today's: the extreme density of matter produced extreme curvature, and the rate of proper time accumulated during those early epochs was different from what we experience now.

3. **Spacetime can be dynamically interesting.** It can expand, contract, develop singularities, form horizons, and exhibit exotic structures like closed timelike curves. These are not fixed features of a background geometry but emergent properties of a dynamical field.

## Cosmological Time and the Expanding Universe

One of the most striking implications of general relativity is that the universe itself has a temporal history. The Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric, which describes a homogeneous, isotropic universe (our best approximation to the large-scale structure of the cosmos), introduces a scale factor *a(t)* that describes how the spatial dimensions of the universe change over time. The Friedmann equations, derived from Einstein's field equations for an FLRW spacetime, tell us how *a(t)* evolves depending on the density and pressure of the matter and energy in the universe.

The observational evidence that the universe is expanding — first systematically documented by Edwin Hubble in 1929 — combined with the Friedmann equations, implies that the universe was smaller and hotter in the past. Running the equations backward in time leads to the conclusion that the universe originated in an extremely dense, hot state approximately 13.8 billion years ago: the Big Bang.

In general relativistic cosmology, "how old is the universe?" has a precise answer: it is the proper time elapsed along a geodesic from the Big Bang (or as close to the Big Bang as classical GR applies) to the present. This is "cosmological time" — not an absolute external time parameter but a physical observable determined by the geometry of the universe.

The expansion of the universe also means that energy is not conserved globally (as noted in Chapter 20 in connection with Noether's theorem): the expanding background breaks time-translation symmetry, and there is no conserved global energy. This is not a pathology of the theory but a consistent consequence of taking time seriously as a dynamical participant in physics.

## The End of the Fixed Background

The transition from Newtonian absolute time → special relativistic flat Minkowski time → general relativistic dynamic spacetime represents a progressive deepening of the integration of time into the physical world. Each step makes time more thoroughly physical and less like a divine or mathematical abstraction.

In Newton, time is present without matter — a feature of God's omnipresence. In Minkowski, time is part of a unified spacetime structure, but still fixed and independent of matter. In Einstein, time is a dynamical field: it is shaped by matter, it curves, it can begin (the Big Bang), it may end (in a Big Crunch or heat death), and in extreme cases it develops singularities where the classical description breaks down entirely.

This progressive dynamicization of time has a philosophical lesson. Each step reveals that what appeared to be a necessary feature of time — its uniformity, its flatness, its independence of matter — was in fact a contingent feature of the physics available at that level of description. General relativity invites us to ask: are there further features of time that appear necessary but may turn out to be contingent as we develop even deeper theories? The problem of time in quantum gravity, which we examine in Chapter 25, suggests the answer may be yes.
