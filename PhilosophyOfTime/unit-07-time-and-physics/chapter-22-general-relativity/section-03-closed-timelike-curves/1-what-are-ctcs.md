# What Are Closed Timelike Curves?

## The Basic Concept

A worldline, recall, is the path traced by a physical object through spacetime — the set of all events at which the object is present. For a massive object, the worldline must everywhere be timelike: the object always moves slower than light, staying inside its local light cone. In ordinary Minkowski spacetime or in the spacetime of our actual universe, worldlines extend from past to future without looping back on themselves. The temporal order along any worldline is strictly increasing: as the object moves along its worldline, its proper time always increases, and it never returns to an earlier event.

A *closed timelike curve* (CTC) is a worldline that is timelike everywhere but also closed: it forms a loop, returning to its starting event. A traveler along a CTC would accumulate proper time (aging normally as they traveled) but would eventually arrive back at the same spacetime event they started from — the same location, the same time. In effect, they would have traveled into their own past.

What would this mean? The traveler would encounter themselves at a younger age. They could, in principle, interact with their past self. The causal structure of spacetime, which in ordinary spacetimes ensures that causes precede effects, would be fundamentally disrupted: events on a CTC are their own causal ancestors. The distinction between past and future becomes, globally, circular.

## Spacetimes with CTCs

General relativity, as a mathematical theory, does not prohibit CTCs. They appear in several known solutions to Einstein's field equations:

**Gödel metric (1949):** Kurt Gödel, the logician, found an exact solution to Einstein's field equations describing a rotating universe filled with dust. In this spacetime, CTCs pass through every point — every event in the universe is located on a CTC. We discuss this solution in detail in the next subsection.

**Kerr metric (1963):** Roy Kerr found the exact solution for a rotating (axially symmetric) black hole. Inside the Kerr black hole, beyond the inner (Cauchy) horizon, CTCs appear. These CTCs are confined to the interior of the black hole and are unreachable by external observers, but they are genuine solutions to the field equations.

**Tipler cylinders (1974):** Frank Tipler showed that an infinite rotating cylinder of sufficiently dense matter, if rotated fast enough, would produce CTCs outside the cylinder (Tipler 1974). While infinite cylinders are unphysical, the example demonstrates that CTC geometries can be supported by seemingly ordinary matter sources.

**Morris-Thorne wormholes (1988):** Morris and Thorne (1988) proposed that traversable wormholes — tunnels connecting distant regions of spacetime — could in principle be converted into time machines by accelerating one mouth relative to the other, using special relativistic time dilation to create a time offset between the two ends. A traveler entering one mouth would emerge from the other at a different time.

## The Causal and Philosophical Implications

If CTCs existed and were traversable, the implications for causation, agency, and the nature of time would be profound.

**Causation.** In ordinary spacetime, causation is asymmetric: causes precede effects. On a CTC, an event can be both a cause and an effect of itself. Consider the "grandfather paradox": a traveler goes back in time and kills their own grandfather before the traveler was born. If the grandfather is dead, the traveler was never born, so the traveler could not have killed the grandfather, so the grandfather survived, so the traveler was born... Inconsistency threatens.

**Consistency constraints.** One response is to require that CTC spacetimes be self-consistent: only those situations in which the traveler's actions are compatible with the history of the spacetime are physically permitted. David Deutsch (1991) and others have argued that quantum mechanics, applied to CTC spacetimes, automatically selects consistent histories. On this view, the grandfather paradox is ruled out not by a physical prohibition but by a consistency requirement: the only states that occur on CTCs are those that are self-consistent.

**The Novikov self-consistency principle:** Formulated by Igor Novikov, this principle states that if an event influences a past event, it must do so consistently: any action taken by a time traveler must be part of the history that led to the time traveler being there in the first place. There are no contradictions, only fixed points of causal consistency.

**Temporal topology.** CTCs would change the topology of time from linear (or branching) to cyclic. Rather than time running from past to future along a line, it would form loops. This would make temporal topology locally cyclic — a possibility that was familiar in ancient philosophical traditions (eternal recurrence, Stoic cycles) but was thought to be empirically ruled out by the directedness of thermodynamic processes.

## Hawking's Chronology Protection Conjecture

The existence of CTC solutions to GR's classical equations does not settle the question of whether CTCs can actually form or exist in our universe. Stephen Hawking proposed the *Chronology Protection Conjecture* (1992): the laws of physics (including quantum effects) conspire to prevent the formation of CTCs, even in situations where classical GR would permit them.

Hawking's physical argument involves the behavior of quantum vacuum fluctuations near a would-be CTC. As the CTC is approached, quantum field theory predicts that the energy density of the vacuum diverges, producing a back-reaction on the spacetime geometry that would prevent the CTC from forming. The conjecture has not been proved in full generality, but it has been supported by calculations in specific models.

Whether the Chronology Protection Conjecture is correct is an open question in quantum gravity. Its resolution would require a complete quantum theory of gravity. For our purposes, it illustrates an important pattern: classical GR is permissive about temporal topology, but quantum effects may impose restrictions. The question of whether time can be globally cyclic is one that rests with quantum gravity, not with classical relativity alone.
