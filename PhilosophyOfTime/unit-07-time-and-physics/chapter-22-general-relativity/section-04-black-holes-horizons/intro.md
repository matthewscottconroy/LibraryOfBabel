# Section 4: Black Holes and Event Horizons

Black holes are among the most extreme objects predicted by general relativity. They are regions of spacetime from which nothing — not light, not matter, not any physical signal — can escape. Their existence has moved from theoretical prediction to observational confirmation: the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration published the first image of a black hole (M87*) in 2019, and gravitational wave observatories have detected dozens of black hole mergers since 2015.

For the philosophy of time, black holes are not merely fascinating astrophysical objects but conceptual laboratories in which the temporal structure of spacetime is stretched to its extremes. The event horizon is a causal boundary that separates a region of spacetime from which signals can reach external observers from a region from which they cannot. Inside a Schwarzschild (non-rotating) black hole, the geometry does something extraordinary: the r-coordinate, normally associated with spatial distance from the center, becomes timelike. In a precise geometrical sense, an infalling observer cannot avoid moving toward the singularity any more than they can avoid moving into the future. This section explores what this means.
