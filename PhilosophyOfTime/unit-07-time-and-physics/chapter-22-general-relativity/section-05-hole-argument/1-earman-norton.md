# The Hole Argument: Earman and Norton

## Background: The Gauge Freedom of General Relativity

General relativity is formulated on a smooth four-dimensional manifold — a mathematical space with local coordinate systems. Physical fields (the metric *g_μν*, matter fields, etc.) are defined on this manifold. The equations of GR — Einstein's field equations — determine the metric given the matter distribution (and boundary conditions).

General relativity has a feature called *diffeomorphism invariance* (or general covariance): the equations of the theory look the same in any coordinate system, and moreover, if you take a solution (a metric and matter distribution on the manifold) and apply a smooth coordinate transformation, you get another solution. This is mathematically equivalent to the fact that we can relabel spacetime points — "drag" the fields around on the manifold — without changing the physics.

More precisely: if *(M, g, T)* is a model of GR (where *M* is the manifold, *g* is the metric, and *T* is the stress-energy tensor for matter), and *φ: M → M* is a smooth diffeomorphism, then *(M, φ*g, φ*T)* — the model with the metric and matter fields "dragged along" by *φ* — is also a model of GR that satisfies the field equations.

## The Hole Argument Stated

Earman and Norton's version of the Hole Argument proceeds as follows:

1. Choose a region *H* of the spacetime manifold (the "hole") that is bounded in the past (it has a well-defined past boundary) and contains no matter. Everything to the past of the hole's boundary is determined by initial data on a spacelike surface.

2. Consider a diffeomorphism *φ* that is the identity outside *H* but non-trivial inside *H* — it "drags" the fields around within the hole but leaves everything outside unchanged.

3. By diffeomorphism invariance, the "dragged" model *(M, φ*g, φ*T)* satisfies the field equations. Inside *H*, the fields have been rearranged — the value of the metric at a given manifold point *p* is different in the two models.

4. Both models agree on all initial data outside the hole. They have identical physics to the past of *H*. Yet they differ inside *H*: the metric field has a different value at each manifold point.

5. If we are *manifold substantivalists* — if we believe that spacetime points are genuine individuals and that *p* is a real, numerically distinct entity from *φ(p)* — then the two models are *distinct physical situations*: the metric has a different value at the same spacetime point *p* in the two models.

6. But both models agree on all observational data (they agree outside the hole, and any observer inside the hole would measure the same physical quantities — they just would be associated with different manifold points). The two models are *empirically equivalent* but *physically distinct*.

7. Yet which model obtains inside the hole is not determined by the initial data. This is *radical indeterminism*: even given complete knowledge of the past, GR cannot determine which physical situation obtains in the future (inside the hole).

Earman and Norton argued that this radical indeterminism is untenable, and that the culprit is manifold substantivalism — the view that spacetime points are real individual entities with their own haecceity (primitive identity). If we abandon manifold substantivalism, the two models represent the same physical situation, and there is no indeterminism.

## What Is at Stake: Substantivalism and Its Variants

The Hole Argument poses a dilemma for substantivalism. Substantivalism about spacetime is the view that spacetime is a genuine entity — not merely a system of relations among material objects, but an entity in its own right. The simplest form of substantivalism, *manifold substantivalism*, holds that the points of the spacetime manifold are genuine individuals with primitive identities.

The Hole Argument shows that manifold substantivalism, combined with diffeomorphism invariance, implies radical indeterminism. This is an uncomfortable position: general relativity is often regarded as a paradigmatically deterministic classical theory (given the initial data and the equations, the future should be determined). Earman and Norton concluded that manifold substantivalism should be rejected.

The alternatives:

**Relationism.** Reject substantivalism entirely: spacetime is just the system of spatiotemporal relations among material events. There are no spacetime points beyond the structure of the relations. The two "models" in the Hole Argument are not genuinely distinct, because they represent the same pattern of relations among the same physical events.

**Anti-haecceitism.** Accept that spacetime points exist, but deny that they have primitive identities (haecceities). On this view, two models that are qualitatively isomorphic (having the same pattern of metric values) represent the same physical situation, regardless of which manifold point has which value. The indeterminism is merely apparent: there is only one possible future, but multiple mathematical representations of it.

**Sophisticated substantivalism.** Defend a form of substantivalism according to which the identity conditions for spacetime points are given by their metrical properties — their structural role in the spacetime manifold — rather than by primitive haecceities. On this view, what it means to be the spacetime point *p* is to have certain metrical relationships with other points, and two models that differ only by a "dragging" of the metric are not genuinely distinct.

**Structural realism.** Hold that what is real is the *structure* of spacetime — the network of relations among spacetime points — rather than the individual points themselves. This is the position associated with Ladyman, French, and others in the structural realism debate. It avoids the Hole Argument by denying that spacetime points are the fundamental ontological individuals.

## Implications for Time

The Hole Argument is relevant to the philosophy of time in several ways.

First, it shows that the identity of spacetime points — and hence the identity of temporal moments — is not a primitive given but is determined (if at all) by the structural relations encoded in the metric. "This spacetime point" is not a label that attaches to a primitive individual; it is a place in a structure. This means that questions like "is this moment the same moment as that one?" are questions about structural role, not about primitive identity.

Second, the argument reveals that the temporal structure of spacetime — encoded in the metric — is not merely a representation of an independently given set of temporal facts. The temporal structure *is* the facts, insofar as there are any. There are no temporal facts over and above what is encoded in the spacetime geometry.

Third, the debate over responses to the Hole Argument has shaped contemporary discussions of structural realism and the ontology of physics in ways that directly bear on questions about the reality of time. If the fundamental ontology of general relativity is structural — a web of geometrical relations without primitive individuals — then time, as part of that structure, is real in the sense of being part of the objective structure of the world, without being a sequence of numerically distinct temporal instants each with its own primitive haecceity.
