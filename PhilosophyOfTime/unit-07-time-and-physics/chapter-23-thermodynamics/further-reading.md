## Annotated Entries

**Albert, David Z. 2000. *Time and Chance*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press.**
The most important philosophical book on the foundations of statistical mechanics and the arrow of time in a generation. Albert develops the Past Hypothesis and Statistical Postulate with precision and argues that they provide the correct foundation for statistical mechanics and ground all the time-asymmetric phenomena of the everyday world. Philosophically sophisticated but accessible to anyone with introductory physics and philosophy. Essential reading.

**Price, Huw. 1996. *Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point*. New York: Oxford University Press.**
A philosophically rigorous analysis of the arrow of time from the perspective of a consistent, temporally neutral viewpoint. Price argues that much confusion about the arrow of time results from adopting a temporally biased perspective, and he develops "the Archimedean standpoint" as a corrective. His treatment of the relationship between the thermodynamic arrow, the causal arrow, and the epistemic arrow is particularly illuminating.

**Sklar, Lawrence. 1993. *Physics and Chance*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.**
A comprehensive and balanced treatment of the philosophical foundations of statistical mechanics. Sklar examines the relationships between the various formulations of the Second Law, the role of probability in statistical mechanics, the Boltzmann equation and its justification, and the philosophical significance of the Past Hypothesis. Written for philosophers of science with some physics background.

**Carroll, Sean M. 2010. *From Eternity to Here*. New York: Dutton.**
An accessible and engaging popular account of the arrow of time, entropy, and cosmology. Carroll covers the thermodynamic arrow, the Past Hypothesis, the cosmological context, and his own eternal cosmology proposal. Clear on the physics and honest about the philosophical difficulties. Recommended for readers who want a broader view than what academic papers alone provide.

**Penrose, Roger. 1989. *The Emperor's New Mind*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
Penrose's wide-ranging meditation on physics, computation, and consciousness, which contains his development of the Weyl curvature hypothesis and his estimate of the improbability of the Big Bang initial state. The thermodynamic sections (chapters on entropy and time's arrow) are among the most original and provocative in the popular physics literature.

**Penrose, Roger. 2010. *Cycles of Time*. London: Bodley Head.**
Penrose's account of his Conformal Cyclic Cosmology, presented in accessible but rigorous terms. Even if one disagrees with the conclusions, the book provides a superb introduction to the entropy puzzle and the cosmological context of the arrow of time.

**Landauer, Rolf. 1961. "Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing Process."**
The original paper establishing Landauer's principle. Remarkably prescient in connecting information theory to thermodynamics decades before the connection became mainstream. Technical but readable; only a few pages long.

**Bennett, Charles H. 1982. "The Thermodynamics of Computation."**
Bennett's review article that systematically developed the implications of Landauer's principle for computing, including the possibility of reversible computing. Connects information erasure, thermodynamics, and entropy in a rigorous and comprehensive way. Essential reading on the physics of information.

**Uffink, Jos. 2007. "Compendium of the Foundations of Classical Statistical Physics."**
The most comprehensive and philosophically careful scholarly treatment of the foundations of statistical mechanics. Covers the history, the various formulations of the Second Law, Boltzmann's H-theorem and its problems, the role of the ergodic hypothesis, and the relationship to probability theory. An indispensable reference for serious study.

**Brown, Harvey R., Wayne Myrvold, and Jos Uffink. 2009. "Boltzmann's H-Theorem, Its Discontents, and the Birth of Statistical Mechanics."**
An excellent historical and philosophical analysis of the H-theorem and the debates it provoked. Shows clearly how the Stosszahlansatz introduces time asymmetry and how Boltzmann's later work addressed the problem. Accessible to readers with basic physics and philosophy background.
