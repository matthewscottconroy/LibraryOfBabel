# Chapter 23: Thermodynamics and the Arrow of Time

Of all the asymmetries between past and future that we experience, the thermodynamic arrow is the most pervasive and most deeply woven into the fabric of ordinary life. Eggs break but do not reassemble. Sugar dissolves in coffee but does not spontaneously re-crystallize. People age forward but not backward. Stars burn hydrogen but do not un-burn it. In every direction we look, macroscopic processes unfold in one temporal direction, and the reverse processes — while physically possible according to the laws of microphysics — are never observed.

The Second Law of Thermodynamics is the physical expression of this asymmetry: in a closed system, entropy (a measure of disorder, or more precisely, a measure of the number of microscopic configurations compatible with a given macroscopic state) never decreases, and in practice tends to increase. This law is one of the most robustly confirmed in all of physics. But it also raises one of the deepest puzzles in science: the fundamental microphysical laws — Newton's equations, quantum mechanics, and to a very good approximation even relativistic field theory — are (very nearly) time-symmetric. How can macroscopic irreversibility emerge from microscopically reversible laws?

This chapter examines the thermodynamic arrow of time from the ground up. We begin with the classical formulations of the Second Law by Clausius and Kelvin, and the statistical mechanical interpretation that Boltzmann developed in the 1870s. We then examine Boltzmann's H-theorem and the paradoxes it raises, the phase-space framework that makes entropy a geometric quantity, and David Albert's influential formulation of the Past Hypothesis as the ground of the thermodynamic arrow.

The chapter continues with Roger Penrose's cosmological approach to entropy — his Weyl curvature hypothesis and estimate of the extraordinary improbability of the Big Bang initial state — and concludes with Maxwell's demon, Szilard's resolution, and Landauer's principle, which connects information theory to thermodynamics in a way that illuminates what the arrow of time is ultimately about.

Throughout, we attend to the philosophical implications: the relationship between time-symmetric laws and time-asymmetric phenomena, the nature of entropy as a measure of probability, and what it means to say that the universe began in a low-entropy state.
