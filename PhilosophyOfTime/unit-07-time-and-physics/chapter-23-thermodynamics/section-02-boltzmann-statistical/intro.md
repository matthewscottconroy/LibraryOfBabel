# Section 2: Boltzmann and Statistical Mechanics

Ludwig Boltzmann's development of statistical mechanics in the 1870s is one of the greatest achievements in the history of physics. Starting from the atomic hypothesis — that matter is composed of atoms and molecules moving according to the laws of classical mechanics — Boltzmann derived the macroscopic laws of thermodynamics, including the Second Law, from purely mechanical foundations. The result was a deep reduction: thermodynamics was shown to be an emergent consequence of mechanics and probability theory.

But Boltzmann's derivations raised conceptual puzzles that have not been fully resolved to this day. His H-theorem purported to prove that entropy always increases — but, as Loschmidt showed, it must have smuggled in a time-asymmetric assumption. That assumption — the "Stosszahlansatz," or molecular chaos hypothesis — is the place where the arrow of time enters the derivation. Understanding why reveals something deep about the relationship between probability, time, and the initial conditions of the universe.

This section develops Boltzmann's H-theorem and the phase-space framework for entropy, laying the groundwork for the later sections on the Past Hypothesis and Penrose's cosmological approach.
