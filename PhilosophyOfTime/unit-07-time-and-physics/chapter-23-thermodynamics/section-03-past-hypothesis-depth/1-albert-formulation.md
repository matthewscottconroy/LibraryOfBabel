# Albert's Formulation of the Past Hypothesis

## The Problem

Statistical mechanics explains entropy increase from the present onward: given that the universe is currently in a low-to-medium entropy state, and given the measure-theoretic considerations about the vastness of high-entropy regions in phase space, it is overwhelmingly likely that entropy will increase in the immediate future. So far, so good.

But here is the problem: the same reasoning, applied to the past, would say that the present state is also overwhelmingly likely to have evolved from a higher-entropy past. If I see a glass of ice water, the naive statistical mechanical prediction is that it is far more likely that the ice formed spontaneously from warm water just now (a rare fluctuation) than that the ice was put in the glass an hour ago and has been melting ever since. Why? Because the probability of spontaneous ice formation from ordinary warm water, while tiny, is vastly greater than the probability that all the complex correlations between the ice, the glass, my memory of putting ice in, the thermal state of the kitchen, and the record of the ice delivery were all in place an hour ago and have been evolving consistently ever since.

This argument, which Boltzmann was aware of (he called it the "Boltzmann brain" type reasoning in a slightly different form), seems to imply that all our memories are unreliable, all historical records are fabrications, and the most probable explanation for our current state is a spontaneous fluctuation from chaos rather than a long historical evolution from a low-entropy beginning. This is clearly unacceptable — it is a reductio ad absurdum of the naive statistical picture.

## The Past Hypothesis

The resolution, on Albert's formulation (Albert 2000), requires an additional postulate: the *Past Hypothesis* (PH). The Past Hypothesis states:

**The macrostate of the universe near the beginning of the universe was one of very low entropy.**

This is a constraint on the initial conditions of the universe — a brute stipulation that the Big Bang was an extremely low-entropy state. Given the Past Hypothesis, the statistical mechanical reasoning works in the right direction: starting from the low-entropy past, entropy increases toward the future. The present state is explained as having evolved from the low-entropy past, not as a spontaneous fluctuation.

Crucially, the Past Hypothesis is about the *macrostate*, not the exact microstate. It says that whatever microstate the universe was in at the beginning, it was one compatible with a very small macrostate region — a region of very low entropy. The exact microstate within that small region is unspecified.

## The Statistical Postulate

The Past Hypothesis alone is not sufficient. We also need to know how probable different microstates are, given that they lie within the Past Hypothesis macrostate region. Albert adds a *Statistical Postulate* (SP):

**The probability distribution over the microstates of the universe, conditioned on the Past Hypothesis and on the present macrostate, is uniform over the phase space region compatible with both.**

This is the simplest natural probability distribution to assume: given what we know (the Past Hypothesis and the present macrostate), all compatible microstates are equally probable. This is the principle of indifference applied to phase space.

The SP is needed to make probabilistic predictions. With both PH and SP, we can derive:
- Why entropy has been increasing from the past to the present.
- Why we remember the past but not the future.
- Why we find reliable records of the past but not of the future.
- Why the causal arrow (causes precede effects) aligns with the thermodynamic arrow.

Albert's framework is philosophically elegant because it derives a vast range of time-asymmetric phenomena from just two postulates added to the laws of mechanics: PH and SP.

## The PH-SP Foundation for Statistical Mechanics

Albert (following Loewer 2001, 2012) argues that the combination of PH and SP provides the correct foundation for Boltzmann's statistical mechanics. Rather than deriving entropy increase from the Stosszahlansatz (which sneaks in time asymmetry illegitimately), the PH-SP framework explains entropy increase through a principled constraint on initial conditions.

On this view, the Second Law of Thermodynamics is not a fundamental law of nature but an emergent consequence of the conjunction of (a) the time-symmetric laws of mechanics, (b) the Past Hypothesis, and (c) the Statistical Postulate. The time asymmetry of thermodynamics derives entirely from PH: the macrostate of the universe near the beginning was very small.

This is a reductionist program: thermodynamics is reduced to mechanics plus constraints on initial conditions. The reduction is not complete in all technical details (especially for quantum systems), but the conceptual framework is compelling.
