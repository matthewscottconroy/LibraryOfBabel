# Criticisms and Alternatives to the Past Hypothesis

## The Brute Posit Objection

The most common objection to the Past Hypothesis framework is that it is explanatorily shallow: the Past Hypothesis is a brute posit. It says that the universe began in a low-entropy state, but it does not explain *why*. We are told that the Big Bang was extraordinarily special, but we are given no account of what made it special. The Past Hypothesis, on this objection, does not so much explain the arrow of time as relocate the puzzle: instead of asking why entropy increases, we now ask why the universe began in a low-entropy state.

This is a genuine limitation. Albert is forthright about it: the Past Hypothesis is an initial condition, not an explanation. But he argues that all physical explanations must bottom out in something unexplained — either fundamental laws or fundamental initial conditions. The Past Hypothesis is, on his view, the most parsimonious fundamental initial condition that accounts for the thermodynamic arrow.

Whether a brute initial condition is explanatorily acceptable depends on one's views about what counts as a scientific explanation. If explanations must always identify mechanisms or causes, then "the universe just started in a low-entropy state" is unsatisfying. If explanations can be postulates that systematize and unify a wide range of phenomena, then PH is explanatorily valuable — it unifies the arrow of time, the reliability of memory, the asymmetry of records, and the direction of causation in a single framework.

## Carroll and Chen: The Cosmological Response

Sean Carroll and Jennifer Chen (Carroll 2004; Carroll 2010) have argued that the Past Hypothesis, while correct as far as it goes, should be given a cosmological explanation. Their proposal is that the low entropy of the Big Bang can be understood as a consequence of the dynamics of an eternal universe that is always expanding (or a multiverse with a similar structure).

Their key argument involves the arrow of time in de Sitter space (the spacetime of a universe dominated by a cosmological constant, which our universe appears to approximate at late times). In de Sitter space, the maximum entropy state is not a compact hot dense state but a very large, cold, empty universe — a "de Sitter vacuum." If the universe fluctuates around this de Sitter vacuum, it will occasionally produce nucleation events (somewhat like the Big Bang) from which entropy-increasing branches emerge. These branches are what we experience as universes with arrows of time.

On Carroll and Chen's picture, the Past Hypothesis is not a fundamental assumption but a consequence of the structure of eternal cosmology: we find ourselves in an entropy-increasing branch because entropy-increasing branches are the ones that produce observers. This is an anthropic or selection-effect argument: given that we are observers, we must be in a region with an arrow of time, which means a region that originated from a low-entropy state.

Whether this argument is successful is contested. Critics argue that eternal cosmologies face their own version of the Boltzmann brain problem: the most common observers in an eternal universe would be Boltzmann brains — isolated observers that fluctuate into existence with false memories. If there are vastly more Boltzmann brains than ordinary observers in Carroll and Chen's multiverse, then we should expect to be Boltzmann brains, which is absurd. Carroll and Chen have responses to this objection, but the debate is unresolved.

## Loewer's Defense of PH-SP

Barry Loewer (2001, 2012) has developed and defended the Albert framework in sophisticated ways. Loewer argues that the combination of PH and SP is not an ad hoc postulate but the simplest and most principled way to ground statistical mechanics. He connects the framework to the Lewisian account of laws of nature (Lewis 1994): the "best system" account, which holds that the laws of nature are those that best systematize the total history of the universe. On this view, PH and SP are part of the best systematic description of the universe's history, and therefore have the status of laws — not just initial conditions.

Loewer's framework, sometimes called the "Mentaculus" (a complete probability distribution over all possible histories of the universe, derived from PH and SP), also has implications for the nature of causation, probability in statistical mechanics, and the relationship between the microscopic and macroscopic levels of description.

## The Status of the Past Hypothesis

Where does this leave us? The Past Hypothesis is the best currently available answer to the question of why entropy has been increasing for the age of the universe. It is well-motivated (the Big Bang is genuinely a low-entropy state, as confirmed by cosmological observations), explanatorily powerful (it grounds a wide range of time-asymmetric phenomena), and compatible with our best physical theories.

Its limitations are real: it is a brute posit, it does not explain *why* the initial state was low-entropy, and it faces challenges from eternal cosmology and the Boltzmann brain problem. These limitations point toward the need for a physical theory of cosmological initial conditions — something that goes beyond current physics.

The philosophical lesson is this: the arrow of time, which appears so fundamental and pervasive in our experience, is not a consequence of the time-symmetric laws of physics but a consequence of boundary conditions. It is a feature of the *universe we happen to inhabit* — one that began in an extraordinarily special, low-entropy state — rather than of the laws of nature as such. This is a profound, if somewhat unsatisfying, conclusion.
