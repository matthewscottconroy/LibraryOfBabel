# Conformal Cyclic Cosmology

## The Entropy Problem for Eternal Cosmologies

If the Big Bang was so improbable, how did it happen? Penrose's Conformal Cyclic Cosmology (CCC), developed in *Cycles of Time* (2010), attempts to answer this question by proposing a specific physical mechanism that generates low-entropy initial states.

The starting observation is this: in the very far future, our universe will (if the cosmological constant remains positive) evolve toward a de Sitter space — an accelerating expansion in which all matter eventually disperses, all black holes evaporate, and the universe approaches a cold, empty, maximally uniform state. In this ultimate state, the dominant degrees of freedom are conformal (massless particles, gravitons), and the conformal geometry of the spacetime is well-defined even as the overall scale factor goes to infinity.

Penrose's key insight is that the conformal geometry of this remote future looks, in a precise mathematical sense, very similar to the conformal geometry of the Big Bang initial state. Both are characterized by low Weyl curvature and are conformally smooth. The end of one aeon of the universe (its ultimate future) has the same conformal geometry as the beginning of the next aeon (a new Big Bang).

## The CCC Framework

In CCC, the universe is structured as a sequence of "aeons" — cosmological epochs each with its own Big Bang and ultimate future. The future conformal boundary of one aeon is identified (conformally) with the past conformal boundary of the next aeon. The conformal geometry passes continuously from one aeon to the next, providing physical continuity across what would otherwise be a singularity.

Penrose argues that the entropy is "reset" at each aeon transition because the relevant degrees of freedom change. In the remote future of an aeon, the dominant degrees of freedom are massless (photons, gravitons) — the universe has "forgotten" about massive particles because all massive particles have either dispersed or been converted to radiation in black hole evaporation. A conformal theory of massless particles has no intrinsic scale: it cannot count the entropy of a state in terms of the number of particles. When this massless state is conformally identified with the beginning of a new aeon, the entropy accounting starts fresh — the new aeon inherits a low Weyl curvature initial state.

## Critical Assessment

CCC is speculative, and it has been criticized on several grounds.

**The entropy accounting.** The claim that entropy is "reset" at the aeon transition is central but controversial. Critics argue that conformal invariance does not actually reset entropy — entropy is a feature of the state of the system, and conformal transformations do not reduce entropy. The information encoded in the gravitational degrees of freedom of one aeon does not disappear at the transition; it must go somewhere.

**Observational tests.** Penrose has proposed that CCC makes testable predictions: the collision of supermassive black holes in a previous aeon would produce concentric rings of enhanced temperature in the cosmic microwave background (CMB). Penrose and colleagues (Gurzadyan and Penrose 2010) claimed to have found such rings, but this claim has been disputed by other cosmologists who find no statistically significant signal.

**The physics of the transition.** The conformal identification of the future boundary of one aeon with the past boundary of the next requires physical processes that are not yet well-understood. The transition involves the conversion of massive degrees of freedom to massless ones, the evaporation of all black holes, and a conformal identification across what would normally be a singularity. Each of these steps involves physics that goes beyond established theory.

## What CCC Illustrates

Despite its speculative character, CCC illustrates something philosophically important: the question of why the universe began in a low-entropy state cannot be answered within the framework of a single aeon of cosmology. It requires a theory of the universe's history that extends beyond the Big Bang. Any such theory — whether CCC, Carroll and Chen's eternal inflation, or some other approach — must grapple with the fact that the initial conditions of our universe are not generic but extremely special.

The puzzle of the Past Hypothesis, and the extraordinary improbability that Penrose has quantified, point toward physics that we do not yet have. Whether the resolution will come from quantum gravity, eternal cosmology, CCC, or some currently unimagined framework is an open question. What is clear is that the arrow of time, in its deepest form, is connected to the origin of the universe — and that origin remains one of the great unsolved problems in physics and philosophy.
