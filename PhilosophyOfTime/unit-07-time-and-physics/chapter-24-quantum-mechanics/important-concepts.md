# Important Concepts: Chapter 24

**Schrödinger Equation**
The fundamental equation governing the time evolution of quantum states: iℏ d|Ψ⟩/dt = Ĥ|Ψ⟩. The Schrödinger equation is deterministic, linear, and time-symmetric. It describes the continuous, smooth evolution of the quantum state between measurements.

**Pauli's Theorem**
The result (Pauli 1933) that there is no self-adjoint time operator canonically conjugate to a Hamiltonian that is bounded below. This means time cannot be a quantum observable in the same sense as position or momentum. Time remains an external parameter in quantum mechanics, not a property of the quantum system.

**Time as External Parameter**
In quantum mechanics, time appears as the argument of the wavefunction — the external variable against which the quantum state evolves — but not as a quantum observable. This contrasts with position, which is a quantum observable (a self-adjoint operator). The asymmetric treatment of time and space in quantum mechanics is a source of tension when trying to reconcile quantum mechanics with general relativity.

**Energy-Time Uncertainty Relation**
The relation ΔEΔt ≥ ℏ/2, where ΔE is the energy uncertainty of a quantum state and Δt is the characteristic timescale of change. Unlike the position-momentum uncertainty relation, this relation does not arise from a canonical commutation relation between conjugate operators. The Δt here is not the uncertainty of a time measurement but the time for the state to evolve appreciably.

**Measurement Problem**
The fundamental interpretive problem of quantum mechanics: the Schrödinger equation describes continuous, deterministic evolution; the measurement postulate describes instantaneous, stochastic collapse to an eigenstate. The measurement problem is the question of what determines when measurement (collapse) occurs and why the two descriptions are apparently necessary. Different interpretations resolve this tension in different ways.

**Wavefunction Collapse**
The postulated sudden change of the quantum state upon measurement from a superposition of eigenstates to a single eigenstate corresponding to the observed outcome. Collapse is not described by the Schrödinger equation; it is an additional dynamical rule. The physical reality, timing, and mechanism of collapse are among the central disputed questions in quantum foundations.

**Many-Worlds Interpretation (MWI)**
The interpretation of quantum mechanics, originating with Everett (1957), in which the wavefunction never collapses but always evolves according to the Schrödinger equation. When a measurement occurs, the system and observer become entangled and the combined state branches into multiple components, each corresponding to a different measurement outcome. All branches exist; all outcomes occur in different "worlds."

**Bohmian Mechanics (Pilot Wave Theory)**
An interpretation of quantum mechanics in which particles have definite positions at all times, guided by the wavefunction via the "guidance equation." The wavefunction evolves according to the Schrödinger equation; the particle positions evolve deterministically according to the guidance equation. The measurement problem is dissolved because measurements are ordinary physical interactions, not special events. Time is classical and external.

**Quantum Equilibrium Hypothesis**
The assumption in Bohmian mechanics that the initial particle configuration is distributed according to the Born rule |Ψ(q, 0)|². This assumption, combined with the equivariance of the Born rule distribution under the Bohmian dynamics, ensures that all quantum mechanical predictions are reproduced exactly.

**Two-State Vector Formalism (TSVF)**
Aharonov and Vaidman's reformulation of quantum mechanics in which a quantum system at an intermediate time is described by two state vectors: a forward-evolving state from the initial condition and a backward-evolving state from the final condition. The TSVF is time-symmetric and treats past and future boundary conditions on an equal footing.

**Retrocausation**
The possibility that the future causally influences the past. In the two-state vector formalism, the future boundary condition contributes to the quantum description of intermediate states, raising the question of whether quantum mechanics involves genuine retrocausal influence. The question of whether retrocausation is a feature of quantum reality or merely of certain representations is philosophically contested.

**Page-Wootters Mechanism**
The proposal by Page and Wootters (1983) that in a universe with no external time parameter (as in quantum gravity), time can emerge from quantum correlations (entanglement) between subsystems of the universe. One subsystem (the "clock") is correlated with other subsystems in a way that, from the perspective of an internal observer, constitutes temporal evolution.

**Wheeler-DeWitt Equation**
The equation Ĥ|Ψ⟩ = 0 that arises in the canonical quantization of general relativity. It describes the quantum state of the universe without any time derivative — the state is apparently static. The question of how time emerges from a universe described by this equation is the "problem of time" in quantum gravity.

**Emergent Time**
The view that time is not a fundamental feature of the universe but arises from more basic physical structures — in particular, from correlations and entanglement between quantum subsystems. The Page-Wootters mechanism is the best-developed account of how time can be emergent. Emergent time is contrasted with fundamental time, which is presupposed as a background parameter in standard quantum mechanics.

**GRW Theory (Spontaneous Collapse)**
The theory of Ghirardi, Rimini, and Weber (1986) in which wavefunction collapse is a real, stochastic physical process that occurs spontaneously at a rate proportional to the number of particles in the system. GRW modifies the Schrödinger equation by adding stochastic collapse terms. It introduces a genuine time asymmetry at the quantum level and can be seen as grounding the thermodynamic arrow.
