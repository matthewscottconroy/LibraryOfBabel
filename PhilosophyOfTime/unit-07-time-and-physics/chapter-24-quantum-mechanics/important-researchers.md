# Important Researchers: Chapter 24

**Wolfgang Pauli (1900–1958)**
Austrian-Swiss theoretical physicist, one of the pioneers of quantum mechanics. His 1925 exclusion principle (no two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state) was a foundational contribution. His 1933 argument that time cannot be a quantum observable (Pauli's theorem) identified a fundamental asymmetry in quantum mechanics that has shaped the problem of time in quantum gravity. Awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1945 for the exclusion principle.

**Hugh Everett III (1930–1982)**
American physicist whose 1957 doctoral dissertation proposed the relative-state (many-worlds) interpretation of quantum mechanics. Everett's work was largely ignored for decades after it was published, partly due to resistance from Niels Bohr and the Copenhagen establishment. It was rediscovered and developed by Bryce DeWitt in the 1970s and has since become one of the most discussed interpretations of quantum mechanics. Everett left physics after his dissertation and worked in defense operations research.

**David Bohm (1917–1992)**
American physicist who independently rediscovered and developed the pilot wave interpretation of quantum mechanics in 1952. Bohm was a student of Robert Oppenheimer and was subject to McCarthyite persecution in the early 1950s, which led him to emigrate from the United States. His pilot wave theory provided the first serious alternative to Copenhagen-style quantum mechanics, demonstrating that quantum mechanics could be formulated with definite particle trajectories and deterministic dynamics. His later philosophical work on "implicate order" explored connections between quantum mechanics and consciousness.

**Yakir Aharonov (1932–)**
Israeli physicist who has made fundamental contributions to the foundations of quantum mechanics over six decades. His 1964 paper with Bergmann and Lebowitz initiated the program of time-symmetric quantum mechanics. His subsequent development of the two-state vector formalism, weak values, and weak measurements has opened new perspectives on quantum reality and the nature of time. The Aharonov-Bohm effect (1959), demonstrating that electromagnetic potentials have physical effects even where fields vanish, is named for him and is one of the most important results in quantum mechanics.

**Don Page (1948–)**
Canadian physicist and cosmologist whose 1983 paper with Wootters proposed the mechanism for emergent time from quantum correlations. Page has also contributed to black hole thermodynamics, quantum cosmology, and the measurement problem. His work on the Page curve for black hole evaporation (showing that information is not lost) has been influential in recent debates about the black hole information paradox.

**William K. Wootters (1951–)**
American physicist at Williams College whose work spans quantum information theory and the foundations of quantum mechanics. His 1983 paper with Page on emergent time is his most philosophically significant contribution to the foundations of physics. He has also made fundamental contributions to quantum information theory, including the no-cloning theorem (with Zurek) and quantum teleportation (with Bennett, Brassard, and others).

**David Z. Albert (1954–)**
See Chapter 23. In the context of quantum mechanics, Albert's contributions include his analysis of quantum mechanics and experience (1994), which provides the best philosophical treatment of the measurement problem for non-physicists, and his work on spontaneous collapse theories and their implications for the arrow of time.

**Tim Maudlin (1958–)**
American philosopher of physics at New York University whose work on quantum mechanics, quantum non-locality, and the philosophy of physics has been among the most rigorous and influential of the past thirty years. His 2019 *Philosophy of Physics: Quantum Theory* is the most philosophically precise current treatment of the foundations of quantum mechanics. His approach is strongly realist and critical of interpretations (like MWI) that he regards as metaphysically evasive.

**David Wallace (1976–)**
British philosopher of physics, currently at the University of Southern California, who has developed the most sophisticated contemporary philosophical defense of the many-worlds interpretation. His 2012 *The Emergent Multiverse* argues that the Born rule can be derived from decision-theoretic considerations within MWI, thereby solving the probability problem. He has also contributed to the foundations of statistical mechanics and quantum field theory.
