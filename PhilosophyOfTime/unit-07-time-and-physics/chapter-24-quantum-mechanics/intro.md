# Chapter 24: Quantum Mechanics and Time

Quantum mechanics is the most precisely tested physical theory in history. Its predictions for the electromagnetic properties of the electron agree with experiment to better than one part in a trillion. It is the foundation of modern chemistry, atomic physics, solid-state physics, and the semiconductor technology on which our civilization depends. And yet its interpretation — what it says about the nature of physical reality — remains genuinely contested after a century of argument.

For the philosophy of time, quantum mechanics raises a distinctive set of puzzles, different from those posed by relativity. Where special and general relativity reshape the geometry and dynamics of time, quantum mechanics challenges our understanding of time's role in physics in more foundational ways. In quantum mechanics, time plays a peculiar double role: it is the parameter against which the quantum state evolves (via the Schrödinger equation), yet it cannot be a quantum observable in the same sense that position and momentum are. The measurement process, which lies at the heart of quantum theory's empirical content, involves a temporal asymmetry that has no clear origin in the time-symmetric evolution of the wavefunction. And some interpretations of quantum mechanics — particularly Everett's many-worlds interpretation — suggest that the "flow of time" and the "branching of possible futures" must be reconceived in ways that defy ordinary intuition.

This chapter examines quantum mechanics and time across five sections. We begin with Pauli's theorem — the argument that time cannot be a quantum observable — and the energy-time uncertainty relation. We then examine the measurement problem and its temporal dimensions. The third section surveys how the major interpretations of quantum mechanics — many-worlds and Bohmian mechanics — handle the concept of time. The fourth examines the two-state vector formalism of Aharonov and Vaidman, which suggests a time-symmetric description of quantum reality. We close with the Page-Wootters mechanism and the question of whether time is emergent from quantum entanglement.
