# Wavefunction, Collapse, and the Temporal Puzzle

## The Two Kinds of Evolution

Standard quantum mechanics describes the evolution of a physical system by a wavefunction (or state vector) |Ψ(t)⟩ in a Hilbert space. This wavefunction evolves according to the Schrödinger equation:

*iℏ d|Ψ⟩/dt = Ĥ|Ψ⟩*

The Schrödinger equation specifies a deterministic, linear, continuous evolution: if you know |Ψ(t₀)⟩, you can calculate |Ψ(t)⟩ for any later time t. This evolution is also reversible: the Schrödinger equation is first-order in time, and the evolution operator e^(-iĤt/ℏ) is unitary — it preserves the inner product structure of the Hilbert space and is invertible.

But there is a second kind of "evolution" described in standard quantum mechanics: the measurement update, or *collapse*. When an observable A is measured and the result is the eigenvalue aₙ, the state is said to "collapse" to the corresponding eigenstate |aₙ⟩:

*|Ψ⟩ → |aₙ⟩* (upon measurement with result aₙ)

This collapse is not described by the Schrödinger equation. It is discontinuous (the state changes instantaneously), non-linear (the resulting state depends on the measurement outcome in a way that cannot be represented by a linear operator), and irreversible (the pre-measurement state cannot be recovered from the post-measurement state and the measurement result alone).

## The Temporal Questions

The tension between the two kinds of evolution raises several urgent temporal questions:

**When does collapse occur?** The Schrödinger equation governs the continuous evolution of the quantum state. The collapse rule is invoked "upon measurement." But when, exactly, does a "measurement" occur? When the quantum system interacts with the measuring apparatus? When the apparatus registers a definite result? When a human observer becomes aware of the result? The boundary between "Schrödinger evolution" and "collapse" is not specified by the standard formalism; it is left as a judgment call.

**Is collapse instantaneous?** The standard collapse rule postulates that the state collapses instantaneously to an eigenstate. But "instantaneously" is a frame-relative concept in special relativity: what is instantaneous in one frame is not in another. This creates a tension between quantum mechanics and special relativity that various interpretations handle differently.

**Does collapse introduce time asymmetry?** The Schrödinger equation is time-symmetric (T-symmetric): if |Ψ(t)⟩ is a solution, so is |Ψ(-t)⟩ (with appropriate complex conjugation). But the collapse rule is not time-symmetric: it takes a superposition of eigenstates to a single eigenstate, losing information about the pre-measurement superposition. This irreversibility is a temporal asymmetry at the quantum level.

Whether this quantum asymmetry can ground or explain the macroscopic arrow of time is contested. Albert (1994) proposed that if collapse is a real, fundamental, stochastic process (as in the GRW spontaneous collapse theory), it provides a time-asymmetric dynamical process that could ground entropy increase. Others argue that the quantum measurement asymmetry is itself a manifestation of the thermodynamic arrow rather than its source.

## The Schrödinger's Cat Problem

Erwin Schrödinger's famous 1935 thought experiment makes the temporal aspect of the measurement problem vivid. A cat is placed in a sealed box with a radioactive atom, a Geiger counter, and a vial of poison. If the atom decays (a quantum event), the Geiger counter triggers, the poison is released, and the cat dies. If the atom does not decay, the cat survives.

After one hour, the atom has a 50% probability of having decayed. According to the Schrödinger equation, the combined state of atom + counter + poison + cat is an entangled superposition: (50%) |decayed, counter triggered, poison released, cat dead⟩ + (50%) |undecayed, counter not triggered, poison intact, cat alive⟩.

When we open the box, we find the cat either definitely dead or definitely alive — not in a superposition. The question is: when did the superposition become a definite outcome? The standard answer ("upon measurement") leaves the temporal boundary undefined. The "many-worlds" answer is that both outcomes occur, in different branches of the universal wavefunction. The "collapse theories" (GRW, CSL) say that collapse is a real, stochastic physical event that happens spontaneously at a rate inversely proportional to the size of the system.

The Schrödinger's cat problem is, among other things, a puzzle about the temporal location of quantum events: when does a superposition become a definite occurrence? The answer depends crucially on one's interpretation of quantum mechanics — which is why we turn to those interpretations in the next section.
