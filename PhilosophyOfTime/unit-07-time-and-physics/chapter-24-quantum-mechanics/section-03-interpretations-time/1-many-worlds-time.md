# Many Worlds and Time

## Everett's Proposal

In 1957, Hugh Everett III proposed a radical resolution to the measurement problem in his doctoral dissertation at Princeton. Rather than introducing a collapse postulate, Everett proposed to take the Schrödinger equation seriously as a complete and universal description of quantum evolution. The wavefunction never collapses; it always evolves according to the Schrödinger equation. What appears to be "collapse" from the perspective of an observer is in fact the observer becoming entangled with the quantum system — with multiple outcomes, all of which occur.

In Everett's formulation, when a quantum measurement is made and the system was in a superposition of eigenstates, the post-measurement state of the combined system (quantum system + apparatus + observer) is an entangled superposition. Each branch of the superposition includes a copy of the observer seeing a definite result. All branches exist; all outcomes occur. The "branching" of the wavefunction is what we, from inside one branch, experience as the selection of a definite measurement result.

The modern form of Everett's interpretation, developed by David Deutsch, David Wallace, Simon Saunders, and others, is called the "many-worlds interpretation" (MWI). It holds that the fundamental ontology is the quantum wavefunction, evolving unitarily in Hilbert space; the "worlds" or "branches" are approximately classical, decohered structures within the wavefunction.

## What Happens to Time in MWI

In the many-worlds interpretation, time remains a classical external parameter. The universal wavefunction Ψ(q, t) is a function of the configuration q (the collective positions of all particles, or the field configuration) and the time t. It evolves according to the Schrödinger equation with a fixed external time parameter.

What changes dramatically is the *structure* of the physical world described by this wavefunction. Rather than a single classical history — a single trajectory through configuration space — the wavefunction describes an enormous number of approximately classical histories, corresponding to different branches. These branches are not sequential; they coexist simultaneously within the wavefunction.

From the perspective of temporal experience, what matters is the branching structure: at each "measurement event" (each interaction that leads to decoherence between branches), the branches diverge. Each branch evolves continuously, with its own consistent temporal sequence of events. Observers within any branch have a well-defined temporal experience — memories of a specific past, anticipation of a definite future — but the "future" they experience is their specific branch, not all branches.

## The Branching of Time

The many-worlds interpretation raises a profound question about the nature of temporal passage and the "flow" of time. In ordinary experience, we move from one moment to the next, experiencing a single stream of events. In MWI, the "branching" of the wavefunction means that at each moment of interaction, there are multiple futures — all equally real, all actually occurring in different branches.

Does this mean that time "branches"? The answer requires care. Time itself — the external parameter t — does not branch in MWI; it continues as a single parameter. What branches is the history of the physical world within that time. More precisely: at any given time t, the wavefunction assigns amplitudes to an enormous set of configurations, including configurations corresponding to multiple classical histories that have diverged due to past quantum measurements.

The "branching" is not a temporal event in the usual sense — it does not happen "at a moment" in any absolute way. Rather, it is a feature of how the wavefunction decomposes into approximately classical, decohered components at any given time. The granularity of this decomposition is set by the decoherence timescale, which is typically extremely short for macroscopic systems (of order 10⁻²³ seconds for a dust grain).

## Implications for Temporal Ontology

The many-worlds interpretation has several philosophical implications for the nature of time.

First, it supports a version of the block universe: all branches of the wavefunction exist equally, at all times. The past is as real as the present (it is encoded in the structure of the wavefunction), and the future — in all its branches — is equally real. The branching structure provides a sense in which the future is "open" (multiple branches are possible) while remaining consistent with the block universe (all branches exist in the wavefunction).

Second, the personal identity of observers becomes complicated. If at each moment of measurement an observer "splits" into multiple copies (one per branch), there is a question of which copy is the "same person" as the pre-measurement observer. This raises issues about the persistence of personal identity through time that are unique to MWI.

Third, probability in MWI is philosophically puzzling. If all outcomes occur (in different branches), what does it mean to say that one outcome has a 70% probability and another 30%? This is the "probability problem" for MWI, and it has been the subject of sustained philosophical work (Wallace 2012; Saunders et al. 2010).
