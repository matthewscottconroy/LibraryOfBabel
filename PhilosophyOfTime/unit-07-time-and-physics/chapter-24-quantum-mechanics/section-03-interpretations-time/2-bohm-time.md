# Bohmian Mechanics and Time

## The Pilot Wave

In 1927, Louis de Broglie proposed a "pilot wave" interpretation of quantum mechanics at the Solvay Conference. His proposal was largely ignored until David Bohm independently rediscovered and developed it in 1952. The de Broglie-Bohm theory, now usually called Bohmian mechanics or pilot wave theory, is a hidden variable theory: it adds to the quantum wavefunction an additional ontological element — the actual position of every particle — which the wavefunction guides.

In Bohmian mechanics:
1. Every particle has a definite position Q(t) at all times — a hidden variable.
2. The wavefunction Ψ(q, t) evolves according to the Schrödinger equation.
3. The velocity of each particle is determined by the wavefunction via the "guidance equation": *dQₖ/dt = (ℏ/mₖ) Im[∇ₖΨ/Ψ]*

The actual particle configuration Q(t) moves continuously under the guidance of the wavefunction. When we "measure" the position of a particle, we simply find the actual position that has been there all along. There is no collapse: the wavefunction continues to evolve according to the Schrödinger equation, and the particles continue to move according to the guidance equation.

The appearance of quantum randomness is explained by the "quantum equilibrium hypothesis": if the initial particle configuration is distributed according to |Ψ(q, 0)|² (the Born rule distribution), then for all future times, the particle configuration remains distributed according to |Ψ(q, t)|². This equivariance property means that all quantum mechanical predictions are reproduced exactly.

## Time in Bohmian Mechanics

Time in Bohmian mechanics is classical, external, and absolute — much closer to Newton's conception than to Einstein's. The guidance equation is a first-order ordinary differential equation for the particle positions, with a classical time parameter t. Given the initial positions and the initial wavefunction (and assuming the quantum equilibrium condition), the dynamics is deterministic and classical in character.

There is no ambiguity about when measurements occur: measurements are ordinary physical processes involving the interaction of the particle configuration with the apparatus configuration. The apparatus is itself a collection of particles with definite positions, and the "result" of a measurement is just the final configuration of the apparatus particles. There is no separate collapse event and no ambiguity about when it happens.

From this perspective, the measurement problem is dissolved: there is no fundamental distinction between measurement and non-measurement interactions. Both are just particle motions guided by the wavefunction. The "wavefunction collapse" we seem to observe is explained by the effective collapse of the wavefunction relative to the particle configuration: after a measurement, the particle is in a specific region of configuration space, and the branches of the wavefunction corresponding to other outcomes become irrelevant for future guidance (they do not contribute to the velocity of the actual particle).

## Advantages for the Philosophy of Time

Bohmian mechanics has several features that philosophers of time find attractive.

**Definiteness.** In Bohmian mechanics, particles have definite positions at all times. This means the world has a definite state at every moment — something that is problematic in standard quantum mechanics, where (before measurement) the state is a superposition and no definite value can be assigned to most observables. The definiteness of the Bohmian configuration space history gives a clear sense in which "what happens" at each moment is determinately specified.

**Classical time.** Time is external, uniform, and classical in Bohmian mechanics. There is no problem about what "time" means in the theory: it is just the Newtonian parameter, albeit relativized in a Lorentz-invariant formulation. This avoids the problems about time that other interpretations face, at the cost of reintroducing something like a preferred frame (a point to which Bohmians have various responses).

**Arrow of time.** In Bohmian mechanics, the arrow of time can be grounded in the initial conditions of the particle configuration and the quantum equilibrium condition, in a way that parallels the classical statistical mechanical treatment. There is no additional quantum arrow; the classical thermodynamic story applies straightforwardly.

## Challenges

Bohmian mechanics faces challenges that relate to time.

**Relativistic extension.** The Lorentz-invariant formulation of Bohmian mechanics is more complex than the non-relativistic version. Several proposals exist, but each faces difficulties — including, in some versions, the need for a preferred foliation of spacetime (a preferred reference frame for defining particle trajectories). This tension between Bohmian mechanics and relativistic spacetime structure is a significant concern.

**Quantum field theory.** Bohmian mechanics has been extended to quantum field theory, but the extension is not fully satisfactory. Particles appear and disappear in quantum field theory (they are excitations of fields), and Bohmian mechanics needs a careful treatment of this.

Despite these challenges, Bohmian mechanics demonstrates an important philosophical point: quantum mechanics can be formulated with classical time, definite particle positions, and no collapse. The additional ontological commitment (definite particle positions) solves the measurement problem and preserves a clear picture of what exists at each moment. Whether this ontological commitment is worthwhile is a matter of ongoing philosophical debate.
