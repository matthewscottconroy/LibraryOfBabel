# The Incompatibility of QM and GR Conceptions of Time

## How Time Functions in Each Theory

In quantum mechanics, as we saw in Chapter 24, time is an external classical parameter. The quantum state |Ψ(t)⟩ is a function of time, and it evolves according to the Schrödinger equation: iℏ d|Ψ⟩/dt = Ĥ|Ψ⟩. The time *t* that appears here is not a quantum operator; it is a classical number, given independently of the quantum system. The Hamiltonian Ĥ acts on the Hilbert space of the system, but time itself is external — not part of the system's Hilbert space, not subject to quantum uncertainty.

This classical external time is assumed to be a well-defined background: we always know what time it is (in principle), and the quantum state evolves against this well-defined background. The superposition principle applies to the quantum degrees of freedom of the system, but not to time itself.

In general relativity, by contrast, time is part of the dynamical content of the theory. The spacetime metric *g_μν* — which encodes how time intervals and spatial distances are measured — is a physical field that satisfies Einstein's field equations. It is not given independently of the matter content; it is determined by it. Different distributions of matter produce different spacetime geometries, different rates of time passage, different causal structures.

The metric is the physical field that carries both spatial and temporal information. In Newtonian mechanics, there was a time coordinate that was independent of everything. In GR, the coordinate *t* has no direct physical meaning: only physical quantities defined in terms of the metric (like proper time along a worldline) are observable.

## The Problem with Quantizing the Metric

Quantum gravity requires quantizing the metric *g_μν*. This means treating the metric as a quantum field, subject to quantum uncertainty, capable of being in superpositions of different values. But if the metric is in a quantum superposition, then the temporal structure of spacetime is in a superposition: different values of the metric correspond to different rates of time passage, different causal structures.

Consider a concrete example. Suppose the metric is in a quantum superposition of two states: one in which a massive planet is to the left of a clock, and one in which the planet is to the right. In the first case, the clock runs at a slightly different rate than in the second (due to gravitational time dilation). If the metric is genuinely in a superposition of these two configurations, then — in a very literal sense — the rate of time passage is in a superposition. The clock is ticking at two different rates simultaneously.

What does this mean? In quantum mechanics, a system in a superposition does not have a definite value of the superposed observable; the value is indefinite until it is measured. Applied to time, this would mean that the rate of time passage — which we ordinarily think of as definite — is indefinite in quantum gravity. Time itself is "smeared out."

This is not merely a verbal puzzle. It has concrete mathematical consequences. In canonical quantum gravity — the approach based on quantizing the Hamiltonian formulation of GR — the Hamiltonian constraint is Ĥ = 0. This constraint, when promoted to a quantum equation, becomes the Wheeler-DeWitt equation Ĥ|Ψ⟩ = 0, which has no time derivative at all. The wavefunction of the universe does not evolve; it is static with respect to any external time.

## The Conceptual Conflict

The conceptual conflict can be stated sharply:

- In QM, time is fixed and classical: the state evolves with respect to it.
- In GR, time is dynamical and physical: it is part of the metric, determined by matter.
- In quantum gravity, the metric (including its temporal component) is a quantum degree of freedom.
- Therefore, in quantum gravity, the temporal background that QM requires is not available: time itself is a quantum variable.

This creates a dilemma. QM needs a classical background time against which quantum states evolve. GR provides no such background: time is dynamical. A quantum theory of gravity must somehow either (a) find a substitute for classical background time, (b) reformulate quantum mechanics to not require a classical background time, or (c) show how classical time emerges from a more fundamental, timeless structure.

All three of these strategies have been pursued. (a) corresponds to "internal time" approaches; (b) corresponds to "timeless" approaches (Page-Wootters, Barbour); (c) corresponds to semiclassical approaches in which classical spacetime (including time) is recovered as an approximation to the full quantum gravitational theory. None is fully satisfactory.

## What This Tells Us About the Nature of Time

The incompatibility between QM and GR conceptions of time is not just a technical inconvenience. It is a signal that our current theories involve contradictory assumptions about time's nature:

- Is time a background, or is it dynamical?
- Is time classical, or can it be quantum?
- Does time flow, or is it fixed?
- Is there a global time, or only local notions of time?

These are not merely questions for physics. They are questions about the fundamental nature of time — questions that have occupied philosophers since before the age of science. The physics of quantum gravity has brought them back with new precision and new urgency.
