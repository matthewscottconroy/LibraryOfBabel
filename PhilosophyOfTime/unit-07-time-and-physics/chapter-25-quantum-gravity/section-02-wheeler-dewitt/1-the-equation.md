# The Wheeler-DeWitt Equation

## The Hamiltonian Formulation of GR

General relativity can be formulated in Hamiltonian form — the same framework used for canonical quantization of other field theories. This was done by Richard Arnowitt, Stanley Deser, and Charles Misner (ADM) in the late 1950s. In the ADM formulation, spacetime is sliced into a sequence of spatial hypersurfaces (three-dimensional "snapshots") evolving in time. The dynamical variables are the three-metric *qᵢⱼ* on each hypersurface (how space is curved at each moment) and its conjugate momentum *πⁱʲ* (related to the extrinsic curvature, how the hypersurface is embedded in spacetime).

In this Hamiltonian formulation, there is no true Hamiltonian that generates time evolution in the usual sense. Instead, there is a *Hamiltonian constraint*:

*H(qᵢⱼ, πⁱʲ) = 0*

This constraint must be satisfied by all physical states. It is a consequence of the diffeomorphism invariance of general relativity — the freedom to relabel spacetime coordinates arbitrarily. The constraint does not say "the total energy is zero"; it is a deeper statement about the gauge freedom of the theory.

The constraint generates time evolution in GR in a specific technical sense: the equations of motion are *entirely* generated by the constraint. This means that what looks like "time evolution" in GR is actually gauge transformation — a relabeling of how we foliate spacetime. There is no Hamiltonian that drives evolution against an external time; instead, all dynamics is generated by the constraints.

## DeWitt's Quantization

In 1967, Bryce DeWitt applied canonical quantization to this framework. The recipe for canonical quantization is: promote the classical variables to operators, impose the canonical commutation relations, and require that the physical states satisfy the quantum version of the constraint equations.

The quantum version of the Hamiltonian constraint is the Wheeler-DeWitt equation:

*Ĥ|Ψ[qᵢⱼ]⟩ = 0*

where Ĥ is the Hamiltonian constraint operator and |Ψ[qᵢⱼ]⟩ is the "wave functional of the universe" — the quantum state of the gravitational field, a functional of the three-metric.

The striking feature: there is no time derivative. Compare the ordinary Schrödinger equation:

*iℏ ∂|Ψ⟩/∂t = Ĥ|Ψ⟩*

which has a time derivative on the left. The Wheeler-DeWitt equation has no time derivative: it is an eigenvalue equation with eigenvalue zero, not an evolution equation. The wavefunction does not change; it is a static solution to a constraint equation.

## The Frozen Universe

The Wheeler-DeWitt equation appears to describe a universe that does not evolve. The wavefunction of the universe |Ψ⟩ satisfies Ĥ|Ψ⟩ = 0 and has no time dependence. It is a static, frozen object.

This is deeply puzzling. We clearly experience a universe that changes over time: stars are born and die, galaxies evolve, people age. How can the fundamental quantum description of the universe be static?

Several responses have been proposed:

**Approximate time.** In the limit of large-scale, nearly-classical spacetimes, the Wheeler-DeWitt equation can be approximated. In this approximation, the heavy degrees of freedom (the large-scale geometry) act as a classical background, and the light degrees of freedom (matter fields) evolve according to a Schrödinger equation with respect to the WKB approximation of the background. This is the "semiclassical" approach: classical time emerges from the dynamics of classical geometry, even though there is no fundamental time in the theory. Time is an approximation, valid when the universe is large and nearly classical.

**Internal time.** Another approach is to identify a physical degree of freedom within the universe that can play the role of a clock. The "scalar field clock" is one common choice: if the universe contains a scalar field φ that increases monotonically, one can use φ as an internal time parameter. Physical observables are then defined as relations between the clock value and other degrees of freedom. This avoids the need for an external time at the cost of requiring a suitably behaved internal clock.

**Timeless approaches.** A third approach, associated with Barbour (1994, 1999), is to take the Wheeler-DeWitt equation at face value: the universe is genuinely timeless at the fundamental level, and time is entirely emergent (via Page-Wootters correlations or similar mechanisms).
