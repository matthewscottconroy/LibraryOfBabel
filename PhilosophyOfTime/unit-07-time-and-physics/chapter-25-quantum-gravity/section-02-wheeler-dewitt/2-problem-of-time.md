# The Problem of Time in Canonical Quantum Gravity

## Isham and Kuchař's Classification

The problem of time in quantum gravity is not a single problem but a constellation of related difficulties. Christopher Isham (1992) and Karel Kuchař (1992) provided the definitive classification in their comprehensive reviews, which remain the standard references. They identified multiple distinct facets of the problem, some of which are genuinely independent.

**The problem of frozen dynamics.** The Wheeler-DeWitt equation has no time evolution. Physical states are annihilated by the Hamiltonian: Ĥ|Ψ⟩ = 0. How do we extract a notion of dynamics or change from a static equation?

**The multiple choice problem.** To define "internal time" (a physical degree of freedom used as a clock), we must choose which degree of freedom plays this role. Different choices give different "time" variables, and the physical predictions of the quantized theory may depend on which choice is made. There is no canonical, unique choice of internal time.

**The global time problem.** Even if we can find a local internal time variable that increases monotonically in a given region of the universe, it may not be globally monotonic: there may be regions where the chosen variable decreases, or where it is undefined. A global time function — one that increases throughout the entire universe — may not exist for a general spacetime.

**The Hilbert space problem.** In ordinary quantum mechanics, physical states live in a Hilbert space with an inner product that defines probabilities. In quantum gravity, the natural inner product (the "kinematic" inner product on the space of all wavefunctions) is not the right one for physical states (which must satisfy the Wheeler-DeWitt equation). Defining a suitable physical inner product — and hence a probability interpretation — for solutions to the Wheeler-DeWitt equation is technically difficult and conceptually non-trivial.

**The problem of time-reparametrization invariance.** The Wheeler-DeWitt equation is invariant under reparametrization of the time coordinate used to define the Hamiltonian constraint. This reflects the diffeomorphism invariance of GR. But this invariance makes it difficult to define observables that correspond to "what is happening at a particular time."

## Approaches to the Problem

The problem of time has generated a rich variety of proposed solutions, which Isham and Kuchař organize into broad categories:

**"Time before quantization" (timefirst) approaches:** Identify a time variable in the classical theory before quantizing. Use this variable as the time parameter in the quantum theory. Problem: the choice of classical time is not unique, and different choices may lead to inequivalent quantum theories.

**"Time after quantization" (timeless) approaches:** Quantize the classical theory without identifying any particular time variable. Recover time as an emergent, approximate concept from the quantum theory, valid in appropriate limits. Problem: it is not always clear how time should emerge, and the semiclassical approximation may not be reliable.

**"Time from quantum correlations":** Use the Page-Wootters mechanism or similar ideas to extract time from correlations between subsystems of the universe. Problem: the mechanism works in simple toy models but has not been fully implemented in a complete theory of quantum gravity.

**"No time" approaches:** Accept that time is genuinely absent from the fundamental theory and that our experience of time is entirely an emergent, approximate, or subjective feature. Problem: explaining how a rich temporal structure, including the arrow of time, emerges from a fundamentally timeless quantum universe remains unclear.

## The Philosophical Significance

The problem of time in canonical quantum gravity is not just a technical obstacle to completing a theory. It is a philosophical crisis about the nature of time at the most fundamental level.

If the problem were purely technical — if we simply needed to find better mathematics — it would be of limited philosophical interest. But the problem goes deeper: it reveals that our two best physical theories embody contradictory assumptions about what time is. Quantum mechanics assumes time is external and classical; general relativity shows that time is dynamical and physical. These assumptions cannot both be right at the most fundamental level.

Resolving the problem requires choosing: Is time fundamental (and if so, what is it, and how does it fit with GR)? Or is time emergent (and if so, from what does it emerge, and why does it have the properties it has)? These are not empirical questions that can be settled by experiment alone — they require philosophical argument as well as physical theory.

The problem of time in quantum gravity is, in this sense, the culmination of the entire history of thought about time that this unit has traced. It brings together the Newtonian question of whether time is absolute or relational, the relativistic question of whether time is fundamental or geometrical, the thermodynamic question of why time has a direction, and the quantum question of whether time can be an observable. All these threads converge in quantum gravity, and their resolution — if it comes — will require new physics and new philosophy in equal measure.
