# Smolin's Real Time

## The Fundamental Reality of Time

Lee Smolin's position, presented most fully in *Time Reborn* (2013) and developed with Ricardo Mangabeira Unger in *The Singular Universe and the Reality of Time* (2015), is diametrically opposed to Barbour's. Smolin argues that time is not only real but is the most fundamental thing in physics. The attempt to eliminate time from physics — from Newton's absolute time through Einstein's block universe to Barbour's Platonia — is, on Smolin's view, the source of the deepest errors in our understanding of the physical world.

Smolin's starting claim is ontological: the present moment is real. The past is gone; the future has not yet happened. Only the now exists. This is a form of presentism, combined with a radical commitment to the reality of temporal becoming. The flow of time is not an illusion produced by thermodynamics or cognitive architecture; it is the most basic feature of reality.

## The Laws Evolve

Smolin's most controversial claim is that even the laws of physics evolve over time. In the standard picture of physics, the laws are eternal and unchanging: they hold at all times, in all places, and govern all the processes of the universe. On Smolin's view, this picture is incoherent: if the laws are timeless, they are outside of time; but then how do they exert influence on the temporal world? The standard answer — "they just do" — is unsatisfying.

Smolin proposes instead that the laws emerge from and evolve with the history of the universe. His "principle of precedence" (Smolin and Unger 2015) states that the universe takes as a guide its own previous behavior: the regularities we call "laws of nature" are patterns in the history of the universe, which the universe follows because similar situations have led to similar outcomes in the past. Laws are habits, not eternal truths; they evolve as the universe evolves.

This is a radical proposal. It means that physics cannot aim at timeless laws in the usual sense. Instead of asking "what are the eternal laws that govern all physical processes?" we should ask "what are the evolving patterns that the universe follows?" This reorients the entire project of physics.

## Against the Block Universe

Smolin is one of the most vigorous critics of the block universe interpretation of relativity. He argues that the block universe — the view that all events, past, present, and future, are equally real — is philosophically incoherent and scientifically unproductive. If all events are equally real, there is no room for novelty, creativity, or genuine change. The universe is static in a four-dimensional sense; the appearance of change is an illusion.

Smolin finds this conclusion not just counterintuitive but incompatible with the success of physics itself. Physics predicts future states from present states — that is its primary function. But if the future is already "out there," as real as the present, then prediction is merely the discovery of what already is, not genuine anticipation of what is coming to be. The entire framework of prediction, hypothesis-testing, and scientific inquiry presupposes that the future is open — not yet determined, not yet real — and that the present is the moment of genuine becoming.

## The Debate with Barbour

The Barbour-Smolin debate crystallizes the deepest options in the philosophy of time. Barbour says: take the Wheeler-DeWitt equation seriously, accept that the fundamental world is timeless, and explain temporal experience as an emergent, approximate phenomenon arising from the structure of the wavefunction. Smolin says: take temporal experience seriously, recognize that physics has systematically failed to account for time's reality, and build a new physics that takes the present moment and the flow of time as fundamental.

Both positions have serious difficulties. Barbour must explain how rich temporal experience emerges from a fundamentally timeless reality. Smolin must explain how evolving laws can be predictive and why the present moment is metaphysically special in a universe described by relativity.

What makes their debate philosophically productive is that it forces us to confront the deepest assumptions of physical theory:

- Are the laws of physics eternal or evolving?
- Is the present moment real or just a mathematical slice of a four-dimensional block?
- Is time a feature of the world to be explained, or the presupposition of all explanation?
- Can physics be done in a genuinely timeless way, or does physics always already assume time?

These questions have no easy answers. But grappling with them — with the rigor that Barbour and Smolin both bring to the discussion — is essential for anyone who wants to understand what physics says about time, and what it does not say.

## A Provisional Synthesis

Where does this unit's survey of time in physics leave us? The picture that emerges is not a tidy one. Each level of physical theory — Newtonian mechanics, special relativity, general relativity, thermodynamics, quantum mechanics, quantum gravity — reveals new aspects of temporal structure while complicating the picture inherited from earlier theories.

What seems clear: time is not a simple, uniform, unchanging background. It is relational (relative to reference frames in SR), dynamical (curved by matter in GR), directional (pointing from low to high entropy by thermodynamics), parameter-like (external in QM), and potentially emergent (in quantum gravity).

What remains genuinely unclear: whether time is fundamental or emergent, whether the present moment is metaphysically privileged, whether time had a beginning and will have an end, and what the correct theory of quantum gravity will say about time's ultimate nature.

The philosophy of time, at the boundary with physics, is not a completed subject but an ongoing inquiry. The questions are ancient; the tools are new. The engagement with physics requires philosophical precision about what we mean by "time," what we need from a theory of time, and what we are willing to accept as an explanation. This is the kind of inquiry this unit has tried to model and inspire.
