# Unit VII: Time and Physics — A Synthesis

*This synthesis chapter develops the philosophical significance of the physics covered in Chapters 20–25. It is designed to help readers connect the physical content to the metaphysical debates of earlier units and to see the philosophical stakes of contemporary physics of time.*

---

## The Two-Way Street Between Physics and Philosophy

The relationship between physics and philosophy of time is neither one of domination (physics settles everything) nor of independence (each discipline goes its own way). It is a genuine intellectual partnership in which advances in either direction create problems and opportunities for the other.

**Physics constrains philosophy.** The discovery of special relativity ruled out certain philosophical views. Specifically, it ruled out naive presentism — the view that there is an objective present moment extending across all of space — unless the presentist is willing to relativise the present to reference frames or to claim that physics is systematically misleading about the structure of time. These are live philosophical options, but they come with significant costs. Presentists must either accept that the present is frame-dependent (there are many coexisting "presents," none of which is metaphysically privileged) or argue that despite appearances, one reference frame is objectively preferred and defines the true present.

**Philosophy constrains physics.** The interpretation of physical theories is always a philosophical task. What does the wave function in quantum mechanics represent? Is the collapse of the wave function a real physical process or merely an update in our epistemic state? Does the Wheeler-DeWitt equation show that time is not fundamental, or does it show that we have formulated quantum gravity badly? These questions require conceptual analysis, not just mathematical calculation. Physics provides the formalism; philosophy provides the interpretation.

**The collaboration is productive.** Some of the most interesting recent work at the physics-philosophy interface involves philosophers contributing to debates about the interpretation of quantum gravity and physicists drawing on philosophical distinctions to clarify their theories. The problem of time in quantum gravity is a paradigm case: it is simultaneously a technical problem in mathematical physics and a conceptual problem about the nature of time that philosophy has been grappling with for millennia.

## Newton: The Philosophical Architecture of Absolute Time

Newton's *Principia* (1687) is not merely a mathematical physics textbook. Its opening scholium on space and time is a philosophical document that encodes specific metaphysical commitments. Understanding these commitments — and why Newton held them — is essential to appreciating why the Einsteinian revolution was so philosophically significant.

Newton's absolute time has several interrelated properties:

1. **Homogeneity**: Time is the same everywhere. There are no privileged moments. All instants are qualitatively identical except for their temporal location.

2. **Isotropy**: Wait, this is spatial. For time: uniformity of flow. Time flows at the same rate throughout the universe, and at the same rate in all physical processes. This is what "equably" means in Newton's scholium.

3. **Externality**: Time is external to the physical processes it contains. The universe is embedded in time, not constituted by it. Time would continue to exist even if the universe were suddenly emptied of all matter and energy.

4. **Independence**: Time is independent of minds. It would exist even if there were no observers to measure it.

These commitments are natural for a mechanist who wants to formulate universal laws of motion. The laws of mechanics — especially $F = ma$ — require a reference frame in which accelerations are defined. Newton identifies this frame with absolute space (which provides the directional reference) and absolute time (which provides the uniform flow). Without absolute time, what does "rate" mean in $v = dx/dt$?

The philosophical vulnerability of absolute time is its causal inefficacy. If time flows independently of everything physical, then it seems to play no causal role in physical processes. But then why posit it at all? This is the force of Leibniz's critique: absolute time is an entity that does no explanatory work (since all physical relations are relational) and therefore should be eliminated by Ockham's razor.

## Special Relativity: What Was Really Discovered

It is easy to mischaracterise what special relativity discovered. It did not discover that "everything is relative" — the laws of physics are the same for all inertial observers. It did not discover that time is "just another dimension like space" — time and space are unified in spacetime, but the metric signature distinguishes temporal from spatial directions. What it discovered, philosophically, is this:

**Simultaneity is not absolute.** Whether two spatially separated events are simultaneous depends on the reference frame of the observer. There is no frame-independent fact about whether the explosion on Proxima Centauri that occurred "right now" by one measure occurred a year ago or a year hence by another.

This discovery has enormous metaphysical consequences:

**For presentism**: If there is no frame-independent "now," then the presentist's claim that only the present exists faces an immediate challenge. The present, relative to an observer on Earth, is a different set of events than the present relative to an observer moving rapidly past the Earth. Which "present" is the one that really exists?

**For the A/B debate**: Special relativity strongly supports B-theoretic approaches. The spacetime manifold of Minkowski spacetime is a four-dimensional block in which all events are equally real. There is no distinguished "slice" of the block corresponding to "the present." The A-series — the division of events into past, present, and future — is observer-dependent.

**For causal structure**: Special relativity provides a precise causal structure: two events are causally connected iff they are separated by a timelike or lightlike interval (|Δt| ≥ |Δx|/c). Events separated by a spacelike interval ($|Δt| < |Δx|/c$) cannot causally influence each other. This causal structure is frame-independent, even though simultaneity is not.

The lesson of special relativity is that the temporal order of events (which is prior?) is frame-dependent for spacelike-separated events, but the causal order (which can influence which?) is absolutely frame-independent. This suggests that the causal structure of spacetime is more fundamental than the temporal order — a suggestion that has been developed by philosophers like Malament and Robb into causal theories of spacetime.

## General Relativity: When Time Is Curved

General relativity extends the Minkowskian spacetime of special relativity to a dynamical curved spacetime. The curvature of spacetime is related to the distribution of matter and energy by Einstein's field equations:

$$G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu}$$

where $G_{\mu\nu}$ is the Einstein tensor (encoding the curvature of spacetime), $T_{\mu\nu}$ is the stress-energy tensor (encoding the distribution of matter and energy), and $\Lambda$ is the cosmological constant.

The philosophical novelty of general relativity, for the philosophy of time, is that **time is now a dynamical quantity**: it is not a fixed background against which events occur, but is itself shaped by the events occurring in the universe. The gravitational time dilation effect — confirmed to extraordinary precision by GPS corrections and atomic clocks at different altitudes — shows that proper time depends on the gravitational potential experienced by a clock.

This makes the relationship between time and material processes even closer than it was in special relativity. In special relativity, time was relative to inertial reference frames — still a fixed structure, just relative. In general relativity, the metric itself is dynamical: spacetime geometry, including temporal geometry, evolves according to Einstein's equations.

For the metaphysics of time, general relativity adds:

**Malleability of time**: Since time is curved by matter, a universe with different matter distribution would have a different temporal geometry. Time is not merely relative to observers; it is shaped by physical processes.

**Exotic temporal structures**: The field equations permit solutions with closed timelike curves, spacetime singularities (where the metric breaks down), and event horizons (where the causal structure of spacetime changes qualitatively). Each of these raises distinct philosophical questions:

- Do singularities mark the end of time, or merely the limits of the theory?
- Are closed timelike curves physically possible (are they stable against perturbations?), and if so, what are their consequences for causation and free will?
- Does time "end" inside a black hole, or does the singularity represent merely a coordinate singularity?

## Thermodynamics and the Arrow: The Deepest Problem

Chapter 23 addresses what is arguably the deepest problem in the intersection of physics and philosophy of time: the origin of the arrow of time.

The problem has a precise formulation: the fundamental laws of physics (with the minor CP-violation exception) are time-reversal symmetric. But the observed world is radically asymmetric: we observe entropy increasing, causes preceding effects, light expanding outward from sources.

**Boltzmann's contribution and the statistical mechanics resolution**: Boltzmann's H-theorem attempted to derive the increase of entropy from mechanics via the assumption of "molecular chaos" (Stosszahlansatz) — that molecular velocities are uncorrelated before collisions. But Loschmidt's reversibility objection showed that this assumption already encodes the temporal asymmetry: if the laws are time-symmetric, then for every forward-evolving trajectory in which entropy increases, there is an equally allowed backward-evolving trajectory in which entropy decreases. The Stosszahlansatz selects for the forward-evolving ones.

**The modern resolution: the past hypothesis**: The most satisfying resolution, developed by Boltzmann himself and refined by Albert and Loewer, is the past hypothesis: the claim that the initial state of the universe was in a condition of extremely low entropy. Given this initial condition and the second law, the subsequent evolution of the universe involves entropy increase.

The past hypothesis is philosophically significant for several reasons:

1. **It makes the arrow of time contingent**: In a universe with different initial conditions (e.g., a high-entropy initial state), the thermodynamic arrow would be absent or reversed. The arrow is not a metaphysical necessity but a physical consequence of our universe's particular beginning.

2. **It transfers the problem**: The past hypothesis explains the arrow of time in terms of special initial conditions. But why were the initial conditions so special? This question is not answered by the hypothesis; it is merely displaced. Cosmological explanations (the Big Bang, inflationary cosmology) can say something about why the initial conditions were low-entropy, but they ultimately require some assumption or other to be in place.

3. **It connects to consciousness**: If the thermodynamic arrow grounds the epistemic arrow (we remember the past because records form in a world of increasing entropy), and if the epistemic arrow is the basis of our subjective experience of temporal flow, then our experience of time as flowing forward is grounded in the cosmological initial conditions of the universe. We are thermodynamic creatures in a thermodynamic universe.

## Quantum Mechanics: Time Without a Clock

Quantum mechanics raises distinctive problems about time that go beyond anything encountered in classical physics. Three are especially important for philosophy:

**Time as external parameter**: In the Schrödinger equation, time appears as an external parameter, not as a quantum observable. This breaks the symmetry between time and space that general relativity requires. The "time-energy uncertainty relation" $\Delta E \Delta t \geq \hbar/2$ is not, strictly speaking, a Heisenberg uncertainty relation (which applies to conjugate observables); time is not an observable in standard quantum mechanics.

**The measurement problem and temporal asymmetry**: Wave function collapse (in interpretations that accept it) is irreversible and temporally asymmetric: once a measurement is made, the pre-measurement superposition cannot be recovered. This temporal asymmetry is puzzling given the time-symmetric character of the Schrödinger evolution. Whether collapse represents a genuine temporal asymmetry or merely a practical irreversibility (due to decoherence in macroscopic systems) is a matter of ongoing debate.

**Quantum gravity and the end of time**: When quantum mechanics is applied to the geometry of spacetime itself, the result is the Wheeler-DeWitt equation, which appears to have no time variable. The universe is described by a static quantum state. Time, if it appears at all, must emerge from the quantum gravitational description as an approximate or emergent notion.

## The Problem of Time in Quantum Gravity: Summary of Chapter 25

The Wheeler-DeWitt equation $\hat{H}|\Psi\rangle = 0$ describes the quantum state of the universe. But this equation has no time-dependence: the state $|\Psi\rangle$ does not evolve. This is the **problem of time**: how do we recover the temporal structure of the classical world from a quantum gravitational theory in which time has apparently disappeared?

Several approaches have been proposed:

**Internal time**: Choose one degree of freedom of the universe (e.g., the total volume of the universe, or some matter field) to play the role of a "clock." Relative to this internal clock, the other degrees of freedom evolve — and this relational evolution is what we experience as time. This approach is attractive but faces the challenge that different choices of internal clock give inequivalent quantum theories.

**Decoherence and emergence**: Time emerges from the quantum gravitational description through decoherence and the classical limit. In the appropriate semi-classical regime, a notion of time reappears as an approximation. This is compatible with a view on which time is not fundamental but emergent — real at the macroscopic level, non-existent at the fundamental level.

**Loop quantum gravity**: In LQG, spacetime geometry is quantised. The fundamental building blocks are spin networks (for space) and spin foams (for spacetime evolution). Time, in this picture, is not a continuous parameter but a discrete, quantised quantity at the Planck scale. The temporal structure we experience at macroscopic scales is an approximation to this discrete quantum geometry.

## Philosophical Assessment

The physics of time raises more questions for philosophy than it settles. What special relativity settles: naive presentism is untenable if we take the physics seriously. What it opens: the question of what metaphysical account of time is compatible with the block universe of special relativity.

What thermodynamics settles: the arrow of time is contingent on the past hypothesis, not a metaphysical necessity. What it opens: the question of why the past hypothesis holds, and whether it is explicable from any more fundamental principle.

What quantum gravity reveals: time may not be fundamental. If time is emergent, then the metaphysical question "what is time?" has a different character: it becomes the question of what, in the fundamental non-temporal description, gives rise to the appearance of time. This is a radically new kind of question, and it points toward a future in which the philosophy of time and the physics of time will be more deeply intertwined than they have been at any earlier moment in the tradition.
