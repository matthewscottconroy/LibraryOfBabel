# Unit VII: Time and Physics

There is something extraordinary about the relationship between physics and time. Physics, after all, is the science that most directly investigates the structure of the natural world — and time is among the most fundamental features of that world. You might expect, then, that physics would tell us definitively what time is: how it flows, what direction it runs, whether it began, and whether it must end. The truth is more interesting. The more deeply physics has probed the nature of time, the stranger and more philosophically unsettled the picture has become.

This unit is an invitation to follow that inquiry wherever it leads. We begin in the seventeenth century, with Newton's grand architecture for mechanics, in which time appears as an absolute, divine backdrop against which all motion unfolds. Newton's vision was intellectually powerful and practically successful beyond any reasonable expectation. But it carried within it philosophical tensions — about the reality of absolute space and time, about the relationship between time and the observer, about whether the equations of physics have anything to say about the direction of time — that would prove productive for three centuries of debate.

Those tensions cracked open dramatically in 1905, when Albert Einstein published his special theory of relativity. Einstein showed that simultaneity is not absolute: two events that happen "at the same time" for one observer need not be simultaneous for another moving relative to the first. This was not a measurement error or a practical inconvenience. It was a discovery about the structure of reality, one that dismantled Newton's absolute time and replaced it with something far more geometrical and strange. In Chapter 21 we follow this revolution with care, attending both to the physical arguments and to the deep philosophical consequences — for the metaphysics of persistence, for the status of the present, and for the debate between presentism and eternalism.

Chapter 22 takes us further, into Einstein's general theory of relativity, where even the geometry of spacetime becomes dynamical: curved and warped by the presence of matter and energy. In general relativity, time does not tick uniformly everywhere — it runs slower in deep gravitational wells and faster in regions of low gravity. GPS satellites must correct for this effect or they would accumulate meters of positional error per day. The theory also permits exotic structures that stretch our intuitions to the breaking point: singularities where the geometry breaks down, black holes where the roles of space and time effectively swap, and closed timelike curves that would allow a traveler to return to her own past.

Chapter 23 turns from the geometry of time to its direction. The laws of physics at the microscopic level are nearly time-symmetric: a film of particles bouncing off each other looks the same running forward as backward. Yet we experience time as deeply asymmetric — eggs break but do not reassemble, stars burn but do not unburn, and we remember the past but not the future. Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that confronts this asymmetry head-on, and the question of why entropy increases — why the future is the direction of disorder — turns out to be one of the deepest puzzles in all of science. It reaches back to the origin of the universe.

Chapters 24 and 25 venture into territory that remains actively contested among both physicists and philosophers. Quantum mechanics, the theory governing the behavior of atoms and their constituents, raises its own profound puzzles about time: time plays the role of an external parameter rather than a quantum observable; the measurement process introduces a temporal asymmetry whose origin is unclear; and some interpretations suggest that the future is genuinely open in ways that classical physics never contemplated. Finally, quantum gravity — the as-yet-unfinished project of reconciling quantum mechanics with general relativity — raises the possibility that time itself is not fundamental at all, but emerges from something deeper. The Wheeler-DeWitt equation, which is the central equation of canonical quantum gravity, appears to describe a universe in which time has dropped out entirely. Whether this means time is an illusion, an approximation, or simply that we need new conceptual tools, is a question on which some of the world's most brilliant physicists and philosophers genuinely disagree.

A note on approach: the physics in this unit ranges from the well-established to the highly speculative. Newton's mechanics and special relativity are among the most tested theories in the history of science. General relativity has been confirmed to extraordinary precision. Statistical mechanics and thermodynamics are foundational and robust. Quantum mechanics is the most precisely verified theory humanity has ever devised. But quantum gravity, loop quantum gravity, causal dynamical triangulation, and the various approaches to emergent time are frontier science: active research programs with no consensus and no experimental confirmation. We will try to be clear throughout about the epistemic status of each claim, distinguishing what is well-established from what is speculative, and what is a physical claim from what is a philosophical interpretation.

What unites these chapters is a conviction that has motivated both scientists and philosophers from Newton to Rovelli: that understanding time is not just a technical problem for physicists or a conceptual puzzle for philosophers, but one of the central intellectual challenges of our age. The universe has a temporal structure, and we are part of it. Working out what that means — carefully, rigorously, with appropriate humility before the difficulty of the questions — is among the noblest things a thinking being can do.
