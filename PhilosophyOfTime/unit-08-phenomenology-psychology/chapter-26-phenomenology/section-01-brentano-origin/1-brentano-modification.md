# Brentano's Modification Theory and Husserl's Critique

## The Problem of Temporal Awareness

Imagine that you are listening to a short melodic phrase — a five-note motif, say. When the fifth note sounds, you hear it as the fifth note. You hear it as completing something, as answering the earlier notes, as the resolution of a tension that was initiated when the first note was struck. None of this would be possible if you heard the fifth note simply as an isolated present sensation. To hear it as the fifth note, as completion and resolution, you must in some sense still have the first four notes with you. But they are over. They have ceased to sound. They are, as physical events, simply gone. How, then, are they "with you"?

This is the problem Franz Brentano identified as the puzzle of temporal awareness. It is not the problem of how we know facts about the past, nor the problem of how memory works in general. It is the more intimate and more puzzling question: how is it that in any given moment of experience, the just-preceding moments are present to me — not as objects of recollection, but as the temporal context within which I am currently perceiving?

## Brentano's "Modification" Account

Brentano's answer, preserved and analyzed by Husserl in the *Lectures on the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* (1991), is that past sensations undergo a modification in consciousness: they persist in the present moment, but in a modified mode. When the fifth note sounds, the fourth note is not absent from consciousness; nor is it recalled by a separate act of memory. Rather, it continues to be present in what Brentano calls a "modified" form — as a "just-now" tone, present with a quality of pastness attached (Husserl 1991: 21–27).

The key feature of this account is that the modification is immanent — it happens within the current act of experience, not through any additional cognitive operation. The just-past tone lingers, transformed, within the present moment of hearing. Brentano calls this the "original association" of temporal consciousness: the way in which past and present are bound together within a single act, not through memory or inference but through an immediate phenomenal connection.

This account has considerable intuitive appeal. It honors the phenomenological datum that the just-past is genuinely present to us — not merely inferred or recollected — while accounting for the fact that it is present with a different quality from the current perception. The fourth note does not sound as loudly or as freshly as the fifth note; it sounds as a note that was. The modification captures that difference.

Brentano also attempts to account for the future: just as past tones are present in a modified mode, anticipated tones are present in a "protentional" modification — a forward-facing variant of the same structure. The melody is grasped as a whole because its past is held in modification and its immediate future is pre-figured in a forward modification.

## Husserl's Appropriation and Critique

Husserl is deeply indebted to Brentano's account and takes it as his starting point. He agrees that the just-past cannot be accounted for by memory, since memory is a re-presentational act — it brings past events back before the mind — whereas the awareness of the just-past in temporal experience is immediate and non-representational. He also agrees that there must be some phenomenal modification by which the just-past is held within the current experience.

But Husserl finds Brentano's account unsatisfactory at a crucial juncture. The problem, as Husserl diagnoses it, is that Brentano treats the modified "just-now" as still a *perceived* tone — just a perceived tone with a temporal quality ("pastness") attached to it (Husserl 1991: 21–27). On Brentano's view, the modification is a modification of the content of the present impression: the fourth note, as perceived, simply acquires a tinge of the-just-past. But Husserl argues that this confuses the act of perceiving with the temporal object perceived. If the past tone were still being perceived — even in a modified mode — then it would still be present, not past. We would be perceiving it as now, albeit with a modified quality. And that is phenomenologically false: the past tone is present to us precisely as *no longer now*, not as a now-with-a-different-quality.

Put differently, Brentano's account faces this difficulty: it cannot explain the genuine temporal *distance* between the present note and the just-past note. For Brentano, both are present in the current moment, differing only in their modification. For Husserl, this collapses the distinction between the now and the just-now — it makes them merely different qualities of the present moment rather than genuinely different temporal positions (Husserl 1991: 25).

## The Implications

Husserl's critique opens the way for his own, far more sophisticated analysis. What is needed, Husserl concludes, is not a theory of how past sensations persist in modified form within the present impression, but a theory of how the *act of consciousness* itself has a temporal structure — a structure in which the present moment is not a knife-edge point but a complex field that extends, through different modes of intentionality, both backward and forward. The modification, on Husserl's account, is not a modification of the perceived content but of the *intentional act itself*.

This shift — from a modification of content to a structure of the act — is what distinguishes Husserl's analysis from Brentano's and what gives it its lasting philosophical importance. It is also what makes it considerably more demanding, as we shall see in the following sections.

What Brentano gives us, then, is something crucial: the right identification of the problem. No account of temporal experience can avoid explaining how the just-past is phenomenally present without being merely recalled. But his own solution, Husserl argues convincingly, does not achieve what it promises. The melody problem remains, and it demands a more radical treatment.
