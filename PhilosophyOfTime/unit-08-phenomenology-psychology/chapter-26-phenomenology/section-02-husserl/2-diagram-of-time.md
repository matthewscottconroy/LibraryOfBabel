# Husserl's Diagram of Time and the Absolute Flow

## The Diagram

Husserl's most celebrated analytical tool is a diagram he introduces in section 10 of the *Lectures on the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time* — sometimes called the "time-diagram" or the "double-headed arrow diagram." Husserl represents it as a coordinate system with two axes. The horizontal axis represents the sequence of now-points — the successive primal impressions as they occur one after another. The vertical axis (actually a diagonal descending to the right) represents the "depth" of retention — how far past a given moment is, relative to the current now.

On this diagram, the current moment (the primal impression) is a point on the horizontal axis. As time passes and a new primal impression arrives, the old primal impression sinks down along the diagonal — it enters retention. As more time passes, it sinks further. At some point, it passes below the threshold of retention and enters the zone of pure past, no longer phenomenally present in any way (available only via secondary memory).

What this diagram elegantly captures is the double character of temporal experience: at any given "now," I have access both to the sequence of nows (represented horizontally) and to the depth of the just-past still present in retention (represented diagonally). Every now is simultaneously the source-point of new experience and the beginning of a sinking into the past.

The diagram also illustrates a key feature of retention that Husserl calls "retention of retention." The moment just before the current now is retained. But that moment was itself retaining its own just-past. So when I retain the previous moment, I also retain, in a kind of second-order way, what that moment was retaining — and so on back through the chain. Retention thus carries a whole "tail" of the past, with each retained moment being itself a retaining of earlier moments, but with diminishing phenomenal intensity as we move further back.

## The Absolute Flow

The most enigmatic aspect of Husserl's account is his notion of the "absolute flow" (*absoluter Fluss*) of consciousness. The structure of retention-primal impression-protention is not simply a property of individual acts of experience; it is the form of consciousness as such, the manner in which consciousness flows. But this raises a question: if we are analyzing the time-consciousness of individual acts, what is the time-consciousness of consciousness itself? What makes the flow of consciousness temporal?

Husserl's answer is that the absolute flow is "self-constituting" — it constitutes its own temporality without needing a higher-level temporal structure to make it intelligible. The flow is not in time in the ordinary sense; it is what constitutes the experience of time. This is why Husserl describes it as "absolute": it is not relative to any further temporal framework. The flow flows, and in flowing, it constitutes both objective time (the time of clocks and events) and the subjective experience of time (the temporal character of experience itself).

This is one of the most difficult passages in Husserl's work, and commentators disagree sharply about how to interpret it. Dan Zahavi, in his careful study *Husserl's Phenomenology* (2003), argues that the absolute flow should be understood as the ultimate pre-reflective self-awareness of consciousness — the way in which consciousness is aware of its own flowing without this requiring a separate act of reflection (Zahavi 2003: 71–90). On this reading, the absolute flow is not a mysterious metaphysical entity but the structure of consciousness's basic self-intimation.

## Difficulties in Husserl's Account

Despite its extraordinary depth and rigor, Husserl's account faces several significant difficulties that have occupied subsequent phenomenologists and philosophers of mind.

The first is the problem of **regress**. If consciousness constitutes time through its flowing structure, what constitutes the temporality of the flow itself? Husserl's answer — that the flow is self-constituting — seems to risk either circularity (the flow constitutes itself) or an infinite regress (the flow requires a meta-flow to be temporal, and so on). Zahavi's defense of the self-constituting character of the flow is plausible, but the worry is not fully dissolved (Zahavi 2003: 83–87).

The second difficulty is the problem of **individuation**. Retention is supposed to hold the just-past as still phenomenally present but as past. But what distinguishes the retained moment from any other moment that might be "present" in memory? Husserl's answer — that retention is a distinct mode of intentionality — is correct in identifying the distinction, but it does not fully explain how retention is possible. What is the phenomenological difference between the retained tone and a momentarily imagined tone, both of which are "present" only as mental objects?

A third difficulty concerns the **applicability of the analysis** to complex temporal objects. Husserl's account works most naturally for short temporal sequences — a five-note melody, a brief spoken word. It is less clear how it accounts for our temporal grasp of longer temporal wholes — a symphony, a conversation, a day. Secondary retention (memory) must play a larger role here, but Husserl does not fully develop the relationship between primary retention and the higher-level temporal synthesis required for grasping extended temporal structures.

## Husserl's Lasting Achievement

Despite these difficulties, Husserl's analysis stands as one of the deepest and most important investigations of temporal experience in the philosophical tradition. He identifies with precision a feature of consciousness — its intrinsic temporal structure, the way in which the living present stretches across retention and protention — that no empirical science of the time was in a position to study, and that remains centrally important for philosophy of mind today.

The diagram is not merely a pedagogical tool. It is a theoretical instrument that captures the topology of temporal experience: the way in which the past is genuinely present (in retention) while being genuinely past (as the object of a retentional act), and the way in which the future is genuinely present (in protention) while being genuinely future (as the object of a protentional act directed toward what has not yet arrived). This topology — the extended, three-part structure of the living present — is Husserl's enduring contribution to our understanding of temporal consciousness.
