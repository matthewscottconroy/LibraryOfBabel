# Section 3: Heidegger and the Temporality of Existence

Martin Heidegger was Husserl's student and, for a time, his closest philosophical collaborator. But when Heidegger published *Being and Time* in 1927, it became clear that he had moved in a fundamentally different direction. Where Husserl had analyzed time-consciousness — the temporal structure of the acts of an experiencing subject — Heidegger argues that this analysis, however careful, begins in the wrong place. The problem is not to explain how a consciousness constitutes temporal objects or how a mind structures its experience of time. The problem is to understand what it means for a human being — what Heidegger calls *Dasein* ("being-there") — to exist at all, and to show that the answer to this question is irreducibly temporal.

For Heidegger, we are not minds that happen to experience time. We are beings whose very mode of existence is temporal — beings who are always already thrown into a past they did not choose, projecting themselves toward a future they cannot fully anticipate, in a present constituted by their commitments and encounters with the world. Temporality, on Heidegger's account, is not a feature of experience; it is the "ontological meaning of care," the deep structure of what it means to be a human being in the world.

This is a radical reorientation. To follow it, we must be willing to approach time not as an object for the gaze of a detached theorist, but as the structure of existence from the inside — from within a life that is always already engaged, always already concerned, always already finite.
