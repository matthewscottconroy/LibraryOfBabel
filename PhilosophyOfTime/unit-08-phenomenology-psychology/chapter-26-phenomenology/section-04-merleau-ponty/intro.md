# Section 4: Merleau-Ponty and Embodied Time

Maurice Merleau-Ponty's *Phenomenology of Perception*, published in 1945, is among the most important works in the phenomenological tradition. Its central claim — that the perceiving subject is not a pure consciousness but an embodied being, a "motor intentionality" that engages with the world through the body — transforms every major topic that phenomenology had addressed, and the analysis of time is no exception.

Merleau-Ponty's critique of Husserl is not as radical as Heidegger's, but it is in some ways more concrete. Where Heidegger argues that the analysis of consciousness must be replaced by an analysis of *Dasein*'s mode of being, Merleau-Ponty argues that the analysis of consciousness must be replaced by — or at least grounded in — an analysis of the lived body (*corps propre*). Temporal experience, on Merleau-Ponty's account, is not constituted by a transcendental subject performing acts of retention and protention; it is lived through a body that is always already oriented in a practical situation, shaped by habits and motor patterns that reach back into the past and forward into anticipated action.

This is a claim that deserves to be felt before it is analyzed. Sit quietly and notice how your body is already positioned in time: the slight anticipatory lean in your muscles as you wait for something; the way a habitual gesture carries the history of a thousand repetitions; the way an unfamiliar posture feels temporally disorienting. The body is not merely a vehicle for a temporal mind — it is itself a temporal organization, a living archive of past experience and a practical orientation toward the future.
