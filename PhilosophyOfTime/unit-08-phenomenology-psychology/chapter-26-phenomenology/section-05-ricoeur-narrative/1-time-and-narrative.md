# Time and Narrative: The Aporia and the Response

## The Aporia of Time

In the first chapter of *Time and Narrative*, Volume 1 (1984), Ricoeur confronts what he calls the fundamental "aporia of time." The aporia is not a single puzzle but a family of related difficulties that arise whenever we try to give a complete philosophical account of time. Ricoeur illustrates it through a careful reading of two foundational texts: Augustine's *Confessions*, Book XI, and Aristotle's *Physics*, Book IV.

Augustine's analysis reveals that time, insofar as it is experienced, is a "distension of the soul" (*distentio animi*). Past, present, and future are not external realities but modes of the present soul: the "present of the past" (memory), the "present of the present" (attention), and the "present of the future" (expectation). This is a profound phenomenological insight: time is not merely "out there" in the world but is constituted in and through the experiencing subject. But Augustine's analysis generates an aporia: if time is a distension of the soul, what is the relationship between this subjective time and the objective time of the world — the time of the sun's course, of decay, of history? Augustine finds himself drawn in two directions at once, and the tension is never fully resolved.

Aristotle's analysis in the *Physics* moves in the opposite direction: time is the "number of motion with respect to before and after." This is a cosmological account: time is a feature of the world, measured by the regular motions of the heavens. But Aristotle's account generates its own aporia: number requires a counter, and a counter requires a soul. So even the cosmological account of time seems to require reference to a subjective experience of counting and registering before-and-after (Ricoeur 1984: 7–22).

The lesson Ricoeur draws is that these two perspectives — the subjective/phenomenological and the objective/cosmological — are both indispensable and irreducible to each other. Every attempt to give a complete account of time from one perspective alone runs into difficulties that can only be addressed by reintroducing the other perspective. The aporia of time is not a problem to be solved but a tension to be negotiated.

## Narrative as Response

Ricoeur's central claim is that narrative is the primary way in which human beings negotiate this aporia. We cannot resolve the tension between subjective and objective time by philosophical analysis, but we can *configure* time — give it a human shape — through the act of story-telling. Narrative is the medium through which we make time livable (Ricoeur 1984: 52).

The key concept is *mimesis* — a term Ricoeur borrows from Aristotle's *Poetics*, where it means the imitation or representation of action in narrative. Ricoeur argues that mimesis is not a single operation but a threefold one, and that the three phases of mimesis correspond to three different relationships between narrative and temporal experience.

**Mimesis I** (*prefiguration*): Before any narrative is constructed, experience is already "pre-figured" — it is already structured in ways that make it amenable to narrative representation. Human action has a temporal character (before and after), a symbolic dimension (meanings that actions carry in their cultural context), and a narrative pre-understanding (we already implicitly understand action in terms of plots, motives, consequences, and so on). Experience is already, in this sense, "proto-narrative": it has the structural features that narrative will articulate more explicitly.

**Mimesis II** (*configuration*): Narrative proper begins with what Ricoeur calls the *configurational act* — the operation of "emplotment" (*mise en intrigue*). Emplotment is the act by which a narrative takes a diversity of events and characters and weaves them into a unified, intelligible whole with a beginning, middle, and end. The plot is not merely a sequence of events but a transformation: it draws together heterogeneous elements — events, characters, circumstances, consequences — and configures them into a narrative whole that is "followable," that makes sense as a story (Ricoeur 1984: 54–77).

The act of configuration is, Ricoeur argues, a response to the aporia of time: it does not resolve the tension between subjective and objective time but holds them together in a new synthesis. A narrative is both objective (it follows a sequence of events) and subjective (it is a configured interpretation from a particular perspective). It is both temporal (it unfolds in time) and atemporal (it grasps a temporal sequence as a whole, in a single act of comprehension). In narrative, time becomes human time — time organized according to human concerns, shaped by human meaning, and directed toward the future that the ending of the story discloses.

**Mimesis III** (*refiguration*): The narrative does not end when the story is told. It continues in the act of reading or hearing — what Ricoeur calls the "intersection of the world of the text and the world of the reader." When a reader engages with a narrative, their own lived experience is *refigured* — reorganized, enriched, challenged — by the temporal configuration that the narrative offers. This is why stories matter: not merely as entertainment or as information, but as configurations of temporal experience that re-shape how the reader understands and lives their own time.

## The Claim Assessed

Ricoeur's account of the threefold mimesis is among the most influential ideas in late twentieth-century philosophy and literary theory. It establishes a principled connection between narrative and temporal experience: not merely the claim that we enjoy stories, but the deeper claim that narrative is the primary medium through which human beings configure time in a humanly intelligible way.

Several questions arise. First, how does the narrative configuration of time relate to the individual's lived temporal experience? Ricoeur's account seems to operate at the level of cultural products — stories, histories, novels — rather than at the level of the individual's ongoing self-understanding. The connection between the two levels requires further development, which Ricoeur provides in *Oneself as Another* (1992) and which we take up in the next section.

Second, is narrative truly necessary for the configuration of time, or is it merely one way among others? One might argue that music, ritual, and practical projects also configure time in humanly meaningful ways. Ricoeur does not deny this, but he insists that narrative has a special status because it is the form in which the relation between event-level time and life-level time is most explicitly and reflexively articulated.

Third, what of the aporia? Ricoeur is explicit that narrative does not resolve the aporia of time but provides a practical response to it: it allows us to live with the tension rather than eliminating it. This may seem like a disappointing conclusion, but Ricoeur argues that it is the most honest and most adequate conclusion available: the aporia of time is not a philosophical failure but a permanent feature of the human condition, to which narrative is the appropriate human response.
