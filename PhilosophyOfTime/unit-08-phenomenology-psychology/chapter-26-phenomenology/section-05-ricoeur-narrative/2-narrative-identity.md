# Narrative Identity: The Self as Story

## The Problem of Personal Identity Through Time

Who are you over time? This question might seem to belong to metaphysics — to the debates about personal identity that we will examine in Unit IX. But Ricoeur argues, in *Oneself as Another* (1992), that the question of personal identity through time cannot be answered by purely metaphysical analysis alone, because it is not merely a question about the persistence of an object but about the unity of a life as lived and understood from the inside.

The metaphysical question — what makes you now the same person as you were ten years ago? — typically invites answers in terms of psychological continuity, physical continuity, or some combination. These are important questions. But they do not, by themselves, address the question of what it means for a person to have a life that hangs together — to be someone with a coherent self-understanding, capable of making and keeping commitments, capable of being held responsible for past actions, capable of having genuine projects that extend over time. These are features of a self that is not merely numerically identical over time but that has a kind of qualitative unity — a unity of character, commitment, and self-understanding. This is what Ricoeur calls the problem of *narrative identity* (Ricoeur 1992: 140–168).

## Idem-Identity and Ipse-Identity

Ricoeur draws a fundamental distinction between two senses of the word "same" — or, since he is writing in French, between two senses of *idem* and *ipse*. 

**Idem-identity** is sameness in the sense of numerical identity and qualitative identity: the same entity, the same properties, the same substance. When we ask whether the ship in harbor today is the same ship as the ship that left last year, we are asking for idem-identity. When we ask whether you are the same person as you were ten years ago in the sense of being numerically identical, we are also asking for idem-identity.

**Ipse-identity** is selfhood — the identity of a self that consists not in remaining the same in the sense of idem, but in remaining true to itself in the sense of a promise or commitment. When you make a promise, you commit your future self to an action. The grounds for keeping the promise are not that your future self will be numerically identical to you (though it presumably will be) but that you — this self, this person — have given your word, and keeping your word is part of what it means to be who you are. Ipse-identity is the identity of the self as a subject of commitments (Ricoeur 1992: 121–125).

The distinction matters because the two kinds of identity can come apart. A person in the advanced stages of Alzheimer's disease may retain idem-identity (they are the same biological organism) while losing much of their ipse-identity (they can no longer maintain the commitments, projects, and self-understanding that constituted who they were). Conversely, a person can undergo radical change in personality and character — becoming, as we sometimes say, "a different person" — while retaining full idem-identity.

## Narrative as Mediating Idem and Ipse

Ricoeur's central claim is that narrative identity mediates between idem and ipse: it is the form in which the self achieves a unity that is neither the brute numerical identity of a thing nor the pure formal selfhood of a promise, but something richer — the coherence of a life story.

To have a narrative identity is to be able to tell a story of one's life that is both truthful (in the sense of conforming to the facts of one's history) and intelligible (in the sense of hanging together as a coherent narrative with discernible patterns, transitions, and significance). Narrative identity is not a fixed essence but an achievement — something constructed and reconstructed over time through the ongoing act of self-interpretation (Ricoeur 1992: 147–151).

This account captures something that purely metaphysical accounts miss: the fact that personal identity through time is not merely a matter of what obtains independently of one's self-understanding, but is partly constituted by one's ongoing interpretive relationship to one's own history. I am, in part, the person who tells this story about this life. Different stories about the same sequence of events can constitute different identities — not because the events change, but because narrative identity involves selection, emphasis, interpretation, and the attribution of meaning, all of which are acts of the interpreting self.

## The Narrative Self and Ethics

The concept of narrative identity has direct ethical implications, which Ricoeur develops at length in *Oneself as Another*. If personal identity is narrative identity, then the ethical life — the life of commitments, responsibilities, and ongoing concern for oneself and others — is not an optional addition to identity but constitutive of it. To be the kind of self that can make and keep promises is to be the kind of self that has a narrative: a past that carries commitments forward and a future toward which those commitments reach.

This connects the phenomenology of time directly to ethical philosophy. Heidegger had argued that authentic existence involves taking over one's thrownness and projecting toward the future with resolution. Ricoeur agrees that the temporal structure of selfhood is connected to authentic self-ownership, but he insists that this temporal structure is fundamentally narrative in form — it requires the kind of explicit self-interpretation that narrative enables. A merely biological or psychological account of continuity cannot explain why I am responsible for my past actions, or why I should care about my future self. Narrative identity can explain this, because it shows that the self who made the promise and the self who must keep it are unified by a shared story that makes both intelligible.

## Connections to the Preceding Analysis

Ricoeur's concept of narrative identity completes the arc of the phenomenological analyses we have traced in this chapter. Husserl shows how the living present is structured by retention and protention. Heidegger shows how temporal existence is the structure of care — the unified reaching of *Dasein* toward its future, its past, and its present situation. Merleau-Ponty shows how temporal experience is grounded in the embodied, habituated body. Ricoeur shows how these different levels of temporal experience are integrated, at the level of a human life, by the practice of narrative self-interpretation.

Each analysis is partial, and each requires supplementation by the others. The lived present of Husserl is the ground-level temporal structure that makes any experience possible. Heidegger's temporality is the structure of a whole human life oriented toward its end. Merleau-Ponty's embodied time is the concrete, habituated body that carries the past into the present through motor engagement. Ricoeur's narrative identity is the interpretive synthesis that draws all of these levels together into the intelligible unity of a life.

To understand temporal experience in its full richness, we need all four levels — and perhaps more. What this chapter has aimed to show is that time, as experienced, is not a simple or transparent datum. It is a complex, multi-layered structure that phenomenological analysis can illuminate layer by layer, revealing at each level something that was hidden at the previous level. The phenomenology of time-consciousness is not finished; it is one of the most active areas of philosophical inquiry, and each of the thinkers we have studied has contributed essential resources for its continued development.
