# Section 5: Ricoeur, Narrative, and the Configuration of Time

Paul Ricoeur occupies a unique position in the phenomenological tradition. Deeply versed in Husserl, Heidegger, and the hermeneutical tradition, he is also a careful reader of narrative theory, literary criticism, and historiography. His three-volume *Time and Narrative* (1984–1988) is the most sustained attempt in twentieth-century philosophy to ask what human beings do with time — not merely how we experience it, but how we configure it, give it shape, make it livable.

Ricoeur begins from an observation that the preceding phenomenological accounts share: each of them runs into what he calls the "aporia of time" — the puzzling character of time that cannot be fully resolved by any phenomenological analysis alone. Augustine shows that time is in the soul; Husserl shows that time-consciousness has a tripartite structure; Heidegger shows that temporality is the meaning of care. But each of these analyses runs into difficulties that send us back to the question. Time remains, in some sense, resistant to full understanding.

Ricoeur's response is not to try yet another phenomenological analysis from scratch, but to look to a different region of human practice for what phenomenology alone cannot provide: narrative. We are, Ricoeur argues, story-telling animals. We configure time by telling stories — about ourselves, about our communities, about the world. And narrative does something for temporal experience that neither the analysis of consciousness nor the analysis of existence had been able to achieve: it gives time a human shape.
