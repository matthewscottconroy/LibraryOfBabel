# Chapter 27 Exercises: The Psychology of Time

## Analytical Exercises

**1. The Psychological Present and Husserl**
Empirical research has established that the psychological present spans approximately two to three seconds. Husserl's phenomenological analysis describes the living present as having a structure of retention-primal impression-protention. In what ways are these two accounts compatible? In what ways do they diverge? Does the empirical finding vindicate, challenge, or remain neutral with respect to Husserl's phenomenological claims? Defend your answer.

**2. Scalar Timing and Its Implications**
Gibbon's scalar timing theory proposes that the variability of duration estimates scales proportionally with the mean estimate (the scalar property). (a) Explain why this is called the "scalar" property, and explain its relationship to Weber's Law. (b) What does the scalar property tell us about the nature of the internal clock? (c) What philosophical implications, if any, do you think scalar timing theory has for the phenomenology of time? Does it support, challenge, or complement the phenomenological accounts?

**3. Prospective vs. Retrospective Duration Estimation**
Explain the asymmetry between prospective and retrospective duration estimation. Why does a boring interval feel long in the moment but seem short in retrospect, while an interesting interval may feel short in the moment but long in retrospect? Use the attentional gate model to explain the prospective asymmetry, and the memory-based (segmentation) model to explain the retrospective asymmetry. Then ask: what does this asymmetry reveal about the relationship between temporal experience and memory?

**4. Episodic Memory and Personal Identity**
Tulving argues that episodic memory involves "autonoetic consciousness" — the self-aware capacity to mentally travel back to a past experience. Explain this concept and explain why Tulving regards it as distinct from semantic memory retrieval. Then assess: is autonoetic consciousness necessary for personal identity over time? What would you say about a patient who loses episodic memory but retains semantic and procedural memory? Do they have a personal identity in the relevant sense?

**5. Mental Time Travel and the Future Self**
Research shows that imagining future events draws on the same neural systems as recalling past events (the "core network" for scene construction). (a) What does this tell us about the nature of future imagination? (b) Parfit (1984) argues that the "future self" is not a separate person to whom we stand in a special relation, but a construction. Does the psychology of mental time travel support or undermine Parfit's philosophical claim? (c) What implications might this have for the rationality of long-term prudential concern?

**6. Temporal Disorders and the Structure of Normal Experience**
Schizophrenia involves disturbances of temporal experience that phenomenological psychiatrists have characterized as a fundamental disruption of the "living present." ADHD involves characteristic impairments in prospective memory and temporal self-regulation. Depression involves the phenomenological "stopping" of time. Choose one of these conditions and explain (a) what the characteristic temporal disturbances are, (b) what mechanism is thought to produce them, and (c) what they reveal about the structure of normal temporal experience that would otherwise be transparent to us.

**7. Piaget and the Nongiven Character of Time**
Piaget's experiments showed that children below approximately seven to eight years do not clearly distinguish duration from distance or speed. What philosophical significance does this finding have? Does it show that temporal experience is a cognitive "construction"? If temporal cognition develops through stages, what does this imply about the phenomenological claim that the structure of the living present is an a priori feature of any consciousness? Is there a coherent position that acknowledges the developmental character of temporal cognition while maintaining a phenomenological account?

**8. Cultural Variation and Temporal Universalism**
Boroditsky's research suggests that the spatial metaphors through which time is linguistically expressed shape temporal thinking. Aymara speakers gesture forward when discussing the past, backward when discussing the future. Hall's distinction between monochronic and polychronic cultures suggests systematic differences in the experience and valuation of time across cultures. Do these findings show that temporal experience is culturally constructed, or merely that temporal cognition and orientation are culturally shaped? Is there a meaningful distinction between these two claims?

## Comparative Essay Exercises

**9. Memory, Anticipation, and the Asymmetry of Time**
Why do we remember the past but not the future? This seems trivially true, but the psychology of mental time travel complicates it: both memory and future imagination draw on the same cognitive systems. Is the apparent asymmetry merely a matter of what information is available (we have traces of the past but not of the future), or does it reflect something deeper about temporal experience? How does the philosophy of time (the B-theory, A-theory, growing block) bear on this question?

**10. Fear, Flow, and the Transformation of Time**
Fear makes time expand (in retrospect); flow makes time contract (in the moment). Both involve transformations of temporal experience in which ordinary temporal structure is disrupted. Using both empirical and phenomenological resources, analyze what these two experiences share and how they differ. What do they reveal about the relationship between attention, emotion, and temporal experience?

## Observational and Experiential Lab Exercises

**11. Duration Estimation Experiment (Lab)**
This exercise has two parts and should be conducted with a partner.

*Part A: Prospective estimation.* Sit quietly with nothing to do for exactly two minutes (measured by your partner with a timer, without telling you when the two minutes are up). You should indicate when you think two minutes have elapsed. Record your estimate. Repeat with an interval of five minutes. Then perform the same exercise while listening to an engaging podcast or music. Compare your estimates.

*Part B: Retrospective estimation.* After an ordinary one-hour period of studying or working, ask yourself how long it felt like without checking the clock. Then deliberately spend one hour doing something repetitive and unstimulating. Again estimate duration retrospectively. Compare the two estimates.

Write a reflection addressing: (a) Were your estimates consistent with the predictions of the attentional gate model (prospective) and the memory-density model (retrospective)? (b) What factors seemed most important in shaping your estimates? (c) Does the asymmetry between prospective and retrospective estimates that the theory predicts match your experience?

**12. Episodic Memory and Autonoetic Consciousness (Lab)**
Choose three specific memories: one from early childhood (before age eight), one from adolescence, and one from the recent past (within the last year). Recall each as vividly as possible and then answer the following questions about each:

(a) In recalling this memory, do you have a sense of "being there" — of re-experiencing the event — or do you have a sense of knowing what happened without the phenomenological sense of re-experiencing?
(b) How confident are you in the accuracy of the memory? Are there aspects that feel uncertain or reconstructed?
(c) Can you identify your emotional state during the original event? Can you identify your emotional state now, recalling it?
(d) Does the memory feel like "yours" — like something you lived through — or does it feel more like a story you have heard about yourself?

Write a short reflection on what these questions reveal about the autonoetic character of episodic memory and about the constructive character of memory. Does your experience confirm or challenge Tulving's account?

**13. Temporal Field in Waiting and Flow (Lab)**
Find two occasions in the same week: (a) an occasion when you are absorbed in an enjoyable or challenging activity for at least thirty minutes, and (b) an occasion when you are waiting, bored, or doing something unstimulating for at least thirty minutes.

During each, check the clock at regular intervals (every five or ten minutes) and note: How long did that interval seem to last? Are you attending to the passage of time, or is time "in the background"? After each occasion, estimate retrospectively how long the entire period lasted.

Write a reflection analyzing both experiences using the theoretical frameworks from this chapter (attentional gate model, memory-density model, Csikszentmihalyi's flow, phenomenological field of presence). What can you conclude from your data about the relationship between activity, attention, and temporal experience?
