## Annotated Entries

**Tulving, Endel. *Elements of Episodic Memory*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1983.**
The foundational text for Tulving's distinction between episodic and semantic memory. Tulving develops the concept of autonoetic consciousness and its relationship to temporal self-location, providing a rigorous empirical framework for thinking about memory and personal identity. This is a demanding but rewarding book that connects psychological research on memory to philosophical questions about selfhood and time.

**Fraisse, Paul. "Perception and Estimation of Time." *Annual Review of Psychology* 35 (1984): 1–36.**
An accessible and comprehensive survey of the empirical literature on time perception by one of the field's founding figures. Fraisse covers the psychological present, duration estimation, filled vs. empty intervals, and the role of attention and emotion in temporal experience. An excellent starting point for readers new to the empirical study of time perception.

**Buhusi, Catalin V., and Warren H. Meck. "What Makes Us Tick? Functional and Neural Mechanisms of Interval Timing." *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 6, no. 10 (2005): 755–765.**
The definitive review article on the neural basis of interval timing, by the two researchers most responsible for identifying the basal ganglia-dopaminergic system as the core neural implementation of the internal clock. Essential for readers interested in the neural underpinnings of duration estimation, and accessible to readers without a strong neuroscience background.

**Zacks, Jeffrey M., et al. "Event Perception: A Mind/Brain Perspective." *Psychological Bulletin* 133, no. 2 (2007): 273–293.**
The foundational paper for Event Segmentation Theory, providing both the theoretical framework and a review of the empirical evidence. Zacks and colleagues show how the continuous stream of experience is segmented into discrete events, and how event boundaries are associated with enhanced memory encoding and specific neural signatures. A rich and accessible paper that connects directly to philosophical questions about the structure of temporal experience.

**Schacter, Daniel L., and Donna Rose Addis. "The Cognitive Neuroscience of Constructive Memory: Remembering the Past and Imagining the Future." *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 362 (2007): 773–786.**
A review article demonstrating the neural and cognitive overlap between episodic memory and future imagination — one of the key papers establishing the "core network" for mental time travel. Schacter and Addis argue that the constructive character of memory reflects its function as a simulation system that uses past experience to anticipate and plan for future events.

**Csikszentmihalyi, Mihaly. *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience*. New York: Harper and Row, 1990.**
The accessible original presentation of flow theory, based on decades of experience sampling research across cultures and activities. The temporal phenomenology of flow is treated throughout, and the implications for human wellbeing and motivation are developed in detail. An important work at the intersection of positive psychology and phenomenology.

**Minkowski, Eugène. *Lived Time*. Translated by Nancy Metzel. Evanston: Northwestern University Press, 1970.**
The foundational work of phenomenological psychiatry on temporal experience. Minkowski's analysis of how schizophrenia involves a fundamental disturbance of lived time — a loss of "vital temporal contact" with the present and with the future — anticipates and shapes much subsequent empirical work. Essential reading for anyone interested in the connections between temporal experience and psychopathology.

**Piaget, Jean. *The Child's Conception of Time*. Translated by A. J. Pomerans. New York: Basic Books, 1969.**
Piaget's account of the developmental stages through which children come to understand time as an independent dimension, distinct from space and quantity of change. The experimental paradigms are clearly described and the results are striking. While Piaget's broader stage theory has been refined and challenged, this book remains essential reading for developmental accounts of temporal cognition.

**James, William. *The Principles of Psychology*. 2 vols. New York: Henry Holt, 1890.**
Chapter 15 of Volume 1, "The Perception of Time," contains James's classic account of the "specious present" and remains one of the most vivid and philosophically rich discussions of the psychological present. James's account anticipates many of the key distinctions in subsequent work and is exemplary as a contribution that combines psychological observation with philosophical analysis.

**Boroditsky, Lera. "Does Language Shape Thought? Mandarin and English Speakers' Conceptions of Time." *Cognitive Psychology* 43 (2001): 1–22.**
The key empirical paper for Boroditsky's research on linguistic relativity and temporal cognition. The experimental design is elegant and the results are clear: linguistic differences in temporal metaphor correspond to systematic differences in temporal reasoning. An important contribution to the debate about whether cultural and linguistic differences in temporal orientation reflect merely different frameworks for interpreting common experience, or something more fundamental.

**Suddendorf, Thomas. *The Gap: The Science of What Separates Us from Other Animals*. New York: Basic Books, 2013.**
An accessible and engaging treatment of mental time travel and other distinctively human cognitive capacities, including their evolutionary origins and their presence (or absence) in other animals. Suddendorf's treatment of mental time travel is the most thorough available for a general audience, and his comparative perspective illuminates what is philosophically distinctive about the human relationship to time.
