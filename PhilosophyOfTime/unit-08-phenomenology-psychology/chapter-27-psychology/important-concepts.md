# Important Concepts: Chapter 27

**Attentional Gate Model**: A cognitive model of prospective duration estimation proposing that a mental "gate" controls the flow of pulses from a pacemaker to an accumulator in the internal clock. When attention is directed toward time itself, the gate is more open and more pulses accumulate, making the interval feel longer. When attention is directed away from time (to an engaging task), fewer pulses accumulate, making the interval feel shorter. The model explains why boredom makes time drag and absorption makes it fly.

**Autonoetic Consciousness**: Tulving's term for the form of self-awareness that accompanies episodic memory — the phenomenological sense of being temporally located, of "being there" in the recalled event, that distinguishes episodic recollection from merely knowing facts about the past. Autonoetic consciousness enables temporal self-location: the awareness of oneself as a subject embedded in a personal history with a before and after.

**Circadian Clock**: The endogenous biological clock centered on the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, which generates approximately twenty-four-hour rhythms in physiology and behavior. The circadian clock is entrained by light-dark cycles and other environmental cues, and it governs the timing of sleep, temperature, hormone secretion, and many other processes. Its relationship to the subjective experience of the passage of days is an underexplored area of temporal psychology.

**Core Network for Mental Time Travel**: The set of brain regions — including the hippocampus, medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and lateral temporal-parietal areas — that are consistently activated by both episodic memory retrieval and future imagination. The convergence of memory and imagination on the same neural network supports the hypothesis that both are applications of a general capacity for "scene construction" — the ability to mentally construct and inhabit a spatial-temporal scene.

**Dopaminergic Modulation of Timing**: The role of dopamine in interval timing, established by studies showing that dopamine agonists speed up the internal clock (leading to overestimation of duration) and dopamine antagonists slow it down. This pharmacological sensitivity connects temporal perception to the brain's reward and motivation systems, suggesting that subjective duration is not a neutral measurement but is linked to the value and significance of the ongoing situation.

**Episodic Memory**: Tulving's term for the memory system that stores records of personally experienced events — memories with a what, where, and when that can be re-experienced from the inside. Episodic memory is characterized by autonoetic consciousness, contextual richness, and the sense of "mental time travel" back to the original event. It is contrasted with semantic memory (context-free factual knowledge) and procedural memory (skills and habits).

**Event Segmentation Theory (EST)**: Zacks and colleagues' theory that the cognitive system continuously segments the stream of experience into discrete events by detecting prediction errors — moments when incoming information is inconsistent with the current event model. Event boundaries are experienced as temporal demarcations and are associated with enhanced memory encoding. EST provides an account of how experience is organized into meaningful units at a timescale larger than the psychological present.

**Filled Duration Illusion**: The finding that a time interval filled with events or stimuli is perceived as longer than an objectively equal empty interval. The direction of this effect reverses between prospective (ongoing) and retrospective (past) estimation: filled intervals feel short in the moment (because attention is diverted from time) but long in retrospect (because many events were encoded in memory). The asymmetry reveals that prospective and retrospective temporal estimation involve different cognitive mechanisms.

**Flow**: Csikszentmihalyi's term for the psychological state of complete absorption in an optimally challenging activity, in which the sense of self, time, and effort disappears. In flow, temporal experience contracts dramatically: hours pass as if they were minutes. Flow is associated with reduced temporal self-monitoring, peak performance, and high levels of intrinsic motivation. Its temporal phenomenology is philosophically interesting as a case of extreme present-absorption contrasting with Heidegger's future-directed authentic existence.

**Internal Clock Model**: The dominant theoretical framework for duration estimation, proposing a pacemaker-accumulator mechanism in which a "pacemaker" generates regular pulses that are counted by an "accumulator." Duration is estimated by comparing accumulated pulse counts with values stored in reference memory. The model is implemented neurally primarily in the basal ganglia-dopaminergic circuit (for seconds-to-minutes intervals) and the cerebellum (for millisecond intervals).

**Mental Time Travel**: The cognitive capacity to project oneself mentally backward to re-experience past events (episodic memory) or forward to pre-experience anticipated future ones (episodic future thinking). Mental time travel involves subjective time (temporal self-location), self-projection (the sense of being the subject of the remembered or imagined experience), and autonoetic consciousness. It appears to depend on the same neural systems (the "core network") for both backward and forward projection.

**Monochronic vs. Polychronic Cultures**: Hall's distinction between cultures that treat time as a linear, segmented resource to be scheduled and managed one item at a time (monochronic) and cultures that treat time as a flexible medium in which multiple activities and relationships are interwoven (polychronic). The distinction has been criticized for overgeneralizing, but it captures real and measurable differences in temporal orientation across cultural groups.

**Prospective Memory**: The capacity to remember to carry out a future intention at the right time or occasion — remembering to take medication at a certain hour, or to pass on a message when you next see a particular person. Prospective memory is impaired in depression, ADHD, and certain forms of prefrontal damage, and its failure is one of the most practically significant forms of memory error in everyday life.

**Psychological Present (Specious Present)**: The temporal interval that is experienced as "now" — the window of experienced simultaneity or immediate succession within which events are integrated into a single present. Empirical research has established this window at approximately two to three seconds. The term "specious present" (introduced by E. R. Clay and made famous by William James) reflects the view that the "present" is, in a sense, illusory: it encompasses an interval that is, from the perspective of physics, already past by the time it is experienced.

**Scalar Property**: The finding that the standard deviation of temporal estimates scales proportionally with the mean estimate — so that the coefficient of variation (SD/mean) is approximately constant across a wide range of intervals. The scalar property is the temporal analogue of Weber's Law and is the defining characteristic of scalar timing theory. It implies that our temporal precision is proportional rather than absolute: we are equally good (or bad) at timing in relative terms, regardless of the absolute interval.

**Semantic Memory**: Tulving's term for the memory system storing general world knowledge — facts, concepts, linguistic meanings, and categorical knowledge — that is not tied to any specific episode of acquisition. Semantic memory retrieval involves a sense of familiarity and certainty rather than the phenomenological re-experiencing characteristic of episodic recollection. Semantic memory is more resistant to hippocampal damage than episodic memory.

**Temporal Self-Regulation**: Barkley's concept of the capacity to organize behavior across time — to maintain future goals in mind against immediate temptations, use anticipation of consequences to guide present action, and retrospectively evaluate one's behavior. Impairment of temporal self-regulation is proposed as the core deficit in ADHD, explaining the characteristic pattern of impulsivity, distractibility, and difficulty with long-term planning.

**Tachypsychia**: The phenomenon of time seeming to slow dramatically during episodes of acute fear or life-threatening situations. Research suggests this is a retrospective distortion: fear activates the amygdala, enhancing memory encoding and producing a dense, richly detailed memory of the event. When this dense memory is retrospectively estimated, the interval is judged as longer than it actually was. The experience is real but the mechanism is memorial rather than involving genuine slowing of temporal experience in the moment.
