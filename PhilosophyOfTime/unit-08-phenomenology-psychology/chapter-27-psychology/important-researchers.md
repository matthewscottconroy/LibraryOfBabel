# Important Researchers: Chapter 27

**Endel Tulving (1927–2023)**: Estonian-Canadian cognitive neuroscientist and psychologist, widely regarded as the most influential figure in the empirical study of human memory. His distinction between episodic and semantic memory, developed from the early 1970s and systematized in *Elements of Episodic Memory* (1983), transformed the field. His subsequent development of the concept of autonoetic consciousness and mental time travel gave the study of episodic memory a rich phenomenological dimension and connected empirical research on memory to fundamental questions in philosophy of mind. He received the Gairdner International Award in 1994 and the Killam Prize in 2005.

**Ernst Pöppel (born 1940)**: German neuropsychologist whose experimental work in the 1970s and beyond established the approximately three-second window as a fundamental unit of temporal integration in human cognition. His 1978 paper identifying this window was a landmark in temporal psychology, and his subsequent work has explored the relationship between temporal integration, consciousness, and neurological conditions. Pöppel's work bridges neuropsychology and phenomenology, and he has engaged directly with questions about the relationship between his empirical findings and Husserlian phenomenology.

**John Gibbon (1934–2001)**: American behavioral neuroscientist whose 1977 paper on "Scalar Expectancy Theory and Weber's Law in Animal Timing" established the theoretical framework that has dominated the study of interval timing for four decades. Gibbon's mathematical formalization of the scalar property — the finding that timing variability scales proportionally with the timed interval — gave the field a precise quantitative framework and generated a large body of experimental predictions, most of which were confirmed. His work on the neural basis of timing, conducted partly in collaboration with Warren Meck, identified the basal ganglia-dopaminergic system as central to interval timing.

**Warren Meck (1953–2020)**: American neuroscientist who was the leading figure in the neural investigation of interval timing. Meck's pharmacological and lesion studies in rats established the role of dopamine and the basal ganglia in the "start-stop" control of the internal clock, and his neuroimaging work with human subjects confirmed and extended these findings. His framework for understanding timing in terms of "clock," "memory," and "decision" processes has been widely adopted. He was also a pioneer in understanding how timing is disrupted in neurological and psychiatric conditions.

**Paul Fraisse (1911–1996)**: French psychologist who made foundational contributions to the psychology of time, including research on the psychological present, duration estimation, and temporal perception. His *The Psychology of Time* (1963, translated from French as 1984) provided the first comprehensive synthesis of the empirical literature on temporal cognition. Fraisse's work on the filled duration illusion and its relationship to attention and processing was particularly influential.

**Jeffrey Zacks (born 1971)**: American cognitive neuroscientist and developer, with colleagues, of Event Segmentation Theory (EST). His research program has used behavioral experiments, neuroimaging, and computational modeling to investigate how the cognitive system segments the continuous stream of experience into discrete events. His book *Flicker: Your Brain on Movies* (2015) brings the science of event segmentation to a general audience. EST has become one of the most productive frameworks in the cognitive science of temporal experience.

**Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1934–2021)**: Hungarian-American psychologist who developed the concept of "flow" — the state of optimal experience characterized by complete absorption in a challenging and intrinsically rewarding activity. His book *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* (1990) is a landmark contribution to positive psychology. The temporal phenomenology of flow — the disappearance of the sense of time's passage — has been widely studied and connects empirical psychology to phenomenological and existential themes.

**Thomas Suddendorf (born 1966)**: Australian developmental and comparative psychologist who, with Michael Corballis, introduced the concept of "mental time travel" to the empirical psychology of cognition. His work on the comparative cognition of mental time travel — asking whether other animals share this capacity, and what its evolutionary origins might be — has been highly influential. His book *The Gap: The Science of What Separates Us from Other Animals* (2013) provides an accessible account of mental time travel and its significance for understanding human uniqueness.

**Lera Boroditsky (born 1975)**: American cognitive scientist whose research on linguistic relativity — particularly on the relationship between temporal language and temporal thought — has been widely influential. Her experiments demonstrating that the spatial metaphors used to talk about time in different languages correspond to systematic differences in temporal reasoning have contributed to ongoing debates about linguistic relativity and the relationship between language, thought, and experience.

**Jean Piaget (1896–1980)**: Swiss psychologist and epistemologist whose developmental account of temporal cognition — presented in *The Child's Conception of Time* (1969) — remains a landmark in the field. Piaget's claim that children below approximately seven to eight years conflate temporal duration with spatial extent and quantity of motion, and his broader theory of cognitive development through stages, have shaped the developmental psychology of temporal cognition for more than half a century, even where his specific claims have been refined or challenged.
