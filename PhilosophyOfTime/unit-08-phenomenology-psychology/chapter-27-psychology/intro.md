# Chapter 27: The Psychology of Time

The question of what time feels like is inseparable, for any complete account of temporal experience, from the question of how the brain and cognitive systems actually implement that experience. Phenomenology can describe the structures of temporal experience with great precision — but it does not, by itself, explain why the psychological present spans roughly two to three seconds rather than two milliseconds or two minutes, why a filled interval feels longer than an empty one, why depression makes time seem to stop, or why some patients with amnesia can still navigate the immediate temporal flow while being unable to form new long-term memories. These are empirical questions, and they require empirical investigation.

This chapter surveys the psychology and neuroscience of temporal experience. The field has developed rapidly since the late twentieth century, and it now offers a rich picture of the cognitive systems that generate our sense of time — their normal operation, their failures, and their development. What emerges is both confirming and complicating for the phenomenological analyses of the previous chapter: confirming, in that the empirical findings often vindicate the basic structures that phenomenologists identified; complicating, in that the mechanisms revealed are sometimes far stranger, and far more variable, than the phenomenological accounts had suggested.

A central theme of this chapter is the plurality of temporal experience. There is not one "sense of time" but many: the sense of the immediate present (the specious present), the estimation of short intervals, the tracking of events and their sequential order, the mental simulation of past and future via episodic memory and mental time travel, and the longer-scale temporal orientation that shapes our sense of where we are in a day, a week, a life. These different systems can be dissociated by brain injury, drugs, and psychological conditions — evidence that they have distinct neural implementations and are not aspects of a single unified mechanism.

At the same time, there is something philosophically important in the convergences between phenomenological and psychological approaches. When Husserl describes the living present as an extended structure of retention-primal impression-protention, and experimental psychologists discover that temporal perception integrates events over a window of approximately two to three seconds, the convergence is striking. It does not prove that Husserl was right, but it suggests that the phenomenological and empirical approaches are tracking the same phenomenon from different directions.

This chapter will equip you to move between those directions with some fluency — to ask what the empirical findings imply for phenomenological claims, and what phenomenological analyses suggest about the interpretation of empirical findings.
