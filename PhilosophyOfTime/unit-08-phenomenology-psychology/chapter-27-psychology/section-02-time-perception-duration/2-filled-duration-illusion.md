# The Filled Duration Illusion, Attention, and Emotion

## The Filled Duration Illusion

One of the most robust and philosophically interesting findings in the psychology of duration estimation is the **filled duration illusion**: a time interval that is filled with events or stimuli is perceived as longer than an equal interval that is empty or homogeneous, even when objective duration is identical. This effect has been demonstrated across a wide range of conditions — filled vs. empty auditory intervals, intervals filled with more vs. fewer discrete events, intervals filled with familiar vs. novel content — and it is among the most reliable findings in time perception research (Fraisse 1984).

The phenomenological reality of this illusion is something everyone can verify from experience. A two-minute wait in a comfortable room with interesting things to look at feels shorter than a two-minute wait staring at a blank wall — or does it? In fact, the empirical findings are more nuanced: the relationship between content and felt duration depends crucially on whether duration is estimated prospectively (while the interval is ongoing) or retrospectively (after the interval has passed). The asymmetry between prospective and retrospective duration estimation is itself one of the most important findings in the field.

## Prospective vs. Retrospective Duration Estimation

When you know you are being timed and are actively monitoring time's passage, you are making a **prospective** duration estimate. The cognitive theory most supported by evidence in this case is an **attentional gate** model: a mental "gate" controls the flow of pulses to the accumulator of the internal clock. When attention is focused on time itself — when you are watching the clock — the gate is open and more pulses accumulate; the interval feels longer. When attention is diverted to other tasks — when you are absorbed in interesting activity — the gate is partly closed and fewer pulses accumulate; the interval feels shorter. A boring, empty interval draws more attention to time and thus feels longer; an interesting, filled interval draws attention away from time and thus feels shorter (Block and Zakay 1997).

When you estimate duration retrospectively — looking back on how long something took after it is over — a different mechanism is engaged. Retrospective estimates appear to be based not on accumulated pulses but on the **number of distinct events, changes, or segments** that can be retrieved from memory for the interval. An interval filled with many distinct events will be remembered as longer, because more temporal landmarks and memory traces are available. An interval that was boring and undifferentiated will be remembered as shorter, because little of it was encoded in memory.

This asymmetry generates a striking pattern: an interval spent doing something boring and repetitive will feel long while you are doing it (because attention is forced back to time) but short in retrospect (because little was encoded in memory). Conversely, an interval spent doing something interesting and varied will feel short while you are doing it (because attention is diverted from time) but long in retrospect (because many rich events were encoded). This pattern is familiar from experience: a tedious afternoon at work seems to drag, but afterwards you may wonder where the week went; a vacation seems to fly by in the moment but fills a rich space in memory.

## Emotional States and Duration

Emotions have powerful effects on duration perception, and the mechanisms are related to but distinct from the attentional effects just described. The principal finding is that **arousal** tends to increase the perceived duration of intervals, while **positive affect** tends to make time seem to pass more quickly in the moment (but may not affect retrospective estimates in the same way).

The "time flies" phenomenon when we are absorbed in pleasurable activity has been the subject of empirical investigation: the evidence is that positive affect does not simply make time "feel fast" but rather leads to reduced monitoring of time, which reduces accumulated pulse counts in prospective timing (Angrilli et al. 1997). The relationship between mood and time perception is bidirectional: not only does mood affect time perception, but temporal experience feeds back into mood — feeling that time is "standing still" is a classic symptom of depression, while the sense that time is "flying by" is associated with engagement and positive affect.

**Fear** has a particularly striking effect on time perception. Numerous studies and first-person reports from car accidents, falls, and other near-death experiences suggest that time seems to "slow down" dramatically during episodes of intense fear. The leading explanation is that fear activates the amygdala and associated stress systems, which increase dopaminergic activity and thereby speed up the internal clock — causing more pulses to accumulate per objective unit of time, and thus producing an impression that more time has passed than actually has. The "slow motion" experience of frightening events may thus be a by-product of the brain's threat-response system rather than a genuine enhancement of temporal resolution (Stetson et al. 2007).

## Flow States and the Contraction of Time

At the opposite extreme from fear lies what Csikszentmihalyi (1990) calls **flow** — the state of complete absorption in a challenging and intrinsically rewarding activity. In flow, the balance between skill and challenge is optimal: the activity is neither too easy (producing boredom) nor too difficult (producing anxiety). In this state, time contracts dramatically: hours pass as if they were minutes, and the person has no awareness of time passing at all.

The psychology of flow connects to classical phenomenological themes. The temporal structure of flow is precisely the inverse of Heidegger's description of authentic temporality: in flow, the subject is entirely absorbed in the present activity, without any awareness of running ahead to death or taking over thrownness. Interestingly, Csikszentmihalyi does not present this as existential inauthenticity; he presents it as a paradigm case of positive human experience. Whether the temporal absorption of flow is a positive loss of reflective awareness or a problematic flight from temporality is a question that connects empirical psychology to existential philosophy in a direct and fruitful way.

## Boredom and the Expansion of Time

Boredom represents the phenomenological and psychological opposite of flow. When we are bored, time expands: minutes seem to stretch indefinitely, and the present moment becomes oppressively heavy. The psychology of boredom reveals something important about the relationship between temporal experience and meaning. Boredom is not simply the absence of stimulation; it is the experience of time as empty of significance — as passing without any connection to goals or values that matter (Eastwood et al. 2012).

This connection between temporal experience and meaning brings psychology and phenomenology into direct dialogue. Heidegger's analysis of boredom (*Langeweile* — literally "long while") in the 1929–30 lecture course published as *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* treats boredom not as a mere inconvenience but as a fundamental attunement that can reveal the structure of temporal existence. When bored, we are exposed to the "emptiness" of our existence in a way that ordinary concern and engagement conceal. The psychologist and the phenomenologist are thus, in an important sense, investigating the same phenomenon at different levels of description.
