# Mental Time Travel: Backward, Forward, and Its Philosophical Implications

## The Concept of Mental Time Travel

The concept of "mental time travel" was introduced by Thomas Suddendorf and Michael Corballis in a landmark 1997 paper proposing that the capacity to mentally project oneself backward to re-experience past events and forward to pre-experience anticipated future events is a uniquely human cognitive achievement — or, at least, a cognitive achievement that is far more developed in humans than in other animals (Suddendorf and Corballis 1997). Tulving (2002) subsequently endorsed and extended this concept, arguing that the capacity for mental time travel is inseparable from the autonoetic consciousness that characterizes episodic memory.

Mental time travel, as Suddendorf and Corballis characterize it, involves three components: (1) a subjective sense of time — the awareness that past and future are distinct from the present, and that there is a "journey" of some kind involved in reaching them; (2) a sense of self — the awareness of oneself as the subject of the remembered or anticipated experience; and (3) "autonoetic" awareness — the phenomenological character of re-experiencing or pre-experiencing, as opposed to merely knowing about, past and future events (Suddendorf and Corballis 2007).

## The Symmetry of Memory and Anticipation

One of the most striking findings of recent research is that the neural systems supporting episodic memory and future-directed imagination are surprisingly overlapping. Neuroimaging studies have consistently found that imagining future events activates many of the same brain regions as recalling past events — including the hippocampus, medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and lateral temporal and parietal areas (Schacter and Addis 2007).

This "core network" for mental time travel is thought to support the construction of mental simulations, whether directed toward the past or the future. The hippocampus appears to play a key role in "scene construction" — the ability to mentally create and inhabit a spatial scene — which underlies both the remembering of past places and events and the imagining of future ones.

The symmetry between memory and imagination has been confirmed by studies of patients with hippocampal damage. Such patients show not only the expected deficit in episodic memory but also a striking deficit in the ability to imagine future events: asked to imagine a scene they might experience tomorrow, they produce sparse, fragmented descriptions rather than the rich, spatially coherent scenes that healthy adults describe (Hassabis et al. 2007). This finding suggests that episodic memory and future imagination are not simply memory and its reverse; they are both applications of the same underlying capacity for constructing mentally inhabited scenes.

## Philosophical Implications of Mental Time Travel

The concept of mental time travel raises several philosophically important questions.

**Does mental time travel reveal anything about the ontological status of past and future?** On a block universe view, the past and future are equally real regions of the four-dimensional spacetime manifold; mental time travel is simply a cognitive simulation of other equally real temporal regions. On a presentist view, the past and future do not exist; mental time travel is, at best, a simulation of something that no longer exists (the past) or has not yet come to exist (the future). Interestingly, the experience of mental time travel is phenomenologically asymmetric in a way that is philosophically significant: we experience recalling the past as accessing something determinate and fixed (even if the memory is actually constructive), while we experience imagining the future as accessing something open and contingent. Whether this phenomenological asymmetry reflects a genuine ontological asymmetry or merely a cognitive one is a deep question that connects temporal psychology to temporal metaphysics.

**What does the constructive character of mental time travel imply for the unity of the self over time?** The finding that future imagination draws on the same constructive processes as episodic memory suggests that our sense of the "future self" is, in important ways, constructed — assembled from fragments of past experience, projected onto an imagined future scenario. This connects to Parfit's (1984) argument that the "future self" is not some separate, determinate entity to whom we stand in a special relation, but a partly fictional construction. If the future self is a construction, then the psychology of mental time travel provides empirical support for Parfit's philosophical point about the conventional character of personal identity over time.

**Are other animals capable of mental time travel?** This is an actively debated question in comparative cognition. The clearest candidates for mental time travel in non-human animals are corvids (jays and crows), which exhibit food-caching behaviors that require something like prospective planning and episodic-like memory. Clayton and Dickinson (1998) showed that western scrub jays cache food and later retrieve it in ways that suggest they remember what they cached, where, and when — the "what-where-when" of episodic memory. Whether this constitutes genuine mental time travel in Tulving's sense — involving the full phenomenological trappings of autonoetic consciousness — or a more limited form of temporal cognition remains contested.

**Cases of amnesia and their philosophical significance.** The philosophical implications of amnesia cases are rich and troubling. Patients like H.M. cannot form new episodic memories, which means that they cannot update their sense of personal identity to include recent events. Their sense of who they are is frozen at the time of their injury, while the world and the people around them continue to change. H.M. was unable to recognize his own reflection in a mirror in his later years, because his self-image was decades out of date. This case illustrates the intimate connection between episodic memory and personal identity: without the capacity to accumulate and integrate new episodic memories, the self cannot develop and cannot locate itself in the present.

At the same time, amnesia cases reveal the resilience of some aspects of selfhood in the absence of episodic memory. H.M. retained a consistent personality, a characteristic emotional tone, and a characteristic way of engaging with the world, even though he could not remember the events that had occurred since his injury. This suggests that personal identity is not constituted entirely by episodic memory — that there are dimensions of selfhood that persist through other mechanisms, including the embodied habits and character traits that Merleau-Ponty and Aristotle, respectively, would emphasize.
