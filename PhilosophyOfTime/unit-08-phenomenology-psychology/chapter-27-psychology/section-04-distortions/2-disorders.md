# Temporal Disorders: Schizophrenia, Depression, ADHD

## Schizophrenia and the Fragmentation of Time

Schizophrenia is among the most philosophically interesting conditions for the study of temporal experience, because phenomenological psychiatrists have long argued that disturbances of temporal experience are not merely a symptom alongside others but may be central to the core pathology of the condition.

The phenomenological psychiatrist Eugène Minkowski (1933) argued, in *Le Temps Vécu* (Lived Time), that schizophrenia involves a fundamental disturbance of "lived time" — specifically, a loss of the capacity for vital temporal contact with the present moment and an inability to project oneself into the future. The schizophrenic patient, on Minkowski's account, is unable to participate in the flowing movement of lived time; they are stuck, enclosed in a kind of eternal present without genuine connection to past or future.

More recent empirical work has confirmed and specified these phenomenological observations. Studies of timing performance in schizophrenia consistently find deficits in both retrospective and prospective duration estimation, in temporal order judgment, and in the ability to track rhythmic sequences (Stanghellini et al. 2016). Some patients report the subjective sense of "time standing still" — a terrifying experience of the present moment stretching without limit, without the normal sense of flow or movement. Others report a fragmentation of temporal experience: events seem disconnected, without the normal sense of before-and-after that makes experience coherent.

Neuroimaging studies suggest that these temporal disturbances are related to disruptions in the dopaminergic and cerebellar systems implicated in normal interval timing, as well as to abnormalities in the fronto-parietal networks responsible for temporal order and sequencing. But the phenomenological description suggests that something more fundamental is at stake: not just the malfunction of specific timing mechanisms but a disturbance of the basic temporal structure of consciousness itself — the Husserlian living present is disrupted at its root.

This is a philosophical hypothesis as well as a clinical description, and it connects the empirical study of schizophrenia directly to the phenomenological analyses of the previous chapter. If the retention-primal impression-protention structure of the living present is indeed the most fundamental structure of temporal consciousness, then conditions that disrupt this structure should produce not merely timing errors but a qualitative transformation of the whole character of experience — exactly what phenomenological psychiatrists have described in schizophrenia.

## Depression and the Stopping of Time

Depression produces a characteristic temporal distortion: time seems to slow dramatically, to become thick and resistant, and in severe cases, to stop altogether. The depressed person is unable to imagine a future — not merely because they expect bad things to happen, but because the future itself seems inaccessible, as if the temporal horizon has closed. The past, by contrast, may feel overwhelmingly present and inescapable: old failures, losses, and regrets seem vivid and immediate while positive memories fade.

Empirical studies confirm the phenomenological characterization. Depressed patients consistently overestimate the duration of intervals — time "passes slowly" in the sense that a given interval feels longer than it objectively is. This pattern is consistent with increased self-monitoring in depression (depressed people monitor the passage of time more closely, which, via the attentional gate mechanism, causes more "clock pulses" to accumulate). Studies of prospective memory — the ability to remember to carry out future intentions — also show impairment in depression, consistent with the phenomenological finding that future-directedness is disrupted.

The philosophical implications of depression's temporal distortions are profound. If, as Heidegger argues, authentic temporality is constituted by an orientated reaching toward the future (running ahead), depression represents, in clinical terms, what inauthenticity represents in existential-ontological terms: the collapse of future-directed temporal projection. The depressed person is unable to run ahead, unable to take over their thrownness, unable to inhabit the moment of vision — not by choice but by pathological necessity. This connection between clinical depression and existential inauthenticity does not mean that depression is simply "bad philosophy"; it means that the existential structures Heidegger describes have a neurobiological implementation that can be disrupted.

## Mania and the Acceleration of Time

Mania, the opposite pole of bipolar disorder, produces temporal distortions in the opposite direction: time seems to accelerate, the future seems vivid and accessible, and the sense of temporal urgency and possibility becomes overwhelming. The manic patient may feel that they can accomplish everything at once, that time is no object, that the present is filled with extraordinary significance and opportunity.

Empirical studies find that manic patients underestimate duration — time "passes quickly" in the sense that given intervals feel shorter than they are — and show characteristic increases in impulsivity and in sensitivity to immediate rewards relative to delayed ones. These findings are consistent with the dopaminergic theory of interval timing: mania is associated with elevated dopaminergic activity, which, on the scalar timing model, speeds up the internal clock and causes intervals to be systematically underestimated.

## ADHD and Temporal Estimation

Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) involves characteristic impairments in temporal cognition that go beyond simple inattention. Studies of children and adults with ADHD consistently find deficits in duration estimation, temporal order judgment, and prospective memory — the ability to remember to carry out future intentions (Barkley 1997). Barkley has argued that ADHD is fundamentally a disorder of "temporal self-regulation" — an impairment in the ability to organize behavior across time, to hold future goals in mind against immediate temptations, and to use anticipation of future consequences to guide present action.

If this characterization is correct, ADHD involves not just difficulties with attention and impulse control but a fundamental alteration in the temporal structure of experience and action. The inability to orient toward the future — to maintain future goals as motivationally effective — is, on Barkley's account, the core deficit that gives rise to the diverse behavioral symptoms of the condition.

The philosophical import of this characterization is that it connects clinical ADHD to the phenomenological analysis of temporality as the structure of care. Heidegger's *Dasein* is constituted by its orientation toward possibilities — its running ahead, its protention, its future-directedness. A condition that disrupts this future-directedness disrupts the unity of care, the structure of purposive agency that makes a human life hang together as a meaningful whole. The clinical study of ADHD thus illuminates, from an unexpected direction, what is at stake in the phenomenological analysis of authentic temporal existence.
