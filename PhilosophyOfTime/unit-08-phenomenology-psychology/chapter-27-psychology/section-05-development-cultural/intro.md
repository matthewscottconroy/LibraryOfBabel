# Section 5: Developmental and Cultural Dimensions of Temporal Experience

If the structures of temporal experience were entirely innate and universal — hard-wired features of human cognition that develop automatically and identically in all individuals across all cultures — then the preceding analyses would be close to complete. But the empirical evidence suggests a more complex picture. The capacity for temporal cognition develops across childhood through stages that are not fully in place until middle childhood, and the specific forms that temporal experience and temporal orientation take vary in systematic ways across cultures. Both of these facts — the developmental trajectory of temporal cognition and the cultural variability of temporal experience — have philosophical implications that deserve careful examination.

The developmental evidence tells us that certain forms of temporal cognition are not given in experience from the beginning but are achievements of a developing cognitive system — achievements that can be characterized, measured, and traced through stages. The cultural evidence tells us that even after development is complete, temporal experience is shaped by the cultural and linguistic environment in ways that go beyond mere "overlay" on a universal substrate. Together, these considerations suggest that temporal experience, however universal its most basic structures, is also partly constructed — shaped by development, culture, and linguistic practice in ways that matter philosophically.
