# Chapter 28 Exercises: The Passage of Time

## Analytical Exercises

**1. The Rate-of-Passage Argument**
Reconstruct the rate-of-passage argument (Williams 1951; Smart 1949) as carefully as you can. Then evaluate: is the argument sound? Does it show that temporal passage is incoherent, or merely that the phrase "rate of passage" requires careful handling? Consider the response that "one second per second" is an adequate answer (since time is its own standard of measurement) and the response that Skow proposes (that rate of passage is a primitive). Which, if any, succeeds?

**2. The Indexicality Response**
Explain the indexicality response to the rate-of-passage problem: the view that "now" is an indexical term, and the "movement" of the now is simply the fact that different times are picked out by "now" at different times of utterance. (a) Does this response dissolve the problem or merely relocate it? (b) Does it adequately account for the phenomenological experience of passage — the sense that we are being carried along by time? Defend your view.

**3. The No-Passage View**
Callender (2017) argues that the sense of temporal passage is generated by the asymmetry between our representations of the past and future, together with other cognitive factors, without any need for objective passage in the physical world. (a) Explain this view as precisely as you can. (b) Does it provide a satisfying explanation of the phenomenological experience of passage, or does it leave something out? (c) What is the relationship between the "cognitive illusion" of passage and the "genuine experience" of passage? Are these compatible?

**4. Skow's Moving Spotlight Theory**
Bradford Skow (2015) defends the moving spotlight theory: there is an objective property of being present that genuinely "moves" along the temporal sequence. (a) Explain the theory. (b) How does Skow respond to the rate-of-passage problem? Is his response convincing? (c) What is the cost of the theory in terms of undetectable physical structure (the preferred foliation), and is this cost acceptable?

**5. Primitive Passage and the Analogy with Consciousness**
The primitive passage thesis holds that temporal passage is irreducible — not explicable in terms of anything more fundamental, just as phenomenal consciousness may not be reducible to functional or physical properties. (a) Explain the analogy between temporal passage and phenomenal consciousness. (b) Does the analogy support the primitive passage thesis, or does it merely show that both phenomena are puzzling? (c) What would it mean to "accept" that passage is primitive? Does this amount to explaining anything?

**6. The Two-Level Account**
The "two-level" account proposed in this chapter holds that physics and phenomenology are legitimate autonomous domains of inquiry that describe different aspects of reality. (a) Explain the account. (b) What are its main advantages over the debunking strategy (option a) and the physics-revision strategy (option b)? (c) What are its main vulnerabilities, and how might they be addressed? (d) Do you find the account persuasive? Explain.

**7. The Phenomenology of Passage and Its Variations**
The experience of temporal passage varies across different states: it is barely noticeable in flow states, oppressively salient in depression and severe anxiety, and dramatically altered in fear. What does this variability tell us about the nature of the passage experience? Does it support the view that passage is a cognitive construction (since constructions can be disrupted) or is it compatible with the view that passage is real (since real features of the world can be perceived more or less clearly)?

**8. Temporal Passage and the A-Theory**
The A-theory holds that the distinction between past, present, and future is a genuine, objective, irreducible distinction — not analyzable in terms of the B-theory's earlier-later relations. How does the A-theory bear on the question of temporal passage? Does accepting A-theory commit one to objective temporal passage? Does accepting B-theory commit one to the no-passage view? Examine the relationship between these metaphysical positions and the question of passage.

## Observational and Experiential Lab Exercises

**9. Phenomenology of Flow (Lab)**
Set aside thirty to forty-five minutes for a structured experience of temporal passage. Divide your time as follows:

*First period* (ten to fifteen minutes): sit quietly with nothing to do and no phone. Pay close attention to the experience of time passing. Note: Is there a felt "movement" to the experience? In what direction? Does the present feel special in comparison to the past moments you remember and the future you anticipate? Try to describe the phenomenological texture of temporal passage as precisely as you can.

*Second period* (ten to fifteen minutes): engage in something you find genuinely absorbing — a puzzle, music, drawing, or reading something interesting. Try to continue paying attention to whether you experience temporal passage.

*Third period* (five minutes): sit quietly again. Compare your experience in the three periods.

Write a paragraph characterizing the differences. Does the absorbed state diminish the sense of passage, or merely relocate attention away from it? Does the return to the quiet state bring a strong sense of passage back? What does this variability suggest about the nature of the passage experience?

**10. The "Nowness" of the Present (Lab)**
Find a moment when you have a few minutes of quiet, undisturbed attention. Focus on the present moment — not on any thoughts about the past or future, but simply on what is occurring right now: sounds, sensations, the quality of the moment.

Try to identify, as specifically as possible, what gives this moment the quality of being "now" as opposed to being merely a remembered moment or an anticipated moment. Is there a felt quality of actuality, of "this is happening," that distinguishes the present from the past and future? Can you sustain attention on this quality? Does it fade when you try to focus on it directly?

Write a reflection on what you found. Connect your observations to the philosophical accounts of the phenomenology of passage discussed in this chapter. Does your experience support the view that "nowness" is a vivid feature of experience, or does it suggest that "nowness" is more elusive than it seems?

**11. Imagining the Block Universe (Lab)**
Philosophical thought experiments can be enriched by attempting to inhabit them phenomenologically. Try the following:

Attempt to imagine the world as described by the block universe view: a four-dimensional "block" of events, all equally real, none objectively more present or more actual than any other. All the moments of your life — past, present, and future — are equally real "parts" of you in this block. There is no flowing, no becoming, no special present; just an extended pattern of events arranged along the time dimension.

Now try to imagine inhabiting this world as a conscious being. What would experience be like if the block universe description were complete? Would experience feel the same as it does, or would something be missing? Can you even fully imagine experience from "within" the block universe, or does the imaginative exercise always reintroduce the sense of passage?

Write a reflection on what you found. Does the thought experiment support or challenge the two-level account? Does it suggest that the sense of passage is ineliminable from experience even when one intellectually accepts the block universe?
