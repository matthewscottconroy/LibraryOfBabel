## Annotated Entries

**Williams, Donald C. "The Myth of Passage." *Journal of Philosophy* 48, no. 15 (1951): 457–472.**
The locus classicus for the no-passage view and the rate-of-passage argument. Williams argues with admirable clarity that the "passage" of time is literally incoherent and that the appearance of flow is a myth generated by confused thinking. Even readers who reject his conclusion will benefit from confronting the argument in its sharpest form. Essential reading.

**Smart, J. J. C. "The River of Time." *Mind* 58, no. 232 (1949): 483–494.**
An independent statement of the rate-of-passage argument, slightly earlier than Williams's, with particularly clear treatment of the regress problem. Smart's paper also connects the no-passage view to a broader scientific realism about the physical world and a skepticism about metaphysical excess. An important companion to Williams.

**McTaggart, J. M. E. "The Unreality of Time." *Mind* 17, no. 68 (1908): 457–474.**
The foundational paper for the A-series/B-series distinction that structures the entire debate. McTaggart's argument that the A-series is both necessary for time and incoherent is famously contested, but the analytic framework he introduces remains the standard conceptual vocabulary of the field. Should be read by anyone who wants to understand the debate about temporal passage.

**Callender, Craig. *What Makes Time Special?* Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2017.**
The most comprehensive and accessible recent defense of the no-passage view, with a positive account of what makes time different from space without requiring objective A-theoretic passage. Callender's treatment of the cognitive sources of the sense of passage is particularly valuable, and his engagement with both physics and philosophy is exemplary. Required reading for the contemporary debate.

**Skow, Bradford. *Objective Becoming*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2015.**
The most careful recent defense of objective temporal passage, developing the moving spotlight theory and responding to the rate-of-passage objection. Skow's treatment is honest about the costs of his view (the undetectable preferred foliation) and rigorous in its engagement with physics. Required reading for anyone who wants to understand the best case for objective passage.

**Prosser, Simon. *Experiencing Time*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2016.**
A systematic philosophical treatment of the relationship between temporal experience and temporal metaphysics. Prosser argues with care and rigor that no objective feature of the world could produce the specific phenomenological character of temporal passage, and provides a positive account of why we experience passage without any objective reality underlying it. An important and underread contribution to the debate.

**Le Poidevin, Robin. *The Images of Time: An Essay on Temporal Representation*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007.**
An excellent treatment of temporal representation — how we mentally represent time, past, present, and future — with close attention to the relationship between temporal representation and temporal metaphysics. Le Poidevin navigates carefully between phenomenological and metaphysical considerations, making this a valuable bridge text.

**Maudlin, Tim. *The Metaphysics Within Physics*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007.**
Contains one of the most serious contemporary defenses of temporal passage from a physicist-philosopher, arguing that temporal passage is a real and fundamental feature of the physical world that standard physics fails to capture adequately. Maudlin's view that temporal passage is primitive and irreducible — like the laws of nature themselves — is developed with characteristic rigor and boldness.

**Dainton, Barry. *Stream of Consciousness: Unity and Continuity in Conscious Experience*. 2nd ed. London: Routledge, 2006.**
A comprehensive treatment of the phenomenological structure of conscious experience, including extended discussion of temporal experience and its relationship to temporal metaphysics. Dainton defends the reality of the experienced flow of consciousness and connects this to debates about personal identity and the unity of experience. An important contribution from a philosopher who takes both phenomenology and metaphysics seriously.

**Metzinger, Thomas. *The Ego Tunnel: The Science of the Mind and the Myth of the Self*. New York: Basic Books, 2009.**
An accessible introduction to Metzinger's self-model theory of consciousness and its implications for the phenomenology of selfhood, including the temporal dimension of self-experience. Metzinger argues that the "self" is a model the brain constructs — the "tunnel" through which we experience reality — and that the sense of temporal passage is part of this constructed self-model. More accessible than *Being No One*, and an excellent starting point for Metzinger's work.
