# Important Researchers and Thinkers: Chapter 28

**J. M. E. McTaggart (1866–1925)**: British idealist philosopher whose 1908 paper "The Unreality of Time" introduced the distinction between the A-series (ordering events as past, present, and future) and the B-series (ordering events as earlier-than, later-than, simultaneous-with) that has structured the debate about temporal passage ever since. McTaggart argued that the A-series is necessary for genuine time but that it is incoherent — generating a vicious regress — and therefore that time is unreal. Most subsequent philosophers have rejected his idealist conclusion while accepting his analytic framework.

**Donald C. Williams (1899–1983)**: American philosopher whose 1951 paper "The Myth of Passage" stated the rate-of-passage problem with unusual clarity and force. Williams argued that the "passage" of time — the idea of a flowing "now" — is a myth, generated by a confusion between the temporal dimension (real) and movement through the temporal dimension (a contradiction in terms). His paper is one of the most important contributions to the B-theory tradition and is still required reading in the philosophy of time.

**J. J. C. Smart (1920–2012)**: Australian-British philosopher who, in "The River of Time" (1949) and later work, developed the B-theory position and the objection to objective temporal passage. Smart's 1949 paper independently anticipated Williams's argument and connected the no-passage view to scientific realism. His contributions to the debate were wide-ranging, covering temporal language, temporal experience, and the relationship between common sense and scientific ontology.

**Bradford Skow (born 1975)**: American philosopher of physics whose *Objective Becoming* (2015) is the most careful recent defense of the moving spotlight theory and of the reality of objective temporal passage. Skow's treatment is notable for its rigorous engagement with both the philosophical literature and the physics, and for its careful response to the rate-of-passage problem. He accepts that the moving spotlight requires a preferred foliation of spacetime (an empirically undetectable physical structure) and argues that this cost is worth paying for a theory that accurately captures the reality of temporal passage.

**Craig Callender (born 1968)**: American philosopher of physics and one of the most prominent defenders of the no-passage view. His book *What Makes Time Special?* (2017) is a comprehensive treatment of what makes time different from space, arguing that most of the apparent "specialness" of time — including the sense of passage, the special status of the present, and the directionality of experience — is explained by the specific way in which temporally embedded creatures process information about a causally and thermodynamically asymmetric world, without any need for objective A-theoretic features of time.

**Simon Prosser**: British philosopher whose *Experiencing Time* (2016) provides the most systematic philosophical treatment of the relationship between temporal experience and temporal metaphysics. Prosser argues that no objective feature of the world could produce the specific phenomenological character of the passage experience, and provides a positive account of why we experience passage without it being real. He is a careful and rigorous defender of the no-passage view who takes the phenomenology seriously even while rejecting its apparent ontological implications.

**Thomas Metzinger (born 1958)**: German philosopher of mind whose work on the "phenomenal self-model" (PSM) — the brain's representation of itself — provides a framework for understanding how the sense of temporal passage is embedded in the broader sense of being a self in the world. Metzinger's account in *Being No One* (2003) and *The Ego Tunnel* (2009) offers a cognitive-scientific grounding for the phenomenological sense of passage without committing to its objective reality. His work is notable for its combination of rigorous philosophical analysis and deep engagement with neuroscience.

**C. D. Broad (1887–1971)**: British philosopher who, in *Scientific Thought* (1923), developed one of the earliest systematic analyses of temporal passage and argued that objective becoming — the genuine coming-into-existence of events at the "cutting edge" of time — is a real and fundamental feature of the world that cannot be captured by any static representation of temporal structure. Broad's growing block theory (in which the past and present exist but the future does not) remains one of the most influential A-theory positions.
