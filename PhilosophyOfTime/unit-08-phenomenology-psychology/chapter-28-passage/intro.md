# Chapter 28: The Passage of Time

There is something that time does — or seems to do — that no other dimension does. Space does not pass. The spatial dimension stretching from here to the wall does not move; it simply is, and I can move through it as I please, going backward and forward, standing still. But time passes. The future approaches, the present comes and goes, the past recedes. This motion — the flow, the passage, the running-away of time — is among the most vivid and pervasive features of experience. It is not something we occasionally notice; it is the medium of everything we notice.

And yet it is philosophically baffling. Consider: if time flows, at what rate does it flow? "One second per second" — but that is not a rate in any meaningful sense; a genuine rate requires two different dimensions (miles per hour, heartbeats per minute). Genuine temporal flow would require a second time dimension against which to measure the first, generating an obvious regress. And consider: modern physics, in its four-dimensional block universe framework, does not represent time as flowing at all. The manifold simply is; all moments are equally real; there is no passage, no becoming, no objective "now" moving from past to future. How, then, can the passage of time be real?

This chapter examines the passage of time from three directions. First, we try to characterize the phenomenon: what is the phenomenology of temporal passage, and how rich is the experience? Second, we examine the arguments that passage is an illusion — either because the concept is incoherent (the rate-of-passage problem) or because the block universe leaves no room for it. Third, we examine serious attempts to defend the reality of passage — including Bradford Skow's "moving spotlight" view and the thesis that passage is a primitive, irreducible feature of reality. Finally, we ask how to reconcile the physics, which seems to leave no room for passage, with the phenomenology, which makes it vivid and inescapable.

This is one of the most genuinely open debates in the philosophy of time, and this chapter aims not merely to survey the positions but to help you think through them for yourself, developing your own considered view about one of the most puzzling features of the world we inhabit.
