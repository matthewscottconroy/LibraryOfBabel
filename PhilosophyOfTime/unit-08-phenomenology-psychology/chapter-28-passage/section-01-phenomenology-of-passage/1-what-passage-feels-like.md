# What Temporal Passage Feels Like

## The Phenomenological Datum

Light a candle and watch it. You can see, or seem to see, the flame flickering in real time, the wax melting, the pool of liquid wax around the wick gradually widening. The candle is different now from what it was five minutes ago, and it is changing as you watch. There is something in this experience that is not merely the observation of a sequence of different states: there is a sense that the changing is happening *now*, that you are watching it as it unfolds, that you are embedded in and carried by the very process of change. This is the phenomenological datum of temporal passage.

Or consider waiting for an important appointment. You arrive early and sit in the waiting room. Time has a specific quality in this situation: it is moving, it seems, but slowly; the future — the moment you will be called in — is approaching, but it is not here yet. There is a kind of "not-yet-ness" about the moment you are anticipating that gives the present a particular texture — a leaning-forward quality, a sense of being poised on the edge of something that has not yet arrived but is coming. This is temporal passage in its future-directed mode: the sense that the future is on its way.

Now consider the strange feeling — one that intensifies, for most people, as they age — that the years are shortening. When you were seven, the summer was an ocean; now a summer is a few quick swells and it is over. This is temporal passage in its retrospective mode: the felt acceleration of time that comes from the changing ratio of any given interval to the totality of one's life, or perhaps from the reduced density of new experiences compared to childhood. Whatever its mechanism, this experience confirms that the sense of passage is not just a feature of the immediate present but shapes our relationship to longer stretches of time.

## The Components of the Experience

What is the experience of temporal passage made of? Careful phenomenological attention reveals several distinguishable components.

**The sense of flow**: There is, in normal waking experience, a background sense that things are happening, that the world is in motion, that moments are becoming and then giving way to new moments. This is not a specific sensation — it is more like a background hum of temporality, the felt medium of all experience. It is most noticeable when it is absent or altered: in states of severe depression, where time seems to stop; in flow states, where time seems to disappear; in episodes of acute fear, where it seems to expand.

**The asymmetry of past and future**: The experience of temporal passage has a characteristic directionality. The future feels open and uncertain; the past feels fixed and determined. We experience ourselves as moving from the open future into the fixed past, or equivalently, as watching the open future approach and become the fixed past. This asymmetry is not merely an intellectual conviction (we know that the future is undetermined) but a felt feature of experience: the future is experienced with the specific quality of the not-yet-determined, the past with the quality of the cannot-be-changed.

**The special quality of the present**: Within the experience of temporal passage, the present moment has a special, privileged quality — the quality of nowness, of actuality, of what is happening as opposed to what has happened or what will happen. This quality is hard to describe precisely, but it is phenomenologically undeniable: there is something about the present moment that is different from moments that are merely remembered or anticipated. It is happening, as philosophers sometimes say — not merely represented but actual.

**The transience of the now**: Closely related to the nowness of the present is the experienced transience of the now — the sense that it is always passing, always giving way to the next moment. Augustine captured this beautifully: "Where did that 'now' come from, when we spoke of it? And where did it go to, when we ceased to speak of it?" (Augustine 1991: XI.15). The present moment cannot be held; it is gone as soon as it is. This transience is felt, not merely known intellectually, and it is one of the most poignant features of temporal experience.

## The Variable Intensity of Passage

The phenomenological experience of temporal passage is not uniformly present in all states of consciousness. In some states — absorption in an engaging task, meditative present-awareness, certain drug states — the sense of passage diminishes or disappears. In others — boredom, anticipatory anxiety, awareness of mortality — it becomes unusually salient and vivid. This variability raises the question of whether the experience of passage is a fundamental feature of consciousness (present always but sometimes suppressed) or whether it is a cognitive add-on that varies with circumstances (and can therefore be absent without loss).

The phenomenological tradition offers conflicting answers. Husserl suggests that the flowing character of the living present — the continuous retentional-protentional structure — is the most fundamental feature of consciousness, always present whenever there is experience at all. On this view, the variability of the experience of passage is a variability in the degree to which this fundamental flowing character is attended to and thematized, not in whether it is present. Heidegger would add that the most originary temporal structure — the temporal ecstases of care — is always in play, even when consciousness is not attending to it. Merleau-Ponty's account of the embodied field of presence suggests that the experience of passage is always bodily present, even when it is not consciously thematized.

Against this, the cognitive psychological findings suggest that the sense of passage is a construction that depends on specific cognitive processes (memory contrast, self-monitoring, asymmetric information about past and future) and that can be disrupted in various ways. This suggests that the experience of passage is not a simple or primitive datum but a product of cognitive processes that can fail or be suspended.

## The Philosophical Significance of the Phenomenology

Before turning to the philosophical arguments about whether passage is real, it is worth pausing on what the phenomenological analysis has established. The experience of temporal passage is:

- **Real as an experience**: Whatever its ultimate explanation or status, the experience is genuinely part of consciousness. Denying that we have this experience would be as implausible as denying that we have an experience of color.
- **Not easily analyzed away**: The experience is not simply a belief or an inference; it has a pre-reflective phenomenological character that is distinct from any propositional attitude about time.
- **Variable in intensity and character**: The experience varies across individuals, states, and circumstances in ways that suggest it is partly constructed by cognitive processes.
- **Connected to deeper existential structures**: The experience of passage is not an isolated perceptual phenomenon; it is embedded in the broader structures of care, mortality, and self-understanding that Heidegger analyzes.

With this phenomenological characterization in hand, we can turn to the philosophical question: is the passage that we experience real — a feature of the world itself — or is it an appearance generated by our cognitive architecture?
