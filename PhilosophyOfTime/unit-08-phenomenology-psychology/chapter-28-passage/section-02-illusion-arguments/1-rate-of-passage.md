# The Rate-of-Passage Problem

## The Classic Objection

The classic objection to the reality of temporal passage was forcefully stated by Donald Williams in his 1951 paper "The Myth of Passage" and was anticipated by J. J. C. Smart's 1949 paper "The River of Time." The objection is deceptively simple: if time flows, at what rate does it flow?

The question sounds odd at first. But it is entirely legitimate. Every genuine rate of change involves a ratio: speed is distance per unit time (miles per hour), acceleration is change in velocity per unit time (feet per second per second), and so on. What is the rate of temporal flow? Apparently it must be something like: one second per second. But "one second per second" is not a rate at all — it is a tautology, a ratio of 1 regardless of units, telling us nothing (Williams 1951: 462).

Smart (1949) pressed the same point by noting that for time to genuinely "pass" — to flow in the way that a river flows — we would need a second time dimension against which the passage could be measured. The river flows because it moves through space, and we can measure how quickly it moves using the dimension of time. But for time itself to flow, it would need to "move" through some second temporal dimension, which would need to move through a third, and so on. The regress is vicious (Smart 1949: 485).

## The Moving Now

A related version of the rate-of-passage problem concerns the idea of a "moving now" — the idea that the present moment is a point that moves along the temporal sequence from past to future (or equivalently, that the boundary between past and future advances into the future). This is the "moving spotlight" conception of temporal passage: time flows because a privileged "now" moves through the temporal sequence, illuminating each moment as it passes.

The problem with this picture is that it requires the "now" to move at some rate — to be at different positions on the temporal sequence at different times. But what times are those? They cannot be positions in the same temporal sequence (that would mean the "now" is at a definite position in the sequence it is supposed to be moving along — which doesn't make sense as a claim about movement). They must be positions in some higher-order temporal sequence. And then the same question arises about that sequence, generating the vicious regress (Williams 1951: 463–465).

## The Indexicality Response

One response to the rate-of-passage problem is to argue that the apparent problem dissolves once we recognize that "now" is an indexical term — like "here" and "I" — that refers to different things in different contexts of utterance. "Now" refers to the time of utterance; "here" refers to the place of utterance. The "movement" of the now is not a genuine movement through time but is simply the fact that the time of utterance changes as time passes. There is no need for a second temporal dimension to explain this.

This response has considerable force. It dissolves the regress by treating the "now" not as an objective feature of the temporal sequence that moves along it, but as a perspective-dependent term whose referent shifts with the temporal position of the speaker. On this view, asking "where is the now?" is like asking "where is here?" — the answer depends on where you are asking the question.

But critics of this response (including Williams and Smart, and more recently Callender 2017) argue that it misses the phenomenological datum. The experience of temporal passage is not merely the recognition that "now" picks out a different time when uttered at different times. It is the felt sense that time is *moving*, that we are being *carried* along, that the present is not merely the current referent of an indexical but the locus of a genuine and dynamic actuality that is different in kind from the past or future. The indexicality response explains away the movement of "now" but leaves the phenomenological experience of passage unexplained.

## The Block Universe and the Absence of Passage

The block universe picture of four-dimensional spacetime, which has been the default framework in physics since the development of special relativity, provides a structural argument against objective temporal passage. In the block universe, all times are equally real; there is no objective "present" that is ontologically privileged over the past or future; the entire four-dimensional manifold simply is. Time, in this picture, is just another dimension: it is different from space in various ways (it appears only once in the metric with the opposite sign, for instance), but it is not dynamic or flowing in any objective sense.

If the block universe is the correct picture of spacetime, there is no room in it for objective temporal passage. Passage would require that different moments have different ontological statuses — that the present is in some sense "more real" or "more actual" than the past or future — but the block universe assigns the same reality to all temporal positions. The experience of passage, on this view, is a feature of our cognitive representation of the world, not of the world itself (Callender 2017: 15–25).

This is a powerful and honest conclusion, but it comes with a significant cost: it implies that one of the most vivid and universal features of conscious experience — the sense that time is passing — is, in some sense, an illusion. This is not simply the claim that our senses mislead us about the surface color of tomatoes or the size of the moon on the horizon. It is the claim that a fundamental feature of the medium of all experience — its flowingness, its temporality as felt — is not reflected in the ultimate structure of reality. This is a philosophically audacious claim, and it demands a serious response.
