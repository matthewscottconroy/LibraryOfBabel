# The No-Passage View: Explaining the Illusion

## The No-Passage Position

The "no-passage" view holds that temporal passage is not a feature of objective reality but is generated by specific features of our cognitive architecture. On this view, there is no objective temporal flow; all moments of time are equally real and equally fixed; the block universe is the whole truth about time's structure. But we experience passage because of the particular way in which our cognitive system represents the world — specifically, because of the asymmetry between our representations of the past and our representations of the future (Callender 2000; Prosser 2016).

The no-passage view is not simply the assertion that passage is illusory. It comes with an explanatory obligation: if there is no objective passage, what generates the experience? A merely debunking argument — "physics has no room for passage, so passage isn't real" — leaves the phenomenological datum unexplained. The no-passage view must provide a positive cognitive-scientific account of why conscious beings like us experience a vivid and pervasive sense of temporal flow in a world that, objectively speaking, does not flow.

## The Memory-Anticipation Asymmetry

The most influential positive explanation for the sense of passage appeals to the asymmetry between memory and anticipation. We have rich episodic memories of the past but no analogous memories of the future. We can anticipate the future in some ways, but anticipation has a fundamentally different phenomenal character from memory: memory is of the determinate and fixed; anticipation is of the open and uncertain.

This asymmetry, on the no-passage view, is what generates the sense that time is moving — that we are "traveling" from the open past through the present into the closed future (or rather, from the open future through the present into the closed past, depending on the directionality of the metaphor). In reality, the block universe is static: there is no traveling, no flow. But because our representations of past and future are radically asymmetric — we have traces of the past but not of the future, we remember the past but anticipate the future — we represent ourselves as oriented in a specific temporal direction, facing toward the future and receding from the past. And this representational asymmetry is what produces the phenomenological sense of passage (Callender 2017: 26–40).

On this account, the sense of passage is, in a specific sense, an illusion: it represents the world as having a feature (dynamic flow) that it does not have. But it is not a random or arbitrary illusion. It has a specific cognitive cause — the memory-anticipation asymmetry — that is itself grounded in real features of the world: the entropic asymmetry of physical processes, which is why we have memory traces of the past but not the future; the causal asymmetry, by which earlier events influence later ones but not vice versa; and the epistemic asymmetry, which follows from both.

## Prosser's No-Passage Argument

Simon Prosser, in *Experiencing Time* (2016), develops the most systematic philosophical treatment of the no-passage view. Prosser's argument proceeds in two stages.

In the first stage, Prosser argues that the experience of passage cannot be a perception of an objective feature of the world. His argument is that no plausible physical property of the world could play the role that objective temporal passage would need to play: specifically, no physical property could be the kind of thing that is perceived as "dynamic" or "flowing" rather than merely "existing at a time." Even if we were to posit some objective passage-like feature of the world (perhaps the "spotlight" of objective presentness moving along the temporal sequence), it is not clear that this feature would be perceivable, or that perceiving it would generate the specific phenomenological character of the passage experience (Prosser 2016: 80–110).

In the second stage, Prosser offers a positive cognitive account of why we experience passage. The core idea is that temporal experience essentially involves a representation of changing neural states. As time passes, the neural states that represent the world change; each new neural state represents the world as it is now. The experience of these changing representations — the succession of representations of "now" in different states — is what produces the phenomenological sense of flow. There is no objective flow "out there" in the world; what flows is the sequence of representing neural states, and it is this that we (misleadingly) experience as the flow of time itself.

## Callender's Deflationary Account

Craig Callender, in *What Makes Time Special?* (2017), takes a complementary approach. Callender argues that what makes time seem special — what generates the sense of passage, the special status of the present, and the asymmetric salience of the future — is not any objective feature of time itself but the specific way in which the temporally extended physical world is "sampled" by a finite, situated cognitive system.

A creature that could process all of spacetime simultaneously — that could be aware of all temporal positions at once — would not experience passage in anything like the way we do. We experience passage because we are finite, located at a particular point in the temporal sequence, with access to information about the past (via memory) but not about the future (via anticipation), and because we are causally embedded in the world in ways that make the future impinge on us in specific and predictable ways. The sense of passage is, on this account, a feature of our situation and our cognitive architecture, not of time itself.

## The Cost of the No-Passage View

The no-passage view is intellectually attractive in its parsimony: it reconciles temporal physics with temporal experience without positing any objective passage that physics cannot accommodate. But it comes with a genuine philosophical cost: it implies that the most vivid and universal feature of our temporal experience — the sense that time is moving, that we are being carried forward — is, in a precise sense, an illusion. The world does not contain the feature (temporal passage) that this experience represents.

This is not an unprecedented conclusion in philosophy of mind. We accept that color experience does not represent an objective property of physical surfaces (which are merely reflective of certain wavelengths) and that temperature experience is partly a construction of the tactile system rather than a direct read-out of molecular kinetic energy. Why should temporal passage be different?

The challenge for the no-passage view is that passage seems more central to experience than color: it is not just one feature among others that we perceive, but part of the very medium of experience — the felt temporality of consciousness itself. Dismissing it as a mere illusion seems to require not just revision of our commonsense picture of the world, but revision of our most basic sense of what experience is. This is why the defenders of passage have reason to insist on a more careful hearing.
