# Passage as Primitive: The Irreducibility Argument

## The Primitive Passage Thesis

A different strategy for defending the reality of temporal passage is to argue that passage is primitive — that it is not reducible to anything else, not explicable in terms of anything more fundamental, and that attempts to explain it away in terms of cognitive architecture or to analyze it in terms of other features of reality all fail for the same reason: they try to reduce something irreducible.

This "primitive passage" thesis is not a new view. It has antecedents in the work of C. D. Broad, who argued in *Scientific Thought* (1923) that the experience of temporal becoming — of events genuinely coming into existence and then passing away — is a fundamental feature of reality that cannot be captured by any static representation of the world. More recently, philosophers like Lynne Rudder Baker and Tim Maudlin have defended versions of the view that temporal passage is a basic, irreducible feature of reality.

The analogy that defenders of primitive passage most frequently invoke is the case of phenomenal consciousness — the "what it is like" of experience. Functionalism about the mind holds that mental states are defined by their functional roles and can be realized in multiple physical substrates. But many philosophers (following Chalmers 1996) argue that phenomenal consciousness cannot be functionally analyzed: no description of the functional or physical structure of the brain, however complete, can explain why there is "something it is like" to be in a brain state. The "what it is like" of experience is a primitive — a basic feature of reality that supervenes on physical properties but is not reducible to them.

The same move is available for temporal passage. The "flowingness" of time — the felt becoming of the present, the genuine advancing of now — may be a primitive that supervenes on (or is intimately connected with) physical temporal structure but is not reducible to it. Just as the "what it is like" of red is not captured by a complete physical description of light and visual processing, the "flowing" character of time may not be captured by any description of the four-dimensional temporal manifold, however complete (Maudlin 2007: 108–125).

## The Analogy with Consciousness

The analogy with phenomenal consciousness is illuminating in several ways. In both cases:

- The phenomenon in question (phenomenal experience / temporal passage) is vivid and undeniable from the first-person perspective.
- Reductionist accounts of the phenomenon (functionalism about mind / no-passage views) are attractive for their parsimony and their compatibility with physics.
- The reductionist account seems to "leave something out" — to describe the functional or structural conditions without capturing what is being explained (the "what it is like" of experience / the "flowing" character of time).
- A primitive or irreducibility claim is proposed as the most honest response to the failure of reductionism.

There are also disanalogies. Phenomenal consciousness is a feature of specific, individual minds; temporal passage (if real) is a feature of time itself, and as such is objective rather than merely subjective. This means that the epistemological situation is different: if passage is objective, our experience of it is supposed to be veridical (at least in broad outline), whereas the phenomenal properties of experience are not typically claimed to be veridical with respect to the external world.

## Evaluating the Primitive Passage Thesis

The primitive passage thesis faces several objections.

**Explanatory impotence**: Invoking primitiveness may be regarded as intellectually unsatisfying — as simply naming the problem rather than solving it. If passage is primitive, then nothing further can be said about what it is or why it is. This contrasts unfavorably with the no-passage view, which at least offers a positive cognitive-scientific explanation of why we experience passage in terms of the memory-anticipation asymmetry.

**Disconnection from physics**: If temporal passage is primitive and irreducible, it seems to float free of any connection with physical temporal structure. The four-dimensional spacetime of physics seems to leave no room for it; and positing passage as a primitive does not explain how it relates to the physical world that physics describes.

**The regress**: Calling something primitive is not a solution to the rate-of-passage problem. Even if passage is primitive, it still has some character — it is something that happens, something that the present does as it "moves." And this character seems to require some further description that the primitive thesis does not provide.

Defenders of the primitive passage thesis can respond as follows. The explanatory impotence objection shows only that primitiveness is not further explicable, which is what primitiveness means. The disconnection from physics objection presupposes that physics gives us a complete description of reality; but there may be features of reality — phenomenal consciousness, temporal passage — that physics, by its methods, is not equipped to describe. And the rate-of-passage problem applies to any account of passage, not specifically to the primitive account; if the problem is unanswerable, perhaps the best response is to accept that the "rate" question is simply inapplicable to a phenomenon of this kind.

## The Deeper Issue

Both the moving spotlight theory and the primitive passage thesis share a commitment to the view that the experience of temporal passage is tracking something real — that there is something about the world (objective becoming, or primitive flow) that the experience is approximately or partially representing. Both are committed to some form of A-theory: the view that the past, present, and future are genuinely distinct in ways that the B-theory's earlier-later relations cannot capture.

The deeper issue is whether this commitment to the A-theory is compatible with a physicalist picture of the world. If physics gives us a complete description of reality, and if that description is a B-theory description (a static four-dimensional manifold without objective passage), then A-theory must either revise our understanding of physics or accept that A-theoretic features of time are not captured by current physics but are nonetheless real. The first option requires courage; the second requires humility about the scope of current physics.

Both options are open, and the choice between them is one of the genuinely difficult and open questions in the philosophy of time.
