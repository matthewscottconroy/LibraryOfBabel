# The Cognitive Sources of the Sense of Temporal Passage

## Theory One: The Asymmetry of Memory and Anticipation

The most widely held cognitive theory of the sense of passage appeals to the fundamental asymmetry between how we represent the past and how we represent the future. We have episodic memories of the past: detailed, phenomenologically vivid records of specific events that place us as having been present at those events. We have no analogous records of the future: our representations of the future are anticipatory projections, not records of experienced events. This asymmetry — rich experiential access to the past, projective anticipation toward the future — is what generates the sense that we are "moving through time" from an experienced past through an experienced present toward an un-experienced future.

On this account, the sense of passage arises from the contrast between two fundamentally different modes of cognitive access: retrospective (experiential, memorial, veridical about having-been) and prospective (anticipatory, imaginative, open about what will be). The experienced "movement" of time is not a representation of something objective but is the phenomenological upshot of the cognitive difference between these two modes of access.

An important question for this theory concerns direction: why do we experience ourselves as moving from past to future rather than from future to past? The answer, presumably, lies in the causal asymmetry of the world — causes precede effects, records form of past events but not future ones, and we act in the present to bring about future outcomes. This directional asymmetry in causation and information flow produces the directional asymmetry in our modes of temporal access, which in turn produces the directional sense of passage.

This theory has the virtue of connecting the sense of passage to the broadly confirmed findings about the asymmetry of episodic memory and future imagination. It also connects naturally to the no-passage view: if the sense of passage is generated by cognitive asymmetry, and if the cognitive asymmetry has a physical explanation (in terms of thermodynamic and causal asymmetries), then there is no need to posit objective passage to explain the experience.

## Theory Two: Metzinger's Self-Model Account

Thomas Metzinger, in *Being No One* (2003) and *The Ego Tunnel* (2009), develops a more comprehensive self-model theory of temporal experience. On Metzinger's account, the sense of temporal passage arises from the way the brain's "phenomenal self-model" (PSM) — the brain's representation of itself as an entity in the world — includes a temporal dimension. The PSM represents the self not only as located in space and in a body but as located in time: as having a past, being in the present, and projecting into the future. The sense of passage is the phenomenal upshot of this temporal dimension of self-modeling.

More specifically, Metzinger proposes that the sense of passage arises from the fact that the PSM is continuously updated: the brain's representation of "now" is continuously revised as new information arrives, and the previous representation is "pushed" into the past within the PSM. It is this continuous revision — the brain modeling itself as moving through time, rather than merely existing at different times — that generates the phenomenological sense that the self is flowing through time.

Metzinger's account is notable for two reasons. First, it grounds the sense of passage not just in memory-anticipation asymmetry but in the comprehensive self-modeling capacity of the brain — the same system that is responsible for the sense of bodily ownership, spatial self-location, and personal identity. This connects the sense of passage to a broader account of the phenomenology of selfhood and potentially explains why disruptions to the self-model (in depersonalization, certain psychedelic states, and severe depression) produce characteristic distortions of temporal experience.

Second, Metzinger's account is explicitly non-committal about whether objective passage exists. The PSM represents the self as flowing through time, but this representation may or may not be veridical. If passage is objectively real, the PSM is (approximately) tracking it. If passage is not objectively real, the PSM is producing a systematic illusion — but a systematic illusion that is intimately tied to the sense of being a self in the world, and therefore unlikely to be dispelled by philosophical argument or intellectual insight.

## Changing Neural States and the Experience of Flow

A third approach to the cognitive sources of the passage experience focuses on the changing neural states that accompany moment-to-moment experience. The basic observation is that the brain's state is never the same from one moment to the next: patterns of neural activity change continuously, and these changing patterns are what correspond to the changing content of experience. The sense of "flow" — of experience flowing from moment to moment — might simply be the phenomenological upshot of this continuous neural change.

On this account, the sense of flow is not a representation of anything outside the brain; it is the felt character of the continuous temporal dynamics of neural processing itself. What feels like time flowing is simply what it feels like from the inside to have a continuously changing neural state. This would explain why the sense of flow is so pervasive and so difficult to eliminate: it is not a representation that can be revised by updating beliefs or shifting attention, but the most basic character of the stream of consciousness itself.

This account converges with Husserl's description of the absolute flow of consciousness as self-constituting — as the form of consciousness rather than an object of consciousness. The difference is that where Husserl locates the absolute flow at the transcendental level of consciousness-as-such, the changing-neural-states account locates it at the level of physical neural dynamics. Whether these are the same phenomenon described at different levels, or genuinely different accounts, is an open and important question.

## Implications for the Illusion Debate

None of these cognitive theories, by itself, settles the question of whether objective temporal passage is real. But they do have implications for how that question should be understood.

If the sense of passage is generated by memory-anticipation asymmetry (Theory One), then the sense of passage is "about" the asymmetry of our cognitive access — not about any objective flow. This supports the no-passage view: there is a cognitive source for the experience that does not require positing objective passage.

If the sense of passage is generated by the temporal dimension of self-modeling (Metzinger), then the question of whether the sense is veridical becomes the question of whether the PSM accurately represents anything in the world. If the PSM's temporal dimension is tracking objective passage, the experience is veridical; if not, it is systematically illusory. The cognitive theory leaves this question open.

If the sense of passage is the felt character of changing neural dynamics, then it is not a representation of anything external at all — it is simply what it is like to be a brain in time. This is perhaps the most radical cognitive theory, and it suggests that the question of whether the sense of passage corresponds to objective passage may be somewhat poorly formed: the "flow" of experience is not a perception of something external but an intrinsic character of experience that neither corresponds to nor fails to correspond to anything external.
