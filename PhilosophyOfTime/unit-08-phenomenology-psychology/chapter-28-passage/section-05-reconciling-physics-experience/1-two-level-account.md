# The Two-Level Account: Physics, Phenomenology, and Their Relationship

## The Three Options Restated

The tension between the block universe of physics and the experienced passage of time has generated three broad strategies of response.

**Option (a): Debunk the phenomenology.** Accept that physics gives us the correct picture of temporal reality: a static four-dimensional manifold with no objective passage. The experienced passage is an illusion — generated by the memory-anticipation asymmetry, the self-model system, or the changing neural states of the brain. The phenomenological tradition has systematically misdescribed the character of experience, or has correctly described experience but incorrectly concluded that experience is tracking an objective feature of the world.

**Option (b): Revise the physics.** Accept that the phenomenology is tracking something real — that temporal passage is objectively real — and revise the physics accordingly. This means adopting some form of A-theory of time: presentism, the growing block theory, or the moving spotlight theory. The physics of the block universe is incorrect, or at least incomplete, as a description of temporal reality.

**Option (c): Accept both levels.** Maintain that physics and phenomenology are describing different levels of reality — or different aspects of the same reality — without either trumping or eliminating the other. Physics describes the objective, observer-independent structure of spacetime. Phenomenology describes how conscious beings with specific cognitive architectures process and experience that structure. Both descriptions are valid within their respective domains, and the relationship between them is a subject for further investigation.

## The Case for Option (c)

The two-level account (option c) has several significant advantages over the alternatives.

**Against option (a)**: The debunking strategy faces the problem of phenomenological residue. Even granting that passage is generated by cognitive asymmetry or neural dynamics, the fact that it is vivid, pervasive, and seemingly ineliminable from experience suggests that it is tracking something real about the character of consciousness — even if not about the objective structure of time. The analogy with color is instructive: we accept that "red" does not correspond to an objective physical property of surfaces, but we do not conclude that the redness of red as experienced is an illusion. It is a real phenomenal property, though its relation to physics is mediated and complex. The same may be said of temporal passage: it is a real phenomenal property of experience, even if its relation to physics is mediated (Chalmers 1996; Callender 2017: 150–165).

More importantly, the debunking strategy threatens to undermine the very notion of perceptual access to the world. If we can dismiss the most vivid and universal feature of temporal experience — its flowingness — as a mere cognitive illusion, what principle prevents us from dismissing other features of experience in the same way? The debunking strategy requires a principled account of when and why cognitive construction diverges from reality that does not extend to the elimination of all phenomenal properties.

**Against option (b)**: The physics-revision strategy faces serious challenges from relativity. As noted above, A-theoretic passage requires a privileged foliation of spacetime — a global "present" — that is not available in the standard formulation of special relativity. While philosophers like Skow have argued that this does not make A-theory incoherent, the cost in terms of undetectable physical structure is significant. Moreover, if we revise physics to accommodate passage, we need an account of how the posited passage-facts connect with the physical facts we can measure. This account has not yet been developed to a satisfactory level.

## The Two-Level Account in Detail

The most defensible position, as we develop it here, holds that physics and phenomenology are legitimately autonomous domains of inquiry with different subject matters and different methods, and that the apparent conflict between them arises from a category mistake: treating phenomenological claims as if they were physical claims, or physical claims as if they settled phenomenological questions.

**Physics describes objective temporal structure.** The four-dimensional spacetime manifold of physics is the best current description of the objective, observer-independent structure of time. The block universe picture — in which all times are equally real and there is no objective "now" — is supported by an enormous body of evidence. It tells us the geometric structure of time as a physical dimension.

**Phenomenology describes the structure of temporal experience.** The phenomenological tradition, from Husserl through Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, and Ricoeur, is in the business of describing the structure of experience as experience — the way in which temporal objects are given to a conscious subject. This is a different enterprise from physics. Phenomenology is not making claims about the objective temporal structure of the physical world; it is describing the a priori structures of any possible temporal experience. The "living present," the "ecstases of temporality," the "field of presence" — these are structures of experience, not features of the physical manifold.

**The sense of passage belongs to phenomenology, not to physics.** The sense that time is passing — that the present is moving, that we are being carried from past to future — is a phenomenological feature of experience. As a phenomenological feature, it is real: it is genuinely present in experience, and phenomenological analysis can describe its character, its variations, and its conditions. As a phenomenological feature, it does not need to correspond to any objective physical property of the temporal manifold to be "real." Just as the redness of red is a real phenomenal property even if there is no objective physical "redness," the flowingness of experience is a real phenomenal property even if there is no objective physical "flow."

**The relationship between levels requires investigation.** Accepting that physics and phenomenology operate at different levels does not mean that they have nothing to say to each other. The relationship between the physical description of time (objective temporal structure, causal asymmetry, entropic asymmetry) and the phenomenological description of temporal experience (living present, sense of passage, temporal self-location) is a genuine and important question — perhaps the central question for a complete account of time. The cognitive science of temporal experience (Chapter 27) is one part of the answer: it shows how specific cognitive mechanisms implement temporal experience in creatures with specific neural architectures embedded in a physically asymmetric world.

## Trade-offs and Remaining Questions

The two-level account is not without costs. Its main vulnerabilities are:

**The relationship problem**: Saying that physics and phenomenology operate at different levels leaves the relationship between them underspecified. A complete account requires not just that the two levels are different but an account of how they connect — how physical temporal structure gives rise to (or supports, or constrains) the phenomenological structures of temporal experience. This "bridge" account is not yet available in satisfactory form.

**The integration question**: If physics provides no objective passage and phenomenology describes passage as real, we face the question of how to integrate these two descriptions into a single coherent picture of the world. The two-level account risks leaving us with two incommensurable pictures: a physical world without passage and an experiential world full of it. How are these two worlds the same world?

**The normativity of physics**: There is a persistent temptation to regard physics as the arbiter of what is real — the "gold standard" for ontological commitment. On this view, if physics does not describe passage, then passage is not real. The two-level account resists this temptation, but it needs to offer a principled reason for doing so rather than merely asserting that phenomenology has independent standing.

Despite these challenges, the two-level account is the most defensible available position, for the following reason: it is the only position that takes both the physics and the phenomenology seriously, without prematurely debunking one or revising the other in ways that are not yet adequately supported. The question of how the two levels relate remains genuinely open — and sitting with an open question honestly is sometimes the most intellectually responsible position available. The philosophy of time is not the only domain in which mind and world face each other across a gap that cannot yet be fully bridged; but it is one of the domains where that gap is most sharply felt, and most philosophically instructive.
