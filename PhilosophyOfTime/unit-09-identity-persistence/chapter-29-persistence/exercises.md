# Chapter 29 Exercises: Persistence Through Time

## Analytical Exercises

**1. Reconstructing the Problem of Temporary Intrinsics**
Explain Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics in your own words. (a) What is an intrinsic property? Give two examples of intrinsic properties and two examples of relational properties. (b) Why do temporary intrinsic properties seem to generate a contradiction? (c) Why does Lewis think this problem is genuinely serious — not just a terminological confusion?

**2. Evaluating the Three Solutions**
Lewis identifies three solutions to the problem of temporary intrinsics: adverbialism, relativization to times, and temporal parts. For each solution: (a) explain what it proposes; (b) explain Lewis's objection to it; (c) consider whether the objection is decisive. Which solution do you find most defensible, and why?

**3. The Case for Endurantism**
Construct the strongest possible case for endurantism. Draw on (a) the intuitive fit with ordinary language and thought; (b) the parsimony of the endurantist ontology; (c) the best available endurantist response to Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics. Then present the strongest objection to endurantism and evaluate whether it can be answered.

**4. The Case for Perdurantism**
Construct the strongest possible case for perdurantism. Draw on (a) the clean solution to the problem of temporary intrinsics; (b) the alignment with the block universe picture of physics; (c) the handling of fission and vagueness cases. Then present the strongest objection to perdurantism — perhaps concerning ordinary language or moral responsibility — and evaluate whether it can be answered.

**5. Stage Theory and Temporal Counterparts**
Explain the key move that distinguishes stage theory from worm theory: the introduction of temporal counterparts. (a) What is the counterpart relation, and how does it differ from identity? (b) Does stage theory solve the problem of temporary intrinsics? (c) Is the introduction of temporal counterparts a satisfying account of what it means to "persist through time," or does it change the subject?

**6. The Ship of Theseus**
Describe Hobbes's variant of the Ship of Theseus puzzle. (a) Why does this variant create a particularly sharp puzzle for views that appeal to material identity as a criterion of persistence? (b) How would an endurantist, a perdurantist, and a stage theorist each resolve the puzzle? (c) Is there a "correct" answer to which ship is the Ship of Theseus, or is the question conventional? What considerations bear on this meta-question?

**7. The Conventional Element in Identity Claims**
The Ship of Theseus puzzle suggests that identity claims for ordinary objects involve a conventional element: which persistence criteria we use partly determines which identity claims we make. (a) Is this conclusion a form of anti-realism about identity, or is it compatible with a realist view? (b) Does the conventional element in identity claims for ships apply equally to identity claims for persons? (c) What are the practical implications of accepting that personal identity claims have a conventional element?

## Observational and Experiential Lab Exercises

**8. A Childhood Object (Lab)**
If possible, locate an object from your childhood that you have kept — a toy, a piece of clothing, a book, a piece of furniture. If no such object is available, use a photograph of one.

Examine the object (or photograph). Answer the following questions:

(a) Is this the "same" object you knew as a child? In what sense? In what senses might it not be the same?
(b) What has changed about the object? What has remained constant?
(c) Apply each of the three persistence theories to this case. What would the endurantist say? The perdurantist? The stage theorist?
(d) Is there a single "correct" answer to whether this is the same object, or does the answer depend on which persistence criteria you apply?
(e) Does your emotional relationship to the object illuminate anything about the metaphysics of persistence — or does it introduce biases that the metaphysics should ignore?

**9. The Ship of Theseus in Your Own Life (Lab)**
You have been gradually changing throughout your life: different beliefs, different memories, different physical constitution, different relationships, different personality traits. Consider yourself five, ten, or fifteen years ago.

(a) In what ways are you the "same person" as that younger version of yourself? In what ways are you different?
(b) Is there a moment when you would say you "became" a different person? Or has the change been fully gradual?
(c) Would you accept responsibility for something that younger person did that you now regret? On what grounds?
(d) Apply the three persistence theories to your own case. How does each account for your persistence through change? Which theory gives the most accurate description of what has happened?
(e) Construct your own version of the "Hobbes variant" for a personal identity case: a thought experiment in which the criteria of psychological continuity and material/physical continuity come apart. What does your intuition say in that case?

**10. Thinking Through Temporal Parts (Lab)**
This exercise requires about twenty minutes of quiet, focused reflection.

Try to imagine yourself as a four-dimensional temporal worm: a sequence of temporal stages extending from your birth to (some future) death. Try to picture "yourself" not as the entity present right now but as the entire extended entity.

(a) Can you form any kind of imaginative grip on this picture? Describe what you can and cannot imagine.
(b) Does the picture feel strange, neutral, or illuminating? Why?
(c) Is the temporal worm that is "you" clearly bounded — a determinate entity with a clear beginning and end? Or does it shade off into the past and future in ways that make its boundaries fuzzy?
(d) Now try the endurantist picture: you are wholly present right now, the same whole entity you were yesterday and will be tomorrow. Does this picture feel more or less natural than the temporal worm picture? Why?

Write a reflection on what your imaginative engagement with these pictures reveals about the relationship between metaphysical theories of persistence and our first-person self-understanding.
