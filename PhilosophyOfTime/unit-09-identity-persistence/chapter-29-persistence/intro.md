# Chapter 29: Persistence Through Time

Think of your favorite piece of furniture — something old that has been through a lot. Perhaps a wooden table that has been scratched, re-finished, had one leg replaced after a mishap, and been moved from house to house over thirty years. Is it the same table? Of course it is — or so we say without hesitation. But what makes it the same? It looks different than it did thirty years ago, is partly made of different matter, and has been in different places. And yet we count it as the same table. What underwrites this judgment?

This question — the question of persistence — applies to all objects that endure through time: rocks, rivers, organisms, institutions, and persons. Any object that exists at two different times potentially looks different, is in a different state, and has undergone change. How can it be the same object? There seem to be only a limited range of possible answers, and each comes with significant philosophical commitments.

This chapter begins with David Lewis's elegant formulation of the problem in terms of "temporary intrinsics" — properties that an object has at one time but not another, which seem to threaten the very coherence of change. Lewis's formulation is philosophically precise and serves as a useful anchor for the subsequent discussion of endurantism and perdurantism. After examining these two main competing views and their objections, the chapter turns to Ted Sider's stage theory as a third option, and concludes with a detailed examination of the Ship of Theseus puzzle — a thought experiment so simple that children find it immediately compelling, and so philosophically rich that it has been discussed for two and a half millennia.
