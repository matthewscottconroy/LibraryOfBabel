# The Problem of Temporary Intrinsics

## Setting Up the Problem

David Lewis, in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), identifies a puzzle that he takes to be central to any adequate metaphysics of persistence. The puzzle concerns what he calls "temporary intrinsics" — intrinsic properties that an object instantiates at one time but not another.

An **intrinsic property** is a property an object has in virtue of itself alone, not in virtue of any relations it bears to other things. Being spherical is intrinsic (a sphere is spherical in itself, regardless of what is around it). Being taller than the Eiffel Tower is not intrinsic — it is a relational property that depends on what other things exist and what sizes they have. Being a sibling is not intrinsic — it depends on the existence of a sibling. Being shaped like an S is intrinsic — an S-shaped wire has that shape in virtue of its own structure.

Now consider a simple case. I am sitting right now. Sitting is an intrinsic property: I am bent into a sitting posture in virtue of how my own body is arranged, not in virtue of any relation to other things. An hour ago, I was standing. Standing is also an intrinsic property. I was then straight; I am now bent. These are incompatible properties. The straightness and the bentness cannot both be true of the very same thing — that would be a contradiction.

But both are true of me — of the same person, who exists both now and an hour ago. How is this possible? This is the problem of temporary intrinsics (Lewis 1986: 203–204).

## Why This Is a Genuine Problem

The problem might seem trivially easy: "I was straight at one time and bent at another." But Lewis argues that this easy answer already presupposes one of the three solutions he is about to evaluate, and that the presupposition needs to be examined carefully.

The difficulty is this. If both properties — being straight and being bent — are genuinely instantiated by me, and if they are genuinely incompatible, then for them both to be true there must be something that relativizes them, something that explains why they do not contradict each other. What plays that role?

Three options are available. First, the properties could be relativized to times — "being straight *at t₁*" and "being bent *at t₂*" are not incompatible because they are different properties that include temporal qualifications. Second, the properties could be understood adverbially — I instantiate straightness *simpliciter* in a "t₁-way" and bentness *simpliciter* in a "t₂-way," with the adverb doing the relativizing work rather than the property itself. Third, the incompatible properties are instantiated by different things — not by me-over-time as a whole, but by different temporal parts of me, each of which instantiates only one of the incompatible properties.

Lewis argues that only the third option — temporal parts — is fully satisfactory (Lewis 1986: 204). The first two options face serious difficulties, and the third, while it requires accepting the existence of temporal parts (and thus a perdurantist metaphysics), is the cleanest and most principled solution.

## The Stakes

Why does this matter? The problem of temporary intrinsics is not just an abstract puzzle. It is the underlying metaphysical issue that separates endurantists from perdurantists about persistence. Endurantists hold that objects are wholly present at each time they exist — that there are no temporal parts; the whole object is there at each moment. But if the whole object is present at each moment, and it has incompatible intrinsic properties at different moments, then the object seems to instantiate contradictory properties, which is impossible.

Perdurantists hold that objects persist by having temporal parts at each time — that what is present at any given moment is not the whole object but a temporal part (a "stage") of it. Different stages have different intrinsic properties, and there is no contradiction because the same whole object does not instantiate both properties; rather, different temporal parts instantiate them.

The problem of temporary intrinsics thus motivates one of the central debates in the metaphysics of time and persistence. But as we will see, the endurantist position is not without resources, and the debate turns on questions about what properties really are, how they are instantiated, and what it means for an object to be "wholly present" at a time.
