# Three Solutions to the Problem of Temporary Intrinsics

## Solution One: Adverbialism

The first solution to the problem of temporary intrinsics is **adverbialism**: the idea that when an object instantiates a temporary intrinsic property, the instantiation itself should be understood adverbially — as qualifying the mode or manner of instantiation rather than the property instantiated.

On the adverbial view, it is not that I instantiate the property "being bent" at time t₂ and the property "being straight" at time t₁ as if these were genuinely incompatible properties both held by the same thing. Rather, I instantiate "bentness" in a t₂-manner and "straightness" in a t₁-manner. The adverbs "in a t₂-manner" and "in a t₁-manner" do the logical work of preventing the incompatibility: the two instantiation facts are not about the same mode of instantiation, so they do not conflict.

The term "adverbialism" comes from a parallel strategy in the philosophy of perception. In perception, we sometimes seem to experience objects with properties they don't have (a red apple that looks pink in certain lighting, a straight stick that looks bent in water). The adverbialist about perception proposes that we should analyze "seeing a pink apple" not as perceiving an object with the property pinkness, but as perceiving pinkly — where "pinkly" is an adverb modifying the act of perceiving.

In the persistence case, the adverb modifies the instantiation of the property by the object. I instantiate bentness at-t₂-ish-ly, and I instantiate straightness at-t₁-ish-ly. These are not contradictory because the mode of instantiation differs.

Lewis objects to adverbialism on the grounds that it is mysterious. What exactly is it to instantiate a property "in a t₁-manner" versus "in a t₂-manner"? The adverb seems to smuggle back in a temporal relativization that we were supposed to have avoided, and it is not clear that adverbialism offers any genuine analysis of what is happening when a single object has different intrinsic properties at different times. At best, Lewis argues, adverbialism is a way of redescribing the problem rather than solving it (Lewis 1986: 204).

## Solution Two: Relativization to Times

The second solution is to **relativize properties to times**: instead of treating "being bent" as a monadic property that an object either has or lacks, we treat "being bent" as a binary relational property that relates objects to times. "I am bent" becomes "I am bent at t₂" — where "bent-at" is a two-place relation between me and the time t₂, not a monadic property I instantiate.

On this view, there are no intrinsic properties in Lewis's sense — no genuine monadic properties that objects simply have or lack. Instead, all apparently intrinsic properties are really disguised relations to times (or to temporal stages of the world). The apparent contradiction dissolves because "I am bent at t₂" and "I am straight at t₁" are not incompatible — they are simply different relational facts.

This view is sometimes called "temporal relativism" or "three-dimensionalism with relativized properties." It has the advantage of being simple and natural: of course being bent is relative to a time; there is nothing mysterious about saying that I am bent at some times and straight at others.

Lewis's objection is that this solution changes the subject. If "being bent" is really a relation to a time, then it is not an intrinsic property at all. And the intuition behind the problem was that properties like "being bent," "being spherical," "having a certain shape" are intrinsic — they are about how the object is in itself, not about its relations to other things (including times). Relativizing them to times treats them as relational and thereby denies that there are any genuine intrinsic properties, or at least that any intrinsic properties can change.

This is a cost. Intrinsic properties seem to play an important role in our understanding of the world — in distinguishing what an object is like "from the inside" versus how it is situated in relation to other things. If the relativization strategy eliminates intrinsic properties, it thereby eliminates something that seems to be real and important about the world.

## Solution Three: Temporal Parts

Lewis's preferred solution is the **temporal parts** view: objects persist by having numerically distinct temporal parts at different times, and each temporal part has its own intrinsic properties. When I was straight at t₁, my temporal part at t₁ was straight. When I am bent at t₂, my temporal part at t₂ is bent. My temporal part at t₁ and my temporal part at t₂ are distinct parts of me — just as my left hand and my right hand are distinct spatial parts of me. The incompatible properties are instantiated by distinct entities (distinct temporal parts), so there is no contradiction.

On this view, the "me" that was straight and the "me" that is bent are both genuine parts of me, related by the same kind of mereological composition that relates my left arm and my right leg to me. Just as my left arm is straight while my right arm is bent without any contradiction (because they are distinct spatial parts), my t₁-stage is straight while my t₂-stage is bent without any contradiction (because they are distinct temporal parts).

Lewis argues that this solution preserves the genuine intrinsicality of properties: being bent is a genuine monadic property, not a relation to a time. It is just that the thing that instantiates the property is not me-as-a-whole but my temporal part at the relevant time. There is no contradiction because the two properties are instantiated by two different things.

The main cost of the temporal parts solution is ontological: it requires accepting the existence of temporal parts as genuine entities in the world. Most philosophers who accept temporal parts regard them as entities "of the same ontological type as spatial parts" (Lewis 1986: 204) — no more mysterious than spatial parts, but also no less real. Endurantists, however, argue that temporal parts are a philosophically problematic posit — that there is no good reason to believe in them, and that the problem they are invoked to solve can be handled by less costly means.

## Lewis's Argument for Temporal Parts

Lewis's argument for preferring the temporal parts solution has a specific logical structure. He argues that adverbialism and relativization are both, in different ways, unsatisfactory. Adverbialism is mysterious and does not genuinely analyze the instantiation of properties at times. Relativization denies the genuine intrinsicality of apparently intrinsic properties, which conflicts with intuitions that are at least as strong as the intuition that objects persist through change.

The temporal parts solution, by contrast, preserves the genuine intrinsicality of properties and gives a principled account of how an object can have incompatible properties at different times: by having distinct parts that instantiate them. This is analogous to the uncontroversially acceptable case of spatial incompatibility: an object can be red on the left and blue on the right without contradiction, because "the left" and "the right" are distinct spatial parts with different properties. The temporal case should be handled the same way.

This argument is not airtight. As we will see in the following sections, endurantists have developed sophisticated responses that do not simply accept Lewis's verdict. But Lewis's formulation has the great advantage of making the stakes of the debate precise and of showing exactly what must be defended or surrendered on each side.
