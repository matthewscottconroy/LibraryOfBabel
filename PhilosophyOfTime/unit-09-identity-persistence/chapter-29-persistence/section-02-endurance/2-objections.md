# Objections to Endurantism

## Lewis's Objection: Relativization Fails

Lewis's primary objection to the endurantist strategy of relativizing properties to times is that it fails to preserve genuine intrinsicality. If "being bent" is not a genuine monadic property but always a relational property (being-bent-at-t) that involves a relation to a time, then there are, strictly speaking, no intrinsic properties that change over time. And if there are no genuine intrinsic properties, a large part of what we thought we were saying about objects turns out to be false or systematically misleading.

The worry is this. When I say that my friend is happy, it seems as if I am saying something about how she is — about her intrinsic state, not her relational situation. When I say she used to be sad, I am saying something about her intrinsic state at that earlier time. These seem like genuine intrinsic characterizations. But if endurantism requires treating happiness and sadness as time-indexed relational properties rather than genuine intrinsic states, then the simple, intuitive picture that objects have intrinsic states that change over time is replaced by a more complex picture in which everything is a relation — and this seems like a significant departure from intuition (Lewis 1986: 204–205).

The endurantist response, as noted above, is to distinguish between two notions of intrinsicality. But Lewis's point stands at one level: the relativization strategy cannot preserve intrinsicality in the narrow, strict sense that he intends. Whether this sense of intrinsicality is important enough to motivate rejecting endurantism is what the debate turns on.

## The Problem of Parthood Relations

A more specific objection to endurantism concerns parthood relations. Suppose that at t₁, my body includes my appendix; at t₂ (after appendectomy), it does not. If I am wholly present at both times, then at t₁ I have an appendix as a part, and at t₂ I do not. But if I am the very same wholly present object at both times, how can I have different parts?

This is sometimes called "the problem of temporary parthood." It is a version of the problem of temporary intrinsics applied to parts rather than properties. If having a certain part is intrinsic (and it seems to be: whether you have a part is a fact about how you are constituted, not about your relations to external things), then having and not having the same part seems to involve having incompatible intrinsic properties.

The endurantist can again respond by relativizing: I have-my-appendix-at-t₁ and I lack-my-appendix-at-t₂, and these are compatible because they are different relational facts. But the regress of objections and responses suggests that endurantism consistently purchases its account of change at the cost of treating all apparently intrinsic properties as relational.

## Van Inwagen's Objection

Peter van Inwagen (1990) raises a different and powerful objection to endurantism. If objects persist by being wholly present at each time, then the relation between the object at t₁ and the object at t₂ is strict numerical identity — they are the very same object. But van Inwagen argues that numerical identity is problematic as a relation between objects at different times, because it seems to require that there be genuine entities "at times" to be identified — and what would such entities be, if not temporal stages?

The worry is that endurantism, in trying to avoid temporal stages, ends up with an account in which the persistence relation between objects at different times is identity, but where the relata of the identity relation are not clearly specified. Perdurantism, by contrast, has a clear account of the relata: temporal stages are genuine, particular entities, and the persistence relation between them is the overlap or "worm-overlap" relation of mereology.

## The Problem for Ordinary Language

A final challenge for endurantism comes from ordinary language and thought. We do not ordinarily say that "the very same thing" is both sitting and standing; we say "she was sitting before and is standing now." The temporal qualification seems built into the way we describe change. Endurantism must explain why this temporal qualification is necessary — if the same whole object is present at both times, why does ordinary language resist saying "she is both sitting and standing" without a temporal qualification?

This challenge does not by itself refute endurantism, but it suggests that the ordinary picture of persistence is more complex than straightforward endurantism acknowledges. The temporal qualification in ordinary language suggests that we are, at some level, already thinking in terms of time-indexed property attributions — which is the relativization strategy that endurantism endorses, but which Lewis criticizes as a departure from genuine intrinsicality.

The debate between endurantism and perdurantism is thus a debate about what we are committed to when we say, without qualification, that an object has a property — and about how much metaphysical work the temporal qualifications we add (when we say "she was sitting") are doing.
