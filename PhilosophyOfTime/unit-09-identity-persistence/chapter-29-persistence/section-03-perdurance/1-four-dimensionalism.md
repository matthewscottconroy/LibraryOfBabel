# Four-Dimensionalism: Objects as Temporal Worms

## The Core Perdurantist Claim

Perdurantism holds that objects persist through time by having distinct **temporal parts** (also called temporal stages or time-slices) at each time during their existence. An object does not exist wholly at any single time; rather, it extends through time as it extends through space. What exists at any given moment is not the whole object but a temporal part of it — a "slice" of the four-dimensional extended object.

The four-dimensional object itself — what we ordinarily call "the person" or "the car" or "the river" — is a "temporal worm" composed of all its temporal parts from beginning to end. Just as a spatial worm (a snake, say) is extended through space and composed of different spatial parts (head, middle, tail), a temporal worm is extended through time and composed of different temporal parts (the Monday-slice, the Tuesday-slice, and so on).

The picture aligns naturally with the four-dimensional block universe of physics: in the spacetime manifold, objects occupy regions of four-dimensional spacetime, and temporal parts are the portions of those regions at particular times. The ontology is elegant and fits naturally with the scientific picture: objects are four-dimensional extended entities, just as physical theory describes.

## How It Handles Temporary Intrinsics

Perdurantism handles the problem of temporary intrinsics cleanly and without any need for the relativization strategies that Lewis finds objectionable. On the perdurantist account, when I was straight yesterday and am bent today, these incompatible properties are not instantiated by the same thing. They are instantiated by numerically distinct temporal parts: my yesterday-stage was straight, and my today-stage is bent. There is no contradiction, because the two stages are different entities — as different as my left hand and my right hand.

The crucial feature of this solution is that "being bent" is a genuine intrinsic property — not a relation to a time, not an adverb on an instantiation relation, but a genuine property an object has in virtue of how it is in itself. My today-stage is bent in virtue of how it is arranged, period. My yesterday-stage was straight in virtue of how it was arranged, period. The problem of temporary intrinsics dissolves because the apparent incompatibility was always between distinct entities (distinct temporal stages), not between incompatible properties of the same entity.

## Change as Difference Between Stages

On the perdurantist account, change is not a process that happens to a single entity but a matter of numerical difference between successive stages. When I "change" from straight to bent, what happens is that my straight yesterday-stage is succeeded by my bent today-stage. These stages are distinct, and the succession of stages with different properties is what we describe (loosely but effectively) as "change."

This account of change is efficient and precise. It does not invoke any mysterious "persisting substratum" that underlies the change — the changing thing does not need to be anything over and above its temporal stages. The persistence through change is just the existence of a connected series of stages, bound together by the appropriate relations (causal continuity, overlapping chains of qualitative similarity, spatiotemporal continuity).

## Temporal Parts and Spatial Parts: The Analogy

Lewis emphasizes the analogy between temporal parts and spatial parts: "temporal parts, like spatial parts, are genuine parts of objects, of the same ontological type as spatial parts" (Lewis 1986: 204). This analogy is both the most intuitive defense of temporal parts and a source of objections.

The spatial case is uncontroversial: we accept without difficulty that a long snake is composed of spatial parts, that different spatial parts can have different colors, and that this does not generate any contradiction. The snake is not contradictorily colored just because its head is red and its tail is blue; the head and tail are different parts. Similarly, the perdurantist argues, a persisting person is not contradictorily shaped just because an early temporal part is straight and a later temporal part is bent; the stages are different parts.

The analogy breaks down, however, in one significant respect: we can perceive spatial parts simultaneously, but we cannot perceive temporal parts simultaneously. When I look at the snake, I see its spatial extent. When I look at you, I do not see your temporal extent — I see your current temporal stage. This asymmetry between our epistemic access to spatial and temporal parts is one source of resistance to the perdurantist picture.

The perdurantist response is that our epistemic limitations do not reflect ontological structure. We cannot see radio waves, but they exist. We cannot see the insides of opaque objects, but they exist. Our inability to perceive temporal extent simultaneously does not show that temporal parts do not exist; it shows that our sensory systems are calibrated to present temporal cross-sections rather than temporal wholes.
