# Worm Theory: Mereology, Gains, and Costs

## The Mereology of Temporal Parts

Lewis's perdurantism, developed in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986) and *Parts of Classes* (1991), employs a mereological framework in which temporal parts are genuine parts of objects, governed by the same principles as spatial parts. Mereology is the formal theory of parthood and composition: it specifies the principles by which parts relate to wholes, and when a collection of parts composes something.

Lewis endorses **unrestricted mereological composition**: any collection of objects has a mereological sum, however gerrymandered or scattered. This is a strong claim that has controversial implications even in the purely spatial case. But the key application to temporal parts is this: for any region of spacetime, there exists a mereological sum of the portions of matter or stuff occupying that region. Temporal parts are simply the occupants of temporal slices of these four-dimensional spacetime regions.

The mereology of temporal parts works as follows. A complete persisting object — a person, say — is the mereological sum of all its temporal parts. Each temporal part is an entity that exists at exactly one time (or more precisely, that is wholly confined to a particular temporal region). The whole person is the sum of all these instantaneous (or short-duration) stages, connected by appropriate relations of psychological continuity, physical continuity, or whatever the appropriate persistence criterion for persons is.

This mereological framework has the advantage of being clean and formally precise: we can say exactly what kind of thing a temporal worm is (a mereological sum of temporal stages), how it relates to its parts (by mereological composition), and when two worms are identical (when they have exactly the same temporal parts). The framework inherits all the virtues of formal mereology: clarity, precision, and the absence of mysterious irreducible wholes over and above their parts.

## What Perdurantism Gains

Perdurantism resolves the problem of temporary intrinsics without requiring the relativization strategies that Lewis criticizes. It preserves genuine intrinsic properties — being bent, being straight, being spherical — as monadic properties of temporal stages, not as relations to times. And it accounts for change in a natural way: change is the difference in properties between successive temporal stages.

Perdurantism also handles the problem of **vagueness in persistence** with a distinctive elegance. Many ordinary persistence questions have no determinate answer: "Is this the same club after all the members have been replaced?" "Is this the same car after all the parts have been replaced?" Perdurantism handles these cases by noting that the identity of a temporal worm is in principle always determinate — it is the mereological sum of its temporal parts. Ordinary persistence questions seem to have no determinate answer because we use terms like "club" and "car" in ways that are vague about which temporal parts they pick out, not because there is any genuine indeterminacy in the underlying mereological facts.

Furthermore, perdurantism handles cases of **fission and fusion** that puzzle endurantists. If an amoeba divides into two amoebae, what happened to the original? On the perdurantist view, the original temporal worm simply branches: there are two post-fission worms, each sharing the pre-fission temporal parts, but diverging after the division point. There need be no identity crisis; we simply have two overlapping worms.

## What Perdurantism Costs

The main cost of perdurantism is one that strikes many philosophers as unacceptably high: **it complicates ordinary talk of identity over time**.

When I say "I will be at the party tomorrow," I seem to be saying something about myself — the person who is presently here — being at a future location. But on the perdurantist view, the entity that will be at the party is not identical to the entity that is here now; they are distinct temporal stages of a single worm. The worm will have a stage at the party, but "I" (if "I" refers to my current stage) will not be there.

This seems to conflict with ordinary usage. We say "I will be there" and mean it sincerely. Perdurantism seems to force the conclusion that this sincere utterance is, literally, false — that I (my current stage) will not be at the party, though my worm will have a stage at the party. Lewis is aware of this difficulty and handles it by arguing that when we use terms like "I" over time, we are typically referring to the whole temporal worm rather than to the present stage. But this requires a systematic reinterpretation of ordinary temporal language that many philosophers find ad hoc.

A related problem is **moral responsibility**. We hold people responsible for past actions. But on the perdurantist view, the person who performed the past action is a distinct temporal stage from the person who is now being held responsible. Do we have the right to punish or reward a temporal stage for what a different temporal stage did? Lewis's answer is that the whole temporal worm is responsible for the actions of all its stages, but this answer, while not obviously wrong, requires careful development.

A final cost concerns the **phenomenology of persistence**. We experience ourselves as wholly present, as persisting through time as unified subjects of experience. The perdurantist picture — in which at any moment only a stage of the temporal worm is present — seems to conflict with this phenomenological datum. The perdurantist response is that this is an artifact of our epistemic position: we can only directly experience our current stage, and this stage represents itself as the whole person, just as a spatial part of a mirror cannot "see" the whole mirror. But this response, while logically adequate, does not eliminate the phenomenological resistance.
