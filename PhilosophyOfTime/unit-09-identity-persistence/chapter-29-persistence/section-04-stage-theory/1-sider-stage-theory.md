# Sider's Stage Theory: Instantaneous Stages and Temporal Counterparts

## The Basic Idea

Ted Sider's stage theory, presented in *Four-Dimensionalism: An Ontology of Persistence and Time* (2001, Chapter 5), makes a crucial distinction that worm theory overlooks: the distinction between **what things there are** (the ontology) and **what our ordinary terms refer to** (the semantics).

Stage theory agrees with worm theory that the fundamental ontology includes four-dimensional spacetime and temporal stages (instantaneous or short-duration slices of four-dimensional worms). But it disagrees about which of these entities our ordinary object-terms refer to. Worm theory says "the cat" refers to the whole four-dimensional worm. Stage theory says "the cat" refers to the **current temporal stage** — the instantaneous (or short-duration) stage that exists at the present moment.

The natural objection is immediate: if "the cat" refers to the current stage, and the current stage is instantaneous (or very brief), then "the cat has existed for ten years" comes out false — the current stage is only a moment old. Stage theory handles this objection with the concept of **temporal counterparts**.

## Temporal Counterparts

The concept of temporal counterparts is borrowed, with modification, from Lewis's counterpart theory in modal logic. In Lewis's modal counterpart theory, when we say "I could have been a doctor," this is true because there is a possible-world counterpart of me — a qualitatively similar individual in another possible world — who is a doctor. I myself am not in that other world; it is my counterpart who is.

Sider's temporal counterpart theory applies the same move to temporal contexts. When we say "I will be at the party tomorrow," this is true because there is a temporal counterpart of me — the tomorrow-stage of the worm to which I belong — who will be at the party. My current stage will not literally be at the party; it is my temporal counterpart that will be there. But our temporal statement is true because of the counterpart's future presence.

Similarly, "I have existed for twenty years" is true for a current stage not because the stage itself is twenty years old (it is an instant old) but because there is a long sequence of temporal counterparts — stages of the same worm — stretching back twenty years.

The counterpart relation is not identity: my tomorrow-stage is not numerically identical to my today-stage. But it is a relation close enough to identity — sharing psychological continuity, physical continuity, and other relevant criteria — that temporal statements about "me" that invoke past or future are made true by the counterparts rather than by strict identity (Sider 2001: 193–208).

## Advantages of Stage Theory Over Worm Theory

Sider argues that stage theory has significant advantages over worm theory in handling ordinary temporal discourse.

**Indexical terms**: When I say "I am tired," the word "I" is an indexical that refers to me — the present speaker, the present context. Worm theory says that "I" refers to the whole temporal worm. But this seems wrong: if the worm is twenty years old, then "I am twenty years old" should come out as straightforwardly true (it is a property of the whole worm), but this does not seem right if I am pointing to a very young person. Stage theory handles this naturally: "I" refers to the present stage, which is indeed very young.

**Temporary overlap in fission cases**: Suppose an amoeba undergoes fission at noon. Before noon, there is one amoeba. After noon, there are two. Worm theory faces a problem: before the fission, is there one worm or two? (Two worms overlap in the pre-fission stages — but can two distinct worms share all the same parts?) Stage theory faces no such problem: before the fission there is one stage; after the fission there are two stages; the pre-fission stage is a temporal counterpart of each of the two post-fission stages (via different counterpart chains), and this generates no contradiction.

**Practical engagement**: Stage theory also handles practical concern for the future more naturally than worm theory for some philosophers. When I worry about what will happen to "me" tomorrow, I am (literally) a present stage worrying about a distinct future counterpart. This opens the door to a Parfitian reflection: if the entity I call "my future self" is not strictly identical to me but is a counterpart, then my prudential concern for my future self is structurally similar to (though not identical with) my concern for others. This convergence with Parfit's conclusions in *Reasons and Persons* is philosophically interesting.

## Objections to Stage Theory

Stage theory faces its own distinctive objections.

**The counterpart relation is not persistence**: Critics argue that genuine persistence requires identity over time, not counterpart relations. If my today-stage and my tomorrow-stage are numerically distinct entities related by counterpart, then strictly speaking "I" will not persist — there will be no "I" tomorrow, only a counterpart. Sider responds that counterpart relations are the right kind of relation for capturing what matters in persistence, but critics find this a departure from what persistence means.

**The ontological commitment**: Stage theory shares with worm theory the commitment to a large ontology of temporal stages. This is a commitment that endurantists reject as unnecessary. If the aim was to avoid the strangeness of worm theory, it is not clear that stage theory succeeds — it retains all the same ontological commitments while adding the machinery of temporal counterparts.

**Arbitrariness of the counterpart relation**: The counterpart relation must be defined precisely enough to determine which future stages count as counterparts of which present stages. But there seem to be many ways to draw this boundary, and the choice between them seems arbitrary. In Lewis's modal counterpart theory, this arbitrariness is acknowledged and treated as a feature (context determines which similarity relation is relevant). In Sider's temporal counterpart theory, the same arbitrariness reappears, and it is not clearly a feature.

Despite these objections, stage theory is a significant contribution to the debate: it shows that the division between endurantism and perdurantism is not exhaustive, and that the choice of ontology (stages or worms or wholly present objects) can be partly independent of the choice of semantics (what ordinary object-terms refer to).
