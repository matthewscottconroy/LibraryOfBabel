# The Ship of Theseus Puzzle

## The Original Puzzle

The puzzle of the Ship of Theseus has ancient origins and is reported by Plutarch in his *Life of Theseus*. The ship in which Theseus sailed to Crete and returned victorious was preserved by the Athenians as a memorial. As the years passed, its planks rotted and were replaced, one by one, until all the original planks had been replaced with new ones. The Athenians continued to call it the Ship of Theseus. Is it?

The intuition pulling toward "yes" is the continuity of the ship: there was a continuous physical and functional object present throughout the process of gradual replacement. At no point was the ship destroyed and a new one constructed; it was constantly repaired and maintained. The gradual replacement was not discontinuous with the original ship; each moment, the ship existed with most of its previous planks and one new plank. Continuity of this kind seems to be exactly what persistence amounts to for artifacts.

The intuition pulling toward "no" — or at least generating puzzlement — is the material replacement: when all the original planks are gone, there is literally nothing material in common between the "original" ship and the "current" ship. If the ship's identity consisted in its material constitution, then replacing all the material should yield a different ship.

## Hobbes's Variant

Thomas Hobbes, in *De Corpore* (1655), sharpened the puzzle with a devastating variant. Suppose that as each old plank is removed from Theseus's ship, it is not destroyed but is carefully preserved. When all the planks have been replaced, someone collects the original planks and reassembles them, in the original arrangement, into a ship. We now have two ships: Ship A (the continuously maintained ship in the harbor) and Ship B (the ship rebuilt from all the original planks). Which is the Ship of Theseus?

This variant is philosophically devastating because each candidate ship has a strong claim:

- Ship A has **continuous spatiotemporal history** and **functional continuity** with the original ship. It was always present in the harbor, always called the Ship of Theseus, always the ship that was used for the annual voyage. It was never destroyed; it simply underwent gradual repair.
- Ship B has **material identity** with the original ship. It is composed of exactly the same planks as the original ship, arranged in the same way. If you think that material constitution is central to identity, then Ship B has a claim to be the "real" Ship of Theseus.

It seems clear that both ships cannot be the original Ship of Theseus — the relation of numerical identity is transitive, symmetric, and reflexive, and if A = original and B = original, then A = B, which is false (they are clearly two distinct ships). So at most one can be the original. But there seems to be no principled reason to prefer one over the other.

## How the Theories Handle the Puzzle

**Endurantism**: An endurantist must say that at most one of the two ships is the Ship of Theseus. The most natural endurantist answer is that Ship A is the Ship of Theseus, because endurantism typically appeals to causal-historical and functional continuity as the criterion of persistence for artifacts, and Ship A satisfies these criteria. Ship B is a reconstruction, not the original. But the endurantist must acknowledge that this answer involves making a decision about which criterion of persistence to apply — causal-historical continuity vs. material identity — and that the decision is not forced by the logic of identity alone.

**Perdurantism**: The perdurantist can give a clean answer: there are two distinct four-dimensional temporal worms. One worm (call it W₁) has the original planks as its early stages and the replacement planks as its later stages; this is the continuously maintained ship in the harbor. Another worm (call it W₂) has the original planks as its early stages and then a gap, followed by the reassembled stages after the reconstruction. Which worm is the "Ship of Theseus" depends on which temporal worm the name "Ship of Theseus" picks out — and this is a semantic/conventional matter, not a metaphysical one.

The perdurantist insight is important: the question "which is really the Ship of Theseus?" may have no determinate metaphysical answer because the name "Ship of Theseus" is vague about which temporal worm it refers to. The puzzle is an artifact of this semantic vagueness, not a genuine metaphysical puzzle about which of two ships "really is" the original.

**Stage Theory**: Stage theory offers a similar resolution. The question "which ship is the Ship of Theseus?" is a question about which ship the name "Ship of Theseus" refers to at the present time. Whether the name tracks the continuously maintained ship (Ship A) or the reassembled ship (Ship B) depends on which counterpart relation is semantically active in the context of the question. In a context where continuity is paramount, Ship A is the counterpart; in a context where material identity is paramount, Ship B is the counterpart. Different contexts yield different answers, and neither answer is "the" correct answer in a context-independent sense.

## What the Puzzle Reveals

The Ship of Theseus puzzle reveals that identity claims for ordinary objects involve a **conventional element**. Whether we say that a given ship is "the same ship" as an earlier one depends not only on objective facts about the world (the causal history, the material composition, the functional continuity) but also on which criteria of identity we are employing — and those criteria are partly a matter of our practices, interests, and conventions.

This conclusion applies with particular force to the case of persons. If the identity of ships is partly conventional, is the identity of persons partly conventional too? This is one of the questions that motivates Parfit's radical revisionism about personal identity, which we examine in the next chapter. Parfit argues that the question "is this person the same person as the earlier person?" may have no determinate answer in puzzle cases, and that this indeterminacy reveals that identity is not what matters for our practical and moral concerns.

The ship puzzle also reveals the limits of thinking about persistence in terms of strict numerical identity. If we ask "is this ship numerically identical to the original?" we generate puzzles that may not have determinate answers. If we ask instead "is this ship continuous with the original in the relevant respects — causally, functionally, materially?" we may get determinate answers, but they may conflict with each other, and we must decide which form of continuity matters for our purposes. The puzzle thus points toward a more pragmatic and less identity-obsessed approach to questions of persistence — an approach that Parfit will develop for the case of persons.
