# Chapter 30 Exercises: Personal Identity Over Time

## Analytical Exercises

**1. Locke's Memory Criterion**
Explain Locke's memory criterion of personal identity in your own words. (a) What is the criterion? (b) What role does the concept of "forensic person" play in Locke's account? (c) What does the Prince and the Cobbler thought experiment demonstrate? (d) What is the most important distinction that Locke draws — between substance identity and personal identity? Why does Locke think this distinction matters?

**2. The Circularity Objections**
Explain both Reid's circularity objection and Butler's objection to Locke. (a) Are these the same objection, or are they subtly different? (b) Explain the neo-Lockean concept of quasi-memory and how it is supposed to answer the circularity objection. (c) Does the move to quasi-memory succeed? Is quasi-memory a genuine psychological concept, or is it an artifical construct invented to solve a philosophical problem? Defend your view.

**3. Hume's Bundle Theory and Its Difficulties**
Explain Hume's bundle theory of the self. (a) What is the starting point: the denial of any impression of a persistent self? (b) What does Hume say the self "really is," according to his analysis? (c) What does Hume confess in the Appendix, and why is he dissatisfied? (d) Does his dissatisfaction show that the bundle theory is wrong, or only that it is incomplete? What would a complete bundle theory need to include?

**4. Psychological Continuity vs. Animalism**
Compare the psychological continuity view (Shoemaker, Parfit) with animalism (Olson, van Inwagen). (a) Describe the central claim of each view. (b) Present the strongest argument for psychological continuity — probably involving brain transplant cases. (c) Present Olson's response. (d) Present the strongest argument for animalism — probably involving the thinking animal problem. (e) How do you adjudicate between these views? Is there a case type that clearly shows one is right and the other wrong?

**5. The Fission Argument**
Reconstruct Parfit's fission argument as precisely as you can. (a) Describe the fission scenario. (b) Why does each of the four possible answers (you = Lefty, you = Righty, you = both, you = neither) face a serious objection? (c) What conclusion does Parfit draw from the indeterminacy of personal identity in fission cases? (d) Is this conclusion convincing? Are there alternative responses to fission that Parfit has not adequately considered?

**6. What Matters in Survival**
Parfit argues that what matters in survival is not identity but psychological continuity and connectedness (Relation R). (a) Explain what Relation R is and why Parfit thinks it captures what matters. (b) Does the fission argument actually show that identity is not what matters, or only that identity may be indeterminate? (c) What are the ethical implications of Parfit's conclusion for the practices of moral responsibility and prudential self-concern? Are these implications acceptable or troubling?

**7. Narrative Identity vs. Analytic Accounts**
Compare MacIntyre and Ricoeur's narrative identity approach with the analytic accounts (Locke, psychological continuity, Parfit). (a) What are the main claims of the narrative approach? (b) In what ways does it challenge the analytic debate? (c) Is narrative identity a genuine theory of personal identity, or is it an account of something else (self-understanding, moral agency, ethical life) that is different from personal identity as the analytic tradition understands it? (d) Can the analytic and narrative approaches be integrated, or are they genuinely rival accounts of different phenomena?

## Comparative Essay Exercises

**8. The Relation Between Memory and Identity**
Locke argues that memory constitutes personal identity. Butler and Reid argue that memory presupposes it. Parfit argues that what matters is not identity but psychological continuity including q-memory. MacIntyre and Ricoeur argue that narrative self-interpretation constitutes identity. Write an essay tracing the development of thinking about the relationship between memory and personal identity from Locke through the contemporary debate. What is the key insight of each thinker, and what is the central difficulty that drives the debate forward?

**9. Personal Identity and Moral Responsibility**
The question of what constitutes personal identity has direct implications for the justification of practices of moral responsibility. Locke grounds responsibility in consciousness continuity. Parfit's revisionism challenges standard accounts of responsibility. Korsgaard argues that identity is connected to agency in ways Parfit misses. Write an essay examining the relationship between personal identity and moral responsibility, assessing what each of the main positions in this chapter implies for the justification of holding people responsible for past actions.

## Observational and Experiential Lab Exercises

**10. Memory and Personal Identity (Lab)**
This exercise involves structured autobiographical reflection. Take 30–40 minutes in quiet.

Think of an important event from your childhood — something that happened when you were between seven and twelve years old, something that you regard as having some significance for who you became. Recall it as fully as you can.

Then answer:

(a) Do you feel that the child who experienced this event was *you*? Not "a person similar to you" or "someone with whom you are continuous" but genuinely, fully *you*? 
(b) Can you identify, in this experience, the difference between (i) merely knowing that this event occurred and (ii) genuinely recalling it from the inside — the autonoetic quality that Tulving describes? Are you sure you can tell the difference?
(c) How much has the memory changed over the years? Is there any sense in which the "memory" you are recalling is partly a product of who you are now rather than a faithful record of the original experience?
(d) Apply Parfit's framework: what is the psychological connectivity between you now and the child who experienced this event? Is it strong or weak? Does this affect whether you feel that child was you?

Write a reflection connecting your experience to the philosophical accounts in this chapter.

**11. The Narrative Self (Lab)**
Take thirty minutes to write a brief autobiographical narrative — a story of your life up to now. Do not write a list of events; write a story, with a through-line, a sense of development, and some identification of what matters and why.

After completing the narrative, reflect on the following:

(a) What did you emphasize and what did you omit? What do the choices of emphasis reveal about your self-understanding?
(b) Are there events in your past that are hard to integrate into the narrative — events that resist narrative coherence? How did you handle them?
(c) Does the narrative feel like a discovery (you are uncovering who you really are) or a construction (you are deciding how to interpret and organize your experience)?
(d) MacIntyre says that the unity of a self is the unity of a narrative. Does writing this narrative support or challenge that claim?
(e) Ricoeur distinguishes idem-identity (sameness) from ipse-identity (selfhood as commitment). Can you identify examples of each in your narrative? Are there commitments that define who you are regardless of changes in memory, belief, or even character?

**12. Imagining Fission (Lab)**
Parfit's fission thought experiment is designed to reveal something about the nature of personal identity by pushing our intuitions to their limit. This exercise asks you to inhabit the thought experiment as concretely as possible.

Imagine, in as much detail as you can, the following scenario. You are told that tomorrow, your brain will be divided and each half transplanted into a different body. Each body is of a different person who has volunteered. Each recipient will wake up with your memories, your personality, your values, your sense of your own history. They will both remember being you, will both continue your projects, will both have relationships with your friends and family.

Reflect on:

(a) Does this scenario strike you as survival or as death? What is your immediate, pre-theoretical intuition?
(b) Does your intuition change if you are told that only one hemisphere will be transplanted (the other being discarded)? If so, what does this tell you about what matters to you?
(c) If both successors are psychologically continuous with you, why does it feel (if it does) different from straightforward survival?
(d) Parfit says that fission reveals that what matters is not identity but psychological continuity, and that we should find fission no worse than ordinary survival. Do you find this convincing? What remains troubling about the scenario, if anything?

Write a reflection on what the thought experiment reveals about your sense of personal identity and what it is you most care about preserving.
