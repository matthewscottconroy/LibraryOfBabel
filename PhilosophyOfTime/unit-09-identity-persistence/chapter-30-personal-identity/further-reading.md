## Annotated Entries

**Parfit, Derek. *Reasons and Persons*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1984.**
The single most important book in the contemporary debate about personal identity. Part III develops the fission argument, the analysis of psychological continuity, and the conclusion that identity is not what matters. Parfit's prose is unusually clear and his thought experiments are carefully constructed. Essential reading for any serious engagement with the contemporary debate.

**Perry, John, ed. *Personal Identity*. 2nd ed. Berkeley: University of California Press, 2008.**
An excellent anthology bringing together the classic texts in the debate, including selections from Locke, Hume, Butler, Reid, and twentieth-century philosophers. An ideal starting point for students who want to read primary sources alongside secondary analysis. Perry's own introductory essay is one of the best overviews of the debate available.

**Noonan, Harold. *Personal Identity*. 3rd ed. London: Routledge, 2019.**
The most comprehensive and up-to-date survey of the debate, covering all the major positions and arguments from Locke through the contemporary literature. Noonan's treatment is both historically thorough and philosophically rigorous. The third edition incorporates recent work on animalism, narrative identity, and Parfit's later views.

**Olson, Eric T. *The Human Animal: Personal Identity without Psychology*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1997.**
The definitive statement of animalism, arguing that we are fundamentally biological organisms and that psychological continuity is neither necessary nor sufficient for personal identity. Olson's thinking animal problem and his responses to brain transplant intuitions are developed with care and rigor. An essential alternative to the neo-Lockean tradition.

**Schechtman, Marya. *The Constitution of Selves*. Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 1996.**
A sophisticated development of the narrative approach to personal identity, arguing that Parfit's reductionism fails to capture what is essential to a self — the capacity for coherent self-narrative. Schechtman's "narrative self-constitution view" is one of the most developed alternatives to the neo-Lockean and animalist accounts. A valuable bridge between analytic and narrative approaches.

**MacIntyre, Alasdair. *After Virtue: A Study in Moral Theory*. 3rd ed. Notre Dame: University of Notre Dame Press, 2007.**
The foundational text for the narrative approach to ethics and personal identity. Chapter 15, "The Virtues, the Unity of a Human Life, and the Concept of a Tradition," develops the claim that personal identity is constituted by narrative unity. MacIntyre's broader argument that modern moral philosophy has lost the narrative context that gave its concepts their sense makes this an important book for both ethics and philosophy of mind.

**Williams, Bernard. *Problems of the Self: Philosophical Papers 1956–1972*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1973.**
Contains several important papers on personal identity, including the influential "Personal Identity and Individuation" and "Are Persons Bodies?" Williams is a critic of the psychological continuity approach, arguing that bodily continuity has more significance for personal identity than Lockeans acknowledge. His thought experiments and arguments are incisive and often serve as correctives to overly permissive applications of psychological continuity.

**Ricoeur, Paul. *Oneself as Another*. Translated by Kathleen Blamey. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1992.**
Ricoeur's major contribution to the theory of personal identity and ethics, developing the concepts of idem-identity, ipse-identity, and narrative identity in a comprehensive framework. Studies 5–6 are directly relevant to the issues of personal identity discussed in this chapter. The book as a whole connects the theory of identity to phenomenology, hermeneutics, ethics, and political philosophy in an ambitious and illuminating synthesis.

**Korsgaard, Christine. "Personal Identity and the Unity of Agency: A Kantian Response to Parfit." *Philosophy and Public Affairs* 18, no. 2 (1989): 101–132.**
A rigorous Kantian critique of Parfit's separation of identity from what matters, arguing that the unity of a self is a practical achievement required for rational agency rather than a metaphysical fact that can be analyzed away. One of the most important papers in the contemporary debate, and essential reading alongside Parfit.

**Shoemaker, Sydney, and Richard Swinburne. *Personal Identity*. Oxford: Blackwell, 1984.**
Two contrasting accounts of personal identity in dialogue. Shoemaker defends a psychological continuity view; Swinburne defends a substance dualist view (the self is an immaterial soul). Together they cover the terrain of the traditional debate with philosophical rigor. Shoemaker's contribution is particularly important for understanding the neo-Lockean development of Locke's account.

**Hume, David. *A Treatise of Human Nature*. Book I, Part iv, Section 6; Appendix.**
The primary source for Hume's bundle theory and his confession of dissatisfaction. Book I, Part iv, Section 6 presents the argument; the Appendix presents the problem of the cement. Reading both sections carefully, in order, is an excellent philosophical exercise: you witness a great philosopher working through a problem with complete intellectual honesty and arriving at a genuine aporia.
