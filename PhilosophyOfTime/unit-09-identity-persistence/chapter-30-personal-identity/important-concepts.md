# Important Concepts: Chapter 30

**Animalism**: The view that human persons are fundamentally biological organisms, and that personal identity over time consists in the continuity of the biological organism. Animalism makes personal identity determinate and grounded in biological fact, avoiding the puzzle cases that beset psychological continuity views. Its main challenge is the brain transplant scenario, where most people intuit that the person goes with the brain rather than the body.

**Autonoetic Consciousness**: Tulving's term for the form of self-awareness that accompanies episodic memory — the first-person sense of "being there" in the recalled event. Autonoetic consciousness enables temporal self-location and is what distinguishes genuine memory from merely knowing facts about the past. Reid's objection to Locke turns partly on the claim that memory, properly understood (with its autonoetic character), presupposes personal identity rather than constituting it.

**Bundle Theory**: Hume's view that the self is nothing more than a collection of distinct perceptions related by resemblance and causation. There is no unified persisting self underlying the bundle — only the perceptions themselves and the psychological relations among them. The bundle theory correctly identifies the phenomenological datum (no impression of a persistent self) but struggles to explain the unity of the bundle, a problem Hume acknowledges in the Appendix to the *Treatise*.

**Fission**: A thought experiment (most fully developed by Parfit) in which a person's brain is divided and each half transplanted into a different body, with each resulting person having equal psychological continuity with the original. Fission cases reveal the tension between the psychological continuity criterion (each successor is equally continuous) and the requirement that identity be one-to-one (you cannot be both successors). Parfit uses fission to argue that identity is not what matters in survival.

**Forensic Person**: Locke's characterization of the concept of "person" as fundamentally a forensic (legal and moral accountability) concept. Persons are the kind of thing that can be held responsible for actions, rewarded and punished, held to commitments. This forensic character of personhood motivates Locke's memory criterion: moral and legal accountability tracks consciousness continuity, not substance identity.

**Idem-Identity**: Ricoeur's term for numerical and qualitative sameness — the kind of identity that consists in being the same entity with the same properties. This is the sense of identity that analytic philosophy of personal identity typically concerns itself with. Idem-identity is contrasted with ipse-identity (selfhood as commitment), and narrative identity mediates between the two.

**Ipse-Identity**: Ricoeur's term for selfhood understood as fidelity to commitments — the identity of a self that remains "the same" not in the sense of numerical sameness but in the sense of keeping its word, honoring its projects, and remaining true to its values. Ipse-identity is dynamic and ethical: it is constituted through the ongoing practice of self-commitment, not through any static metaphysical fact.

**Memory Criterion (Locke)**: The criterion of personal identity according to which person A at t₁ is the same person as person B at t₂ if and only if B can remember (from the inside) experiences had by A. More precisely (on the neo-Lockean development): personal identity consists in overlapping chains of memory connections (or quasi-memory connections) over time. The criterion is motivated by the forensic character of personhood and by the intuition that what makes a future person "mine" is my psychological connection to them.

**Narrative Identity**: The view, associated especially with MacIntyre and Ricoeur, that personal identity over time is constituted by the coherence of a life story — the interpretive narrative by which a self makes its life intelligible as a whole. Narrative identity is not a static metaphysical fact but an ongoing achievement: the self is continually reconstituted through self-interpretation. It mediates between idem-identity and ipse-identity.

**Psychological Connectedness**: The direct psychological link between two mental states — a specific memory, a specific belief held at two times, a specific intention carried out, a specific character trait maintained. Psychological connectedness is stronger when there are many direct psychological links and weaker when there are few. Parfit distinguishes connectedness from continuity: continuity is the weaker relation of overlapping chains of direct connections.

**Psychological Continuity**: The weaker relation that obtains between mental states connected by overlapping chains of direct psychological connections (memories, beliefs, intentions, character traits, etc.). Personal identity, on the neo-Lockean view, consists in sufficient psychological continuity (with the right kind of cause). Psychological continuity can come in degrees and can obtain between persons who are not strictly numerically identical.

**Quasi-Memory (Q-Memory)**: Shoemaker's concept of a memory-like state that has the phenomenological character of episodic memory — representing a past experience from the inside — but that does not analytically presuppose the numerical identity of the q-rememberer and the original experiencer. Q-memory is introduced to respond to Reid's circularity objection: psychological continuity accounts can be stated in terms of q-memory without presupposing the identity they are meant to analyze.

**Reduplication Problem**: The problem for the psychological continuity criterion arising from the logical possibility that psychological continuity might obtain between one person at an earlier time and two persons at a later time. Since identity is one-to-one (A cannot be numerically identical to both B and C if B ≠ C), the psychological continuity criterion must either add a uniqueness requirement or accept that psychological continuity can be what matters without being identity.

**Relation R (Parfit)**: Parfit's term for the relation of psychological continuity and connectedness with the right kind of cause — the relation that Parfit argues is what actually matters in survival, as distinct from strict numerical identity. Relation R can obtain between entities that are not strictly identical (as in fission cases), and it can come in degrees. Parfit's central claim is that it is Relation R, not identity, that grounds our deepest practical and prudential concerns.

**Thinking Animal Problem**: Olson's objection to non-animalist views: if you are a psychological subject distinct from the human organism that currently occupies your chair, then there are two thinking things in the chair — you and the animal. This seems ontologically extravagant. Animalism avoids the problem by identifying persons with organisms.

**Transitivity of Identity**: The logical requirement that identity is transitive: if A = B and B = C, then A = C. Reid's objection to Locke's memory criterion is that it violates transitivity: a person can remember the experiences of a middle period, which remembered the experiences of an early period, without the person remembering the early period's experiences — generating the apparent result that A = B, B = C, but A ≠ C.
