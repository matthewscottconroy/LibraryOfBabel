# Important Researchers and Thinkers: Chapter 30

**John Locke (1632–1704)**: English philosopher whose chapter on identity in the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689/1694) established the modern terms of the debate about personal identity. Locke's key move — separating personal identity (constituted by consciousness continuity) from substance identity (constituted by material or spiritual continuity) — remains the starting point of the contemporary debate. His forensic conception of personhood, connecting identity to moral responsibility, has been deeply influential. Locke's philosophy more broadly — his epistemology, his political philosophy, and his theology — was among the most influential in the Enlightenment tradition.

**David Hume (1711–1776)**: Scottish philosopher whose radical empiricist analysis of the self in the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40) argued that the self is a "bundle of perceptions" with no underlying unity or persistence. Hume's bundle theory is remarkable both for its consistency (he follows the empiricist principle rigorously wherever it leads) and for his honest acknowledgment, in the Appendix, that his own account fails to explain the unity of the bundle. Hume's broader philosophy — his skepticism about causation, his theory of moral sentiment, his naturalistic account of religion — had enormous influence on subsequent philosophy, and his treatment of personal identity connects naturally to Buddhist philosophy of no-self.

**Thomas Reid (1710–1796)**: Scottish philosopher and founder of the Common Sense school of philosophy. His *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785) contains the famous objection to Locke's memory criterion (the "brave officer" argument, now usually presented in a different version) establishing the circularity and non-transitivity of the memory criterion. Reid's broader philosophical project — defending common sense against Hume's scepticism — is a major contribution to the Scottish Enlightenment.

**Joseph Butler (1692–1752)**: English bishop and philosopher whose appendix "Of Personal Identity" in *The Analogy of Religion* (1736) anticipated Reid's objection to Locke, arguing that memory presupposes personal identity rather than constituting it. Butler's argument is the earliest clear statement of the circularity problem for the memory criterion. His broader philosophical work, particularly *Fifteen Sermons* (1726), is an important contribution to moral philosophy.

**Sydney Shoemaker (born 1931)**: American philosopher who has been the most important developer of the neo-Lockean psychological continuity approach since Locke himself. His *Self-Knowledge and Self-Identity* (1963) developed the philosophical analysis of personal identity in terms of psychological continuity, and his introduction of the concept of quasi-memory was a key move in responding to the circularity objection. His work on functionalism, personal identity, and causation has been central to analytic philosophy of mind for six decades.

**Derek Parfit (1942–2017)**: British philosopher whose *Reasons and Persons* (1984) is arguably the most important single book in analytic ethics and personal identity since the mid-twentieth century. Parfit's analysis of personal identity in Part III, culminating in the argument that identity is not what matters, challenged fundamental assumptions about the self, moral responsibility, and rational prudence. He was known for the extraordinary care and honesty of his philosophical writing — he reportedly revised his papers dozens of times — and for a personal commitment to philosophy that was evident in both his work and his life. *On What Matters* (2011) was his final major work.

**Eric Olson (born 1966)**: American philosopher and leading defender of animalism — the view that we are fundamentally biological organisms. His book *The Human Animal* (1997) presents the most systematic and philosophically careful case for animalism, developing the thinking animal problem and responding to the puzzle cases that motivate psychological continuity views. Olson's work has established animalism as a serious alternative to neo-Lockean accounts in contemporary analytic philosophy.

**Alasdair MacIntyre (born 1929)**: Scottish-American philosopher whose *After Virtue* (1981) is one of the most important contributions to ethics since the mid-twentieth century. His argument that the virtues are intelligible only in the context of the narrative unity of a human life connects the theory of personal identity to virtue ethics in a way that challenges both the analytic and utilitarian traditions. His broader philosophical project — recovering Aristotelian virtue ethics in the context of modern social life — has influenced philosophy, theology, and political theory.

**Marya Schechtman (born 1961)**: American philosopher who has developed one of the most sustained critical responses to Parfit and defenses of a narrative approach to personal identity. Her book *The Constitution of Selves* (1996) argues that Parfit's reductionism about personal identity fails to capture what is essential to a self — the capacity for self-narrative — and develops a "narrative self-constitution view" on which personal identity is constituted by the coherence of one's first-person self-understanding. She is one of the most important contributors to the contemporary debate.

**Christine Korsgaard (born 1952)**: American philosopher whose work in Kantian ethics connects personal identity to practical reason in a way that challenges Parfit's conclusion. Her paper "Personal Identity and the Unity of Agency" (1989) argues that Parfit's view neglects the normative identity of a unified agent — the identity that is required for rational deliberation and action. On Korsgaard's view, the unity of a self is not primarily a metaphysical fact but a practical achievement: we constitute ourselves as unified agents through the act of rational self-governance, and this practical identity is what grounds our deepest concerns about survival.
