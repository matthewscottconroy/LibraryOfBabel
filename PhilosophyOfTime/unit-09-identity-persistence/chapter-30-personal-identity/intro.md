# Chapter 30: Personal Identity Over Time

Imagine waking one morning with all your memories intact — your childhood recollections, your accumulated knowledge, your remembered relationships, your sense of your own history — but in a different body. Or imagine waking with no memories at all: a blank slate, knowing who you are only because people tell you, seeing your own past as a stranger's history. Would you be the same person in either case?

These are not merely science-fictional curiosities. They are test cases for one of the most practically significant questions in philosophy: what makes you the same person over time? The question matters because our practices of moral responsibility, promise-keeping, prudential concern, and personal relationship all rest on assumptions about personal identity. We hold people responsible for past actions because we think they are the same people who performed those actions. We make and keep promises because we think our future selves are the same persons who made them. We care about our futures because we think the person who will exist tomorrow is in some important sense us.

If personal identity turns out to be less determinate, more conventional, or more superficial than we normally assume — as Parfit (1984) argues — then these practices may need revision. If personal identity is constituted by memory — as Locke argues — then amnesia cases become profoundly troubling for our practices of responsibility. If we are fundamentally animals — as Olson (1997) argues — then brain transplant scenarios have a different upshot than most people's intuitions suggest.

This chapter traces the philosophical debate about personal identity from Locke through the contemporary literature. It begins with Locke's classical memory theory and the devastating objections raised by Reid and Butler, moves through Hume's bundle theory and its acknowledged failure, arrives at the neo-Lockean psychological continuity views of Shoemaker and Parfit, considers the animalist alternative, and concludes with Parfit's radical argument that identity is not what matters — and with the narrative identity views of MacIntyre and Ricoeur that offer a richer account of what mattering in personal life actually involves.

The goal throughout is not merely to catalogue positions but to understand why each position is driven to the next by difficulties it cannot resolve — and to develop your own considered view about what personal identity is and why it matters.
