# Locke's Memory Criterion of Personal Identity

## The Background: Substance and Identity

Locke approaches the question of personal identity against the background of his broader discussion of identity in *Essay* II.27. He argues that the identity of any thing — a mass of matter, an organism, a machine — consists in nothing more than its continuity as the same kind of thing: the identity of a mass of matter consists in being composed of the same atoms; the identity of an organism consists in the continuity of life — the organization of matter to perform the functions characteristic of that kind of organism.

The question of personal identity is different, Locke argues, because the relevant kind of thing — a person — is defined differently from either a mass of matter or a biological organism. A person, for Locke, is "a thinking intelligent Being that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing in different times and places" (*Essay* II.27.9). Personhood is defined by reflective self-awareness — the capacity to take oneself as an object of consciousness, to recognize oneself across time.

Given this definition of personhood, Locke argues that personal identity must consist in the continuity of consciousness rather than in the continuity of any underlying substance. Whether the substance that thinks is a soul, a brain, or any other material or immaterial thing, what makes the thinker at one time the same person as the thinker at another time is not the identity of the substance but the continuity of consciousness.

## The Memory Criterion

More specifically, Locke proposes that personal identity consists in the continuity of **memory**: "as far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any past action or thought, so far reaches the identity of that person" (*Essay* II.27.9). I am the same person as the person who performed some past action if and only if I can remember performing that action (or can remember remembering it, etc.).

This criterion has considerable intuitive appeal. Our ordinary practices of attributing identity seem to track memory: we treat someone as the same person over time partly because they remember their own past. When we hold someone responsible for a past action, we typically expect them to be able to remember (at least in principle) having performed it. When a person suffers from severe amnesia and can no longer remember their past, we recognize something distinctively troubling about their situation — not just that they lack certain information, but that their connection to their own past has been severed in a way that bears on their identity.

## The Prince and the Cobbler

Locke illustrates his criterion with what is now called the "prince and the cobbler" thought experiment (*Essay* II.27.15). Suppose that the soul of a prince — with all the prince's memories and consciousness — passes into the body of a cobbler at death. And suppose the cobbler's soul passes into the prince's body. Locke argues that the person who wakes in the cobbler's body is the prince, not the cobbler: for the waking consciousness is continuous with the prince's consciousness (has the prince's memories, the prince's self-awareness), and personal identity follows consciousness, not the body.

The thought experiment makes a sharp point: even if all the physical facts remain constant — the same bodies exist, the same brains are in the same skulls — personal identity can (on Locke's account) track consciousness rather than physical constitution. And even if the same soul (in Descartes's sense) remains in the same body, if the consciousness associated with that soul changes entirely, a different person is present.

## Why Consciousness Rather Than Substance

Locke's argument for consciousness over substance as the criterion of personal identity has a moral and forensic dimension that is often overlooked. He argues that the concept of a "person" is primarily a forensic concept — a concept relevant to legal and moral accountability (*Essay* II.27.26). Persons are agents who can be held responsible for their actions, who can be rewarded and punished, who can be asked to justify what they have done.

For moral and legal purposes, what matters is not which substance performed the action but who — which conscious self — performed it. If my current consciousness is continuous with the consciousness that performed some past action, then I am the person who performed it, and I can be held responsible for it. The consciousness continuity criterion thus reflects and grounds our practices of moral accountability.

This is one of the most important insights in Locke's discussion, and it remains central to the debate: the question of personal identity is intimately connected to the question of moral responsibility, and any adequate account of personal identity must explain how our practices of responsibility are justified (or must revise those practices if they turn out not to be justifiable).
