# The Circularity Objections: Reid and Butler

## Reid's Objection

Thomas Reid, in *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785), raises an objection to Locke's memory criterion that has become a classic of the philosophical literature. The objection is simple and devastatingly clean.

Reid asks us to consider the following case. Suppose a young boy is flogged for stealing an orchard. He grows up to be a distinguished general, who can remember the flogging clearly. The general in turn grows old, becomes a retired general, and can no longer remember the flogging — though he clearly remembers the battles of his career. Now:

1. The general is the same person as the boy (he remembers the flogging, satisfying Locke's criterion).
2. The retired general is the same person as the general (he remembers the general's battles, satisfying Locke's criterion).
3. But the retired general is not the same person as the boy (he cannot remember the flogging, so Locke's criterion is not satisfied).

This is a contradiction. Identity is transitive: if A = B and B = C, then A = C. But Locke's criterion yields: (1) A = B; (2) B = C; (3) but A ≠ C. Something is wrong.

The problem is not merely that memory fades. It is that Locke's criterion is not transitive, and transitivity is a logical requirement of the identity relation (Reid 1785: Essay 3, Ch. 6). Any criterion of identity must be transitive — if the criterion says that A is the same as B and B is the same as C, it must also say that A is the same as C. Locke's memory criterion fails this requirement.

## Reid's Deeper Objection

Reid also raises a deeper objection. Memory, he argues, presupposes personal identity rather than constituting it. When I remember performing an action, my memory is of me performing it — I am aware, in the memory, that the person who performed the action is me. Memory of an action is not merely awareness that a person performed an action; it is self-awareness of oneself as the performer.

This means that memory of an action already involves the recognition that the rememberer and the performer are the same person. If I use memory to analyze personal identity — if I say that A at t₁ is the same person as B at t₂ because B can remember A's experiences — I am presupposing that B recognizes A's experiences as her own. And this recognition of personal identity cannot be analyzed further in terms of memory, or the analysis becomes circular (Reid 1785: Essay 3, Ch. 6).

## Butler's Objection

Bishop Joseph Butler, in the appendix "Of Personal Identity" to *The Analogy of Religion* (1736) — published before Reid's essays — makes essentially the same point. Butler argues that when we say "I remember" doing something, we are already presupposing that the "I" who remembers is the same person as the "I" who did it. Memory, properly understood, involves a first-person awareness of one's own past that already presupposes personal identity. Therefore, to use memory as the criterion that *constitutes* personal identity is viciously circular: you use the concept you are trying to define in the defining conditions themselves.

Butler's formulation of the objection is: "to say that present consciousness of past actions is what constitutes the same person with him who did those actions, is to say that a person's being conscious of himself is what constitutes him to be the same person" — which is plainly circular. Memory cannot constitute identity without already presupposing it (Butler 1736: Appendix I).

## The Neo-Lockean Response: Quasi-Memory

The standard neo-Lockean response to the circularity objection is to introduce the concept of **quasi-memory** (q-memory), attributed to Sydney Shoemaker. A quasi-memory is a memory-like state that has the phenomenological character of memory (it represents a past experience from the inside, as if from the perspective of the subject of the experience) but that does not presuppose the numerical identity of the rememberer and the original experiencer.

Ordinary memory, in Reid's sense, is "q-memory + the assumption that the q-rememberer is the same person as the original experiencer." But we can abstract away the identity assumption and work with q-memories alone. If a person has q-memories of past experiences — experience-like representations from the inside of past events — then they have a form of psychological connectedness to those past events that does not presuppose identity.

The neo-Lockean criterion of personal identity can then be stated in terms of q-memory: personal identity consists in overlapping chains of q-memory (and other forms of psychological continuity) between successive mental states. This criterion does not presuppose personal identity in its defining conditions — q-memory does not require the identity of the q-rememberer and the original experiencer.

Whether this response succeeds is debated. Critics argue that quasi-memory is a philosopher's stipulation with no real psychological analogue — that the concept of remembering from the inside essentially involves the first-person perspective, which cannot be separated from the presupposition of identity. But neo-Lockeans maintain that q-memory is a coherent concept and that its coherence is demonstrated by thought experiments in which someone could have q-memories of experiences that were not their own — for example, if memories could be transplanted from one person to another.

The quasi-memory response is important not just for responding to the circularity objection but for the broader neo-Lockean project of analyzing personal identity in terms of psychological continuity. Parfit builds on this foundation, and the concept of q-memory is a key part of his argument about fission cases and the indeterminacy of personal identity.
