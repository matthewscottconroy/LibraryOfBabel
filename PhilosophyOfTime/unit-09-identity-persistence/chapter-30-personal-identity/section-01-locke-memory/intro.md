# Section 1: Locke and the Memory Theory

John Locke's account of personal identity, developed in Chapter 27 of Book II of the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (first published 1689, with the chapter on identity added in the second edition of 1694), is the founding text for the modern philosophical debate. Locke's insight — that personal identity consists not in the identity of a substance (a soul or a body) but in the continuity of consciousness, specifically memory — was revolutionary and remains the starting point for every subsequent discussion.

Locke writes with characteristic clarity and boldness, and his analysis is more careful than critics sometimes acknowledge. But it also generates two decisive objections — Reid's circularity objection and Butler's related point — that push the debate immediately onto more sophisticated ground. Understanding both the power and the limits of Locke's account is essential for appreciating the subsequent development of the literature.
