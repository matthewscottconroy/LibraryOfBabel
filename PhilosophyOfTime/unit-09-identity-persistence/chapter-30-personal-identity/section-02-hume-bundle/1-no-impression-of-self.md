# Hume: No Impression of the Self

## The Empiricist Challenge

Hume's discussion of personal identity appears in Book I, Part iv, Section 6 of the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40). It begins with his empiricist criterion of meaningfulness: a concept is genuine only if it can be traced back to an impression (a direct sensory or introspective experience) or a complex of impressions. If a concept cannot be traced to any impression, it is empty — a mere word without genuine content.

The question Hume poses is: can the concept of a "self" — a persisting, unified mental substance — be traced to any impression? He subjects himself to rigorous introspection: "When I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and can never observe anything but the perception" (*Treatise* I.iv.6).

There is no impression of a self — no smooth, continuous, unitary experience of "being me" that underlies all the particular experiences. What Hume finds, when he looks inside, is a succession of distinct and momentary impressions: this sensation, this thought, this feeling, this memory. Each impression is a distinct existence. None of them is a self; they are merely what is occurring in consciousness at each moment.

## The Bundle Theory

From this introspective finding, Hume draws his famous conclusion: "the mind is nothing but a heap or collection of different perceptions, united together by certain relations, and supposed, though falsely, to be endowed with a perfect simplicity and identity" (*Treatise* I.iv.6). The self is not a substance or a subject but a **bundle** of perceptions — a collection of distinct impressions and ideas bound together by relations of resemblance and causation.

The relations that hold the bundle together are not relations of identity — they are relations of resemblance (one impression resembles another), causation (one impression is caused by or causes another), and temporal succession (impressions follow one another in a regular sequence). These relations create an appearance of unity and continuity, which is then mistaken for the genuine identity of a persisting self. But the appearance is an illusion: there is no genuine underlying self, only a sequence of impressions that resembles and causally connects with prior and subsequent impressions.

Hume's analogy is telling: the mind "is nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement" (*Treatise* I.iv.6). It is like a commonwealth or a republic: a political entity constituted by the individuals who compose it, with no separate identity over and above those individuals.

## What Generates the Illusion of Identity

Hume explains the illusion of personal identity through the psychology of association. The imagination naturally tends to mistake qualitative similarity (resemblance between successive perceptions) for numerical identity. When we observe a sequence of closely resembling perceptions, the imagination runs along the sequence so smoothly that it is inclined to treat the sequence as a single, continuously existing thing rather than a succession of distinct things.

Moreover, the causal relations between our perceptions — the way earlier experiences cause later ones, the way memories and anticipations connect experiences across time — create what Hume calls a "constancy" and "coherence" in the sequence of perceptions. This constancy and coherence is misinterpreted by the imagination as the identity of a persisting self.

The philosophical upshot is that personal identity is a fiction — a useful fiction, perhaps, that serves practical and social purposes, but a fiction nonetheless. There is no fact of the matter about which bundle of perceptions is "really" the same person as an earlier bundle; there is only a convention, grounded in psychological tendencies, to describe sequences of perceptions as belonging to "the same person" when they exhibit appropriate resemblance and causal connections.
