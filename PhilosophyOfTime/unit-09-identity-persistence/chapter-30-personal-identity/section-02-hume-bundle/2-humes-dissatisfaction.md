# Hume's Own Dissatisfaction and the Problem of the Bundle

## The Appendix Confession

One of the most remarkable passages in the history of philosophy appears in the Appendix to the *Treatise of Human Nature*, written after Books I and II had already been published. Hume confesses that he is not satisfied with his own account of personal identity:

"Upon a more strict review of the section concerning personal identity, I find myself involv'd in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent. If this be not a good general reason for skepticism, 'tis at least a sufficient one (if I were not already abundantly supplied) for me to entertain a diffidence and modesty in all my decisions" (*Treatise*, Appendix).

What is the problem that has driven Hume to this unusual confession? He identifies two principles that both seem to him true but that seem to conflict with each other:

**Principle 1**: All our distinct perceptions are distinct existences. Each impression or idea is a separate entity, not depending on or requiring any other for its existence.

**Principle 2**: The mind never perceives any real connexion among distinct existences. We perceive succession and resemblance, but no genuine connecting tie.

These two principles together seem to imply that there is nothing that binds perceptions into a unified mind or self. If all perceptions are distinct existences, and if there is no genuine connection between distinct existences, then the mind is simply a heap of unconnected entities — not a bundle with any genuine unity. But then it is unclear what even makes a particular set of perceptions into a bundle at all, rather than a mere collection.

## The Problem of the "Cement"

Hume's difficulty is sometimes described as the problem of finding the "cement" of the bundle. The bundle theory says that personal identity consists in a bundle of perceptions related by resemblance and causation. But what makes a particular collection of perceptions into a single bundle? Why is my perception of this morning's coffee "part of" the same bundle as my memory of last Tuesday's lecture? Resemblance and causation are relations that hold between perceptions throughout the universe; something more is needed to distinguish the perceptions that constitute a single mind from those that do not.

Hume acknowledges that he cannot find this "cement." He cannot identify any feature of perceptions that makes some of them bundle-mates of each other in the way that would ground the unity of a single mind. And since his own principles commit him to the view that all we ever observe are distinct perceptions, there seems to be nothing in his ontological toolkit from which to construct the required glue.

This is a deep and honest difficulty. It is not merely that Hume fails to find the cement; his own principles seem to rule out any candidate for the role. Any "connection" between perceptions would have to be an impression or an idea (by the empiricist principle) — but no such impression of genuine connection is available.

## What Hume's Dissatisfaction Reveals

Hume's dissatisfaction with his own account reveals something philosophically important. The bundle theory correctly identifies a genuine datum: when we look inside, we do not find a Cartesian ego or a persisting soul or anything like a "pure self" underlying the flow of experiences. What we find is the flow itself.

But the bundle theory cannot explain the unity of the bundle — cannot explain why a particular collection of perceptions constitutes a single mind, a single point of view, a single subject of experience. This is what Kant, writing a generation after Hume, would call the "unity of apperception" — the formal unity of consciousness that is presupposed by any experience, since any experience is the experience of a single subject. Hume denies that there is such a unity, but his dissatisfaction with his own account suggests that something is missing in his picture.

The philosophical legacy of Hume's bundle theory is thus double. On the positive side, it introduces the important idea that the self may be less unified and more "constructed" than common sense assumes — an idea that anticipates Parfit's revisionism and that resonates with certain traditions in Buddhist philosophy and in postmodern critiques of the unified subject. On the negative side, it reveals that a purely empiricist account — one that refuses to posit anything beyond what can be directly observed in experience — cannot account for the unity that makes experience possible at all.

The search for a middle path — between the Cartesian self as a substantial entity and the Humean bundle without any genuine unity — drives the subsequent development of the debate.
