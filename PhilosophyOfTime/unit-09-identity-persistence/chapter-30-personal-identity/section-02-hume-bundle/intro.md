# Section 2: Hume and the Bundle Theory

Hume's approach to personal identity represents a radical break from both the rationalist tradition (which posited an immaterial soul) and Locke's more moderate empiricism (which posited a continuous consciousness). For Hume, introspection reveals no persistent self — only a flux of perceptions, each discrete and momentary. What we call the "self" is, at bottom, a fiction — a useful construction rather than a genuine discovery.

This is among the most provocative positions in the history of philosophy of mind, and Hume himself was not entirely satisfied with it — a dissatisfaction he confesses with remarkable honesty in the Appendix to the *Treatise of Human Nature*. Understanding both Hume's argument and his subsequent dissatisfaction is important for grasping what is genuinely difficult about the question of personal identity.
