# Psychological Continuity: Shoemaker and Parfit

## The Psychological Continuity Criterion

The psychological continuity criterion of personal identity, developed by Sydney Shoemaker in *Self-Knowledge and Self-Identity* (1963) and extended by Derek Parfit in *Reasons and Persons* (1984), holds that personal identity over time consists in overlapping chains of **psychological continuity** — specifically, continuity of memories, beliefs, desires, intentions, and character traits, connected by appropriate causal relations.

The basic idea is that what makes you the same person as you were five years ago is not the identity of your body or your brain substance but the psychological connections between your current mental states and your earlier mental states. You remember events from five years ago; your current beliefs and values have developed from beliefs and values you then held; your current projects are continuations of projects you then began. These psychological connections — both backward-looking (memory and recalled experience) and forward-looking (intentions carried out) — constitute the continuity that is personal identity.

**Psychological connectedness** is the direct form of this connection: a specific memory, a specific belief, a specific intention, a specific character trait, linking one mental state to another. **Psychological continuity** is the broader relation that obtains when there are overlapping chains of direct psychological connections: even if there is no direct connection between very temporally distant mental states, there can be a continuous chain of shorter connections linking them.

## Quasi-Memory and the Response to Circularity

A key move in the neo-Lockean account is the use of quasi-memory (q-memory) to respond to Reid's circularity objection. A quasi-memory is a memory-like state that phenomenologically resembles memory — it represents a past experience "from the inside" — but that does not analytically presuppose the numerical identity of the q-rememberer and the original experiencer.

Shoemaker argues that q-memory is a coherent concept. We can imagine a scenario in which memory traces from one person are somehow transplanted into another person's brain. The recipient might then have memory-like states representing experiences they never themselves had. These states would be quasi-memories of the donor's experiences, not memories in the ordinary sense (which would require the recipient to have been the donor). The psychological continuity account of identity uses q-memory: personal identity consists in overlapping chains of q-memory and other forms of psychological continuity, where the circularity is avoided because q-memory does not presuppose identity.

## The Role of the Body

A natural question for the psychological continuity view is: what is the role of the body in personal identity? The pure psychological continuity view says that what matters for personal identity is the psychological connections, not the physical substrate in which they are realized. If your psychological states were transferred to a different body (or to a computer), you would persist as the person now in that body (or computer).

This implication has struck many philosophers as both philosophically liberating (it opens up possibilities for survival in scenarios of body transplant or uploading) and philosophically troubling (it seems to make personal identity independent of the physical reality of the person). Shoemaker's position is that psychological continuity is primary and that bodily continuity matters for personal identity primarily insofar as it normally underlies and enables psychological continuity. In normal circumstances, bodily continuity and psychological continuity go together. In extraordinary cases (brain transplants, uploading), they can come apart, and it is psychological continuity that tracks identity.

## Objections

The psychological continuity view faces several important objections.

**The fission problem**: If your brain is divided and each half is transplanted into a different body, and both halves generate psychological continuity with your pre-fission self, then (by the psychological continuity criterion) you are identical to both successors. But you cannot be identical to both, since they are distinct. This is the fission problem, and it is the basis of Parfit's argument that identity is not what matters — we examine it in detail in the next section.

**The reduplication problem**: Even in non-fission cases, the psychological continuity criterion faces the possibility of reduplication: if psychological continuity is what constitutes identity, then in principle two people could be psychological continuants of a single person. But identity is one-to-one, not one-to-many. This suggests either that psychological continuity does not constitute identity (only identity-with-uniqueness constitutes identity) or that the criterion must be modified to require uniqueness.

**The circularity concern revisited**: Some philosophers argue that the concept of quasi-memory, while technically avoiding circularity, introduces a stipulated concept that has no genuine psychological analog. Real memory involves the first-person perspective, which cannot be separated from the presupposition of identity. The move to q-memory thus avoids the circularity problem only by introducing an artificial concept.
