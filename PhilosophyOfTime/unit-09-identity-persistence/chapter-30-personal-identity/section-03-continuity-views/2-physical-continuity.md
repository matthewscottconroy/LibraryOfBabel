# Animalism: The Biological Continuity View

## The Animalist Claim

Animalism — the view that persons are fundamentally animals — is the most influential alternative to the psychological continuity approach in contemporary analytic philosophy. Its leading defenders are Eric Olson (*The Human Animal*, 1997) and Peter van Inwagen (*Material Beings*, 1990). The animalist claim is simple: we are human organisms, and personal identity over time consists in the biological continuity of the organism.

You are not a psychological subject who happens to be housed in a human body. You are the human animal itself. Your persistence conditions are the persistence conditions of the biological organism — approximately, continued vital functioning, metabolic continuity, and the organic continuity characteristic of a living human body. If the biological organism persists, you persist; if it ceases to persist, you cease to persist.

This view has a striking clarity and simplicity. It does not require any special analysis of psychological connectivity or any concepts as technical as quasi-memory. It treats persons as a subclass of animals, subject to the same persistence conditions as animals generally, and it derives the identity of persons from the biological facts about the persistence of the organism.

## Advantages of Animalism

**Determinateness**: The animalist view makes personal identity determinate in cases where the psychological continuity view generates indeterminacy. When does a person begin to exist? When the organism begins — which biological science can identify with reasonable precision. When does a person cease to exist? When the organism dies — again, a question for biology. This determinateness is philosophically attractive compared to the vagueness that pervades the psychological continuity account.

**Avoiding puzzle cases**: Many of the puzzle cases that beset psychological continuity accounts — fission, gradual psychological replacement, uploading — do not generate genuine puzzles for animalism. Fission of a human organism is biologically impossible in the relevant sense; gradual psychological change does not threaten biological continuity; uploading does not produce a biological organism at all. The animalist can say, without any sense of paradox, that you cannot survive uploading — because uploading does not continue the biological organism.

**Natural fit with biology**: We take the identity of other animals seriously as a biological matter: "Is this the same wolf pack that was here last year?" is a question about biological continuity. Animalism treats human personal identity as continuous with animal identity, rather than positing a special principle for humans alone.

## The Brain Transplant Problem

Animalism faces a severe objection: it seems to give the wrong answer in brain transplant cases. Imagine that your brain is transplanted into a different body. Intuitively, most people think that you would go with your brain — the person who wakes up in the new body, with all your memories and personality, is you. The person who wakes up in your old body, with none of your psychological characteristics, is not you.

But animalism says: you are the organism. If your brain is transplanted out of your organism, what persists is the organism — the body — not the transplanted brain. The person who wakes in the recipient body is not you; you are the person who remains in your original body, albeit severely brain-damaged (Olson 1997: 41–55).

This result strikes many philosophers as deeply counterintuitive. Surely if your psychology — your memories, your personality, your sense of who you are — goes with the brain, then you go with the brain. If anything survives the transplant in the relevant sense, it is the person with your psychology, not the vegetable in your original body.

Olson's response is that the intuition reflects our tendency to identify ourselves with our brains (or with the psychological features our brains implement) rather than with our bodies as wholes. This tendency is natural — our brains are more central to our identity than our livers — but it does not follow that the brain is the person. The person is the organism, which includes the brain but is not identical to it.

## The Thinking Animal Problem

Olson also raises an objection to psychological continuity views that he calls the "thinking animal problem." If you are a psychological subject constituted by psychological continuity, and you are currently sitting in a chair, then there is a human animal also sitting in that chair — the biological organism that constitutes you. Is the human animal thinking? If so, there are two thinking things in the chair, which seems ontologically extravagant. If not, what is the human animal doing if not thinking?

This problem suggests that any view that distinguishes persons from organisms must explain the relationship between the person and the organism — and this explanation turns out to be surprisingly difficult. Animalism avoids the problem by identifying persons with organisms.

## Evaluating the Alternatives

The debate between the psychological continuity view and animalism turns largely on intuitions about puzzle cases. The brain transplant case strongly favors psychological continuity. The advantages of determinateness and parsimony favor animalism. Neither view is clearly superior to the other, and the difficulty of the debate reflects a genuine tension in our concept of a person: we are both psychological subjects and biological organisms, and these two aspects of our nature pull our identity intuitions in different directions.

One response to this impasse — perhaps the most honest one — is to distinguish between different questions we might be asking when we ask "who is this person?" For some purposes (legal identity, moral responsibility), what matters is psychological continuity. For other purposes (biological and medical identity, the question of what has happened to the organism), what matters is biological continuity. The concept of personal identity may be irreducibly context-dependent, serving different purposes in different contexts.

This context-dependence is itself philosophically important. It suggests that the question "what is personal identity?" may not have a single, unified answer — and that recognizing this complexity is a philosophical advance rather than a failure.
