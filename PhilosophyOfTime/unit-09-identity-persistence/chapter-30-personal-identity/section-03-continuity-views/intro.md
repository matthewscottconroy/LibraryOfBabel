# Section 3: Psychological and Physical Continuity Views

The neo-Lockean tradition — which grounds personal identity in psychological continuity rather than substance identity — has been the dominant approach in analytic philosophy since the mid-twentieth century. Its most important exponents, Sydney Shoemaker and Derek Parfit, have developed increasingly sophisticated versions of the psychological continuity criterion, responding to the objections of Reid and Butler through the concept of quasi-memory, and probing the implications of the criterion through a series of increasingly challenging thought experiments.

In parallel, a rival tradition — broadly called animalism or the biological view — has argued that personal identity is not constituted by psychological continuity at all, but by the continuity of the biological organism. This view, defended most carefully by Eric Olson and Peter van Inwagen, has the advantage of making personal identity determinate and resistant to puzzle cases, but it seems to get the wrong answer in brain transplant scenarios, which most people find philosophically troubling.

This section presents both views and assesses their relative merits.
