# Parfit and Fission: Identity Is Indeterminate

## The Fission Case

Parfit's most important thought experiment in Part III of *Reasons and Persons* (1984) is the brain fission case. Suppose that your brain is divided — each cerebral hemisphere is removed and transplanted into a different body. Each hemisphere, on its own, can sustain normal conscious functioning. Each recipient has psychological continuity with you: each has your memories, your personality, your values, your sense of your own history.

After the operation, two people wake up. Call them Lefty (the recipient of the left hemisphere) and Righty (the recipient of the right hemisphere). Each has an equal claim to psychological continuity with you. Which one is you?

The available answers are: (a) you are Lefty; (b) you are Righty; (c) you are both of them; (d) you are neither of them.

Each answer is problematic. (a) and (b) seem arbitrary: there is no reason to prefer one hemisphere over the other as "the real you" — they are symmetrically related to your pre-fission self. (c) is impossible: Lefty and Righty are distinct persons, and identity is transitive. If you = Lefty and you = Righty, then Lefty = Righty, which is false. (d) seems wrong for a different reason: you seem clearly to survive in some sense — there are people who remember being you, who carry on your projects, who are continuous with you in all the psychologically relevant ways. Saying that you cease to exist seems too strong.

Parfit's conclusion is that fission reveals something deep about personal identity: in fission cases, there may be no determinate answer to whether you survive. The question "is Lefty you?" may have no correct answer — not because we don't know the answer, but because there is no fact of the matter to know. Personal identity, in such cases, is indeterminate (Parfit 1984: 254–261).

## Identity Is Not What Matters

From the indeterminacy of personal identity in fission cases, Parfit draws a more radical conclusion: identity is not what matters. Even in ordinary cases — cases without fission — what matters for survival and for our deepest practical concerns is not identity but psychological continuity and connectedness (R-relation: the relation of "directly or indirectly R-related," combining connectedness and continuity).

The argument proceeds by noting that in the fission case, you survive in two successors — each is psychologically continuous with you — even though neither is strictly identical to you. If what mattered were identity, then your situation before fission should be worse than it would be if only one hemisphere survived (since then you would be identical to the unique survivor). But this seems wrong: having two successors who are each psychologically continuous with you seems better, not worse, than having one. What matters — psychological continuity — is present twice over, rather than once.

If identity is not what matters even in the fission case, then perhaps identity is not what matters in ordinary cases either. What we actually care about when we care about "our future selves" is not the strict numerical identity of those future selves with us, but the psychological continuity — the memories, beliefs, projects, and character — that connect us to them. And psychological continuity is a matter of degree, can exist between individuals who are not strictly identical, and is what our deepest practical concerns should track (Parfit 1984: 280–282).

## The Analogy with Fission Along the Bundle

Parfit connects his argument to Hume's bundle theory in a suggestive way. On Parfit's view, persons are not metaphysically simple or unified — we are, in a sense, more like Hume's bundles than like Cartesian egos. What gives us unity and identity is not a metaphysically special ingredient but the same kinds of psychological relations that connect any sequence of mental states. Fission reveals that these relations can survive without identity, which shows that identity is not essential to what matters in them.

Parfit takes this conclusion to be in some ways liberating. If what matters is not identity but psychological continuity, then we should care about our future selves in proportion to the psychological continuity we will have with them. And since that continuity naturally diminishes over long time spans, extreme long-term self-concern (e.g., planning everything around one's condition at age ninety) is somewhat less rationally mandatory than we had thought. More importantly, the diminished significance of identity implies that the division between self-concern and other-concern is less sharp: concern for others who are psychologically continuous with us is not categorically different from concern for our "future selves."
