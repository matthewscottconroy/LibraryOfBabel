# What Matters in Survival: Continuity, Not Identity

## The Revised Picture

Parfit's conclusion in Part III of *Reasons and Persons* (1984) is expressed in a memorable and provocative sentence: "My life seemed like a glass tunnel, through which I was moving faster every year, and at the end of which there was darkness. When I changed my view, the walls of my glass tunnel disappeared. I now live in the open air" (Parfit 1984: 281).

What he means is this. Before his philosophical investigation, Parfit had shared the common assumption that personal identity is what matters — that the question "will I survive?" is the deep question, and that the answer determines everything of prudential significance. His investigation of the fission cases shows that this assumption is wrong. Personal identity can be indeterminate; psychological continuity can obtain without identity; and what actually grounds our practical and moral concerns is not identity but continuity. Once we recognize this, we are freed from the "glass tunnel" — the oppressive sense that the self is a metaphysically special thing that must be preserved at all costs.

What matters in survival, on Parfit's account, is **Relation R**: the relation of psychological continuity with the right kind of cause. What "right kind" means is that the continuity must be caused by the normal physical processes that sustain psychological continuity — not by duplication, copying, or other deviant causal chains. If my psychology were duplicated in a computer simulation, the simulation would not be psychologically continuous with me in the relevant sense, because the causal process is not the right kind.

## Implications for Moral Psychology

If what matters is not identity but Relation R, several important revisions to our moral psychology follow.

**The demandingness of self-concern**: Parfit argues that if what matters is not identity but psychological continuity, then our present obligations to future "selves" are more like our obligations to others than common sense assumes. Our future selves are not strictly identical to us; they are psychologically continuous with us to varying degrees. Just as my concern for a close friend might be stronger than my concern for a distant acquaintance, my concern for my tomorrow-self should be stronger than my concern for my twenty-years-hence self — and both should be understood as forms of concern for continuers, not as necessarily self-centered concern.

**The ethics of resource allocation and compensation**: If personal identity over time is less determinate and less metaphysically significant than we assumed, then various practices that depend on strong assumptions about identity come into question. The justification of punishment, for example, typically requires that the person being punished is the same person who committed the crime. If identity is indeterminate or if what matters is not identity, the justification of punishment requires reconsideration.

**The rationality of concern for distant future selves**: Parfit argues that we should care about our future selves in proportion to the psychological continuity we will have with them. Since psychological continuity naturally diminishes over long spans of time, extreme concern for our very distant future selves — the kind of concern that leads people to make extreme sacrifices for what they will be like in fifty years — is somewhat less rationally mandatory than common sense assumes.

## Objections to Parfit

Parfit's argument has been enormously influential and equally controversial. Three main objections deserve mention.

**The "too-many-thinkers" objection**: Before fission, if both successors are equally related to the pre-fission person, then (it seems) both successors are equally present before the fission — which means there were already two persons there before the operation. But this is absurd: there was clearly only one person before the fission. This "too many thinkers" problem threatens the coherence of Parfit's position.

**The intimate connection between identity and what matters**: Critics including Marya Schechtman (1996) and Christine Korsgaard (1989) argue that Parfit's separation of identity from what matters is a philosophical mistake. What matters is not bare psychological continuity but the kind of psychological continuity that constitutes a genuine self — the kind of continuity that is constituted by a self-interpreting, self-governing agent. This kind of continuity presupposes identity rather than being separable from it.

**The dismissal of first-person concerns**: Some critics argue that Parfit's treatment of the fission case undervalues the first-person perspective. From the inside, the prospect of fission — of ceasing to exist as a unified subject while two others take up one's psychological heritage — is not equivalent to the prospect of surviving intact. Parfit treats the two situations as equally good (since what matters — R-relation — is preserved in each), but many people find the fission scenario profoundly troubling even granting equal psychological continuity. This suggests that what matters is not just psychological continuity but psychological continuity that is my own — which presupposes some notion of identity that Parfit cannot accommodate.

Despite these objections, Parfit's argument remains one of the most important and provocative contributions to the debate. It has shifted the terms of the discussion from asking "what is personal identity?" to asking "what matters in survival?" — a shift that has been philosophically productive and that connects the metaphysics of personal identity to practical ethics in direct and fruitful ways.
