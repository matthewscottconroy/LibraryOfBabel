# MacIntyre, Ricoeur, and the Narrative Self

## MacIntyre: The Unity of a Narrative Life

Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981) argues that the fragmentation of modern moral philosophy reflects a deeper fragmentation of the moral life — a loss of the narrative frameworks within which virtues and practices had their intelligibility. Central to MacIntyre's project is a claim about personal identity: what makes a person the same person over time, and what makes a life intelligible as a unified whole, is the narrative unity of that life.

MacIntyre argues that human actions are only intelligible in the context of a narrative. When you ask "why is he running?" the answer is never just a physical description but always a narrative embedding: "he is running because he is late for a meeting, which he needs to attend because it concerns a project he has been working on for months, which he began because of a commitment he made to a colleague two years ago." Every action takes its meaning from its place in a longer story (MacIntyre 1981: 205–210).

This narrative embedding extends to the unity of a life. "The unity of a human life is the unity of a narrative quest," MacIntyre writes (*After Virtue*, 1981: 219). What makes a life hang together as a single life — rather than a series of disconnected episodes — is the presence of an overarching narrative that gives the individual's actions, relationships, and experiences a coherent trajectory. The narrative may be a quest for virtue, for understanding, for meaningful relationships, for contribution to a community. What matters is that it is a narrative with a recognizable direction, with events that make sense as parts of a whole.

MacIntyre is explicit that this narrative unity is not merely psychological continuity in the neo-Lockean sense. Psychological continuity is a necessary but not sufficient condition for narrative unity. What is needed beyond psychological continuity is interpretive coherence: the events of a life must not merely be causally connected but must be interpretively related — must make sense as parts of a story, must have the kind of significance that narrative gives to events.

This account of narrative unity connects directly to ethics. MacIntyre argues that the virtues are traits of character that enable persons to pursue the goods internal to practices and to pursue the good of their lives as wholes. Virtues are intelligible only in the context of the narrative of a life that is directed toward goods. Without a narrative framework, virtue has no home — which is why, MacIntyre argues, modern moral philosophy is so confused: it has abandoned the narrative framework that gave its concepts their sense.

## Ricoeur: Idem, Ipse, and Narrative Mediation

Paul Ricoeur's contribution to narrative identity, developed in *Oneself as Another* (1992, Studies 5–6), builds on the distinction between idem-identity and ipse-identity introduced in the context of *Time and Narrative* (we discussed this briefly in Chapter 26's section on Ricoeur).

**Idem-identity** is numerical and qualitative sameness: the same entity, the same properties. This is the kind of identity that analytic discussions of personal identity typically concern themselves with — the question of what makes you the same entity as you were ten years ago.

**Ipse-identity** is selfhood — the kind of identity that consists in remaining true to oneself, in keeping one's word, in honoring one's commitments. When I say "I will be there" or "I promise," I am committing my future self to an action. The grounds for keeping the commitment are not that my future self will be numerically identical to me (though it presumably will be) but that I — this self — have given my word, and keeping it is part of being who I am. Ipse-identity is the identity of a self understood as a subject of commitments (Ricoeur 1992: 121–125).

Narrative identity mediates between idem and ipse. It is not the brute numerical identity of a substance (idem) nor the purely formal identity of a promise-keeper (ipse). It is the dynamic, evolving identity of a self that is constituted and reconstituted through the act of self-interpretation — through the ongoing effort to make one's life hang together as a coherent and livable story.

## The Role of Narrative in Ethical Life

Both MacIntyre and Ricoeur connect narrative identity to ethics in essential ways. For MacIntyre, the narrative unity of a life is the precondition of virtue: only a life that is organized around an intelligible narrative quest can be a life of genuine character and genuine moral achievement. For Ricoeur, narrative identity is the medium through which the ethical demand — the demand to live well and to treat others justly — is appropriated and enacted in a particular life.

On Ricoeur's account, the ethical life is not primarily a matter of following rules or calculating consequences; it is a matter of becoming a certain kind of person — a person whose life story exhibits the virtues of care, justice, and self-respect. The question "how shall I live?" is not answered by applying a principle but by composing a life story that embodies the values one has recognized as genuinely one's own (Ricoeur 1992: 172–179).

This ethical dimension of narrative identity connects to the central themes of the entire unit. Personal identity over time is not merely a metaphysical puzzle about numerical sameness; it is the context within which the ethical demand is encountered and the context within which it must be answered. Who am I? What should I do? How should I live? These questions are not separate; they are the same question, asked from different angles. Narrative identity is the framework within which their unity is visible.

## How Narrative Identity Challenges Purely Analytic Accounts

The narrative identity tradition challenges the analytic debate about personal identity in several ways.

**The wrong question**: Analytic accounts focus on numerical identity — the same entity at two times. But this is not what matters for ethical self-understanding, for agency, or for the coherence of a life. What matters is the kind of self-interpretive coherence that narrative provides: the ability to tell a story of one's life that is truthful, intelligible, and that sustains commitment and self-ownership.

**The omission of interpretation**: Analytic accounts of psychological continuity describe causal connections between mental states without asking what those states mean to the person who has them. But meaning is not reducible to causal connection: the same causal chain can be interpreted in many ways, and the interpretation is part of what constitutes the self. Narrative identity includes the interpretive dimension that pure psychological continuity omits.

**The dynamic character of identity**: Analytic accounts tend to treat personal identity as a static fact — either you are identical to the past person or you are not. Narrative identity is dynamic: it is constituted and reconstituted through ongoing self-interpretation. The question "am I the same person I was?" is not answered by checking a criterion; it is answered by engaging in the ongoing work of self-understanding and self-commitment that constitutes who one is.

**The social dimension**: Both MacIntyre and Ricoeur insist that narrative identity is not a purely individual achievement. The stories we tell about our lives are told within traditions, communities, and relationships that provide the narrative frameworks and values within which our lives are intelligible. We are, as MacIntyre says, the co-authors of our own narratives, but we are never the sole authors. The social and communal dimension of narrative identity is something that purely psychological continuity accounts systematically understate.

Narrative identity does not replace the analytic debate; it reframes it. The analytic debate asks: "what is personal identity?" and looks for a criterion. The narrative tradition asks: "what does it mean to be a self over time, and what does that mean for how we should live?" and looks for a way of understanding that connects metaphysics, psychology, and ethics in a single, coherent picture. Both questions are worth asking, and the richest accounts of personal identity will draw on both traditions.
