# Section 5: Narrative Identity

The debate between psychological continuity views, animalism, and Parfit's revisionism has operated primarily within an analytic framework that takes the question "what makes person A at t₁ the same person as person B at t₂?" as the central question. This is the question of numerical identity over time, and the theories we have examined offer different criteria for it.

But there is another tradition — drawing on virtue ethics, hermeneutics, and phenomenology — that argues this question is too thin to capture what personal identity really involves. Personal identity, on this view, is not primarily a metaphysical fact about numerical sameness but an achievement of self-interpretation — the achievement of a coherent and livable story about one's life.

Alasdair MacIntyre and Paul Ricoeur are the most important figures in this tradition. Their accounts of narrative identity do not simply add a literary gloss to the analytic debate; they propose a fundamentally different understanding of what personal identity is and why it matters.
