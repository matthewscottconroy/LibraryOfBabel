# Unit IX: Time, Identity, and Persistence

Every object in the world exists in time, and most objects undergo change. The chair in which you sit has existed for years; it has been scratched, perhaps re-painted, possibly repaired. The river you cross today is the same river you crossed last week, though none of the water molecules are the same. You yourself are — or so we ordinarily say — the same person you were ten years ago, though you have changed in appearance, in knowledge, in belief, in circumstance, and perhaps in character. How is this possible? What makes a thing at one time the same thing as a thing at another time? And what makes you, in particular, the same person across the arc of your life?

These questions may sound like puzzles for specialists in metaphysics, and in some respects they are. But they have stakes that extend well beyond academic philosophy. The question of personal identity over time is implicated in our practices of holding people responsible for past actions, in our capacity to make and keep promises, in our concern for our future selves, and in the meaning we attach to our lives as wholes rather than as disconnected sequences of moments. If personal identity is, as Parfit argues, not what matters — if what matters in survival is some form of psychological continuity that can come apart from strict numerical identity — then large parts of our moral psychology may need revision. If personal identity is constituted by narrative, as MacIntyre and Ricoeur argue, then the coherence of a life is not given but achieved, and the philosophical stakes of how we tell our stories are immediately practical.

Chapter 29 examines the metaphysics of persistence more broadly — how any object persists through time and change. It begins with David Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics, which is perhaps the sharpest formulation of the underlying puzzle, and then examines the two main competing theories: endurantism (the view that objects are wholly present at each time they exist) and perdurantism (the view that objects persist by having distinct temporal parts at different times). It also examines Ted Sider's stage theory as a variant of the perdurantist approach, and concludes with the Ship of Theseus as a test case for all three theories.

Chapter 30 turns to the special case of persons. Beginning with Locke's classical memory theory of personal identity and the objections raised by Reid and Butler, the chapter traces the development of the debate through Hume's bundle theory, the neo-Lockean psychological continuity views of Shoemaker and Parfit, and the animalist/biological view of Olson. It concludes with Parfit's revolutionary argument that identity is not what matters in survival, and with the narrative identity views of MacIntyre and Ricoeur that offer a richer and more humanistic framework for understanding what it means to be the same person over time.

Throughout both chapters, the goal is to combine analytical rigor with a clear sense of the human stakes of the questions. These are not merely technical puzzles about the persistence of ordinary objects; they are questions about what it means to be us — extended in time, shaped by a past we inherit and oriented toward a future we can only partly anticipate.
