# Chapter 31 Exercises: Time and Free Will

---

## Conceptual Exercises

**1. Reconstructing the Master Argument**
Reconstruct Diodorus Cronus's Master Argument in your own words, stating the three premises clearly. Then identify which premise you find most vulnerable to rejection, and give your best argument for rejecting it. Does your rejection have consequences for other areas of philosophy that you are willing to accept?

**2. Bivalence and the Sea Battle**
Aristotle's response to logical fatalism is to deny bivalence for future contingent statements. Write a one-page analysis addressing the following: (a) What exactly does Aristotle deny — that future contingent statements have truth values at all, or that they have the same kind of truth values as other statements? (b) Is the law of excluded middle affected by this denial? (c) What would a logician who insists on classical two-valued logic say in response?

**3. Hard and Soft Facts**
Fischer's distinction between hard and soft facts about the past is central to the Ockhamist response to theological fatalism. Apply this distinction to the following three cases, determining whether each is a hard or soft fact, and justifying your classification:
- (a) "In 1980, Smith was the teacher of the first person to cure cancer."
- (b) "In 1980, Jones was five feet tall."
- (c) "In 1990, Brown believed that the president elected in 2000 would be a Republican."
Does your classification affect whether these facts fall under the "necessity of the past"?

**4. Boethius and the Relocation Objection**
State Pike's objection to Boethius as precisely as you can. Then write Boethius's best possible response, drawing on the distinction between conditional and absolute necessity. Is the response successful? What would you need to believe about the nature of God and time for it to work?

**5. The Block Universe and Fatalism**
A student argues: "If the block universe is true, my breakfast choice tomorrow is already 'there' in spacetime. So I can't really choose; the choice is already made." Write a careful response to this student explaining the distinction between ontological completeness and causal necessitation, and showing why the block universe does not entail fatalism. What, if anything, does the block universe imply for free will?

**6. Frankfurt Cases**
Consider Frankfurt's (1971) famous case: Black wants Jones to vote for a particular candidate. Black has implanted a device in Jones's brain that will cause Jones to vote for the candidate if Jones shows any inclination to vote otherwise. In fact, Jones decides to vote for the candidate on his own, and Black's device plays no role. Is Jones's vote free? Does it matter that Jones "could not have done otherwise" (in the sense that the device would have intervened)? What does this case show about the relationship between freedom and the ability to do otherwise?

**7. The Consequence Argument**
Van Inwagen's Consequence Argument is one of the most powerful arguments in philosophy of action. State the argument precisely, identifying the transfer principle it relies on. Then present the strongest compatibilist response you can find (using at least one source from the Further Reading), and evaluate whether it succeeds in blocking the argument.

**8. Agent Causation and the Randomness Problem**
The randomness problem for libertarianism holds that if an agent's choice is not determined by prior events, then it is merely random — no better than a coin flip, and not the kind of thing for which agents deserve credit or blame. Evaluate Kane's (1996) response: that indeterminism can be free if the agent's reasons shape the probabilities of outcomes. Does this response show that the choice is genuinely *the agent's*, or does it merely show that the agent has influence over the odds?

## Discussion and Essay Questions

**9. Comparative Analysis**
Compare and contrast three different strategies for dealing with the problem of divine foreknowledge: (a) Ockham's hard/soft facts distinction; (b) Boethius's eternal simultaneous apprehension; (c) Open theism (the view that God voluntarily refrains from knowing future free actions). Which approach requires the least revision of our ordinary concepts? Which requires the least revision of traditional theological commitments?

**10. A-Theory and Libertarianism**
Some philosophers argue that libertarian free will fits most naturally with an A-theory of time in which the future is genuinely open, while compatibilism fits most naturally with the block universe. Critically evaluate this claim. Is the connection between temporal metaphysics and the free will debate as tight as this suggests, or can one hold any combination of positions?

## Lab and Observational Exercises

**11. Deliberation Diary**
Over the course of three days, keep a "deliberation diary." On at least five separate occasions when you face a genuine decision (not a trivial habitual choice), pause and record: (a) what alternatives you experience as genuinely available; (b) what considerations seem to influence you; (c) whether you experience yourself as "settling" something, or merely discovering what you were always going to do. After three days, analyze your diary: does the phenomenology of deliberation support libertarianism, compatibilism, or neither? What features of the deliberative experience bear most directly on the philosophical positions?

**12. Historical Case Study**
Research one historical trial in which the defense of the accused included claims about diminished control or determinism (examples: the 1924 Leopold and Loeb case in which Clarence Darrow appealed to determinism; recent cases involving neuroscience evidence). Prepare a two-page analysis examining: (a) what theory of free will is implicit in the defense; (b) how the court and prosecution responded; (c) what philosophical issues the case illuminates. Does the legal treatment of responsibility track any of the philosophical positions discussed in this chapter?

**13. Interdisciplinary Investigation**
Quantum mechanics introduces genuine indeterminacy at the sub-atomic level. Investigate the following question empirically and philosophically: Do contemporary neuroscientists or physicists believe that quantum indeterminacy "scales up" to the level of neural decision-making in a way that could ground libertarian free will? Find at least two sources — one from neuroscience and one from physics or philosophy of physics — and write a summary of what they say. Does the empirical evidence support the view that quantum indeterminacy plays a role in human choices? What would it take for quantum indeterminacy to ground genuine freedom rather than mere randomness?
