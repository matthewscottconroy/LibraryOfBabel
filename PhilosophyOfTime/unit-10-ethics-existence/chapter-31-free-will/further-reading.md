## Annotated Entries

**Aristotle, *De Interpretatione* 9**
The locus classicus for the problem of future contingents. Aristotle's argument and proposed solution have generated two millennia of commentary. Ackrill's translation is reliable and the introduction to the Clarendon Press edition is excellent. Begin here for the historical origins of the problem.

**Belnap, Perloff, and Xu, *Facing the Future* (2001)**
The most technically sophisticated development of Aristotle's branching-time semantics for future contingents. Introduces the framework of "agent stit" ("sees to it that") logic, which connects temporal branching with a formal theory of agency and action. Essential for those interested in the logic of free will and time.

**Fischer, *God, Foreknowledge, and Freedom* (1989)**
An indispensable anthology containing the major classical and contemporary readings on theological fatalism. Includes Pike's original paper, Ockham's texts in translation, Stump and Kretzmann on eternity, and Fischer's own analysis of hard and soft facts. The introductory essay is especially helpful.

**Fischer and Ravizza, *Responsibility and Control* (1998)**
The most comprehensive contemporary compatibilist account of moral responsibility. Develops the concept of reasons-responsiveness and the notion of "guidance control." Clear, careful, and responsive to the major objections. Required reading for anyone working on compatibilism.

**Frankfurt, "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" (1971)**
A short, beautifully written paper that transformed the free will debate. The hierarchical account of free will and the Frankfurt cases have generated an enormous secondary literature. Read the original before the secondary literature.

**Kane, *The Significance of Free Will* (1996)**
The most systematic contemporary defense of libertarian free will that does not appeal to agent causation. Kane attempts to show that quantum indeterminism at the neural level can ground the kind of self-forming actions required for ultimate moral responsibility. Richly argued and historically informed.

**O'Connor, *Persons and Causes* (2000)**
The most developed contemporary version of agent causation. O'Connor argues for the irreducibility of agent causation and defends a non-reductive naturalist picture of persons as genuine substances with emergent causal properties. Technically demanding but rewarding.

**Pereboom, *Living Without Free Will* (2001)**
The most influential contemporary defense of hard incompatibilism. Pereboom argues through a series of manipulation cases that compatibilism fails, then addresses the existential implications of living without free will. Honest and philosophically serious about the human stakes.

**Pike, "Divine Omniscience and Voluntary Action" (1965)**
The article that launched the modern debate about theological fatalism. Pike argues that Boethius's solution fails — the problem resurfaces at the level of eternal knowledge — and that the only available solution is to deny divine foreknowledge, which is theologically unacceptable. Still the best statement of the problem.

**Prior, *Past, Present and Future* (1967)**
The foundational text of tense logic. Prior develops formal logical systems for reasoning about time and uses them to address philosophical problems including future contingents. Difficult in places but historically important; tense logic begins here.

**Stump and Kretzmann, "Eternity" (1981)**
The most rigorous contemporary defense of Boethius's doctrine of divine eternity. Introduces the concept of "ET-simultaneity" — a relation holding between eternal and temporal existence — to make sense of God's timeless knowledge of temporal events. A model of careful analytic philosophy of religion.

**van Inwagen, *An Essay on Free Will* (1983)**
One of the most important books in philosophy of action. Develops the consequence argument for incompatibilism with great precision and care. Van Inwagen is admirably clear about what he takes to be proved and what remains uncertain. Required reading for anyone working on free will.
