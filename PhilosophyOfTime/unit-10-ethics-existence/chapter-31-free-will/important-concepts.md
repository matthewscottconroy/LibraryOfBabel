# Chapter 31 Important Concepts: Time and Free Will

---

**Agent Causation**
The view that persons as substances (not merely as collections of events) initiate causal sequences in a way that is irreducible to event causation. Associated with O'Connor (2000), agent causation holds that the agent herself — not a prior event — is the cause of her action. The view requires that persons have irreducible causal powers not fully analyzable in physical terms.

**Bivalence**
The logical principle that every declarative statement is either true or false, with no third option. Classical logic accepts bivalence universally. Aristotle's response to the sea battle problem requires denying bivalence for future contingent statements — those describing events that are genuinely open and might or might not occur.

**Compatibilism**
The philosophical position that free will and causal determinism are logically compatible. Compatibilists analyze free will in terms of the right kind of causal relationship between an agent's reasons, desires, and actions — not in terms of the absence of prior causes. Historical compatibilists include Hume and Hobbes; contemporary versions include hierarchical accounts (Frankfurt) and reasons-responsiveness accounts (Fischer and Ravizza).

**Consequence Argument**
Van Inwagen's (1983) argument that determinism is incompatible with free will. Since no one has a choice about the laws of nature or the remote past, and since our actions follow necessarily from these, no one has a choice about their actions. The argument deploys a transfer principle: if you have no choice about X, and X entails Y, then you have no choice about Y.

**Diodorean Modality**
The modal logic associated with Diodorus Cronus, on which "possible" means "true now or at some future time." This collapses unrealized possibilities into impossibilities: if something never happens, it was never genuinely possible. Contrasts with the standard Leibnizian notion of possibility as truth in some possible world.

**Fatalism**
The view that what will happen is in some sense already settled, irrespective of what agents do. Logical fatalism grounds this in the logic of truth; theological fatalism grounds it in divine foreknowledge; physical fatalism grounds it in the causal structure of the universe. Distinct from determinism: fatalism does not require that outcomes are causally determined, only that they are in some sense fixed.

**Frankfurt Cases**
Thought experiments introduced by Frankfurt (1971) designed to show that the ability to do otherwise is not necessary for moral responsibility. In the standard case, a counterfactual intervener would have prevented the agent from doing otherwise, but the agent acts on their own without needing the intervention. If the agent is responsible, then responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise.

**Hard and Soft Facts**
A distinction due to Ockham, formalized by Fischer (1989). A hard fact about the past concerns only what happened at that time; a soft fact about the past has essential reference to what will happen at later times. God's foreknowledge of a future action is a soft fact: its truth conditions essentially involve the future action. Soft facts do not fall under the principle that past facts are necessary, and so foreknowledge does not foreclose future freedom.

**Hard Incompatibilism**
The position that neither determinism nor indeterminism is compatible with genuine free will and moral responsibility. Pereboom (2001) argues that compatibilist accounts fail because manipulated agents and deterministically produced agents are relevantly similar, and libertarian accounts fail because they cannot coherently characterize the special causal power required. The implication is that praise and blame based on desert are unjustified.

**Hierarchical Account of Free Will**
Frankfurt's (1971) analysis on which free will involves a harmony between first-order desires and second-order desires (desires about desires). An agent acts freely when their first-order motivating desire is one they endorse at the level of second-order volition. This account preserves the significance of freedom without requiring indeterminism.

**Incompatibilism**
The view that free will and causal determinism are logically incompatible — that the truth of determinism would imply the absence of genuine freedom. Incompatibilists divide into hard incompatibilists (who conclude that free will does not exist) and libertarians (who conclude that the world is not deterministic in the relevant way).

**Libertarianism (about Free Will)**
The metaphysical position that genuine free will exists and requires indeterminism. The libertarian holds that human agents sometimes make choices that are not causally determined by prior events, and that these choices are genuinely attributable to the agent rather than being merely random. Contemporary libertarian theories include agent causation and event-causal probabilistic theories (Kane 1996).

**Logical Fatalism**
The argument that future-tense statements, if determinately true or false, entail that the future is necessary. If "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is true today, then the battle must happen; if it is false today, the battle cannot happen. Either way, the outcome is necessitated. Logical fatalism does not depend on causal determination but only on the logic of truth and time.

**Necessity of the Past**
The principle that true propositions about the past are necessary — not in the sense of logical necessity, but in the sense of being outside the range of present agency. What happened, happened; nothing can make it otherwise. This principle is central to both logical fatalism and theological fatalism. Its precise scope — whether it applies to hard facts only, or also to soft facts — is contested.

**Open Future Semantics**
A family of semantic frameworks, developed by Prior (1967), Thomason (1970), and Belnap et al. (2001), that represent the future as a branching tree of possibilities rather than a single determined sequence. Future contingent statements are true at a branch-time pair but may be "unsettled" at a time — neither settled true nor settled false — because they are true on some branches and false on others. Implements Aristotle's intuition that the future is genuinely open.

**Reasons-Responsiveness**
Fischer and Ravizza's (1998) criterion for the kind of control sufficient for moral responsibility. A mechanism (such as a deliberative process) is reasons-responsive if it would produce different outputs in response to different reasons-inputs — if a good reason to act otherwise would, in some circumstances, lead the agent to act otherwise. Responsibility requires that the mechanism be "one's own" and reasons-responsive in the right way.

**Temporal Necessity**
Also called "accidental necessity" or "necessity per accidens." The type of necessity that past truths acquire simply by becoming past — not because they are logically necessary, but because the past is fixed and unchangeable. Contrasts with logical necessity (truth in all possible worlds) and with causal necessity (determination by prior causes). Central to the fatalism arguments examined in this chapter.

**Theological Fatalism**
The argument that divine foreknowledge is incompatible with human freedom. If God infallibly knew yesterday that you would do A today, then you cannot refrain from doing A without making God's knowledge false — which is impossible. The problem arises because divine foreknowledge combines temporal necessity (the past is fixed) with infallibility (God cannot be wrong) in a way that seems to foreclose alternatives.
