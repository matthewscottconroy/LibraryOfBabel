# Chapter 31 Important Researchers: Time and Free Will

---

**Aristotle (384–322 BCE)**
The most influential philosopher in the Western tradition, Aristotle's contribution to the free will and time debate is centered on *De Interpretatione* 9 and its treatment of future contingents. By diagnosing the threat of logical fatalism and proposing the denial of bivalence for future contingent statements, Aristotle framed an alternative to fatalism that has influenced logicians, philosophers, and theologians for over two thousand years. His solution — the "open future" semantics — remains a live option and has been developed into sophisticated formal systems in the twentieth century. The discussion of contingency in *De Interpretatione* connects with his broader treatment of potentiality and actuality in the *Metaphysics* and the analysis of deliberation in the *Nicomachean Ethics*.

**Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (c. 477–524 CE)**
Roman philosopher, statesman, and theologian whose *Consolation of Philosophy*, written while awaiting execution, stands as one of the masterpieces of late antique philosophy. Book V contains the definitive formulation of the eternal-present solution to theological fatalism: God is outside time and apprehends all temporal events simultaneously, so divine "foreknowledge" is not foreknowledge at all but timeless simultaneous perception. This solution has shaped Christian theology and philosophy of religion ever since. Boethius also translated Aristotle's logic into Latin, ensuring its transmission to medieval Europe.

**Diodorus Cronus (c. 340–280 BCE)**
Megarian logician famous for the Master Argument, which shows that three apparently compelling claims about possibility, necessity, and the past cannot all be true simultaneously. Diodorus resolved the tension by collapsing the possible into the actual: only what is or will be true is genuinely possible. His influence on Stoic logic was considerable, though almost none of his work survives in direct form. We know the argument primarily from Epictetus and later commentators. Despite the fragmentary evidence, his contribution to modal logic is of the first rank.

**John Martin Fischer (b. 1952)**
Contemporary analytic philosopher at the University of California Riverside whose work on free will, moral responsibility, and theological fatalism has been among the most influential of the past four decades. Fischer's analysis of hard and soft facts clarified the Ockhamist response to theological fatalism; his development of reasons-responsiveness compatibilism (with Ravizza) provides one of the most rigorous accounts of guidance control in the literature. His "semi-compatibilism" — the view that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism even if free will strictly construed is not — represents a significant methodological advance. He has also written on death, immortality, and the value of finite life (see Chapter 32).

**Harry Frankfurt (b. 1929)**
American philosopher whose 1971 paper "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" is one of the most cited and discussed papers in twentieth-century philosophy. Frankfurt's hierarchical model of free will — distinguishing first-order desires from second-order volitions — transformed the debate by showing that freedom is about the structure of motivation, not the absence of causal determination. The Frankfurt cases, designed to show that the ability to do otherwise is not necessary for moral responsibility, have generated an extensive secondary literature and remain controversial. Frankfurt has also written importantly on love, necessity, and the ethics of care.

**Robert Kane (b. 1938)**
Contemporary philosopher at the University of Texas whose work represents the most systematic and sustained defense of libertarian free will from within a broadly naturalist framework. Kane's key contribution is the concept of "self-forming actions" (SFAs) — moments of decision in which an agent's character and values are being formed, not merely expressed. Kane argues that quantum indeterminism at the neural level, shaped by the agent's reasons and values through their influence on probability distributions, can ground the kind of openness required for ultimate responsibility. His *The Significance of Free Will* (1996) is the definitive statement of this position.

**Timothy O'Connor (b. 1965)**
Contemporary philosopher at Indiana University whose work on agent causation represents the most technically developed version of that theory in the contemporary literature. O'Connor argues that persons are substances with irreducible causal powers — they are genuine initiators of causal sequences, not merely nodes in an event-causal network. His *Persons and Causes* (2000) defends a non-reductive naturalism about agent causation and responds systematically to the main objections. O'Connor has also worked on emergence and the metaphysics of mind.

**Derk Pereboom (b. 1957)**
Contemporary philosopher at Cornell University and the leading contemporary advocate of hard incompatibilism. Pereboom's four-case argument (the "manipulation argument") contends that compatibilism fails because manipulated agents and deterministically produced agents are relevantly similar in the ways that matter for free will, and his critique of libertarianism argues that agent causation and quantum-indeterminist theories both fail to provide the required metaphysical foundation. His *Living Without Free Will* (2001) and *Free Will, Agency, and Meaning in Life* (2014) work out the implications of hard incompatibilism for moral responsibility and practical life with unusual honesty and depth.

**Peter van Inwagen (b. 1942)**
Contemporary philosopher at Notre Dame whose *An Essay on Free Will* (1983) contains the Consequence Argument, one of the most carefully worked-out arguments in contemporary philosophy. Van Inwagen argues that no compatibilist account can succeed and that the only alternative to hard incompatibilism is libertarianism — but he regards the prospects for libertarianism as unpromising, leaving him in the uncomfortable position of being unable to accept any satisfying account of free will. His candor about this predicament is philosophically admirable. He has also written extensively on the metaphysics of material constitution and persistence.

**William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347)**
Medieval English Franciscan friar and logician whose distinction between "hard" and "soft" facts about the past provides one of the most influential tools in the theological fatalism debate. Ockham argued that God's foreknowledge, while a genuine past fact in a grammatical sense, is a "soft" fact with forward-directed content, and therefore does not fall under the principle that past facts are necessary. This move preserves both divine foreknowledge and human freedom. Ockham's broader logical work — including his development of terminist logic and his nominalism — makes him one of the most important figures in medieval philosophy.
