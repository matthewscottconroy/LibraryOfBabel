# Chapter 31: Time and Free Will

---

## Chapter Introduction

The problem of free will is ancient, but its connection to time is more intimate than is often recognized. The question is not merely whether our choices are caused, but whether the future — the domain into which our choices are supposed to reach — is the kind of thing that can be genuinely *open*. If the future is already fixed, if there is a fact about what you will do before you do it, then the deliberation you experience as you weigh your options begins to look like theater: a performance of decision in a world where the outcome was never in question.

This chapter traces four interlocking lines of inquiry. The first is the ancient logical problem of fatalism: can future-tense statements be determinately true or false without thereby robbing the future of its contingency? Diodorus Cronus, writing in the fourth century BCE, saw that three seemingly plausible claims about possibility and necessity cannot all be true simultaneously; his resolution amounts to collapsing the possible into the actual. Aristotle, confronting the sea battle that may or may not occur tomorrow, proposed a more radical solution: deny that future contingent statements have a determinate truth value at all. The logic of time, it turns out, is philosophically loaded.

The second line of inquiry concerns theological fatalism. If an infallible God knew yesterday that you would perform action A today, can you do otherwise? The problem seems to imply divine foreknowledge and human freedom cannot coexist. But careful analysis reveals that the issue turns on subtle distinctions — between types of necessity, and between what Ockham called "hard" and "soft" facts about the past. Boethius offered a different escape: God is not in time at all, and so "foreknowledge" is a misnomer. We examine whether this solution resolves the problem or merely relocates it.

The third line examines the block universe and its implications. If eternalism is true — if past, present, and future events all exist equally — does that settle the free will question? The answer requires distinguishing ontological completeness from causal determination. A block universe is compatible with either determinism or indeterminism; the mere fact that future events "exist" does not entail that they were causally inevitable from any earlier time.

The fourth line concerns libertarian approaches: the view that genuine free will requires indeterminism and something more — some form of agent causation that is neither random nor determined. The challenge here is acute: mere indeterminism (quantum randomness) does not obviously help, since random events are not free choices. Libertarianism requires a distinctive metaphysics of persons as irreducible causal agents.

Throughout, keep in mind what is at stake. If free will is illusory, the foundations of moral responsibility — praise, blame, punishment, desert — become deeply problematic. The temporal dimensions of this problem are not incidental; they go to the heart of what it means to be an agent in a world that moves through time.
