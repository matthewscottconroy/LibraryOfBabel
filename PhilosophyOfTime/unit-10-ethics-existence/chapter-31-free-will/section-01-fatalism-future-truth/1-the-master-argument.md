# 31.1.1 The Master Argument of Diodorus Cronus

---

Diodorus Cronus (c. 340–280 BCE) was a logician of the Megarian school whose influence was disproportionate to what has survived of his writing — which is almost nothing. We know his arguments primarily through their reception in later authors, above all Epictetus, who reports the Master Argument in *Discourses* II.19 with evident frustration at its difficulty, and through the extensive ancient commentary tradition. Yet despite this fragmentary preservation, the Master Argument deserves to be counted among the great achievements of ancient logic, not because it is obviously correct, but because it isolates a genuine tension at the heart of our thinking about possibility and time.

## The Three Premises

Epictetus reports that the argument consists of three propositions that cannot all be simultaneously true:

1. Every true proposition about the past is necessary.
2. The impossible does not follow from the possible.
3. There is something possible that neither is nor ever will be true.

Each premise has genuine intuitive support. Premise (1) captures the sense in which the past is "fixed" — whatever happened, happened, and nothing can make it otherwise. If it is now true that Caesar crossed the Rubicon in 49 BCE, that truth is necessary: there is no available world in which it is false. This is sometimes called *temporal necessity* or *the necessity of the past*, and it has deep roots in common sense and in later philosophical tradition.

Premise (2) is a basic principle of modal logic: if something is possible, it should be possible without contradiction; specifically, it should not entail something impossible. If doing A is possible, and doing A necessarily leads to B, then B must be possible too. To deny this would be to say that a possible thing can have impossible consequences, which seems incoherent.

Premise (3) is what we might call the "open possibilities" thesis: there are genuine possibilities that go unrealized — things that could happen but will not. This is the common-sense view that the future is not entirely settled, that some things are genuinely optional in a way that goes beyond mere uncertainty about what will in fact occur.

## The Incompatibility

Diodorus's insight was that these three premises are mutually inconsistent. Here is a reconstruction of why.

Suppose, for reductio, that (3) is true: there is some proposition P that is possible but neither is nor ever will be true. Since P is possible, consider it as the starting point of a chain of consequences. As time passes, each moment at which P does not come true becomes part of the past. And by premise (1), it is now necessary that P did not come true at those moments — past truths are necessary. Eventually, P's failure to come true at any point becomes an entrenched past fact, and by (1), necessarily true. But then it was never genuinely possible for P to be true — since its failing to be true is necessary. This contradicts the assumption that P was possible. So premise (3) is false: the only things that are possible are things that either are or will be true.

Alternatively: if some proposition P is genuinely possible (3), and P's truth at some future time follows from the present state of things, then by (2), P's coming true must be possible. But P's coming true is either something that will happen (in which case it is actual, not a counterexample to anything) or something that will not happen (in which case its not happening becomes a necessary past truth, making it impossible). The three premises together generate a contradiction.

## Diodorus's Resolution: Collapsing Possibility into Actuality

Diodorus's own resolution was to reject premise (3). On his account, *possibility* is not a separate modal status beyond the actual. A thing is possible if and only if it either is or will be the case. This is sometimes called the *Diodorean* definition of possibility, and it amounts to a radical restriction of the modal space. There are no unrealized possibilities; the range of the possible is identical to the range of the actual past, present, and future.

The implications are immediately fatalistic. If only what will happen is possible, then everything that happens is in some sense necessary — not in the sense that it was causally inevitable from any earlier moment, but in the deeper sense that "possible but won't happen" is an empty category. Whatever you will do tomorrow is possible; whatever you will not do tomorrow is impossible. Your choices between genuinely open alternatives are illusory, because the alternatives you do not take were never genuinely available.

## The Connection to Fatalism

It is important to distinguish logical fatalism from causal determinism. The fatalist argument does not claim that your actions are caused by prior events in a way that makes them inevitable. It claims something more abstract: that there are facts about the future, and that those facts, because they are facts, are in some sense necessary. The worry is not that the causal order forecloses your options, but that the *logical* structure of truth and time does.

This distinction matters because many compatibilist responses to determinism do not work against logical fatalism. Compatibilism typically argues that determinism is compatible with free will because free will concerns the right kind of causal relationship between your desires and your actions — not the absence of prior causes. But if the fatalist is right, the problem is not about prior causes but about the logical status of propositions. You could be the sole cause of your action and still, in the relevant sense, have been fated to perform it.

## Responses and Assessment

The Master Argument has attracted numerous responses in both ancient and modern times. Three deserve mention.

First, one can distinguish different senses of "necessary" in premise (1). Perhaps past truths are necessary only in the sense that we cannot now change them — but this is a relatively weak, epistemic necessity, not a deep metaphysical one. The fatalist needs a stronger sense: that past truths are necessary in a sense that transfers to present possibilities. Critics argue this stronger sense is not obviously warranted (Rescher and Urquhart 1971: 108–115).

Second, one can question whether the "necessity of the past" in premise (1) applies to future-directed content. If "it will rain tomorrow" was true yesterday, is it true yesterday that the truth of *that proposition* is now necessary? This anticipates the Ockhamist distinction between hard and soft facts, developed more fully in the context of theological fatalism.

Third, one can accept the argument as a sound demonstration that we must choose: either reject classical logic's bivalence for future contingents (Aristotle's move, Section 31.1.2), or accept that unrealized possibilities are empty (Diodorus's move), or find a way to restrict the scope of the necessity of the past. There is no free lunch here; something has to give.

What makes the Master Argument permanently instructive is not its conclusion but its clarity. It shows that our ordinary thinking about time, truth, and possibility contains unresolved tensions that cannot be dissolved simply by appealing to intuition. Whether or not Diodorus was right, the problem he identified is real.
