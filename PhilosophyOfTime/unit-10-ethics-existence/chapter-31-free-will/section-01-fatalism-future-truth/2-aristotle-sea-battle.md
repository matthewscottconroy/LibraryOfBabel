# 31.1.2 Aristotle's Sea Battle and the Open Future

---

*De Interpretatione* Chapter 9 is one of the most commented-upon passages in the entire Aristotelian corpus, and with good reason. In fewer than two pages, Aristotle formulates what remains one of the central puzzles in the philosophy of logic and time: what is the truth value of a statement about a contingent future event? The example he reaches for has lodged itself permanently in philosophical vocabulary: a sea battle that may or may not take place tomorrow.

## The Problem

Aristotle begins from the principle of bivalence: every affirmative statement is either true or false (*De Interpretatione* 9, 18a28–34). This seems unobjectionable — it is part of what it means for something to be a genuine statement rather than, say, a question. But Aristotle notices that if bivalence holds universally, it seems to collapse contingency.

Consider the statement "There will be a sea battle tomorrow." If this statement is true now, then the sea battle is already determined to happen — anything else would make the statement false, contrary to the assumption. But if the statement is false now, then the sea battle is already determined not to happen. Either way, the outcome appears necessary, not contingent. And this applies to every future-tense statement about contingent events. The logic of bivalence seems to entail that the future is already fixed.

Aristotle's argument for this conclusion runs approximately as follows. Take any future-tense statement S. Either S is true or S is false (bivalence). If S is true, then whatever S describes must occur; if it were going to fail to occur, S would be false, contradicting the assumption. If S is false, then whatever S describes must not occur. In either case, the outcome is necessitated. Since this reasoning applies to every future-tense statement, every future event is necessitated. Deliberation, planning, and the entire apparatus of human agency become performative illusions.

## Aristotle's Response: Denying Bivalence for Future Contingents

Aristotle's solution is radical. He denies that the principle of bivalence applies to future contingent statements. A statement like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is, he suggests, neither determinately true nor determinately false at the time of utterance. It is *indefinite* — poised between truth and falsity in a way that mirrors the genuine contingency of the event it describes.

This move has a certain elegance. If statements track reality, and reality includes genuinely open possibilities, then statements about open possibilities should themselves be "open" — not yet settled in their truth value. The logic of statements should respect the ontology of the world they describe.

Aristotle is careful about what he is and is not claiming. He does not deny that *either* the battle will happen *or* it will not (the law of excluded middle might still hold in some form). He denies that the disjunction distributes so that *one particular disjunct* is already determinately true. The statement "there will or will not be a sea battle tomorrow" may be true, while neither "there will be" nor "there will not be" is determinately true individually.

## The Open Future Semantics

Aristotle's position has been developed in the twentieth century into a systematic semantic framework, often called "open future semantics" or "indeterminist semantics." The key move is to extend the Kripke-style possible-worlds semantics for modal logic to temporal contexts.

In standard possible-worlds semantics, a statement is true if it is true at the actual world. In open-future semantics, we do not treat the future as a single actual "branch" but as a tree of possibilities — many branches extend from the present, and only one will be actualized. A future-tense statement is true at a branch-time pair if the event occurs on that branch, false if it does not. The statement is *settled true* at the present if it is true on every branch extending from the present, *settled false* if false on every branch, and *unsettled* if it is true on some branches and false on others.

This framework, developed formally by Prior (1967), Thomason (1970), and Belnap et al. (2001), provides a precise logical account of the open future. It makes room for genuine contingency without abandoning classical logic entirely. Future contingent statements receive neither classical truth nor classical falsity; they receive the status of genuinely unsettled possibilities.

## Philosophical Implications

The open future semantics has immediate implications for free will. If future contingent statements are not yet determinately true or false, then there is no *fact of the matter* now about what you will choose tomorrow — and the fatalistic inference from "true now" to "necessary now" cannot get started. The future is ontologically open in the sense that matters for agency.

But this solution carries costs. It requires revising classical logic, which is not a small concession. Many logicians and metaphysicians consider bivalence a non-negotiable feature of any serious logical system. If we give up bivalence for future contingents, we commit ourselves to a kind of logic that many find technically awkward and philosophically surprising.

Moreover, there is a tension between the open future view and eternalism about time (the block universe). If all temporal positions are equally real, and the future "exists" just as much as the past and present, then there would appear to be facts about future events — and bivalence would hold for future-tense statements. Aristotle's solution requires, at minimum, that the future is ontologically different from the past in some relevant way. A-theorists will find this natural; B-theorists will find it harder to accommodate.

## Bivalence vs. Determinism

It is important to notice that the fatalist argument in *De Interpretatione* 9 is distinct from the problem of causal determinism. Aristotle's argument does not assume that events are causally necessitated by prior events; it assumes only that statements about them are determinately true or false. The problem is logical, not physical. This is why Aristotle's solution is a logical one — revise the logic — rather than a physical one — posit indeterminism.

Contemporary philosophers have been divided about whether the two problems are really independent. Tooley (1997: 45–68) argues that the open future view and causal indeterminism naturally fit together. Others, including Horwich (1987: 24–30), argue that the semantics can be evaluated independently of causal questions. What is clear is that Aristotle opened a line of inquiry that has proved inexhaustible: the relationship between truth, time, and the openness of the future remains one of the liveliest areas of work in analytic philosophy.

## Legacy and Contemporary Relevance

The sea battle example continues to generate scholarly work. The branching-time framework of Belnap, Perloff, and Xu (2001) in *Facing the Future* provides the most sophisticated formal development of Aristotle's intuitions. The ockhamist tradition (explored in Section 31.2) provides an alternative response that preserves bivalence by distinguishing what is true from what is *now fixed as necessary*. MacFarlane (2003; 2014) has developed a relativist semantics on which the truth of future contingents is assessor-relative — true or false depending on when one evaluates them. Each of these approaches is a descendant of the sea battle.

What Aristotle achieved in *De Interpretatione* 9 was to show that time is not a neutral backdrop for the evaluation of statements. The temporal location of an utterance, and the temporal direction of its reference, affect its logical behavior. Logic and the philosophy of time are not separate disciplines; they illuminate each other at every turn.
