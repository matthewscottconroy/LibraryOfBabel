# Section 31.1: Fatalism and Future Truth

---

## Section Introduction

Logical fatalism is the oldest version of the worry that the future might be closed. It does not depend on God, or physics, or the block universe. It depends only on logic — specifically, on the claim that every declarative statement is either true or false, that truths about the past are necessary in some sense, and that what follows from the necessary is itself necessary.

This section reconstructs two ancient formulations of the problem. The first is Diodorus Cronus's Master Argument, which operates at the level of modal logic — the logic of possibility and necessity. Diodorus showed that three intuitively compelling claims about these concepts cannot all be true together, and resolved the tension by drastically restricting what counts as "possible." The implications are radical: on his view, the only things that are genuinely possible are things that either are or will be actual. Unrealized possibilities are impossibilities.

The second formulation is Aristotle's, and it is more tractable because Aristotle offers a genuine solution — though one that carries its own costs. Faced with the apparent implication that future-tense statements must already be determinately true or false, Aristotle suggests that future contingent statements are neither. This "open future" semantics preserves contingency but requires revising classical logic. The revision has been enormously influential in subsequent formal work on temporal logic and the semantics of tense.

Together these two thinkers set the agenda for everything that follows. The basic question they raise — what is the relationship between truth, necessity, and time? — turns out to be one of the deepest questions in the philosophy of time.
