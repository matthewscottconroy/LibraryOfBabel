# 31.2.1 Foreknowledge and Freedom

---

## The Basic Argument

The problem of theological fatalism can be stated with disarming simplicity. Suppose that God is omniscient — knowing all truths, past, present, and future, infallibly and necessarily. Suppose you freely perform action A at noon today. Then yesterday God knew that you would perform A at noon. But if God knew yesterday that you would perform A, then it seems you cannot refrain from performing A. For if you were to refrain, God's yesterday-knowledge would be false. But God's knowledge cannot be false — it is infallible by definition. Therefore you cannot refrain. But if you cannot refrain from performing A, then you do not perform it freely.

The argument can be stated more formally. Let K be the proposition "God knew at time t₋₁ that person S would perform action A at time t₀." Let the following premises be granted:

(P1) God knew at t₋₁ that S would do A at t₀. (foreknowledge claim)
(P2) Necessarily, if God knew at t₋₁ that p, then p is true. (infallibility)
(P3) If p was true at t₋₁, and t₋₁ is in the past relative to t₀, then it is now necessary (at t₀) that p. (necessity of the past)
(P4) What is now necessary is not something one could refrain from. (ability principle)

From (P1), (P2), and (P3), it follows that it is now necessary that S does A at t₀. By (P4), S cannot refrain from doing A. Therefore S does not do A freely.

The argument is valid. The question is which premise to deny.

## Distinguishing Types of Necessity

Before examining the responses, it is worth distinguishing the different senses of "necessity" that appear in the argument. This distinction is not merely terminological; different senses of necessity interact with the argument differently.

*Logical necessity* holds of propositions that are true in all possible worlds — "all bachelors are unmarried" is logically necessary. *Temporal necessity* (or "accidental necessity") holds of propositions simply in virtue of being about the past — what happened, happened, and nothing can change it. This is the necessity captured in premise (P3). *Causal necessity* holds of events that were determined to occur by prior causal conditions.

The theological fatalism argument invokes temporal necessity, not causal necessity. God's foreknowledge doesn't *cause* you to act as you do; it merely tracks what you will do. This is why the argument is not simply a version of the determinism problem: it does not claim that your action was causally inevitable. It claims that the existence of infallible past knowledge of your action makes it temporally necessary that you perform it.

## Fischer's Analysis and the Hard/Soft Distinction

John Martin Fischer (1989: 11–44) provides a rigorous examination of the theological fatalism argument that draws on a distinction originating with William of Ockham. The distinction is between *hard facts* about the past and *soft facts* about the past.

A hard fact about the past is a fact that concerns *only* what happened at that past time — it has no essential reference to what will happen at later times. "It rained in London on July 4, 1066" is a hard fact about that date: its truth conditions are entirely confined to that day.

A soft fact about the past is a fact that appears to be about the past but has *essential* reference to what will happen at later times. Consider: "In 1990, Jones was sitting two miles from where the first person to walk on the moon in 2050 would eventually stand." This fact is about 1990, but its truth conditions reach forward to 2050. Soft facts are, in a sense, partially about the future, even though they are expressed in the past tense.

Fischer's claim is that God's foreknowledge is a soft fact about the past. The proposition "God knew at t₋₁ that S would do A at t₀" essentially refers to t₀, since "knowledge" is factive — to know that p is to know a truth, and what makes this knowledge true is what S does at t₀. The truth conditions of God's past knowledge essentially involve S's future action.

If divine foreknowledge is a soft fact, then premise (P3) — the necessity of the past — does not apply to it. The necessity of the past applies to hard facts. Soft facts, even though they are "about" the past in a grammatical sense, do not carry the same modal weight. S can retain the ability to do otherwise, because the past fact that God knew S would do A is not a hard constraint on S's options.

## Evaluating the Hard/Soft Distinction

The hard/soft distinction is elegant but has faced challenges. Wierenga (1991: 19–34) argues that God's foreknowledge, properly understood, should be classified as a hard fact, because God's knowing is a mental state that fully occurred at the earlier time, not a disposition that waits on future events. There is a difference, Wierenga suggests, between what makes a fact *true* and what the fact *is*: God's knowing at t₋₁ is a temporally located event, even if its accuracy depends on later events.

Fischer responds that what matters for the necessity-of-the-past principle is whether the fact *could have been different* in a world where S acts differently at t₀. Since God is infallible, God's past belief would have been different if S's future action were different. This counterfactual dependence is what makes the foreknowledge fact a soft one — it is counterfactually dependent on the future in a way that genuine hard facts are not.

This debate reaches a stalemate that reveals something important: the distinction between hard and soft facts, however intuitively compelling, does not have a fully precise characterization that all parties accept. The dispute about divine foreknowledge exposes a genuine ambiguity in our concept of "facts about the past" and their modal status.

## The Ability Principle

Premise (P4) — that necessity forecloses ability — also deserves scrutiny. Free will requires more than mere ability in some minimal sense; it requires the ability to do otherwise in a sense that is compatible with all actual past facts being held fixed. If we hold fixed God's past foreknowledge as a hard fact, and if that foreknowledge entails S's action, then S lacks the relevant kind of ability. But if foreknowledge is a soft fact, the "fixed past" does not include it, and S's ability is intact.

The debate therefore turns on precisely what we hold fixed when we assess "ability to do otherwise." Fischer and Ravizza (1998: 55–86) develop an account of guidance control — the kind of control sufficient for moral responsibility — that does not require the ability to do otherwise in the strong sense implicated by theological fatalism. On this compatibilist approach, moral responsibility requires that one's action be produced by one's own reasons-responsive mechanism, not that one could have acted differently given an absolutely fixed past.

## The Deeper Issue

The problem of foreknowledge and freedom illustrates a deep tension that recurs throughout the philosophy of time. Past facts bear on the present in a way that future facts (apparently) do not. We feel that the past constrains us; the future is ours to shape. But if there are — or were — facts about the future embedded in past knowledge-states, then this asymmetry is harder to maintain.

What theological fatalism forces us to examine is the precise relationship between the *fixity* of the past, the *openness* of the future, and the ground of *ability*. These are not merely theological questions; they are questions about the temporal structure of practical agency. And they cannot be answered without taking a stand on some of the deepest issues in the metaphysics of time.
