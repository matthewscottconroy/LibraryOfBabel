# 31.2.2 Boethius and the Eternal Present

---

Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius wrote *The Consolation of Philosophy* in 524 CE while awaiting execution on charges of treason — charges he denied. The circumstances lend the work a peculiar urgency; its calm, methodical examination of providence, fate, and human freedom is the work of a man who had every personal reason to find the arguments either compelling or devastating. Book V of the *Consolation* contains what has become the most celebrated single response to the problem of divine foreknowledge and human freedom: the doctrine of God's eternity as simultaneous apprehension of all times.

## The Problem as Boethius Frames It

Lady Philosophy, Boethius's interlocutor, articulates the tension clearly: if God's foreknowledge is certain, there can be no contingency in human affairs; if there is contingency, there can be no foreknowledge. These two claims seem to exclude each other. Yet Christian theology requires both: an omniscient God and genuine human freedom that grounds moral responsibility.

Boethius's solution depends on rejecting a hidden assumption in the foreknowledge problem: the assumption that God's knowledge of the future is *foreknowledge* — knowledge acquired from a position within time, looking forward. This assumption seems natural, but Boethius argues it is mistaken. God does not exist in time at all.

## God's Eternity

For Boethius, *eternity* does not mean "existing for an infinite time" — an endless temporal duration stretching before and after the present. That would make God sempiternal, everlasting, but still temporal. True eternity is qualitatively different: it is the *simultaneous and complete possession of unlimited life* (*Consolation* V, Prose 6). God apprehends all moments of time not successively but all at once, in a single, timeless present.

The model Boethius offers is of a person standing on a high hill who can see the entire plain below simultaneously, while travelers on the plain can see only the stretch immediately before them. God's perspective on time is like the hilltop view: all moments are visible at once, laid out before a timeless gaze. Human perspective is like the traveler's: we see only the present and remember the past, while the future remains hidden.

On this account, what looks from our perspective like *foreknowledge* is, from God's perspective, simply *knowledge* — simultaneous, timeless apprehension of events that occur at different times. God does not *predict* your action from a position in the past; God *observes* your action in the eternal present, even though your action occurs at a particular time.

## Does This Resolve the Problem?

The question of whether Boethius's solution genuinely resolves the problem has occupied philosophers for fifteen centuries, and the debate has not converged. The solution's proponents argue as follows. The problem of foreknowledge arises because God's past knowledge seems to fix the future — since the past is necessary, and God's past knowledge entails your future action, your future action is necessary. But if God is outside time, there is no past knowledge; there is only eternal knowledge. And eternal knowledge, being outside time, does not fall under the principle that *past* facts are necessary. The necessity of the past has no grip on a timelessly eternal being.

Moreover, Boethius argues, there is a distinction between *conditional* and *absolute* necessity. Even if God eternally knows that you will do A, this amounts only to: *given that you do A, God eternally knows it*. Your doing A is not absolutely necessary; it is necessary only given the conditional fact that God's knowledge tracks what you do. The truth of God's knowledge is conditioned on what you freely do, not the other way around. God's eternal perception is like a human witness observing an event: the observer's seeing that you do something does not make your doing it necessary — it merely tracks the contingent event.

## The Relocation Objection

The most persistent challenge to Boethius is that his solution does not resolve the problem but *relocates* it. Even if God is outside time, God's eternal knowledge stands in a relationship to your action: God eternally knows that you will do A. If this eternal knowledge is true, and if the truth of an eternal proposition cannot be altered, then it seems your action is necessitated by the eternal truth, not by a past temporal fact.

Pike (1965: 36–46) pressed this objection with particular force. Suppose God exists outside time but knows eternally that Jones does A at noon on Monday. Is it "in Jones's power" to refrain from A? If Jones were to refrain, God's eternal knowledge would be false — which is impossible. Therefore Jones cannot refrain, whether or not God's knowledge is "foreknowledge" in a temporal sense. The impossibility does not depend on the temporal location of God's knowledge.

Boethius's supporters (Stump and Kretzmann 1981: 429–458) argue that "at the same time" and "before" are temporal relations that do not apply to a timeless God, and therefore the inference from eternal knowledge to necessity is blocked. But this response invites the question: if God is genuinely outside time, in what sense does God "know" your present action at all? The relationship between a timeless being and a temporal event needs to be characterized with great care, and many philosophers have found existing characterizations incomplete.

## Philosophical Assessment

The Boethian solution illuminates something important even if it does not ultimately succeed. It shows that the problem of foreknowledge is, at its core, a problem about the relationship between the *modal status* of facts and their *temporal location*. The reason past facts seem to constrain the present is that they are "fixed" — outside the range of present agency. But what exactly makes them fixed? Is it their pastness as such, or is it some more fundamental feature — their being outside the causal reach of the present agent?

If the latter, then a timeless God's eternal knowledge might indeed be analogously "fixed" from a human perspective, reinstating the problem. If the former, then Boethius's appeal to divine atemporality blocks the fixity argument, but at the cost of making divine knowledge harder to understand.

Contemporary philosophy of religion has explored these questions with considerable sophistication. Hasker (1989: 150–186) argues that neither Ockhamism nor the Boethian solution adequately preserves genuine freedom, and that the only coherent theism compatible with libertarian free will is *open theism* — the view that God voluntarily limits foreknowledge of free human actions. Whatever one thinks of that conclusion, the Boethian discussion remains indispensable: it forces precision about eternity, necessity, and the temporal structure of divine cognition that illuminates the philosophy of time as much as the philosophy of religion.
