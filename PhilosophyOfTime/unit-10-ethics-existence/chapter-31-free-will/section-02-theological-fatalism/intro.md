# Section 31.2: Theological Fatalism

---

## Section Introduction

Logical fatalism, as Aristotle presented it, rests on the structure of truth and time alone. Theological fatalism adds a further element: an omniscient God whose knowledge of the future seems to guarantee that the future is fixed in a way that eliminates human freedom. If God knew yesterday, with perfect and infallible knowledge, that you would perform action A today, then how could you possibly do otherwise? The question is not whether God forces you — nothing in the argument requires coercion. The question is whether God's infallible foreknowledge is compatible with your having had a genuine alternative.

This section examines two major responses to theological fatalism. The first, associated with William of Ockham, accepts that God has genuine foreknowledge of contingent events but denies that this forecloses alternatives. The key move is a distinction between different kinds of facts about the past: "hard" facts, which concern only what happened at that past time, and "soft" facts, which have essential reference to other times (including the future). God's past knowledge is a soft fact — it has future-directed content — and so it does not fall under the principle that past facts are necessary. Our past freedom to do otherwise is therefore intact.

The second response, associated with Boethius, denies the premise that God's knowledge is a form of *foreknowledge* at all. God does not know the future from within time; God exists outside time and apprehends all moments simultaneously in an eternal present. On this view, the problem of foreknowledge is dissolved by relocating God outside the temporal framework in which the problem arises.

Both responses have their defenders, and both face serious objections. What this section aims to show is that the theological fatalism problem is philosophically substantive — not merely a theological curiosity, but a window into deep questions about necessity, temporal direction, and the nature of the past.
