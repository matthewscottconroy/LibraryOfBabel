# 31.3.1 The Block Universe and Human Agency

---

## The Block Universe Recapped

The block universe view — also called eternalism or four-dimensionalism — holds that temporal reality is not confined to the present moment or to the past and present together. All times are equally real: the year 1066 and the year 2350 exist just as much as 2024, though of course only 2024 is present *now*, in the indexical sense. The entire four-dimensional manifold of spacetime events exists as a single, unified whole. Time is like space in one crucial respect: the fact that I am not currently in Paris does not mean Paris does not exist; similarly, the fact that the Battle of Hastings is not current does not mean it does not exist. It exists at its own time.

This view has strong support from physics. Special relativity establishes that simultaneity is relative to reference frame — different inertial observers will disagree about which events are "simultaneous" and hence about what counts as "the present." If the present were privileged in the way presentism requires, the relativity of simultaneity would generate troubling ambiguities. The block universe treats all times as equally real and thereby sidesteps these complications (Sider 2001: 42–52; Putnam 1967).

## The Apparent Threat to Freedom

It is easy to see why the block universe might seem threatening to free will. If future events exist — if your decision tomorrow is already "there" in the four-dimensional structure — then in what sense is it up to you what happens? The future seems as fixed as the past. The person who will make a choice tomorrow is already, in a sense, making it: that choice-event is already part of the eternal furniture of the universe.

This line of thought can be formulated more precisely. On the block universe, for any future event E, there exists an event-location L in spacetime, and E is located at L. The proposition "E occurs" is true simpliciter — not "true now" or "true at some times and not others," but just true, as a timeless fact. If it is just true that you will choose option A tomorrow, then there is a sense in which the outcome is settled — it is part of the eternal structure of reality.

## The Conflation of Ontology and Causation

Here is where careful diagnosis is needed. The argument above conflates two distinct claims:

(i) Future events exist (ontological claim).
(ii) Future events were causally determined by prior events (causal claim).

The block universe establishes (i). It does *not* by itself establish (ii). A block universe is compatible with a world in which some events are not causally determined by prior events — a world with genuine quantum indeterminism, for instance, or one with genuinely chance events at the macro-level. In such a world, the future events still "exist" in the block universe sense; they are just not the inevitable products of prior causes. Their existence is a feature of the four-dimensional structure, but their occurrence relative to prior events is not causally necessary.

To put this differently: the block universe tells us about the *ontological structure* of time — which times and events exist. It does not by itself tell us about the *causal structure* — which events are necessitated by which prior events. These are separate questions, and conflating them generates a spurious fatalism.

Mellor (1998: 127–130) makes this point clearly: the block universe "says nothing about causation" — it is a thesis about what exists, not about the relationships of necessitation between events. Similarly, Sider (2001: 42–52) notes that eternalism is compatible with a wide range of causal stories.

## The Block Universe and Compatibilism

If the block universe does not by itself generate fatalism, what does it imply for free will? The most natural answer is: relatively little, directly. The question of free will is not "are future events real?" but "are our choices the right kind of cause of our actions?" This is the terrain of the compatibilism debate.

A compatibilist can accept the block universe without difficulty. In the four-dimensional manifold, there are events of deliberation, events of action, and causal relationships between them. The deliberation events causally influence the action events; the agent reasons, weighs options, and acts on the basis of that reasoning. Whether this constitutes genuine freedom is a question about the nature of the causal relationship between deliberation and action — a question that is independent of whether future events exist timelessly.

It is worth noting that the block universe might even be a *friendly* framework for compatibilism about agency. In the block universe, there is no "open future" that agents can influence; what there is, instead, is a rich causal structure in which some event-types (deliberations, reasons-responses) are systematically related to subsequent event-types (actions). The agent's freedom consists in being the right kind of node in this causal network — a node whose outputs are sensitive to reasons-inputs. This is exactly what sophisticated compatibilists like Wolf (1990: 30–35) and Fischer and Ravizza (1998: 36–41) have argued.

## The Residual Problem

There is, however, a more subtle way in which the block universe might create difficulty for free will, beyond the naive fatalism critique. This concerns the relationship between the *direction of explanation* and the *direction of time*.

In the block universe, temporal relations are just relations among events; the "direction" of time is not built into the fundamental ontology but emerges from asymmetries in the causal structure (as discussed in Chapter 21). In such a framework, the agent's deliberation and the resulting action are both equally real events, related by causal and explanatory connections. But there may be a question about whether the agent's deliberation genuinely *explains* the action in the sense required for genuine agency — or whether the explanation runs the other way (from action back to deliberation) just as legitimately, given the symmetry of the underlying ontology.

This is a deep worry, but its resolution does not obviously favor fatalism. The asymmetry of causation — that causes precede their effects, and that explanations typically run forward in time — is compatible with the block universe once we recognize that causal asymmetry is an additional feature of the world beyond temporal asymmetry (Price 1996: 155–162). Free will requires the right kind of causal relationship; the block universe does not foreclose having such relationships.

The upshot is that the block universe is genuinely neutral with respect to free will. Students who feel threatened by the picture of a four-dimensional universe in which the future is "already there" should resist the inference to fatalism. The future is "already there" in the sense that it exists, not in the sense that it was preordained by prior causes. Whether our choices are genuinely free depends on the causal structure of the world and the nature of our agency — not on which theory of temporal ontology is correct.
