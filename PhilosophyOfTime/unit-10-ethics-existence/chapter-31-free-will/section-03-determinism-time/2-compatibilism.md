# 31.3.2 Compatibilism, the Consequence Argument, and Hard Incompatibilism

---

## The Compatibilist Tradition

Compatibilism is the view that free will and determinism are not only logically compatible but that free will can be fully analyzed within a deterministic framework. The tradition is old — Hume and Hobbes are classical compatibilists — but its contemporary development is considerably more sophisticated than the classical versions.

The classical compatibilist claimed that free will simply means acting in accordance with one's desires, without external compulsion. If I do what I want to do, without physical restraint or coercion, I act freely — regardless of whether my desires were themselves causally determined by prior events. But this simple analysis faces the obvious objection: what if your desires themselves were determined by factors outside your control? You may act in accordance with your desires and yet feel that those desires are not genuinely *yours* — they were, say, the product of manipulation, addiction, or psychological conditioning that bypassed your rational agency.

Frankfurt (1971: 5–20) proposed a hierarchical response to this objection that transformed the debate. The key distinction is between *first-order desires* (desires to act in some way) and *second-order desires* (desires about one's first-order desires). A free agent is one whose first-order desires *conform to* their second-order desires — who wants to want what they in fact want. The addict who desperately wishes they did not want drugs has a first-order desire (for drugs) that conflicts with their second-order desire (not to want drugs). Their action is therefore unfree, not because it is caused, but because it is driven by a desire that is not endorsed at the higher level.

Frankfurt's account has two virtues. First, it provides a principled distinction between free and unfree action that does not invoke indeterminism — freedom is about the *structure* of motivation, not about the *absence* of causal determination. Second, it shows why mere absence of external coercion is insufficient: a manipulated agent who acts without external compulsion, but whose motivations have been engineered without their endorsement, does not act freely.

Later developments refined the hierarchical model. Wolf (1990: 67–85) argued that free will requires the ability to act in accordance with *Reason* — to recognize and respond to moral and practical reasons. Fischer and Ravizza (1998: 38–87) developed the concept of *reasons-responsiveness*: a mechanism is reasons-responsive if it would produce different outputs in response to different reasons-inputs. An agent acts freely when the mechanism producing their action is their own (appropriately) and is reasons-responsive in the right way. Neither account requires indeterminism.

## The Consequence Argument

The most powerful challenge to compatibilism is Peter van Inwagen's Consequence Argument (1983: 55–105). The argument aims to show that no compatibilist account of free will can succeed, because the very notion of "ability to do otherwise" is incompatible with determinism.

The argument runs as follows. Let P₀ be a complete description of the state of the world at some time in the distant past (before any living human existed), and let L be the conjunction of all the laws of nature. Determinism is the thesis that P₀ and L together entail every subsequent state of the world, including your action A at time t. Now:

(1) No one has, or ever had, any choice about P₀. (It is in the past, before we existed.)
(2) No one has, or ever had, any choice about L. (Laws of nature are not up to us.)
(3) Therefore, no one has any choice about anything that is a consequence of P₀ and L.
(4) But our actions are consequences of P₀ and L (determinism).
(5) Therefore, no one has any choice about their actions.

The argument deploys a transfer principle: if you have no choice about X, and X entails Y, then you have no choice about Y. Van Inwagen defends this principle against objections. The consequence argument has generally been regarded as one of the most compelling arguments in contemporary philosophy of action.

Compatibilists have contested the argument at the level of the transfer principle. McKenna (2008: 177–192) argues that the principle, while intuitively compelling, cannot be formulated precisely without either being false or presupposing incompatibilism. Fischer (2006: 177–200) develops an account of "semi-compatibilism" that accepts the incompatibility of free will (in the sense of "could have done otherwise") with determinism, while maintaining that *moral responsibility* is compatible with determinism — and that moral responsibility is what really matters.

## Hard Incompatibilism

Hard incompatibilism is the view that neither compatibilism nor libertarianism is correct: genuine free will does not exist, because neither determinism nor indeterminism provides the conditions required. Derk Pereboom (2001: 1–8; 2014: 1–35) is the most systematic advocate of this position.

Pereboom argues that the manipulated-agent cases show that compatibilism fails: a deterministically produced action and a manipulated action are relevantly similar in all the ways that matter for free will, yet we clearly hold that manipulated agents do not act freely. If we accept that intuition, we must accept that deterministically produced actions are equally unfree. And libertarian alternatives — agent causation, quantum indeterminism — fail to provide a metaphysically coherent account of the special causal power they require.

The implications for moral responsibility are significant. Pereboom argues that reactive attitudes — praise, blame, resentment, indignation — that presuppose libertarian free will should be revised or abandoned. But he maintains that a "forward-looking" conception of moral responsibility is still available: we can hold people accountable for pragmatic reasons (deterrence, protection, rehabilitation) without presupposing they "could have done otherwise" in the libertarian sense.

The difficulty with hard incompatibilism is not only philosophical but existential. Our self-understanding as agents, our practices of holding each other responsible, and our experience of deliberation all seem to presuppose something like libertarian free will. Pereboom is aware of this; he argues that we can revise our practices without catastrophic loss. Whether that revision is psychologically or socially achievable is a further question that philosophy cannot answer alone.

## Time and the Responsibility Question

One further connection between temporal metaphysics and the free will debate deserves mention. The incompatibilist worry about the "fixed past" is, in a sense, a worry about the asymmetry of time. What makes the past constraining is precisely that it is *past* — outside the range of present agency. The future, by contrast, can be "influenced" by present action. If the block universe collapses this asymmetry, and future events are no more "influenceable" than past ones, then the worry resurfaces at the level of the entire temporal manifold.

But as we noted in Section 31.3.1, the asymmetry that matters for agency is causal, not merely temporal. Causes precede their effects; deliberation precedes action; reasons influence outcomes in a forward-directed way. This causal asymmetry is fully compatible with the block universe. The free will debate is ultimately a debate about the nature of causation and agency, not about which theory of temporal ontology is correct.
