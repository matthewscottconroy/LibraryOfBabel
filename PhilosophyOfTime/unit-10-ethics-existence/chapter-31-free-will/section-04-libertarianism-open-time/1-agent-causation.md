# 31.4.1 Agent Causation and the Open Future

---

## The Libertarian Requirement

The intuition behind libertarianism is simple and powerful. When you deliberate about what to do, you experience alternatives as genuinely available — not just as things you are uncertain about, but as things that are *up to you* to bring about or not. You feel that the deliberation has a point: by reasoning carefully, by weighing the considerations, you are doing something that genuinely matters for the outcome, not merely observing a process whose result is predetermined. This experience of open alternatives is central to our self-understanding as agents.

Libertarianism takes this experience at face value and argues that it is veridical. For the experience to be veridical, two conditions must hold. First, the future must be genuinely open: at the moment of decision, more than one outcome must be causally possible given everything that has happened so far. This requires indeterminism at some level relevant to human action. Second, the agent must be the source of the determination of outcomes: among the genuinely open possibilities, the agent's choice is what actualizes one rather than another.

The second condition is the hard one. Quantum indeterminism exists — elementary particles behave probabilistically — but this provides at best the *space* for libertarian freedom, not freedom itself. If my neural processes are quantum-indeterminate, the outcome might be random rather than chosen. A coin flip is indeterminate but not free. Libertarianism needs something that bridges the gap between mere indeterminism and genuine agency.

## The Randomness Problem

This is sometimes called the "luck objection" or the "randomness problem": if determinism forecloses free will, and if indeterminism just introduces randomness, then neither determinism nor indeterminism yields freedom. The libertarian appears stuck.

The force of this objection should not be minimized. Van Inwagen (1983: 126–150) articulated it sharply: if the laws of physics determine that an agent will either choose A or choose B, with each having some probability, then the actual choice appears to be a matter of luck — the agent did not determine which possibility was actualized. The agent's reasons may have influenced the probabilities, but they did not settle the outcome. And a choice that is partly a matter of luck — of which quantum event happens to fire — is not obviously the kind of robust, reason-sensitive agency that matters for moral responsibility.

Various responses have been developed. Kane (1996: 115–140) argues that genuine indeterminism can be free if the reasons that influence the probabilities are themselves the agent's own — if the agent's character, values, and deliberative processes shape the probability distribution over outcomes, then even a probabilistically-determined choice is an expression of the agent's self. The outcome may be undetermined, but which outcome is more likely is shaped by who the agent is.

This is a significant advance, but critics find it unsatisfying. If the reasons set the probabilities and the physics determines the outcome, the agent seems to be in a position analogous to a gambler who has influenced the odds but not fixed the result. The result that falls out may not be attributable to the agent in the strong sense that moral responsibility seems to require.

## Agent Causation

The most philosophically ambitious libertarian response is the theory of *agent causation*, developed in contemporary analytic philosophy above all by Timothy O'Connor (2000; 2009). Agent causation holds that persons are a distinctive kind of causal entity — they are not merely part of the event-causal nexus, passive transmitters of causal influence from antecedent events. Persons are *substances* that initiate causal sequences, and their initiating of a causal sequence is not itself caused by prior events in the standard way.

O'Connor's account distinguishes two modes of causation. *Event causation* is the familiar kind: one event (a neural firing, a physical impact) causes another event. *Agent causation* is irreducible to event causation: the agent herself — as a substance, not merely as a collection of events — is the cause of her action. The agent's causing is not mediated by a prior event that caused her to cause; the agent is the causal origin.

This is an ontologically heavy commitment. It requires that agents are genuine substances with irreducible causal powers, not fully describable or analyzable in terms of the physical events that constitute them. It requires, in short, that persons are not just physical systems. O'Connor is explicit about this: agent causation implies a modest dualism about persons — not a substance dualism of Cartesian minds and physical bodies, but a recognition that persons as wholes have causal properties that are not reducible to properties of their physical parts.

## Agent Causation and Open Time

The connection to the philosophy of time is direct. Agent causation naturally fits with an A-theory of time in which the present is ontologically privileged and the future is genuinely open. If the future is not "already there" — if presentism or the growing block view is correct — then there is genuine openness in the world, and the agent's causal initiative is what determines which of the genuinely open possibilities is actualized. The agent does not merely select from a range of predetermined options; the agent *brings into existence* the state of affairs that actualizes.

By contrast, agent causation sits uneasily with the block universe. If the future is just as real as the past and present, and if the agent's action at time t is already "there" in the four-dimensional manifold, then it is harder to make sense of the agent's causal initiative as *genuinely open*. The block universe does not make the action unfree — as we argued in Section 31.3.1 — but it does make the metaphysics of agent causation harder to formulate clearly.

## Objections to Agent Causation

O'Connor's view has attracted significant criticism. Pereboom (2014: 65–87) argues that agent causation, even if coherent, does not help with moral responsibility: if the agent's act of causing is itself undetermined by anything — not by prior events, not by the agent's character or reasons — then the choice is arbitrary at the deepest level. Moral responsibility requires that choices be appropriately connected to the agent's reasons and values; an uncaused causal initiation seems to sever this connection.

A different objection concerns the coherence of the concept of substance causation. Hume's analysis of causation as constant conjunction between event-types is the background assumption of most scientific and philosophical work on causation. On event-causal models, to say that A caused B is to say something about regularities between event-types, or about counterfactual dependence between event-tokens. Substance causation does not fit naturally into either analysis: what is the regularity or the counterfactual relationship that characterizes agent causation?

O'Connor (2000: 45–68) responds that agent causation is a genuinely irreducible explanatory category — it explains events in terms of agents, not in terms of prior events. This is a real explanatory kind, not a disguised version of event causation. The demand that it be analyzed in event-causal terms begs the question against agent causation.

## The Irreducibility of Agency

What is most interesting about the libertarian debate, in the context of a philosophy of time course, is what it reveals about the relationship between the temporal structure of the world and the nature of agency. Libertarians take seriously the phenomenology of deliberation — the experience of genuine openness — and attempt to provide an ontological basis for it. The basis they appeal to — genuinely open possibilities, agent causation — requires taking the openness of the future seriously at a metaphysical level.

Whether libertarianism succeeds is genuinely uncertain. The view is difficult to make coherent and faces powerful objections. But it is not obviously wrong, and dismissing it without engagement would be philosophically incurious. The questions it raises — about the kind of causal power persons possess, about the relationship between physical indeterminism and freedom, about the ontological requirements of genuine agency — are among the most important in philosophy. Their resolution, or failure to be resolved, says something deep about what we are.
