# Section 31.4: Libertarianism and the Open Future

---

## Section Introduction

Libertarianism about free will is not a political position but a metaphysical one: it holds that genuine free will requires the ability to do otherwise in the strongest, indeterminist sense, and that human beings (at least sometimes) exercise such freedom. The libertarian agrees with the incompatibilist that determinism forecloses free will, but disagrees with the hard incompatibilist: rather than concluding that free will does not exist, the libertarian concludes that the world is not deterministic in the relevant ways, or that human agency involves something beyond ordinary physical determination.

Libertarianism is the position that takes the openness of the future most seriously as a metaphysical and ethical requirement. For the libertarian, the future is not merely epistemically uncertain; it is ontologically open in a way that is necessary for genuine freedom. This connects directly to the themes of the previous sections: A-theories of time, presentism, and the growing block all have natural affinities with libertarian free will, while the block universe sits more naturally with compatibilism.

This section examines the most developed and philosophically serious form of libertarianism — agent causation — and confronts the hardest challenge it faces: showing that genuinely free choices are neither predetermined nor merely random.
