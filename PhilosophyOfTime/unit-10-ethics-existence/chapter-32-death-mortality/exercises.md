# Chapter 32 Exercises: Time, Death, and Mortality

---

## Conceptual Exercises

**1. The Epicurean Argument**
State the Epicurean argument against fearing death as precisely as you can, identifying each premise. Then assess each premise: which do you find most plausible? Which is most vulnerable? The argument's conclusion is that death is not bad for the person who dies. Is there a distinction between "death is not bad" and "we should not fear death"? Could both the Epicurean argument and the deprivation account be simultaneously correct in some sense?

**2. Lucretius's Symmetry**
Lucretius argues that we should be as unconcerned about post-mortem non-existence as we are about pre-natal non-existence. Nagel responds that there is an asymmetry: we could not have had more pre-natal existence, but we could have had more post-mortem existence. Evaluate this response carefully. Is Nagel's asymmetry sufficient to justify the claim that death is bad while pre-natal non-existence is not? What would Lucretius say in reply?

**3. The Deprivation Account and Its Logic**
The deprivation account says death is bad because it deprives us of goods. But this seems to imply: (a) a shorter life is worse than a longer one, all else equal; (b) dying at 30 is worse than dying at 80; (c) dying at 80 is worse than dying at 120. Do you accept all these implications? Is there a point at which death ceases to be a deprivation? If so, how does this affect the deprivation account?

**4. The Location Problem**
The location problem asks: when is death bad for the person who dies? Evaluate the three proposed answers: (a) ante-mortem; (b) at the moment of death; (c) posthumously. Which do you find most plausible? Can you think of an argument for each? Is the location problem a genuine philosophical problem or a pseudo-problem generated by misapplying the concept of "when" to states that are not temporally located?

**5. The Existence Requirement**
Rosenbaum (1986) defends the principle that you can be harmed only if you exist at the time of the harm. Evaluate this principle by considering the following cases: (a) your reputation is destroyed after your death; (b) your will is violated after your death; (c) your secret is revealed and causes embarrassment to your descendants. Do these cases constitute genuine posthumous harm to you? Does your answer depend on whether you believe that interests can survive death?

**6. Heidegger on Being-toward-Death**
Heidegger claims that death is Dasein's "ownmost, non-relational, not to be outstripped" possibility. Explain what he means by each of these three characteristics. Then evaluate the claim that confronting death "authentically" liberates Dasein for genuine existence. Is there empirical support for this claim? Are there forms of authentic existence that do not require confronting one's own death?

**7. Williams on Categorical Desires**
Williams argues that categorical desires — desires that give one a reason to live, not merely to act while alive — are constitutively finite and tied to mortal personal identity. Assess this claim. Can you identify categorical desires that seem genuinely non-finite? What would it take to satisfy the desire to understand the cosmos, or the desire to love and be loved? Does Fischer's response — that recurring pleasures can sustain an immortal life — answer Williams's deepest concern?

**8. Parfit and What Matters**
Parfit argues that what matters in survival is not strict personal identity but psychological continuity and connectedness. If Parfit is right, how does this affect the claim that death is bad? Would a successor person who is psychologically continuous with you but not strictly identical suffice for the goods associated with continued life? Does the answer depend on whether identity or continuity is "what matters" in the practical sense?

## Discussion and Essay Questions

**9. Comparative Analysis**
Compare Heidegger's analysis of death with the analytic deprivation account. What does each approach capture that the other misses? Is it possible to combine insights from both traditions into a more comprehensive account of the significance of mortality? What would such a synthesis look like?

**10. The Desire for Immortality**
Many religious traditions promise some form of continued existence after death. In light of the Williams debate, is this promise genuinely appealing, or does it raise as many problems as it solves? If the afterlife involved radical transformation of desires and character, would it be *your* afterlife? If it preserved your current desires and character unchanged, would it eventually become tedious?

## Lab and Observational Exercises

**11. Phenomenology of Mortality**
Over the course of a week, pay deliberate attention to how awareness of mortality appears in your daily life. Keep a journal noting: (a) moments when you become aware of your own finitude; (b) how this awareness affects your engagement with present activities; (c) whether the awareness is experienced as threatening, motivating, liberating, or some combination. At the end of the week, analyze your observations in light of Heidegger's claim that authentic being-toward-death liberates Dasein for genuine existence. Does your experience support or challenge his account?

**12. Attitudes Toward Death Across Cultures**
Interview at least three people from different cultural, religious, or generational backgrounds about their attitudes toward death. Ask: (a) Do they fear death? (b) What, if anything, do they believe happens after death? (c) Do they think about death as something personal and individual, or as part of a larger communal or natural cycle? Write a comparative analysis examining how the philosophical positions discussed in this chapter illuminate or fail to illuminate the variety of attitudes you encountered. Does the Epicurean or the deprivation account better match the actual attitudes you found?

**13. Case Study: Assisted Dying**
Research the philosophical arguments deployed in current debates about physician-assisted dying or euthanasia (for example, the debates in Oregon, the Netherlands, or Canada). Identify which philosophical positions about the badness of death — Epicurean, deprivationist, or Heideggerian — are implicitly or explicitly invoked by different parties. Write a 500-word analysis showing how the abstract philosophy of death connects to concrete policy debates. What features of the philosophical debate are most relevant to the policy question?
