## Annotated Entries

**Bradley, *Well-Being and Death* (2009)**
The most comprehensive and technically rigorous contemporary treatment of the badness of death. Bradley examines the deprivation account, the location problem, the existence requirement, and posthumous harm with great precision. Engages the full range of contemporary positions. Essential reading for serious work on the philosophy of death.

**Dreyfus, *Being-in-the-World* (1991)**
The standard English-language commentary on Division I of *Being and Time*. Dreyfus makes Heidegger's dense text accessible without oversimplifying, and his contextualist interpretation — reading Heidegger against the background of social practice rather than isolated consciousness — is highly influential. The chapter on Being-toward-death provides an excellent critical entry point.

**Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus***
The primary source for the Epicurean view. The Long and Sedley volume (*The Hellenistic Philosophers*) is the best scholarly edition, with Greek text, translation, and philosophical commentary. Essential to read the original, even if brief, before engaging the secondary literature.

**Feit, "The Time of Death's Misfortune" (2002)**
The clearest and most systematic treatment of the location problem, examining each candidate time with care. Feit defends a version of the ante-mortem harm view and provides a useful taxonomy of the options. A good starting point for understanding the philosophical difficulty.

**Feldman, "Some Puzzles About the Evil of Death" (1991)**
Important paper that develops the deprivation account within a precise framework of "world-indexed" welfare comparisons. Feldman introduces the notion of "flourishing" as the relevant comparator and addresses the problem of timing. Technical but rewarding.

**Fischer, *Our Stories* (2009)**
Collection of essays on life, death, and free will by the most systematic contemporary defender of the view that an immortal life could be worth living. The essays on Williams are clear, fair, and philosophically sharp. Fischer's responses to Williams are the best developed in the literature.

**Heidegger, *Being and Time*, §§46–53**
The primary source for Heidegger's analysis of death. The Macquarrie and Robinson translation is the standard scholarly English version; Stambaugh's 2010 translation (SUNY Press) is more recent and sometimes clearer. Sections 46–53 can be read without the full context of the book, though Division I provides essential background.

**Nagel, "Death" in *Mortal Questions* (1979)**
Short, beautifully written, and enormously influential. The clearest statement of the deprivation account and the best treatment of the Lucretian symmetry argument. Essential first reading in the philosophy of death.

**Parfit, *Reasons and Persons*, Part III (1984)**
Parfit's treatment of personal identity and what matters in survival is indispensable background for the philosophy of death. Part III argues that personal identity is not what matters — psychological continuity is — and explores the implications for rationality, ethics, and the fear of death. Dense but rewarding.

**Pitcher, "The Misfortunes of the Dead" (1984)**
Short, influential paper defending the possibility of posthumous harm. Pitcher argues that the deceased's ante-mortem interests can be frustrated posthumously, and that this constitutes a genuine harm to the person. A useful counterweight to the Epicurean existence requirement.

**Rosenbaum, "How to Be Dead and Not Care" (1986)**
The best contemporary defense of the Epicurean existence requirement. Rosenbaum argues that the deprivation account violates the principle that welfare attributions require an existing subject, and defends this principle against objections. Should be read alongside Bradley's critique.

**Williams, "The Makropulos Case" in *Problems of the Self* (1973)**
Probably the most important single paper in the contemporary philosophy of immortality. Williams's argument is elegant, surprising, and deeply connected to questions about personal identity and the structure of a worthwhile life. Essential reading for any serious engagement with the question of whether immortality is desirable.
