# Chapter 32 Important Concepts: Time, Death, and Mortality

---

**Ante-Mortem Harm**
The view that death's harm occurs before the person dies — that the living person is already harmed by the fact that they will soon lose access to future goods. This is one solution to the location problem: it locates the harm of death at a time when the subject exists. The difficulty is explaining in what sense a currently living person is harmed by an event that has not yet occurred.

**Authentic Being-toward-Death**
Heidegger's term for the mode of existence in which Dasein genuinely owns its finitude — acknowledges death as its ownmost, non-relational, and certain possibility, rather than fleeing into the impersonal reassurances of "the they." Authentic Being-toward-death is not morbid preoccupation with dying but an honest orientation toward one's finitude that, paradoxically, frees one for genuine engagement with present possibilities.

**Categorical Desires**
Bernard Williams's term for desires that provide reasons to go on living, as opposed to desires that presuppose one is already alive. Williams argues that categorical desires are constitutively finite: they are tied to particular projects, relationships, and forms of engagement that can be satisfied or exhausted. The finitude of categorical desires is central to his argument that immortality would eventually become intolerable.

**Comparative Harm**
A harm defined by comparison with a counterfactual rather than by anything intrinsically bad about the actual situation. The deprivation account of death analyzes it as a comparative harm: the person who dies is worse off than they would have been if they had lived longer. Comparative harm does not require the subject to experience anything bad; it requires only that their actual welfare fall short of a possible better outcome.

**Dasein**
Heidegger's term for the kind of being that humans are — "being-there," a being for whom its own being is an issue. Dasein is characterized by care (Sorge) in its three temporal dimensions: thrownness (situatedness in a past), projection (orientation toward future possibilities), and fallenness (immersion in present concerns). Dasein's fundamental character includes Being-toward-death as a structural feature of its temporal existence.

**Deprivation Account**
The view, associated with Nagel (1979), that death is bad for a person because it deprives them of goods — experiences, relationships, achievements — they would otherwise have enjoyed. On this account, death is a *comparative* harm: the deceased is worse off than they would have been if they had lived longer. The deprivation account does not require the deceased to experience anything bad; the harm consists in what is excluded, not in what is undergone.

**Epicurean Argument**
The argument, originating with Epicurus, that death cannot harm the person who dies because harm requires an experiencing subject and the dead have no experience. "Death is nothing to us; for what has been dissolved has no sensation, and what has no sensation is nothing to us." This argument depends on the experiential account of welfare: what is good or bad for a person is constituted entirely by their experiences.

**Existence Requirement**
The principle, defended by Rosenbaum (1986), that a state of affairs can be good or bad for a person only if that person exists at the time. The existence requirement is the Epicurean's main weapon against the deprivation account: if the deceased no longer exists, they cannot be harmed, and there is no subject of the deprivation. Critics argue that this principle is too strong and excludes genuine cases of posthumous harm.

**Experiential Account of Welfare**
The view that what is good or bad for a person is constituted entirely by their experiences — by how things feel, what they undergo, what they consciously encounter. On the experiential account, only what can be experienced can be good or bad for you. The Epicurean argument against fearing death depends on this account. It contrasts with objective list and desire-satisfaction accounts that allow non-experiential goods and bads.

**Location Problem**
The puzzle, arising from the deprivation account, of specifying when death is bad for the person who dies. The three candidate times are: (a) ante-mortem, when the subject exists but has not yet been harmed; (b) at the moment of death; (c) post-mortem, when the subject no longer exists. None seems fully satisfactory, and various proposals — ante-mortem harm, posthumous harm, timeless harm — have been advanced as solutions.

**Mortality**
The condition of being subject to death — of having an existence that will inevitably end. Mortality is not merely a biological fact but an existential one: the awareness of one's own mortality shapes the structure of a life, the urgency of projects, the depth of care for others. Both the analytic philosophy of death and Heidegger's phenomenology take mortality as philosophically central, though they analyze it in different terms.

**Posthumous Harm**
The view that events occurring after a person's death can harm that person — by frustrating their ante-mortem interests, violating their wishes, or damaging their reputation. The view is defended by Pitcher (1984) and Feinberg (1984). The subject of the posthumous harm is typically identified as the ante-mortem person, whose interests were formed before death and can be frustrated by posthumous events.

**Psychological Continuity**
Parfit's criterion for what matters in personal survival: the overlapping chains of memories, beliefs, desires, intentions, and character traits that connect stages of a psychological life. Parfit argues that psychological continuity, not strict numerical identity, is what matters in survival. Applied to the philosophy of death, this view implies that the question "would immortality be good for *me*?" is less important than "would an indefinitely extended psychologically connected life be worth living?"

**Symmetry Argument**
Lucretius's argument (*De Rerum Natura* III) that we should not fear post-mortem non-existence because we do not regard pre-natal non-existence as bad, and the two are structurally symmetric — both are periods of non-experience on either side of a life. Nagel's response is that the symmetry is illusory: post-mortem non-existence deprives an existing person of possible goods, while pre-natal non-existence does not.

**Tedium of Immortality**
Williams's phrase for the inevitable fate of an immortal person: the exhaustion of categorical desires and the resulting condition of boredom, emptiness, or loss of self. Williams argues that any immortal life would eventually reach this condition — either because the identity-constituting desires are exhausted, or because the person's identity dissolves through radical change. The phrase captures his central argument against the desirability of immortality.

**Timeless Harm**
One proposed solution to the location problem: death is bad for a person but its badness is not located at any particular time. It is simply a timeless fact that the person's life was worse than it could have been. Feldman (1991) explores this approach. The advantage is that it avoids the difficulty of locating the harm at a time when the subject either doesn't exist or hasn't yet been deprived. The difficulty is that it requires welfare attributions that lack temporal location, which is philosophically unusual.
