# Chapter 32 Important Researchers: Time, Death, and Mortality

---

**Ben Bradley (b. 1970)**
Contemporary philosopher at Syracuse University whose book *Well-Being and Death* (2009) is the most technically rigorous contemporary treatment of the badness of death. Bradley examines the deprivation account, the location problem, the existence requirement, and posthumous harm with exemplary clarity and precision. He defends a version of the deprivation account that addresses the main objections and provides the most complete philosophical framework currently available for thinking about death's harm. His work also includes important papers on well-being and moral status.

**Epicurus (341–270 BCE)**
Founder of the Epicurean school of philosophy and the originator of the most influential argument against fearing death. Epicurus's philosophy was systematic and practical: it aimed at *ataraxia* (freedom from disturbance) through a combination of philosophical understanding and chosen social arrangements (the Garden community). His argument that death is nothing to us — because the dead have no sensation, and sensation is the ground of all good and ill — is one of the most discussed arguments in the history of philosophy. The *Letter to Menoeceus* is the most accessible primary source.

**John Martin Fischer (b. 1952)**
Dual presence in this chapter, Fischer contributes both to the free will debate (Chapter 31) and to the philosophy of death and immortality. His *Our Stories: Essays on Life, Death, and Free Will* (2009) contains the most developed responses to Williams's Makropulos argument. Fischer defends the view that an immortal life of varied recurring pleasures and engagements could be genuinely worth living. He has also written on the relationship between death, mortality, and meaning in life, bringing a rare combination of technical precision and genuine philosophical depth.

**Martin Heidegger (1889–1976)**
The most original and arguably the most influential philosopher of the twentieth century in the continental tradition, Heidegger's *Being and Time* (1927) transformed the philosophical analysis of temporality, existence, and death. His concepts of Being-toward-death, authenticity, Dasein, and temporal care have influenced philosophy, theology, literary theory, and psychiatry. Despite his enormous influence, Heidegger remains controversial both for the difficulty of his prose and for his political activities (his membership in the Nazi party from 1933 to 1945 and his failure to repudiate these activities after the war). These biographical facts do not invalidate his philosophical contributions but require that they be received with critical awareness.

**Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99–55 BCE)**
Roman Epicurean poet whose *De Rerum Natura* ("On the Nature of Things") is a philosophical masterpiece in verse. Books III and IV contain Lucretius's most famous philosophical arguments, including the symmetry argument against fearing death. Lucretius extended and poeticized Epicurean philosophy, giving its arguments a lyrical power they would not have had in purely prose form. The *De Rerum Natura* is one of the few texts from antiquity that conveys both the content and the spirit of the philosophical tradition it represents.

**Thomas Nagel (b. 1937)**
Philosopher at New York University whose work has ranged across ethics, philosophy of mind, and the philosophy of death. His essay "Death" in *Mortal Questions* (1979) is the most widely read and most influential statement of the deprivation account of death's badness, and his response to Lucretius's symmetry argument is a model of philosophical precision. Nagel has also written importantly on the nature of consciousness, the objectivity of ethics, and the relationship between the objective and subjective perspectives — themes that intersect with the philosophy of death in his account of what makes death bad for this particular person.

**Derek Parfit (1942–2017)**
One of the most original philosophers of the twentieth century, Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) is a landmark work in ethics and personal identity. His argument that personal identity is not what matters in survival — that psychological continuity is what we actually care about — has profound implications for the philosophy of death, the evaluation of immortality, and our ethical obligations to future generations. Parfit was unusual among philosophers in being simultaneously technically rigorous and genuinely concerned with the practical and existential implications of his conclusions. His later work, *On What Matters* (2011), attempts a grand unification of moral theories.

**Bernard Williams (1929–2003)**
One of the finest philosophical stylists of the twentieth century, Williams made major contributions to ethics, philosophy of mind, and the philosophy of action. His essay "The Makropulos Case" in *Problems of the Self* (1973) is the definitive statement of the view that immortality would be intolerable, and it has generated a cottage industry of responses. Williams was consistently suspicious of philosophical arguments that were too neat — that avoided the complexity and particularity of actual human values in favor of elegant theoretical simplicity. His work on integrity, moral luck, and the limits of ethical theory reflect this same sensibility.

**Stephen Rosenbaum**
Contemporary philosopher whose papers in the 1980s and 1990s provided the most systematic contemporary defense of the Epicurean position on death. Rosenbaum's formulation of the existence requirement — the principle that welfare attributions require an existing subject — is the standard formulation against which the deprivation account must defend itself. His papers "How to Be Dead and Not Care" (1986) and "The Symmetry Argument" (1989) remain essential reading for anyone working in the philosophy of death.
