# Chapter 32: Time, Death, and Mortality

---

## Chapter Introduction

No feature of our temporal existence is more certain or more consequential than the fact that we will die. We are beings who exist for a limited time — who come into existence, develop and change through time, and then cease to exist. This is not merely a biological fact; it is a constitutive feature of the kind of beings we are. Our projects, our relationships, our sense of urgency and value are all shaped by the awareness that time is finite. The philosophy of death is, in this sense, a philosophy of time as it applies to the structure of a life.

Yet the philosophical analysis of death is extraordinarily difficult. The central puzzle is this: death seems bad — we fear it, we mourn the dead, we treat the prolongation of life as a basic good. But if we take seriously the claim that experience is what matters, then death, which involves no experience at all, seems incapable of being bad *for the person who dies*. After all, once you are dead, there is no subject of harm. You are not suffering; you are not being deprived; you simply are not. Epicurus saw this clearly and concluded that death is not an evil. The difficulty is that this conclusion, though logically compelling, seems to miss something important.

This chapter traces the main lines of the philosophical debate. Section 32.1 examines the Epicurean challenge in both its formulations: the argument from the absence of experience (Epicurus himself) and the symmetry argument from pre-natal non-existence (Lucretius). Section 32.2 develops the "deprivation account" of death's badness — the view that death is bad not because it involves any harm to the dead person, but because it deprives them of future goods — and examines the notorious "location problem" this account generates: *when* is death bad, if the subject no longer exists to be harmed?

Section 32.3 returns to Epicurean territory with a sharper focus: the "existence requirement" for welfare and whether it can be defended against the deprivation account. Section 32.4 turns to Heidegger's phenomenological alternative, which reframes the question entirely: death, for Heidegger, is not primarily a future event but a structural feature of existence, a possibility that shapes and orients everything we do. Section 32.5 examines the other side of the temporal coin: if mortality is bad, is immortality good? Bernard Williams's celebrated argument that immortality would be intolerable tedium challenges this assumption, and responses to Williams force a deep examination of what we value about our lives and why.

Throughout this chapter, the connection to the philosophy of time is more than incidental. Whether death is bad, and in what sense, depends on questions about the relationship between past, present, and future in the constitution of a human good; about the conditions under which welfare attributions are possible; and about the nature of personal identity across time.
