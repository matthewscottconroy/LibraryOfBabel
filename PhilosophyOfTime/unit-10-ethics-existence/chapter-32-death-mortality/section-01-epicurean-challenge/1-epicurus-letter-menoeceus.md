# 32.1.1 Epicurus and the Argument from Non-Existence

---

## The Text

The central passage is brief and famous. In the *Letter to Menoeceus*, Epicurus writes: "Death is nothing to us; for what has been dissolved has no sensation, and what has no sensation is nothing to us" (Long and Sedley 1987: §24). This statement is the conclusion of a therapeutic argument, not merely a metaphysical observation. Epicurus is telling Menoeceus — and through him, all of us — that the fear of death is based on a philosophical error, and that understanding the error dissolves the fear.

The *Letter to Menoeceus* was not a scholarly treatise but a practical guide to philosophical living. It survives in full in Diogenes Laertius's *Lives of the Eminent Philosophers* (X.122–135), and its influence on subsequent Hellenistic and Roman thought was enormous. The passage about death is its most famous section, but it needs to be read in context: Epicurus is articulating a whole account of what makes life good (pleasure, understood as freedom from pain and disturbance) and what can genuinely threaten that good.

## The Argument Reconstructed

The argument has the following structure:

(P1) We can only be harmed when we exist — harm requires a subject that is experiencing or undergoing something.
(P2) When we are dead, we do not exist (as experiencers, as subjects of welfare).
(C) Therefore, being dead cannot harm us.

The further step: if death cannot harm us, and our fear of death is a fear of being harmed by death, then the fear of death is groundless. We should no more fear death than fear our pre-natal non-existence.

This argument is not a disguised form of denial. Epicurus does not deny that death is real, or that it terminates your existence. He accepts both. His claim is that the proper response to this fact, once understood, is equanimity — not because death doesn't matter, but because the mattering of anything requires a subject, and death removes the subject.

## The Experiential Account of Welfare

The argument depends on a particular account of welfare: what is good or bad for a person is constituted by their experiences. If you are experiencing nothing — as the dead do not — then nothing is good or bad for you. This is the *experiential account* of welfare, and it is broadly plausible: pain is bad because it hurts; pleasure is good because it feels good. What cannot be experienced cannot be bad or good.

This account connects to Epicurus's broader hedonism: pleasure is the sole intrinsic good, and pain is the sole intrinsic evil. If death involves neither pain nor pleasure — if it involves no experience at all — then it is neither intrinsically bad nor intrinsically good. It is nothing.

## The Appeal of the Argument

There is genuine wisdom here, and it deserves to be acknowledged before we criticize it. One of the most pervasive forms of anxiety about death involves imagining what it will be like *to be dead* — imagining oneself in a darkened, silent void, cut off from light and company. But this picture is incoherent: it imagines a subject experiencing a state of non-experience, which is a contradiction. The dead do not experience anything, including the absence of experience. Epicurus's argument diagnoses this incoherence and invites us to see that the horrible scenario we fear is not a coherent scenario at all.

There is also something liberating in the Epicurean view. If death is genuinely nothing to us, then the appropriate emotional response to our finitude is neither anguish nor frantic avoidance, but a fuller engagement with the life we actually have. Epicurus's practical goal — *ataraxia*, tranquil enjoyment of present goods — is well served by the theoretical argument.

## The Limits of the Argument

Yet most people find the Epicurean conclusion unconvincing, and this is not merely a failure of philosophical sophistication. The dissatisfaction runs deep and points to a real limitation in the argument.

The experiential account of welfare — that only what can be experienced can be good or bad for you — is controversial. There are apparent counterexamples that many find compelling. If your spouse is unfaithful while you are on a long voyage, unaware of the betrayal, are you not harmed by the betrayal even while you experience nothing but contentment? Most people's intuition is: yes, the betrayal harms you, even if you never know about it. Your relationship, your trust, your life-project of being faithful to a faithful partner — these are damaged, even though your experience registers nothing.

This suggests that welfare is not exhausted by experience. Some things are bad for you in virtue of what they *do to* your life, not merely in virtue of how they *feel*. If this is right, then Epicurus's first premise — that we can be harmed only by what we experience — is too strong.

There is also a question about the constituency of the "self" that is supposedly unaffected by death. Epicurus identifies the self with the experiencing subject. But if personal identity extends through time — if what I care about is not merely what I am experiencing now but the whole trajectory of my life, including its future — then death's termination of that trajectory is a harm to a *temporally extended* self, even if the moment-to-moment experiencing self is unaffected.

## The Timing and the Subject

Perhaps the deepest difficulty with Epicurus's argument concerns the relationship between harm and time. Even granting that the *dead* person cannot be harmed (since they no longer exist), there remains a question about whether the *living* person, who has expectations and projects about a future that death will eliminate, is not harmed by the termination of those expectations. The harm might be suffered now — in the form of potential unrealized — even before death occurs.
