# 32.1.2 Lucretius and the Symmetry Argument

---

## The Argument from *De Rerum Natura*

Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99–55 BCE) was an Epicurean poet whose *De Rerum Natura* is one of the most sustained philosophical poems in Latin literature. In Book III, lines 830–842, he advances what has come to be known as the "symmetry argument" — a striking extension of the Epicurean case against fearing death. Here is the key passage in Bailey's translation:

"Look back again to see how the past eternity of time before our birth is nothing to us. This is a mirror which nature holds up, showing us the time to come after our death. Is there anything terrible there? Does anything appear sad? Is it not calmer than any sleep?" (*De Rerum Natura* III.972–977, paraphrase).

The argument is this. Before you were born, you did not exist. That pre-natal period of non-existence is not something you regard as bad. You do not look back on the aeons before your birth with regret or anguish. But the post-mortem period of non-existence is structurally identical: it is a period of non-experience, like the pre-natal period, just located on the other side of your life. If the pre-natal non-existence was not bad for you, why should the post-mortem non-existence be bad for you?

The argument can be stated formally:

(P1) Pre-natal non-existence is not bad for us.
(P2) Pre-natal non-existence and post-mortem non-existence are structurally symmetric — both are periods of non-experience, differing only in their temporal location relative to our lives.
(P3) If two situations are structurally symmetric with respect to welfare, they should be evaluated the same way.
(C) Post-mortem non-existence is not bad for us.

The conclusion is the Epicurean one, reached by a different route. Instead of arguing that death cannot harm us because we no longer exist, Lucretius argues by analogy: since a structurally similar non-existence before our birth is not bad, neither is the non-existence after death.

## The Argument's Strengths

The symmetry argument has an appealing simplicity. It invites us to notice that our asymmetric attitude toward pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence is not obviously rational. We do not grieve that we were not alive during the Renaissance, experiencing its art, its scholarship, its drama. We do not mourn the friendship of Leonardo da Vinci. Yet if we were told that we would die in twenty years, before some future cultural flourishing, we would regard this as a serious loss. Why the asymmetry?

The only apparent difference is the temporal direction: post-mortem non-existence lies in the *future*, while pre-natal non-existence lies in the *past*. But why should the temporal direction of a non-existence make it better or worse? We do not regard spatial distance as making things better or worse: a friend who lives far away is not better off than a friend who lives nearby, merely because of the direction in which they are located. Why should temporal location matter?

## Nagel's Challenge: The Asymmetry of Personal Time

Thomas Nagel (1979: 3–10) offered the most compelling challenge to the symmetry argument. He argued that there is a genuine and rational asymmetry between prenatal and posthumous non-existence that the Lucretian argument ignores.

The key point concerns the structure of personal time — the timeline of a particular person's existence. A person's life begins at birth (or thereabouts) and proceeds forward. There is a fact about when a person *starts* to exist; their existence has a beginning. But their existence can be extended forward, in principle, by surviving longer. The person who dies at 60 could have survived to 80; the goods of those extra years were available to them in principle. This is why the prospective non-existence of death feels like a loss: it is the termination of a life that could have continued, depriving the person of goods they could otherwise have enjoyed.

Pre-natal non-existence, by contrast, is not the deprivation of goods that could otherwise have been enjoyed. A person who was born in 1990 could not have had the experiences of 1960 — they were not yet a person, and there was no one to enjoy those experiences. The pre-natal period is not a period of *missed* goods; it is simply the period before the person existed. You cannot miss what was impossible for you to have had.

The asymmetry, then, is this: post-mortem non-existence deprives an existing person of goods they could otherwise have had; pre-natal non-existence does not deprive a person of anything, because the person did not yet exist to be deprived. The two non-existences are not symmetric after all.

## Parfit's Response and the Puzzle of Non-Identity

Parfit (1984: 353–358) offered a different analysis of the asymmetry. He accepted that there is an asymmetry between the two non-existences, but argued that its explanation is different from Nagel's. We care about futures more than pasts, Parfit suggested, because of a fundamental feature of our psychology: we have a "bias toward the future." We are more concerned about future pains and future pleasures than about equal pains and pleasures already in the past, even setting aside knowledge and the possibility of preparation.

This bias may be rational or it may not. Parfit himself was uncertain. But even if it is an irrational bias, it might explain *why* we fear death and not pre-natal non-existence without showing that the fear is *justified*. The symmetry argument, if this reading is correct, identifies a genuine irrationality in our attitudes even if it does not show that all concern about death is irrational.

A third possibility, defended by Brueckner and Fischer (1986: 213–223), is that the asymmetry is explained by the different relations we stand in to goods on each side of our lives. A good that awaits in the future is one we can *anticipate* and that will be *added to* our life if we survive; a good in the past that occurred before our birth is not one that was added to our life and cannot be anticipated. This asymmetry in our temporal relationship to goods explains and justifies different evaluative responses.

## The Deeper Issue

What makes the symmetry argument philosophically important is not merely its conclusion about whether to fear death. It forces us to confront a deep question about the temporal structure of value. Is value time-neutral — does it not matter *when* in time a good occurs, relative to a life? Or does the location of goods relative to a person's existence and temporal perspective make a difference?

The Epicurean tradition assumes that welfare is exhausted by what can be experienced, and that temporal location is irrelevant beyond that. Critics of the symmetry argument suggest that personal time — the sequence of events that constitutes a particular life — has a structure that makes temporal direction relevant to welfare in ways that go beyond experience. Whether this is so, and how precisely to characterize it, is one of the deepest questions in the philosophy of death.
