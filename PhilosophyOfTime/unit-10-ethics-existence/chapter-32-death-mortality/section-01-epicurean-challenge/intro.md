# Section 32.1: The Epicurean Challenge

---

## Section Introduction

Epicurus of Samos (341–270 BCE) founded one of the most practically oriented philosophies of antiquity: a systematic account of how to live well, organized around the goal of *ataraxia* — tranquility, freedom from disturbance. Among the chief sources of disturbance he identified was the fear of death. His philosophical project was partly therapeutic: to show that this fear, though understandable, is rationally groundless, and that those who understand what death is have no reason to fear it.

The therapeutic argument comes in two parts. Epicurus himself provided the first: since death is the cessation of all experience, and we can only be harmed when we exist to be harmed, death cannot harm us. His follower Lucretius provided a striking supplement: since we existed before birth and did not regard our pre-natal non-existence as bad, there is no asymmetry that would make post-mortem non-existence bad either. Both arguments have a kind of logical elegance that has never quite been refuted — yet they have never quite convinced most people either. The task of this section is to understand why.
