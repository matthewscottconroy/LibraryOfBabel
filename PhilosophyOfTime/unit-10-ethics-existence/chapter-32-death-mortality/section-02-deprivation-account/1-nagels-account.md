# 32.2.1 Nagel's Deprivation Account

---

## The Central Claim

Thomas Nagel's essay "Death," published in *Mortal Questions* (1979: 1–10), is the most philosophically precise and widely influential formulation of the view that death is bad for the person who dies. Nagel does not attempt to refute the Epicurean argument by showing that it is logically invalid. Instead, he argues that the argument rests on assumptions about the nature of welfare that are false, and that once these assumptions are corrected, the badness of death can be understood without supposing that the dead person must experience something bad.

Nagel's central claim is this: "Death is an evil because it brings to an end all the good things that we have while alive, including all the experiences, activities, and projects that constitute a worthwhile human life" (1979: 2). What makes death bad is not anything it *involves* — no pain, no distress, no deprivation as an experienced state — but what it *excludes*: the goods that life would otherwise have contained.

This is a comparative harm. To assess whether someone has been harmed by death, we compare the life they actually had with the life they would have had if they had not died (at that time). If the counterfactual life would have gone better — if it would have contained more goods — then death harmed the person by cutting off access to those goods. The harm is real even though it is not experienced.

## The Contrast with Other Evils

Nagel draws a crucial contrast. Most evils harm a person by *affecting* them — by causing them to suffer, to be frustrated, to have their goals thwarted. These evils are bad in virtue of what they do to the person's experienced life. Death is different: it is bad not because of what it does to the experienced life but because of what it *eliminates*. The person who dies does not undergo the evil; the evil is the absence of ongoing life.

This means death belongs to the category of *comparative harms* — harms defined by comparison with a counterfactual rather than by anything intrinsically bad about the actual situation. Consider: if you are offered a job that pays $50,000 but are simultaneously offered a better job that pays $100,000, and through negligence you end up with the worse job, you have been harmed even though the $50,000 job is perfectly fine. The harm is comparative: you are worse off than you would have been. Death is like this: the actual condition (non-existence) is not bad as such, but it is worse than the counterfactual condition (continued life with its goods).

## Goods That Can Be Deprived

Nagel emphasizes that what death deprives us of is a rich complex of goods, not merely pleasurable experiences. These include:

- *Experiential goods*: the pleasures, satisfactions, and joys of continued life
- *Relational goods*: ongoing friendships, love relationships, family connections
- *Achievement goods*: the completion of projects, goals, and ambitions
- *Narrative goods*: the continued unfolding of a life-story with meaning and coherence

This pluralism about the goods of life is important. It means that death deprives us of more than Epicurean hedonism acknowledges. Even if we grant that the dead cannot experience, the goods that a living person would have enjoyed are not limited to experiences: they include objective achievements, relational states, and the continuation of a meaningful life-narrative — all of which have value beyond their experiential component.

## The Experiential Account Rejected

The deprivation account implicitly rejects the experiential account of welfare that underpins Epicurus's argument. On the experiential account, what is good or bad for you is constituted entirely by your experiences: you are well off when your experiences are predominantly pleasant, poorly off when they are predominantly painful, and unaffected by whatever happens when you are not experiencing anything.

Nagel's account requires instead that welfare can be assessed relationally — in terms of the goods one has access to, enjoys, or is connected to, even when these goods are not purely experiential. This is an *objective list* or *desire-satisfaction* conception of welfare extended to include the goods of life as a whole. Something can be bad for you even when you have no experience of its badness; the betrayal by a faithless friend harms you even if you never know about it; death deprives you of goods even though you are not there to experience the deprivation.

## Death Compared with Other Bad Non-Events

Nagel acknowledges that the deprivation account entails something initially strange: "the evil of death is not defined in terms of a state of the person, but in terms of what the person is deprived of" (1979: 4). This leads to a question: if death is bad because it deprives us of future goods, is the failure to be born bad for the would-be person? Are there non-existent people who are "deprived" of the goods of life?

Nagel's answer is carefully qualified. The deprivation account applies to the death of *existing persons* — people who have come into existence and whose lives can be compared with the counterfactual of longer life. The non-existent potential person is not a subject of welfare and cannot be deprived in the relevant sense. This restriction distinguishes death from non-existence and preserves the asymmetry that Nagel defended against Lucretius.

## Challenges to the Deprivation Account

Nagel's account has faced three main challenges. First, the Epicurean might simply insist on the experiential account of welfare: if there is no subject to experience the deprivation, there is no deprivation in the relevant sense. Nagel is aware of this and regards the question of which account of welfare is correct as genuinely difficult; he ultimately finds the objective account more plausible, but does not claim to have proved it.

Second, there is a question about the credibility of the counterfactual. "The life the person would have had" is not always clear — people might have died at multiple possible times, and different deaths would have yielded different counterfactual lives. How bad death is depends on which counterfactual we use, and there may not be a unique, determinate answer (Bradley 2009: 32–44).

Third, and most pressing, there is the location problem: even granting that death is a comparative harm, the account must specify *when* that harm befalls the person. This problem is so serious that it deserves its own treatment.
