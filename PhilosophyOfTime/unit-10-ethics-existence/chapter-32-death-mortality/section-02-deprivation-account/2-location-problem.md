# 32.2.2 The Location Problem

---

## Stating the Problem

The deprivation account tells us *why* death is bad: it deprives us of future goods. But a natural and important follow-up question is: *when* is death bad? At what time does the harm of death befall the person? This is the "location problem," and it is more troubling than it might initially appear.

Neil Feit (2002: 359–384) and Ben Bradley (2009: 43–71) have provided the most detailed analyses. The problem can be stated as a trilemma. There appear to be three candidate times at which death could be bad for the person: (a) while the person is still alive; (b) at the moment of death; (c) after death, when the person no longer exists. But none of these seems satisfactory.

## The Three Candidate Times

**(a) Before death.** Could death be bad for you while you are still alive? There is something plausible here: the person who will die tomorrow is already deprived, in a prospective sense, of the goods of the day after tomorrow. They are in the situation of someone who will lose something valuable. But the deprivation account says death is bad because it *deprives* you of goods — and as long as you are alive, you have not yet been deprived. The goods of tomorrow are still potentially available. The badness seems to be prospective but not yet actual.

Moreover, if the harm of death falls while you are still alive, then the harm falls on *you* — a subject who exists and can be harmed. But this seems odd: the harm of death is typically thought to befall you in your dying, not in your living. If you are alive and healthy, it seems wrong to say you are currently being harmed by a death that will occur in thirty years.

**(b) At the moment of death.** Could the harm of death occur at the moment of dying? This is also problematic. At the moment of death, you may experience pain or distress (if the dying process is painful) — but those harms are the harms of the dying process, not of death itself. Death itself is the cessation of life, which is either instantaneous or a gradual process. If death is instantaneous, there is no moment of experience "in" death at which the harm can occur. If death is a process, the harm of the process is one thing, and the harm of death (the terminal non-existence) is another.

**(c) After death.** Could the harm of death occur after death, when you no longer exist? This is where the Epicurean insight cuts most sharply: after death, there is no subject. You do not exist to be harmed. If harm requires a subject, and the subject no longer exists, then posthumous harm seems incoherent.

Yet there are strong intuitions that posthumous events can affect a person's welfare. If a faithful friend betrays you after your death, spreading false rumors that destroy your reputation, it seems natural to say that this is bad for you — that something bad has happened *to* you. The "posthumous harm" view holds that welfare attributions can be true of a person relative to a time at which the person does not exist (Pitcher 1984: 183–203; Feinberg 1984: 79–95).

## Proposed Solutions

Several solutions have been proposed to the location problem.

**The ante-mortem harm solution.** Silverstein (1980: 403–424) argues that the harm of death occurs ante-mortem — before death. The person who is about to die is worse off than they would be if they were to continue living; this comparative disadvantage is a present harm, not a future one. The difficulty is specifying in what sense a living person is *currently* harmed by a future death. Silverstein invokes the concept of a "timeless" harm: the harm is real but not temporally located.

**The timeless harm solution.** Some philosophers argue that the harm of death has no temporal location. It is simply a fact that person P is worse off than they would have been had they not died — and this comparative fact is timelessly true, not true at any particular time. Feldman (1991: 217–234) explores this approach. The advantage is that it sidesteps the location problem by denying that harms must be temporally located. The disadvantage is that it seems to require an unusual metaphysics: typically, facts about what is bad for someone are tied to particular times.

**The posthumous harm solution.** Pitcher (1984) and Feinberg (1984) defend a form of posthumous harm on which events after death can retroactively determine that something was bad for the ante-mortem person. On this view, it is not that the dead person *now* has a bad experience, but that the ante-mortem person had interests — in reputation, in the success of projects, in the faithfulness of friends — and posthumous events can frustrate or satisfy these interests. The person "as they were before death" is the subject of the harm, even though the harm is constituted by posthumous events.

Bradley (2009: 55–71) argues that this solution has the best claim to being correct, but that it requires a careful account of how ante-mortem interests can be constituted as the right kind of subject for posthumous harm. The key is that the person's interests were real and located at the ante-mortem time, even if their frustration comes later.

## The Metaphysical Significance

The location problem is not merely a technical puzzle; it reveals something important about the metaphysics of welfare and time. Our ordinary concept of welfare assumes a subject who exists at the time of the harm. The deprivation account of death requires either relaxing this assumption (posthumous harm) or revising the ordinary concept (timeless harm, ante-mortem harm). None of these moves is cost-free.

There is a deeper point here about the relationship between welfare and temporal existence. Welfare is typically an instantaneous or time-indexed property: I am doing well at t if the conditions that constitute my welfare obtain at t. The deprivation account requires assessing welfare comparatively across possible histories — comparing the actual history (in which I die at t) with a counterfactual history (in which I live longer). This is a more complex, temporally extended form of welfare assessment, and it is not obvious how to embed it in a standard theory of welfare that takes time-indexing seriously.

The location problem is, in this sense, a problem in the intersection of the philosophy of death and the philosophy of time. How we answer it depends on what we take welfare to be, whether harms require subjects, and whether temporal location is an essential feature of welfare attributions. These are not easy questions — which is why, after several decades of careful work, the location problem remains genuinely open.
