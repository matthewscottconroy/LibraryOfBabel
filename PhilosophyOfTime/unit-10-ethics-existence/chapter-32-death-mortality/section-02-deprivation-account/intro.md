# Section 32.2: The Deprivation Account

---

## Section Introduction

If the Epicurean argument is correct, death is not bad for the person who dies. But this conclusion runs against a powerful and widely shared intuition: that dying young is a tragedy precisely because of what it cuts short — the projects unfinished, the relationships severed, the experiences never had. When we mourn a friend who died at thirty, we are not merely mourning our own loss; we are mourning something that happened *to them*. The deprivation account of death tries to capture this intuition with philosophical precision.

On the deprivation account, death is bad for a person in virtue of the goods it deprives them of — the experiences, relationships, and achievements they would have had if they had lived longer. Death is a comparative harm: the person who dies is worse off than they would otherwise have been, because the goods of the unlived life are goods that they, had they lived, would have enjoyed.

This section examines the most careful formulation of the deprivation account, associated with Nagel, and then confronts the notorious "location problem" it generates: even if death is bad in the deprivation sense, the account seems unable to specify *when* it is bad. This is not a merely technical difficulty — it forces a fundamental rethinking of the relationship between welfare, existence, and time.
