# 32.3.1 The Existence Requirement and the Epicurean Counterattack

---

## The Existence Requirement Stated

The Epicurean tradition's most systematic recent defender is Stephen Rosenbaum, whose papers in the 1980s and 1990s developed the "existence requirement" as a principled constraint on welfare attributions. The existence requirement holds:

**ER:** A state of affairs S is bad (or good) for person P at time t only if P exists at t.

This principle captures something deep about the ordinary concept of welfare. When we say that something is bad *for* someone, we seem to be attributing a property to a subject — saying that they are in a worse state, or that their interests are frustrated, or that they are worse off in some way. But attributing a property to a subject requires that the subject exists. There is no subject to be in a bad state if the person has ceased to exist.

Rosenbaum (1986: 219–234; 1989: 353–373) argues that the deprivation account violates ER. If death deprives a person of goods, the deprivation must occur at some time — but by the time death has occurred, the person no longer exists. And before death, the person has not yet been deprived. There is no time at which the deprivation occurs and the subject exists.

This is, in essence, a sharper version of the location problem. ER provides a principled reason for thinking that the location problem is not a technical puzzle to be solved by clever philosophy, but a fundamental refutation of the deprivation account. If there is no time at which P exists and is harmed, then P is not harmed.

## The Welfare Comparison Problem

A related difficulty concerns comparative welfare analyses. The deprivation account says that death is bad because P-who-died is worse off than P-who-would-have-lived-longer. This is a comparison between two counterparts: the actual P (who died) and a counterfactual P (who lived longer). But the basis of comparison seems to require that both counterparts exist at the time of comparison. The counterfactual P exists in the counterfactual scenario; the actual P does not exist after death.

Rosenbaum argues that comparisons of welfare require a subject of comparison, and that death removes the subject. He draws an analogy: we can say that a painting would have been more beautiful if the artist had added more detail, but we cannot say that the painting *suffers* from lacking that detail after the canvas is destroyed. The destroyed canvas has no properties that can be compared with a counterfactual intact canvas; the comparison requires something to be compared.

Similarly, after death there is no person who can be in a welfare state that is worse than some counterfactual welfare state. The comparison loses its subject.

## The Counter-Response: Relational Properties and Subjects

The deprivationist has several resources for responding to the existence requirement.

The most powerful response is that relational properties can be true of subjects even when the terms of the relation include non-existent things. Consider: Sherlock Holmes is *more famous* than most real detectives. This is a comparison involving a fictional character and real people; the comparison can be true even though Holmes does not exist. Or consider: Napoleon was *taller than the person who will become the most famous gymnast of 2150* — this comparison involves a person who does not yet exist. If relational comparisons can involve non-existent terms, then comparative welfare assessments might be possible for persons who do not exist at the time of assessment.

Bradley (2009: 68–87) develops this line of response carefully. The key is to distinguish between the *subject* of the welfare attribution and the *terms* of the comparison. The subject is the ante-mortem person — a fully real, existing individual with interests, projects, and a welfare level. The comparison is between the welfare level of that actual person and a counterfactual version of that person. The subject of the attribution exists; the counterfactual comparison partner does not need to exist at the same time.

On this view, what ER requires is that the *subject* of the harm exists, not that the subject exists at every time mentioned in the analysis. The ante-mortem person exists, and it is true of that person that they will be worse off (in terms of lifetime welfare) than they would have been had they not died. This is a harm to the ante-mortem person, and the ante-mortem person is the subject.

## Does the Existence Requirement Extend to All Cases?

A further difficulty for the Epicurean is that the existence requirement, applied consistently, seems to yield implausible conclusions in other cases.

Consider *ante-mortem interests*. You make a will, expressing your desire that your estate go to a particular charity. After your death, your executor violates your wishes and distributes the estate to other causes. Most people's intuition is that you have been wronged — your interests have been frustrated, even though you no longer exist to experience the frustration.

Consider *posthumous reputation*. You spend your life devoted to honesty and integrity; after your death, a false biographer claims you were a fraud. This seems bad for you, even though you no longer exist. The posthumous harm view, defended by Pitcher (1984) and Feinberg (1984), takes these intuitions at face value and argues that ante-mortem interests can be frustrated posthumously.

Rosenbaum (1989) is aware of these cases and argues that we should revise the intuitions rather than revise ER. He suggests that our language about posthumous interests is a kind of loose talk — a way of expressing the importance of the deceased person's wishes without committing to the claim that those wishes can literally be frustrated after death.

This response seems strained. The intuitions about posthumous harm are widely shared and deeply felt, and the philosophical work of Pitcher and Feinberg has articulated why they are not merely confused. Discarding ER in favor of the deprivation account, suitably refined to address the location problem, seems more philosophically defensible than insisting on ER in the face of counterexamples.

## The Stakes

The debate between the Epicurean and the deprivationist is not merely academic. If the Epicurean is right, then our fear of death is irrational, and much of what we regard as tragic — dying young, dying before completing important projects, dying before relationships reach their fullness — is a confusion. The appropriate response to mortality is equanimity, not grief or dread.

If the deprivationist is right, then the fear of death has a rational basis. Death really is a serious harm, not because of anything it involves, but because of what it excludes. This does not mean that death must be met with panic or despair; equanimity remains a possible and even admirable response. But it is equanimity in the face of something genuinely bad, not equanimity achieved by showing that the bad thing is nothing.

The philosophical difference matters for how we understand human life and the moral significance of keeping people alive. It matters for medical ethics, for the assessment of risk to life, and for the weighing of mortality against other goods. Philosophy of death is not a morbid intellectual game; it is a necessary component of a serious account of what matters and why.
