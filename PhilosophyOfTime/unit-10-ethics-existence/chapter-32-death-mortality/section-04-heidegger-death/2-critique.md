# 32.4.2 Criticisms of Heidegger's Analysis of Death

---

## The Main Objections

Heidegger's analysis of death is among the most discussed and most criticized in twentieth-century philosophy. The criticisms come from multiple directions — analytic philosophers who find the analysis confused, continental philosophers who find it incomplete, and social theorists who find it culturally specific. Understanding these criticisms is essential for assessing whether Heidegger's framework is a genuine contribution or an elaborate misdirection.

## The "Ownmost Possibility" Problem

The most direct criticism concerns Heidegger's characterization of death as Dasein's "ownmost possibility." As Nagel (1979: 3) notes, being dead is not something one can *undergo* — death is the cessation of all undergoing. To call death a "possibility" seems to miscategorize it: possibilities are things that might happen, states that might be realized, outcomes that might come about. But if death is realized — if it "comes true" — there is no experiencer left to be in the state of death. Death does not describe a state you can be in; it describes the elimination of all your states.

Heidegger would respond that this objection misunderstands the analysis. He is not claiming that death is a possibility in the sense of a possible future experience. He is claiming that the *possibility of the impossibility of existence* — the structural fact that one's existence is always potentially finished — is a constitutive feature of Dasein's way of being. The "possibility" he analyzes is not a possible state of the deceased but a feature of the living person's existential structure: the fact that you could always already be about to not-be.

But this response, while clarificatory, creates a different problem. If being-toward-death is about the structure of the living person's relationship to their own finitude, then Heidegger's analysis seems to be an analysis of *awareness of mortality* rather than of death itself. The claim that authentic existence requires confronting one's own finitude is interesting and plausible. But it is not obviously an analysis of *what death is* — it is an analysis of *what it is like to be a mortal being who knows it*.

## The Cultural Specificity Objection

Dreyfus (1991: 306–319) raises a different kind of criticism. Heidegger's characterization of inauthentic Being-toward-death — the "one dies" of das Man — is a description of how death is handled in a particular cultural milieu: the bourgeois German society of the early twentieth century, in which death was increasingly medicalized, institutionalized, and removed from everyday life. The claim that authentic Being-toward-death requires individualized, anxiety-laden confrontation with one's own mortality reflects specific cultural values — Protestant Christian emphases on individual responsibility and personal salvation, and modern existentialist valorization of authenticity as individuation.

In many cultures, death is faced collectively rather than individually; communal mourning practices, ancestral reverence, and shared narratives about death serve important social and psychological functions that Heidegger's individualism ignores or dismisses as "fleeing." The claim that authentic death must be faced alone, as one's "ownmost" possibility, reflects a specific cultural ontology rather than a universal structure of human existence.

This is a serious objection. If Heidegger's analysis is culturally parochial — if different cultures have different, equally valid ways of relating to death — then the claim that his analysis reveals the fundamental existential structure of Being-toward-death is overblown. He may be articulating one culturally specific mode of authentic existence, not the universal form.

## The Neglect of Other Finitudes

A related criticism is that Heidegger's exclusive focus on death as the "limit" of existence ignores other equally important forms of human finitude. Birth is also a "limit" of Dasein — we are always already thrown into a particular historical, cultural, and linguistic situation that we did not choose. Illness, disability, and cognitive limitation are forms of finitude that shape existence as profoundly as death. Relationships of dependency — on caregivers, on language, on community — constitute the structure of existence in ways that Heidegger's heroic individualism underplays.

Young (1992: 57–83) and Olkowski (1999: 122–138) have argued from feminist perspectives that Heidegger's analysis reflects a fantasy of self-sufficient individuality that ignores the constitutive role of embodied dependency and care relationships. The individual confronting death "alone," owning their death as their ownmost possibility, is an idealization that overlooks the fundamentally relational character of human existence.

Heidegger is not unaware of the relational dimension — being-with (Mitsein) is a fundamental structure of Dasein — but he does not integrate it fully into his analysis of death. The relationship between authentic being-toward-death and authentic being-with-others remains underdeveloped.

## The Relationship to Analytic Philosophy

How should analytic philosophers of death respond to Heidegger? There are two tempting but wrong responses. The first is to dismiss Heidegger as producing pseudo-profound confusion — a technically elaborate way of saying very little. This response is common but unfair; Heidegger is addressing genuine questions with genuine depth.

The second tempting response is to treat Heidegger as simply "playing a different language game" — as using terms like "authentic" and "ownmost possibility" in a specialized way that can be understood only within his framework, and therefore neither confirmed nor refuted by arguments from outside. This response is also inadequate, because it insulates Heidegger's claims from rational assessment.

The right response is to recognize that Heidegger and the analytic tradition are asking related but distinct questions. The analytic debate asks: *is death bad, and in what sense?* Heidegger asks: *what is the structure of a life lived in awareness of its own finitude?* These questions are not independent — the analytic claim that death is bad by deprivation is partly a claim about why awareness of mortality is appropriate — but they are distinct, and each tradition has insights the other lacks.

Heidegger's analysis captures something important that the deprivation account does not: the way in which death is not merely a future event but a present feature of existence that shapes how we experience time, projects, and relationships. The urgency of our commitments, the depth of our care for projects that will outlast us, the poignancy of beauty — all of these are inflected by the awareness of finitude. This is a phenomenological observation of genuine depth, even if the philosophical apparatus Heidegger deploys to articulate it is sometimes obscure.
