# 32.5.1 Williams and the Makropulos Case

---

## The Opera and the Argument

Bernard Williams (1929–2003) opens his essay "The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality" (*Problems of the Self*, 1973: 82–100) with the plot of Janáček's opera of the same name. Emilia Marty is a woman who, at the age of 42, was given an elixir of immortality by her father, a physician at the court of Rudolf II. The opera takes place 300 years later, when she is still 42 in appearance but has lived for more than three centuries. She is cold, bored, hollow — incapable of genuine care, connection, or enthusiasm. She eventually chooses death, and her final gesture is to offer the formula for the elixir to a younger woman, who burns it.

Williams uses this literary case as a lens for a philosophical argument. He wants to know: would immortality be a good thing? His answer is that it would not — that at some point, an immortal life would cease to be a life worth living, and would become either intolerable tedium or something that is no longer *you*.

## Categorical Desires and the Conditions for Life

Williams's central concept is the *categorical desire* — a desire that provides one with reasons to go on living, as opposed to a *conditional desire*, which provides a reason to do something given that you are alive. The desire to eat breakfast is conditional: given that you are alive and hungry, you want to eat. The desire to finish your novel, to see your children grow up, to understand the mind — these are categorical: they are reasons to be alive, not merely things to do with your aliveness.

Williams argues that categorical desires are the foundation of what makes life worth living. They are the desires that give life its forward momentum, its projects, its point. Without categorical desires, you would have no reason to get out of bed, no sense of purpose, no investment in the future. Life would be mere persistence, not genuine living.

The argument then proceeds: categorical desires are inherently finite. They are tied to particular projects, particular relationships, particular forms of engagement with the world that have their own natural rhythms and endpoints. A desire to write a great novel can, in principle, be satisfied; a desire to raise this child reaches its natural conclusion; a desire to understand the cosmos can, in time, exhaust its available territory. For any finite set of categorical desires, there is a point at which they are satisfied, exhausted, or no longer tenable.

What happens next? Williams considers two possibilities.

**Option A:** The immortal person develops new categorical desires as old ones are satisfied. But Williams argues that this option has a cost for personal identity. If your new desires are continuous with your old ones — if you develop them from within the same character, the same values, the same personality — then you will eventually exhaust all the variations available to someone of your character. You will have *done it all*, many times over, and there will be nothing genuinely new for someone with your particular constitution to desire.

**Option B:** The immortal person changes so radically, developing desires and a character so different from their original self, that they are genuinely no longer the same person. A person who has lived for a thousand years, with radically altered values and character, may bear only the most tenuous connection to the original person. But if this is so, then what was preserved by immortality is not *you* — it is a series of persons connected by a chain of psychological continuity but so attenuated as to be, for all practical purposes, different people.

Williams concludes that immortality, for *you* — for the person you actually are — is not possible without one of these costs. Either you remain genuinely *you* and eventually exhaust your categorical desires, or you persist indefinitely but cease to be you in any meaningful sense.

## The Constitutive Role of Finitude

There is a deeper point behind the categorical-desires argument. Williams suggests that our categorical desires are not merely contingently finite; they are *constitutively* tied to our finite nature. We want to finish the novel, to be present for our children's lives, to see the project through — and these desires have their character partly in virtue of their relationship to finite time. The desire to finish the novel is not just "I want to complete a task"; it is "I want to complete a task before it is too late." The urgency, the poignancy, the depth of investment in categorical desires is partly a function of the finitude against which they are framed.

This is a Heideggerian point dressed in analytic clothing. Williams is saying, in effect, that mortality is not merely a contingent limitation on a life that could have been infinitely good; it is partially constitutive of the kind of good that life involves. A life framed by finitude has a character — urgency, care, depth — that could not be preserved in a life without finitude.

## The Identity Condition

Williams adds a condition that makes his argument more precise: for immortality to be *your* immortality — for it to be genuinely you who lives forever — there must be some set of character-defining traits that persist throughout the immortal life. He calls these "categorical commitments" or identity-defining desires. If the immortal person is to remain you, they must retain the features that constitute your personal identity.

But here the problem reasserts itself. If these identity-defining features persist, they will eventually be exhausted of their interest or frustrated in their realization. If they do not persist, the immortal person is not you. Williams sees no escape: immortality is either tedious or non-personal.
