# 32.5.2 Responses to Williams on Immortality

---

## Fischer's Challenge

John Martin Fischer (2009: 79–113) has developed the most sustained and philosophically careful response to Williams. Fischer argues that Williams's argument rests on an empirical assumption that is not obviously correct — the assumption that categorical desires are necessarily exhaustible. Fischer contends that for many people, certain types of categorical desires are not exhaustible: specifically, what he calls *recurring desires* — desires for experiences and activities that can be repeated indefinitely without losing their value.

Consider the pleasure of good music. Someone who deeply loves music does not simply satisfy this desire once, by hearing some music, and then have done with it. The desire recurs; new music is always being created; the same great works yield different richnesses on repeated hearing. Similarly for friendship, love, intellectual exploration, and aesthetic experience — these are forms of engagement that can, for many people, be renewed and refreshed indefinitely.

Fischer's argument is that an immortal life of sufficiently varied *recurring pleasures and engagements* would be worth living. The immortal person would not need to remain rigidly the same, cycling through the same fixed set of desires. They would develop new tastes, explore new domains, form new relationships — and the variety could in principle be great enough to sustain genuine enthusiasm indefinitely.

Williams's response, Fischer acknowledges, is to question whether such a life would still be *your* life — whether the immortal person undergoing these changes remains meaningfully you. Fischer argues that this sets the bar for personal identity too high. We already undergo significant changes across a normal lifespan — the person you are at 70 may have quite different tastes, interests, and relationships than the person you were at 20 — and yet we do not deny that these are stages of a single life. Why should the same process, extended indefinitely, cease to constitute *one life*?

This is a genuine point, but it meets Williams's argument at a particular juncture rather than refuting it wholesale. Williams's claim is not that identity requires rigid sameness of desires; it is that the identity-constituting features of a person set a limit on how much transformation is possible while remaining the same person. Fischer's response is that this limit is higher than Williams thinks. The debate is partly empirical: it depends on how much psychological change is consistent with personal identity, which is itself a contested question (see Chapter 25).

## Parfit and What Matters

Derek Parfit (1984: 279–300) approaches the question from a different angle that has the potential to dissolve the problem rather than resolve it. Parfit argues that personal identity, strictly construed, is not what matters in survival. What matters is *psychological continuity and connectedness* — the overlapping chains of memory, intention, desire, and character that constitute the psychological connections between stages of a life.

If Parfit is right, then the question "would immortality be good for *you*?" may be ill-posed. An immortal being connected by chains of psychological continuity to the present person may not be *numerically identical* to the present person in any deep sense — but this need not matter. What matters is the preservation of connected psychological states, not numerical identity. The immortal being's life might be good — might contain genuine goods, sustained by a continuous stream of psychological connections — even if strict personal identity does not persist.

On Parfit's account, the fear of death is partly irrational because it presupposes that *identity* matters in a strong sense that it does not. And the question of whether immortality is desirable becomes: would an indefinitely extended stream of psychologically connected stages contain enough goods to be worth having? This seems more tractable than Williams's question, and the answer is at least not obviously negative.

## The Question of What We Value

Fischer (2009: 111–113) also makes a point that is philosophically important beyond the debate about immortality. The question of whether immortality is desirable is partly a question about what we value in life. Those who find value primarily in *depth* — in the kind of deep investment in a finite set of projects and relationships that Williams describes — may find Williams's argument compelling. Those who find value primarily in *breadth* — in variety, novelty, and repeated renewal of experience — may find Fischer's response more congenial.

This suggests that the answer to "would immortality be good?" may vary across individuals, depending on their particular constitution of values and desires. For someone with Williams's character — deeply invested in particular projects, valuing depth over breadth, identifying strongly with a particular set of categorical commitments — immortality might indeed become tedious or identity-dissolving. For someone with different values, it might not.

This is a humbling conclusion for philosophy: on one of the most profound questions about human existence, the answer may be irreducibly individual. What we can say with confidence is that the question of immortality is not merely a question about biology or about the continuation of experience. It is a question about what we value, what constitutes a life, and how identity, time, and the good are related. These are among the deepest questions in philosophy, and the debate between Williams and his critics has illuminated them with unusual clarity.

## Connections to the Philosophy of Time

The Williams debate connects to the philosophy of time in at least two ways. First, it concerns the relationship between *personal time* (the sequence of events that constitutes a life) and value. Williams's claim that categorical desires are finite is partly a claim about the structure of personal time: lives have a narrative shape, and that shape is partly constituted by the fact that they end. Immortal lives would lack this narrative closure, and Williams suggests this is not merely an aesthetic deficiency but a limitation on the kind of goods available.

Second, the Parfitian response depends on a particular theory of personal identity — psychological continuity rather than strict identity — which has direct connections to the debate about persistence over time examined in Chapter 25. If what matters is continuity rather than strict identity, then the temporal extent of a life is less metaphysically fraught than Williams supposes. The deep connections between the philosophy of death, the philosophy of personal identity, and the philosophy of time are once again visible.
