# Chapter 33 Exercises: Ethics Across Time

---

## Conceptual Exercises

**1. Grounds for Future Obligations**
List and explain three distinct grounds that have been proposed for our obligations to future generations: (a) interests; (b) rights; (c) welfare consequentialism. For each, identify one advantage and one difficulty. Which ground do you find most defensible, and why? Does your answer depend on which ethical theory you accept generally?

**2. The Non-Identity Problem**
Reconstruct Parfit's non-identity problem using your own example (not the energy policy case). Your example should involve a large-scale decision that affects which future people will exist. Explain why standard person-affecting moral views cannot condemn the worse option. Then assess whether the problem shows that those views are wrong, or merely incomplete.

**3. The Repugnant Conclusion**
State the Repugnant Conclusion as precisely as you can. Why does Parfit think it follows from the total welfare view? Do you find it repugnant? Try to articulate exactly what intuition it violates. Can you think of any argument for accepting it — that is, for concluding that a world with many barely-worth-living lives really is better than a world with fewer flourishing ones? What does this debate reveal about the nature of value?

**4. Pure Time Preference**
Construct the best possible argument *in favor of* pure time preference (the view that future goods matter less simply because they are further away). Then construct Parfit's rebuttal. Which do you find more convincing? Does the strength of the case against pure time preference depend on whether personal identity persists across long stretches of time?

**5. Discounting Types**
Distinguish clearly among the following types of reasons for discounting future welfare: (a) pure time preference; (b) uncertainty about future existence; (c) diminishing marginal utility based on expected growth; (d) opportunity cost of current investment. For each, state whether it is a principled reason for discounting or an unjustified form of temporal bias, and defend your assessment.

**6. Historical Injustice and Non-Identity**
Waldron (1992) argues that historical injustice claims can be superseded by changed circumstances. Evaluate this argument with respect to one specific historical injustice (such as land expropriation from indigenous peoples, or the transatlantic slave trade). Does the non-identity problem apply to this case — i.e., are the current descendants of victims different people from those who would have existed without the injustice? Does this affect the validity of present reparative obligations?

**7. Longtermism and Expected Value**
The longtermist argues that because the potential future population is so large, reducing existential risk has enormous expected value. Assess this argument carefully. Is the expected value calculation reliable given the depth of our uncertainty about long-run effects? What decision procedure should we use when expected values are highly uncertain? Does cluelessness (Lenman 2000, Greaves 2016) genuinely undermine longtermism, or can it be accommodated?

**8. MacAskill and Non-Identity**
MacAskill (2022) acknowledges that longtermist interventions face the non-identity problem. If a successful longtermist intervention brings into existence different people from those who would have existed under the unreformed scenario, does this undermine the case for longtermism? How might a longtermist respond? Does the response require abandoning person-affecting ethics entirely?

## Discussion and Essay Questions

**9. Climate Change and Future Generations**
Climate change is often described as an injustice to future generations. Using the philosophical frameworks discussed in this chapter, evaluate this claim: (a) Which grounds for future obligations support it most strongly? (b) Does the non-identity problem threaten the claim? If so, how? (c) What discount rate should be used in cost-benefit analysis of climate policy, and why? (d) Does the longtermist view strengthen or complicate the case for aggressive climate action?

**10. The Distant and the Future**
Peter Singer argues that geographical distance is morally irrelevant — we have as strong an obligation to help distant strangers as to help those nearby. If this is right, does it follow that temporal distance is also morally irrelevant — that we have as strong an obligation to future generations as to present ones? Evaluate this parallel argument. What disanalogies between spatial and temporal distance might justify different treatment?

## Lab and Observational Exercises

**11. Intergenerational Interview**
Interview three people from different generations (ideally, someone in their 20s, someone in their 50s, and someone in their 70s or 80s) about their sense of obligation to future generations. Questions to explore: (a) Do they feel any obligation to future people they will never meet? (b) How do they think about environmental decisions that will affect people in 100 years? (c) Do they think current generations are adequately accounting for future welfare? Analyze the responses philosophically: which positions in this chapter do they implicitly support or challenge?

**12. Policy Analysis**
Choose a current policy debate that has significant long-term intergenerational implications (suggestions: a national pension system, a major infrastructure project, nuclear waste disposal, a climate treaty, or immigration policy). Identify: (a) who bears the costs; (b) who bears the benefits; (c) what discount rate (implicit or explicit) is used in the policy analysis; (d) whether non-identity considerations are relevant. Write a philosophical assessment of the intergenerational justice dimensions of the policy.

**13. Longtermist Calculation**
The longtermist argument depends on numerical reasoning about expected value. Investigate the following empirically: (a) What probability estimates do researchers give for various existential risks (consult Ord 2020 or recent academic literature)? (b) What is the expected number of future people under different scenarios? (c) Using a near-zero discount rate, calculate the expected value (in person-years of welfare) of a 1% reduction in existential risk. Reflect: does the number you calculate actually change your moral intuitions about current priorities? If not, what does this reveal about the relationship between philosophical argument and moral motivation?
