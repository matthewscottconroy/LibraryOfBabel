## Annotated Entries

**Greaves and MacAskill, "The Case for Strong Longtermism" (2021)**
The most rigorous academic formulation of the longtermist thesis. Greaves and MacAskill present "strong longtermism" — the view that the most important determinant of the moral value of current actions is their expected long-run effect — and defend it against the main objections. Technical in places but accessible with philosophical background. The key starting point for academic engagement with longtermism.

**Greaves, "Cluelessness" (2016)**
The most philosophically careful treatment of the cluelessness problem for consequentialism. Greaves distinguishes different forms of cluelessness and assesses their implications for expected value reasoning. Essential reading for anyone interested in the limits of consequentialist reasoning about long-run effects.

**Lenman, "Consequentialism and Cluelessness" (2000)**
The article that launched serious philosophical discussion of the cluelessness problem. Lenman argues that consequentialism faces a deep difficulty because of our systematic ignorance of indirect effects, and that this undermines the practical authority of consequentialist recommendations. A sharp challenge to longtermist reasoning.

**MacAskill, *What We Owe the Future* (2022)**
The accessible book-length statement of the longtermist case by one of its principal philosophical architects. MacAskill covers existential risk, population ethics, the non-identity problem, and the practical implications of longtermism with intellectual honesty and genuine depth. Essential reading for understanding the contemporary longtermist movement.

**Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974)**
The foundational text of libertarian political philosophy, containing Nozick's entitlement theory and the principle of rectification of historical injustice. While Nozick's own application is limited, the framework provides the most systematic account of how historical wrongs generate present obligations. Required reading for any serious discussion of reparations.

**Ord, *The Precipice* (2020)**
A comprehensive, accessible assessment of existential risk and the moral importance of humanity's long-run future. Ord combines empirical assessment of specific risks (pandemic, nuclear war, AI misalignment, climate change) with philosophical argument for why existential risk deserves priority attention. The best single-volume introduction to existential risk from a philosophical perspective.

**Parfit, *Reasons and Persons*, Part IV (1984)**
The definitive statement of the non-identity problem, the Repugnant Conclusion, and the challenges for population ethics. Parfit's honest acknowledgment that he cannot find a satisfying solution is itself philosophically important. This remains the essential starting point for any philosophical work on future generations.

**Ramsey, "A Mathematical Theory of Saving" (1928)**
One of the most important papers in welfare economics, notable here for its dismissal of pure time preference as "ethically indefensible." Ramsey's argument has been enormously influential; it is the source of the philosophical consensus against pure time preference. The paper is accessible to non-economists and worth reading in the original.

**Stern, *The Economics of Climate Change* (2007)**
The most influential application of near-zero discount rate reasoning to climate policy. Stern's methodology is philosophically informed and explicitly engages with the debate about time preference and future welfare. The responses by Nordhaus (2007) and Weitzman (2007) in the same journal issue illuminate what is philosophically at stake in the discount rate debate.

**Waldron, "Superseding Historic Injustice" (1992)**
A philosophically sophisticated challenge to strong forms of historical rectification. Waldron argues that the passage of time can supersede historical claims by changing the moral circumstances in which they apply. Essential reading for balanced consideration of reparations and historical justice, even for those who ultimately reject his conclusions.
