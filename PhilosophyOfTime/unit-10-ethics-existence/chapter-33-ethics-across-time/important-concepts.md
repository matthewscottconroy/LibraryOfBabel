# Chapter 33 Important Concepts: Ethics Across Time

---

**Asymmetry Problem**
The observation, noted by Narveson (1967) and others, that our intuitions about the ethics of creating people are asymmetric: we have strong reasons not to bring into existence people who will live miserable lives, but no comparably strong reasons to bring into existence happy people. This asymmetry is difficult to accommodate within a simple welfare-maximizing framework and reveals a deep tension between person-affecting and impersonal approaches to population ethics.

**Cluelessness**
The condition of systematic ignorance about the indirect, long-run effects of one's actions. Lenman (2000) and Greaves (2016) argue that for most actions, the indirect long-run consequences swamp the direct effects in scale but are essentially unpredictable. This creates a serious problem for expected value reasoning about long-run interventions, including longtermist ones: if we cannot estimate the direction of long-run effects, expected value calculations may be unreliable guides to action.

**Consumption Discount Rate**
The discount rate applied to future consumption in economic analysis, reflecting the reduced present value of future goods due to expected growth and the diminishing marginal utility of income. Distinguished from the pure rate of time preference: the consumption discount rate is partly justified by principled considerations about economic growth, while pure time preference is not. The Ramsey equation relates these components formally.

**Diminishing Marginal Utility**
The principle that the marginal utility of consumption decreases as consumption increases: additional units of consumption matter less to wealthy people than to poor ones. Applied to intergenerational ethics, if future people are expected to be wealthier (due to economic growth), the marginal utility of resources transferred to them may be less than for present people. This provides one principled basis for some economic discounting of future benefits.

**Existential Risk**
Risk of events that would permanently curtail humanity's long-run potential — through extinction, civilizational collapse, or permanent "lock-in" to a bad trajectory. Ord (*The Precipice*, 2020) systematically assesses existential risks including pandemics, nuclear war, climate change, and advanced AI misalignment. The longtermist argument gives existential risk reduction high priority because of the enormous stakes involved in humanity's long-run future.

**Historical Entitlement**
Nozick's (1974) principle that legitimate property holdings must trace back to just original acquisitions and just transfers. If current holdings resulted from unjust acquisitions or transfers (theft, conquest, slavery), they are unjustly held and rectification is owed. The entitlement framework grounds obligations of historical rectification even when the original perpetrators are long dead, because unjust advantages are transmitted through chains of subsequent holders.

**Impersonal Welfare View**
An ethical framework that assesses outcomes by their overall welfare level, without restriction to identified individuals who are better or worse off. Contrasts with person-affecting views, which only recognize benefits or harms to specific identifiable individuals. The impersonal view can explain why Policy B (resource depletion) is worse than Policy A (conservation) even when the people harmed by B would not exist under A; but it also generates the Repugnant Conclusion.

**Intergenerational Equity**
The principle that the interests of future generations deserve equal moral weight, per person, as the interests of present generations. If temporal location is morally arbitrary — as most philosophers argue — then intergenerational equity follows from basic egalitarian commitments. The principle grounds the philosophical objection to pure time preference and the argument for conservation of resources and reduction of environmental damage.

**Longtermism**
The ethical position, associated with MacAskill (*What We Owe the Future*, 2022) and Ord (*The Precipice*, 2020), that the most important moral priorities lie in improving humanity's long-run future, given the enormous scale of future populations and the moral equality of all persons across time. Strong longtermism (Greaves and MacAskill 2021) holds that the expected long-run effects of our actions are the most important determinant of their moral value.

**Non-Identity Problem**
Parfit's (1984) discovery that large-scale decisions about policy, environment, and institutions affect not only the welfare of future people but which people will exist. Since personal identity depends on specific causal circumstances, different policies lead to different people being born. People who suffer under a bad policy would not have existed under a good policy, and cannot therefore be said to have been harmed in the standard comparative sense. This problem threatens person-affecting moral views that assess actions by their effects on identifiable individuals.

**Person-Affecting View**
The ethical position that only effects on actual or forthcoming identifiable persons can constitute benefits or harms. On a person-affecting view, an action is wrong only if it makes some specific person worse off. Person-affecting views are intuitively attractive (they capture the sense that harm requires a victim) but face the non-identity problem: if different policies lead to different people existing, there may be no identifiable victim.

**Pure Time Preference**
The preference for benefits now over equivalent benefits later, simply because they are closer in time — not because of differences in probability, circumstances, or marginal utility. Pure time preference implies that future welfare intrinsically matters less than present welfare. This position has been condemned as rationally indefensible by Ramsey (1928), Rawls (1971), Sidgwick (1907), and most contemporary philosophers, because temporal location is morally arbitrary.

**Repugnant Conclusion**
Parfit's (1984) term for the implication of total welfare views that a world with an enormous population each living lives barely worth living is morally preferable to a smaller population each living flourishing lives, because the total welfare of the former may exceed that of the latter. The conclusion is "repugnant" because it implies that the creation of vast numbers of barely-subsistent lives is a moral good. No accepted theory of population ethics successfully avoids this conclusion while avoiding other counterintuitive implications.

**Supersession Thesis**
Waldron's (1992) argument that historical injustice claims can be superseded — rendered no longer morally actionable — by subsequent changes in circumstances. Even if a historical wrong generated a legitimate claim to rectification, subsequent changes (displacement, changed livelihoods, development of new community structures) may have altered the conditions under which the original claim applied, reducing or eliminating its present force. Critics argue that this proves too much: it could be used to justify any injustice after sufficient time has passed.

**Uncertainty Discounting**
The reduction in the present value of future benefits or harms due to uncertainty about whether they will materialize. Unlike pure time preference, uncertainty discounting is principled: if the probability of a future benefit being received is less than one, its expected value is proportionally reduced. The difficulty is that estimates of long-run probability are themselves highly uncertain, and systematic uncertainty may require a different decision framework than simple expected value maximization.
