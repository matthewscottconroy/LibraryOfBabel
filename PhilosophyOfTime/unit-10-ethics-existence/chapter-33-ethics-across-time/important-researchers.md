# Chapter 33 Important Researchers: Ethics Across Time

---

**Hilary Greaves (b. 1978)**
Philosopher and co-director of the Global Priorities Institute at Oxford University, whose work has significantly advanced both population ethics and the philosophical foundations of longtermism. Her paper "Cluelessness" (2016) is the most careful philosophical treatment of the uncertainty problem facing consequentialist expected value reasoning, and her joint paper with MacAskill on "strong longtermism" (2021) is the definitive academic statement of that position. Greaves has also worked on decision theory under moral uncertainty, probabilistic ethics, and the methodology of moral philosophy.

**Joel Feinberg (1926–2004)**
American philosopher of law and ethics whose work on rights, harm, and the moral status of future and non-existent persons was foundational for the field. His paper "The Rights of Animals and Unborn Generations" (1974) is among the earliest and most influential philosophical treatments of our obligations to future people. Feinberg's broader work on the concept of harm, legal paternalism, and the limits of the criminal law (*The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law*, four volumes) provides essential background for thinking about intergenerational obligations.

**James Lenman**
British philosopher whose 2000 paper "Consequentialism and Cluelessness" articulated the most serious structural challenge to expected-value reasoning about long-run effects. Lenman argues that for most actions, indirect effects swamp direct effects and are essentially unknowable, creating a deep difficulty for any consequentialist ethics that takes long-run consequences seriously. The paper has been essential to subsequent debates about longtermism and the limits of consequentialist reasoning.

**William MacAskill (b. 1987)**
Oxford philosopher and prominent advocate of effective altruism whose *What We Owe the Future* (2022) brought longtermism to a wide audience. MacAskill has made significant academic contributions to population ethics, moral uncertainty, and the philosophical foundations of effective altruism. His intellectual honesty about the challenges facing longtermism — including the non-identity problem and cluelessness — is notable. He has also co-authored important papers with Greaves on strong longtermism and on acting under moral uncertainty.

**Robert Nozick (1938–2002)**
American political philosopher whose *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) is one of the most influential works of twentieth-century political philosophy. Nozick's entitlement theory — grounding just distribution in just acquisition and transfer — provides the main libertarian framework for thinking about historical rectification. Though Nozick did not develop the implications of his theory for large-scale historical injustices in detail, his framework has been widely applied by subsequent philosophers to issues of reparations, indigenous land rights, and colonial legacy.

**Toby Ord (b. 1975)**
Oxford philosopher and co-founder of the effective altruism movement whose *The Precipice: Existential Risk and the Future of Humanity* (2020) provides the most comprehensive philosophical and empirical assessment of existential risk. Ord combines rigorous probability estimates of specific risks with philosophical argument for the moral importance of reducing existential risk, given the enormous scale of humanity's potential long-run future. He has also contributed to debates about moral progress and the history of moral circle expansion.

**Derek Parfit (1942–2017)**
See the biographical entry in Chapter 32. Parfit's contribution to Chapter 33's themes is centered on Part IV of *Reasons and Persons* (1984), which contains the definitive formulation of the non-identity problem, the Repugnant Conclusion, and the challenge of finding a theory of population ethics that avoids both. Parfit was intellectually honest about not finding a satisfying solution, and his final work, *On What Matters* (2011), attempts but does not fully resolve the challenge. His influence on the ethics of future generations is unparalleled.

**Frank P. Ramsey (1903–1930)**
Cambridge mathematician, logician, economist, and philosopher who died at 26 but produced work of the first rank in all four fields. His 1928 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Saving" is the foundational paper in welfare economics on optimal savings rates and contains the famous dismissal of pure time preference as "ethically indefensible." Ramsey's contributions to logic (the Ramsey sentence), probability theory (subjective probability), and the foundations of economics were all made before his death at 26 from jaundice.

**Jeremy Waldron (b. 1953)**
Legal philosopher and political theorist whose work ranges across constitutional theory, human rights, and the philosophy of law. His paper "Superseding Historic Injustice" (1992) is the most philosophically sophisticated challenge to historical rectification claims, arguing that changed circumstances can morally supersede historical wrongs. Waldron's broader work on dignity, equality, and the rule of law provides important context for understanding how historical injustice claims fit within a theory of justice and rights.
