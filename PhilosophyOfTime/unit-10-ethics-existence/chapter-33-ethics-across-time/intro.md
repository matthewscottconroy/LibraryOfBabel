# Chapter 33: Ethics Across Time

---

## Chapter Introduction

Most ethical theories were developed with contemporaries in mind: people who exist alongside us, with whom we share a world that we can jointly shape, and whose interests we can directly affect through our choices. But time is not merely a personal feature of individual existence; it is a dimension of moral life. We stand in ethical relationships not only with those who live alongside us but with those who lived before us and those who will live after us. The philosophy of time, once it reaches ethics, cannot be confined to the present.

This chapter examines three dimensions of ethical life that span time. The first is the ethics of obligations to future generations: on what grounds do we have duties toward people who do not yet exist, whose interests we cannot even fully specify, and who will come into existence partly as a result of our choices? The second is Parfit's non-identity problem, which threatens to undermine any person-affecting ethics of the future: if our major decisions affect which people will come to exist, we cannot claim to harm those people even when our decisions make the world objectively worse for them. The third dimension moves backward as well as forward: pure time preference in ethical theory, the discounting of future welfare, and the problem of historical injustice — obligations that arise from wrongs committed before we were born.

Running through all three dimensions is a puzzle about the moral status of non-actual persons. Future people do not yet exist; their non-existence makes it hard to give them rights, interests, or the moral standing we attribute to actual persons. Past people no longer exist; the victims of historical injustice are dead, but their descendants bear the effects of the injustice. How do we construct an ethical theory that takes seriously the temporal extension of moral obligations while preserving the insights of theories built around actual, existing persons?

The chapter closes with a discussion of longtermism: the view that, because the potential number of future people vastly exceeds current people, and because our choices can significantly affect the long-run trajectory of civilization, the most important moral priorities lie in the far future. This view is philosophically serious and practically consequential — it bears on how we think about existential risk, climate change, and institutional design. It also faces deep challenges, and this chapter examines both its force and its limits.
