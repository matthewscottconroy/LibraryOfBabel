# 33.1.1 Grounds for Obligations to Future Generations

---

## The Problem of Non-Existence

Future people face a distinctive philosophical challenge as potential objects of moral concern: they do not exist. The moral patient — the being whose interests or rights ground an obligation — is not yet present. This creates difficulties for several standard ethical frameworks.

*Rights-based frameworks* typically ground obligations in the rights of existing beings. Rights require a rights-holder, and a rights-holder is typically an individual person with a certain moral status — a status usually derived from capacities such as rationality, sentience, or the ability to form a conception of one's own good. Future people have no such capacities currently; they do not even exist. Can they have rights now?

*Interest-based frameworks* ground obligations in the interests of those affected. Future people will have interests — they will have preferences, they will experience pleasure and pain, they will have projects and relationships. But they do not have these interests now. Can current obligations be grounded in interests that do not yet exist?

*Welfare consequentialism* says that what matters morally is the overall welfare of all sentient beings. On this view, future welfare matters just as much as present welfare (setting aside discounting considerations, to which we return in Section 33.3). Future people's welfare is part of the moral calculus even before those people exist. This is perhaps the most natural framework for grounding obligations to future generations, but it faces the challenge of specifying *which* future people and their welfare, given that our choices affect which people come to exist.

## The Interest-Based Account

Despite the difficulties, the most natural and widely accepted account of our obligations to future generations is interest-based. Future people will have interests in not being harmed, in having adequate resources, in living in a habitable environment, in not inheriting the consequences of our irresponsible choices. These interests are real — they will be real when those people exist — and this reality grounds current obligations.

The key philosophical move is to distinguish between *current interests* and *interests that will exist in the future*. We can have obligations with respect to future interests even when those interests do not currently exist, just as we can have obligations that will come due in the future. A promissory obligation is not less real because it is not yet time to discharge it; an obligation to future generations is not less real because the bearers of those interests do not yet exist.

This approach is defended by Feinberg (1981: 159–183), who argues that future people have interests — in the sense of objective stakes in various states of the world — that ground present obligations. The temporally indexed character of these interests does not eliminate them; it specifies when they will be most directly relevant.

## The Rights-Based Account

An alternative approach, developed by de-Shalit (1995: 13–42), grounds obligations to future generations in the rights of future persons. Future people will be persons, and as such they will have rights. These rights can ground current obligations just as the rights of people in distant countries ground obligations on us even though those people are not currently present.

The challenge for the rights-based account is explaining how rights can exist before their bearer does. One response is to hold that rights are grounded in *generic properties* rather than *particular existing individuals*: persons as such have rights, and the fact that future people will be persons is sufficient to ground their rights now. This is analogous to how we can legislate protection for future endangered species without knowing exactly which individuals will exist in those species.

## The Asymmetry Problem

A significant challenge for any account of obligations to future generations is the asymmetry in our intuitions noted by Jan Narveson (1967: 62–72) and analyzed at length by Benatar (2006: 11–42). Consider two claims:

(A) We have strong reasons not to bring into existence people who will live miserable lives.
(B) We have comparably strong reasons to bring into existence people who will live happy lives.

Most people find (A) plausible but (B) implausible. We feel genuinely obligated to avoid creating suffering lives, but we do not generally feel that the world is morally impoverished by the non-creation of merely possible happy people. Adding a happy person to the world does not seem to discharge a pre-existing moral obligation.

This asymmetry is difficult to accommodate within a straightforward welfare-maximizing framework. If welfare is what matters, then creating a happy person seems just as good as preventing a miserable one — both affect the total welfare ledger. But the asymmetry of intuitions suggests that *person-affecting* considerations matter: what matters is not total welfare but welfare effects on particular existing or forthcoming people.

The asymmetry problem connects directly to Parfit's non-identity problem, to which we now turn, and to the broader question of population ethics — a field of moral philosophy that is still very much in formation.

## Practical Implications

Despite the philosophical difficulties, there is enough convergence across frameworks to support some substantive conclusions about future generations. All of the main approaches agree that we have strong obligations not to make the world significantly worse for future people — not to leave them a degraded environment, depleted resources, or the consequences of irresponsible risk-taking. The case for this is most straightforward on the interest-based view: future people's interests in a decent life are real, and seriously harming those interests for the sake of current benefit is a paradigm case of inter-generational injustice.

The case for obligations to create good conditions for future people, rather than merely not to harm them, is harder to make and may rest ultimately on welfare consequentialist grounds. But even without resolving this more ambitious claim, the convergence on the prohibitive obligations is practically significant: it grounds robust ethical support for environmental protection, resource conservation, and long-term institutional design.
