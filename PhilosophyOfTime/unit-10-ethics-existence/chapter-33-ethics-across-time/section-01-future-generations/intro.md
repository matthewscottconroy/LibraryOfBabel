# Section 33.1: Future Generations

---

## Section Introduction

The question of our obligations to future generations is both philosophically ancient and politically urgent. Ancient enough that every major ethical tradition has addressed it: whether through the concept of intergenerational covenant, the karma-cycle of future lives, or the utilitarian summing of all sentient welfare. Urgent enough that climate policy, nuclear waste disposal, national debt, and institutional design all hinge on answers to it.

The philosophical difficulty is that future people are, in an important sense, unlike other potential objects of moral concern. They are not suffering now; their interests cannot be represented in the present; they cannot consent or refuse consent to arrangements made on their behalf; and — most peculiarly — which people will exist in the future depends partly on what we do now.

This section examines the main grounds that have been proposed for obligations to future generations, and introduces the asymmetry problem: the widespread intuition that we have strong reasons not to bring miserable lives into existence but no comparably strong reasons to bring happy lives into existence.
