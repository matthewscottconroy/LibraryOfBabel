# 33.2.1 Parfit's Formulation of the Non-Identity Problem

---

## The Discovery

Derek Parfit introduced the non-identity problem in *Reasons and Persons* (1984), Chapter 16, as part of a broader investigation into the ethics of future generations and the implications of different theories of personal identity for moral philosophy. The problem has since become central to both population ethics and the philosophical treatment of climate change and environmental policy.

The core observation is this: our choices affect not only the *conditions* under which future people will live but *which people* will exist at all. Since personal identity depends on very specific causal circumstances — including precisely which sperm fertilizes which egg — any significant change in history alters the identities of virtually everyone born more than a generation or two later. Different energy policies, different trade decisions, different social arrangements lead to different encounters, different conceptions, and ultimately different people.

## A Simple Case

Parfit illustrates the problem with a case he calls the "14-year-old girl" case. Suppose a young woman decides to conceive a child now, at age 14. The child will have a difficult life due to the mother's poverty and immaturity — harder than the life the same woman's child would have had if she had waited until age 25 to conceive. But the child who would be born at 25 is a *different child* — different egg, possibly different father, different development. The child who actually exists was caused to exist by the decision to conceive at 14. Has the mother wronged this child? The child's life is worth living; the child is better off existing (with a hard life) than not existing at all. Yet it seems morally problematic for a teenager to conceive a child she cannot adequately care for.

## The Policy Version

The more significant version involves large-scale policy decisions. Consider two energy policies:

**Policy A (Conservation):** Society adopts stringent conservation measures. Resource depletion is slow; pollution is minimal; the world 200 years hence is livable and moderately prosperous. The people alive in 200 years have decent lives.

**Policy B (Depletion):** Society continues current high-consumption patterns. Resource depletion is rapid; pollution is extensive; the world 200 years hence is significantly degraded. The people alive in 200 years have harder, shorter lives.

The key observation: because these policies involve different economic activities, different social arrangements, different patterns of human interaction, they lead to different people being born. The person born in 2224 under Policy B is a different person from anyone who would have been born under Policy A. The people who will suffer under Policy B *would not have existed* under Policy A.

Does Policy B harm these people? The standard account of harming requires that the harmed person be *worse off* than they would have been in the absence of the harmful action. But the people harmed by Policy B would not exist under Policy A; they are not worse off than they would otherwise have been, because without Policy B there is no "them" to be better or worse off.

Parfit calls this the "Non-Identity Problem": the very policy that harms people is also the policy that brings them into existence. We cannot straightforwardly say we have harmed them, because their existence is partly constituted by the very decisions that make their lives worse.

## The Conclusion and Its Scope

Parfit concludes that "person-affecting" moral views — views that ground moral obligations in the effects of actions on particular persons — cannot fully account for our intuitive sense that Policy B is worse than Policy A. If no identifiable person is worse off under Policy B than they would have been otherwise (because the comparison case involves non-existence), then no identifiable person has been harmed. Yet it seems clear that Policy B is morally inferior to Policy A: the world it produces is objectively worse.

The non-identity problem has extremely broad scope. It applies not only to grand policy choices but to any decision that affects, over generations, which people come to exist. Climate change, nuclear waste policy, biodiversity loss, institutional design — all of these involve decisions whose effects are felt generations later, by people who would not exist but for those very decisions. For all of these cases, the person-affecting argument that we are harming future people is, if Parfit is right, invalid.

## Is the Intuition Salvageable?

Parfit did not conclude that there is nothing wrong with Policy B. He concluded that our ordinary moral framework — the person-affecting view — cannot explain what is wrong with it. The problem is a challenge to articulate the ground of the wrongness, not a proof that there is no wrongness.

His own suggestion was to move toward *impersonal* moral views — views that assess outcomes by their overall quality, not by their effects on particular identifiable individuals. On an impersonal view, Policy B produces a worse outcome than Policy A because it produces worse welfare overall, regardless of which particular people are involved. This move is natural for utilitarian and consequentialist theories.

But the impersonal move has its own problems — above all, the "Repugnant Conclusion" that Parfit himself identified. If what matters is total welfare, then a world with a vast population each living lives barely worth living might be morally preferable to a world with a smaller population each living excellent lives, because the sheer numbers swamp the difference in individual welfare. Almost everyone finds this conclusion repugnant. Parfit spent the rest of his philosophical career trying to develop a theory that avoids both the person-affecting problem and the repugnant conclusion, and did not find one he was satisfied with.
