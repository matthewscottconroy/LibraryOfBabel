# 33.2.2 Proposed Solutions to the Non-Identity Problem

---

## The Challenge

The non-identity problem poses a dilemma for ethical theory. Person-affecting views cannot explain what is wrong with policies that harm future people through making them exist in worse conditions, because the harmed people would not have existed otherwise. But impersonal views that avoid this problem generate the Repugnant Conclusion: that a vast population of barely-worth-living lives is morally preferable to a smaller, flourishing population.

Any satisfying solution must: (a) explain why policies like Policy B are morally objectionable; (b) do so in terms that do not apply exclusively to identifiable persons who are worse off; (c) avoid the Repugnant Conclusion; and (d) remain consistent with other well-grounded moral intuitions. No current proposal satisfies all four conditions, which is why the problem remains genuinely open.

## Impersonal Consequentialism

The most straightforward solution is impersonal welfare consequentialism: what matters morally is the total or average welfare produced, not whose welfare it is. Policy B is worse than Policy A because it produces a worse total or average welfare level. This judgment does not depend on identifying particular victims.

*Total view*: maximize total welfare (the sum of all individual welfare across all persons). This view straightforwardly condemns Policy B and avoids the non-identity problem. But it entails the Repugnant Conclusion: a large population of lives barely worth living can have a greater total welfare than a smaller, flourishing population.

*Average view*: maximize average welfare. This avoids some forms of the Repugnant Conclusion (adding a barely-worth-living life reduces the average). But it generates different problems: it implies that a world with one very happy person is better than a world with many happy people if the average is higher, and it has difficulty handling populations of different sizes.

*Critical level approaches*: add people to the population only if their welfare exceeds some critical level above "barely worth living." Blackorby, Bossert, and Donaldson (2005: 146–178) develop this approach. It avoids the Repugnant Conclusion but requires defending a particular welfare threshold.

## The Rights-Based Approach

An alternative strategy grounds the wrongness of Policy B in rights rather than welfare. Even if no particular identifiable person is *worse off* under Policy B, the people who exist under it have rights — including rights to certain environmental conditions, certain resources, certain life prospects — that Policy B violates or fails to respect.

The rights-based approach faces a version of the existence problem: how can a future person have rights before they exist? But as noted in Section 33.1, there are defensible ways to ground future rights in generic properties of persons as such. The right to a decent environment, for instance, is not a right of any specific future individual but of future persons as such — anyone who will exist deserves adequate conditions.

This approach has been developed by Buchanan et al. (2000: 249–271), who argue that obligations to future generations are grounded partly in rights that are conditional on existence: if P comes to exist, then P has a right to certain conditions. The wrongness of Policy B is that it will bring into existence people whose conditional rights will be violated.

## Parfit's "Wide Consequentialism" and Moral Residue

Parfit himself, unable to find a fully satisfying theoretical solution, proposed a "wide person-affecting" view on which what matters is not only effects on actual persons but effects on what-might-be-persons (Parfit 1984: 396–401). This view acknowledges a kind of moral impersonalism without fully embracing the total welfare view.

Several philosophers have argued that even if we cannot frame the wrongness of Policy B in terms of harming specific identifiable victims, there remains a *moral residue* — a sense in which the world produced by Policy B is worse in ways that matter morally, even if no specific person can file a complaint (Roberts 1998: 124–156). The task is to articulate this residue without appealing to impersonal total welfare.

Scanlon (1998: 224–236) suggests that contractualist theory — the view that principles are justified if no person could reasonably reject them — can handle non-identity cases by asking what principles the affected persons could reasonably reject, even if they do not compare their actual situation with a counterfactual existence. A person who exists in degraded conditions can reasonably reject the principles that produced those conditions, even if their alternative is non-existence.

## The Repugnant Conclusion and Population Ethics

The Repugnant Conclusion remains the most serious obstacle to any purely impersonal solution. Parfit named it and spent decades trying to avoid it. He eventually concluded that the problem is not merely technical but reflects a deep indeterminacy in our moral concepts: our intuitions about value, welfare, and population size do not fit together into a consistent theory (Parfit 1984: 443–444).

This is a sobering conclusion from one of the most careful moral philosophers of the twentieth century. It suggests that the ethics of future generations is not a domain in which we can expect full theoretical closure. The task instead is to work with partial principles that converge on conclusions in the most important practical cases — obligations not to leave an uninhabitable world, not to take high-variance risks with civilization's future, not to discount future welfare arbitrarily — while remaining open about the theoretical foundations.
