# 33.3.1 Pure Time Preference and Its Critics

---

## What Pure Time Preference Is

Pure time preference is the preference for benefits now over equivalent benefits later, *simply because they are closer in time* — not because of any difference in the circumstances, the probability of receipt, the identity of the recipient, or the marginal utility of the goods. It is the preference structure that underlies the common experience of wanting to eat the cake now rather than later, not because the cake will deteriorate, not because you might die before later, not because you need the energy now — but simply because now is closer.

In welfare economics and cost-benefit analysis, pure time preference is formalized as the *pure rate of time preference* or the *utility discount rate*: future welfare (utility) is multiplied by a discount factor (1+r)^(-t), where r is the discount rate and t is the number of years in the future. If r > 0, future welfare counts for less than present welfare simply because of the time difference.

Applied to intergenerational ethics, a positive pure rate of time preference implies that the welfare of people who will live in 100 years matters less — intrinsically, just by virtue of their temporal location — than the welfare of people alive today. A person who will suffer greatly in 2124 deserves less moral weight than someone who suffers equally today, simply because 2124 is further away.

## The Philosophical Condemnation

This position has been condemned as morally indefensible by philosophers of widely different theoretical orientations. The condemnation is unusually uniform.

Frank Ramsey, in his celebrated 1928 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Saving," called pure time preference "a practice which is ethically indefensible and arises merely from the weakness of the imagination" (Ramsey 1928: 543). This was an early and influential statement of a position later endorsed by nearly every major philosopher who has examined the question.

Henry Sidgwick, the systematizer of classical utilitarianism, argued that temporal distance is not morally relevant in itself: "the time at which a man exists cannot affect the value of his happiness from the universal point of view" (*The Methods of Ethics*, 1907: 414). Rawls made the same point in *A Theory of Justice* (1971: 293): "the discount rate must be zero" for the reason that spatial and temporal distance do not, in themselves, reduce the moral weight of a person's interests.

The philosophical argument is straightforward: temporal location is morally arbitrary in the same way that spatial location is morally arbitrary. We do not accept that the suffering of someone who lives far away matters less than the suffering of someone nearby, simply because of the distance. Why should the suffering of someone who lives *far in the future* matter less simply because of the temporal distance? Both involve the interests of real persons; both involve genuine suffering; the only difference is the dimension of distance.

## The Stern Review Controversy

The practical stakes became dramatically visible in the 2006 debate over the Stern Review on climate change. Nicholas Stern (*The Economics of Climate Change*, 2007) used a near-zero pure time preference rate (0.1%) in calculating the costs and benefits of climate action. This yielded a conclusion that aggressive early action on climate change is economically justified — even demanded. The present value of future climate damages, even far in the future, is large when the discount rate is near zero.

William Nordhaus (2007: 686–702) challenged Stern's discount rate, arguing that a higher rate — closer to market rates — is more appropriate. At a higher discount rate, the present value of future climate damages shrinks dramatically, making less aggressive current action appear cost-effective.

The disagreement is not primarily empirical. Both economists understand the data on climate science. The disagreement is about the ethical appropriate treatment of future welfare. Stern's near-zero pure time preference reflects the philosophical consensus that temporal location is morally arbitrary. Nordhaus's higher rate reflects a view that market preferences — which do exhibit time preference — provide a legitimate basis for the discount rate.

Weitzman (2007: 703–708) and Dasgupta (2008: 141–190) provided measured assessments of the controversy, noting that the choice of discount rate encodes deep normative judgments that economics cannot settle on its own. The Stern-Nordhaus debate is a vivid illustration of the practical consequences of abstract philosophical positions about time and value.

## A Bias to Be Corrected?

Parfit (1984: 357–361) argued that pure time preference is a bias to be corrected, much like the bias toward the nearby. We recognize that the suffering of distant strangers has the same moral weight as the suffering of those nearby, and we try to correct for the natural tendency to discount spatial distance. Similarly, we should recognize that the suffering of future people has the same moral weight as the suffering of present people, and correct for the natural tendency to discount temporal distance.

Whether this correction is psychologically achievable is a separate question. Our practical decision-making is inevitably limited by cognitive constraints, and the inability to vividly imagine the suffering of people who will live in 2124 makes it difficult to give that suffering its full moral weight. But difficulty is not impossibility, and the philosophical argument establishes that the attempt should be made.
