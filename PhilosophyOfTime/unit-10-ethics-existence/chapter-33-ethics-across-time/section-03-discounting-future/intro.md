# Section 33.3: Discounting the Future

---

## Section Introduction

Even if we agree that future people's welfare matters morally, a further question arises: how much should we weight it relative to the welfare of present people? The practice of *discounting* — giving less weight to future goods and harms than to present ones — is standard in economics, where the discount rate is a key parameter in cost-benefit analysis. Applied to intergenerational comparisons, it has enormous practical stakes: a small difference in the discount rate, applied over centuries, can mean the difference between spending trillions now to prevent future climate damage and doing almost nothing.

This section distinguishes two very different rationales for discounting future welfare. The first is *pure time preference* — the claim that future goods and harms are inherently less important simply because they are further in the future. This view has been almost universally condemned by philosophers as rationally indefensible. The second encompasses several *principled* rationales for discounting that do not depend on pure time preference but on contingent facts about uncertainty, economic growth, and opportunity costs. These may be defensible, but they need to be applied carefully.
