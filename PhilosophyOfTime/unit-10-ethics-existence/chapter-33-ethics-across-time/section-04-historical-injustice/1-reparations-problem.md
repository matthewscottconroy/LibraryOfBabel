# 33.4.1 Historical Injustice, Reparations, and the Problem of Time

---

## The Structure of the Problem

Historical injustice claims typically have the following structure: a wrong was committed in the past (the enslavement of a people, the seizure of land, the suppression of a culture); the effects of that wrong persist into the present (inherited poverty, loss of property, cultural damage, structural disadvantage); people alive today are affected by those persisting effects; and therefore there are present obligations to address and rectify the wrong.

This structure is compelling on its surface but raises philosophical difficulties at several junctures. First, who are the obligated parties? The descendants of the perpetrators did not themselves commit the wrongs. Why should they bear obligations to rectify what their ancestors did? Second, who are the beneficiaries of rectification? The direct victims of historical injustice are dead; their descendants may have suffered persisting effects, but the harm they suffer is different from the harm the direct victims suffered. Third, the non-identity problem resurfaces in reverse: the people alive today — both the descendants of perpetrators and the descendants of victims — are partly products of the historical situation, including the injustice itself. Different circumstances (no slavery, no colonization) would have led to different people existing.

## Nozick's Entitlement Theory

Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974: 150–182) provides one of the most systematic frameworks for thinking about historical rectification, even though Nozick himself does not apply it extensively to large-scale historical injustices.

Nozick's theory has three components: (a) *justice in acquisition* — the conditions under which one can legitimately come to hold property for the first time; (b) *justice in transfer* — the conditions under which holdings can be legitimately transferred; (c) *rectification of injustice* — the principle that if one's holdings resulted from historical injustice (unjust acquisition or transfer), rectification is owed.

The rectification principle implies that current holdings are not fully justified if they resulted from historical injustice. If land was stolen from indigenous peoples, and that land (or wealth derived from it) is now held by the descendants of the thieves, the holdings are unjust and rectification is owed. The fact that the original thieves are dead does not eliminate the injustice, because the current holders benefit from the unjust taking.

The practical challenge is enormous: tracing the lineage of specific holdings through centuries of transfer, ascertaining what rectification would look like (restoration, compensation, or some other form), and dealing with cases where rectification would itself involve significant disruption to current just holdings. Nozick acknowledges these difficulties and suggests that in some complex cases, the principle of rectification may converge with patterned distributive principles (like the difference principle) as a heuristic.

## Waldron's Supersession Thesis

Jeremy Waldron's "Superseding Historic Injustice" (1992: 4–28) provides the most systematic challenge to strong forms of the historical rectification claim. Waldron argues that historical injustice claims can be *superseded* — made no longer morally actionable — by subsequent changes in circumstances that alter the moral landscape.

Waldron's argument proceeds from a consideration about what grounds the historical claim. If indigenous people have a claim to their ancestral lands because those lands were their means of livelihood, the basis of their community, and the ground of their cultural practice, then the strength of that claim depends on the extent to which those facts remain true. Over time, circumstances change: people move away, adopt different livelihoods, develop new community structures. If the loss of the original land no longer constitutes the harm it once did — if the same people can now flourish without it — the original claim may no longer apply.

More strikingly, Waldron argues that the passage of time can make rectification unjust in a new way, by imposing serious costs on people who are not themselves perpetrators. The descendants of colonizers who have lived for generations on appropriated land may have legitimate entitlements to that land based on present circumstances — they have invested in it, built communities on it, made it the basis of their own livelihoods — even though their original title was unjust. Rectification that simply returned the land would impose serious injustices on currently innocent parties.

## Non-Identity Applied in Reverse

A particularly troubling application of the non-identity problem concerns historical injustice. Suppose that the descendants of enslaved people are alive today partly because of the specific circumstances of slavery — different historical circumstances would have led to different people being born. Does this mean that those descendants cannot claim to have been harmed by slavery, since they owe their existence to it?

This inference is almost universally rejected as morally wrong, even by those who accept the non-identity problem in its forward-looking form. But it is philosophically difficult to say precisely why.

One response is that the non-identity problem is less decisive in the historical case because the harm is not just comparative (worse off than a counterfactual alternative) but involves the violation of rights and dignitary wrongs: slavery was wrong not merely because it produced bad welfare outcomes but because it violated the enslaved persons' rights and human dignity. Dignitary wrongs persist in their effects even when the counterfactual comparison is complicated by non-identity.

Another response, developed by Fabre (2007: 110–138), is that reparative obligations can be grounded not in the welfare harm to specific individuals but in the continuing structural injustice — the unjust distribution of resources, opportunities, and social status — that results from historical wrongs. Even if we cannot identify specific individuals who were made worse off by comparison to a non-identity-problem-free alternative, we can identify structural injustices that persist and demand rectification.

## The Persistence of Obligation

Whatever one thinks of the specific debates about reparations, the philosophical framework of historical injustice raises deep questions about the temporal structure of moral obligation. Obligations, it seems, do not simply expire with time. The effects of unjust actions persist, and where those effects constitute ongoing injustice, obligations of rectification persist with them.

At the same time, the supersession thesis is not without force. The passage of time does change moral circumstances, and a purely retrospective account of obligation — one that mechanically tracks historical chains of wrongdoing regardless of present circumstances — can itself generate injustice. The challenge is to identify when historical claims retain their force and when changed circumstances diminish or supersede them — a genuinely difficult question that requires both philosophical rigor and careful attention to the specific facts of each case.
