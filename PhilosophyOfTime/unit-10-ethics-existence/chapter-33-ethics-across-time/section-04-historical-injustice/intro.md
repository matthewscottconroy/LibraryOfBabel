# Section 33.4: Historical Injustice

---

## Section Introduction

The discussion so far has focused on our obligations to future generations — people who do not yet exist. But temporal ethics also reaches backward: we are shaped by, and in some respects implicated in, injustices that occurred before we were born. The legacies of slavery, colonization, genocide, and other historical wrongs continue to structure the social, economic, and political conditions under which we live. The question of whether these legacies generate present obligations of rectification or reparation is both philosophically difficult and politically contentious.

The philosophical difficulties are significant. Historical wrongs were committed by people now dead against victims now dead. The agents of the injustice are gone; the direct victims are gone. What grounds obligations for people who were not the perpetrators, toward people who were not the direct victims?

This section examines two major contributions to this question: Nozick's historical entitlement theory, which provides a framework for thinking about rectification, and Waldron's "supersession thesis," which argues that historical injustice claims can be overtaken by subsequent changes that alter the moral landscape.
