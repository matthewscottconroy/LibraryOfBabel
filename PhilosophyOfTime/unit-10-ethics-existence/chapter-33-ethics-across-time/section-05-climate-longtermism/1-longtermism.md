# 33.5.1 The Longtermist Thesis

---

## The Basic Argument

Longtermism, as developed by MacAskill (*What We Owe the Future*, 2022) and Ord (*The Precipice*, 2020), rests on three claims:

(1) *The scale of the future is enormous*. If humanity survives and flourishes for thousands or millions of years, the number of future people who will live will vastly exceed the number currently alive — perhaps by a factor of billions or more.

(2) *Future welfare matters*. Pure time preference is indefensible; the welfare of future people has the same moral weight, per person, as the welfare of present people.

(3) *Our actions now can significantly affect the long-run trajectory*. By reducing existential risks (risks that could permanently truncate humanity's future), improving institutions, and cultivating values and practices that can sustain a long-run flourishing, we can have an enormous positive impact on the total amount of welfare that will exist.

From (1) and (2), it follows that the expected welfare at stake in decisions that affect the long-run trajectory is astronomically large — it may dwarf the welfare at stake in even the most significant short-run interventions. From (3), it follows that some of our actions can significantly influence this enormous welfare stake. The conclusion is that working to improve the long-run trajectory — reducing existential risk, building robust institutions, preventing civilizational collapse — is among the most important moral priorities available.

## Philosophical Foundations

The longtermist argument does not depend on any single metaethical theory. MacAskill (2022: 12–30) presents it as following from any plausible theory that takes seriously the welfare of all persons, appropriately discounted for uncertainty, without applying pure temporal discount.

On a utilitarian view, the expected value of reducing existential risk is enormous: reducing the probability of human extinction by even a small percentage has an expected value equal to a large fraction of the enormous total welfare of all future people. On a rights-based view, future people have rights, including rights not to be deprived of existence by our current choices. On a contractualist view, principles of justice cannot reasonably exclude the interests of future people. The convergence across frameworks strengthens the longtermist conclusion.

Greaves and MacAskill (2021) develop the view formally, calling it "strong longtermism": the thesis that the most important determinant of the moral value of current actions is their expected effect on the long-run future. This is a claim about relative moral importance, not about ignoring present needs.

## Existential Risk

Ord (*The Precipice*, 2020: 47–68) provides a systematic assessment of existential risks — risks that could permanently curtail humanity's long-run potential. These include asteroid impacts, pandemic diseases (natural and engineered), climate change, nuclear war, and advanced artificial intelligence misalignment. Ord argues that the aggregate probability of existential catastrophe in the next century is significant — perhaps as high as one in six — and that reducing these risks by even a small amount has enormous expected value given the scale of what is at stake.

The concept of "existential risk" is broader than extinction risk. It includes "civilizational collapse" — scenarios in which humanity survives but is permanently locked into a much worse trajectory (such as stable global totalitarianism that permanently eliminates the possibility of flourishing). The badness of these scenarios, on the longtermist view, is proportional to the long-run flourishing they preclude.
