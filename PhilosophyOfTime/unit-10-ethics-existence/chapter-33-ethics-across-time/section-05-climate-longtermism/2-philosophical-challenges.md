# 33.5.2 Philosophical Challenges to Longtermism

---

## The Astronomical Stakes Problem

The longtermist argument relies on the enormous scale of the potential future to generate enormous moral urgency. But this reliance creates a problem: the conclusions seem to follow too strongly. If reducing existential risk by even a tiny fraction has greater expected value than any intervention aimed at present welfare, then it seems to follow that all other moral priorities are dwarfed by existential risk reduction. Helping people who are currently suffering from poverty, disease, or oppression matters comparatively little.

This conclusion is uncomfortable, and it may reflect a flaw in the argument rather than a genuine moral truth. Beckstead (2013: 58–79) defends a version of this implication; critics argue that it is a reductio of the longtermist position.

There are several responses. First, the longtermist need not claim that *only* the long run matters. If the three longtermist premises are right, the long run is *among* the most important considerations — but current suffering still matters morally, and obligations to relieve it are not eliminated. The practical implication is not "ignore current suffering" but "give appropriate weight to both."

Second, the astronomical stakes problem arises only if the expected probability of affecting the long run is high enough. If it is very uncertain whether specific current actions will have a positive effect on the long-run future — if we are in a state of "cluelessness" about long-run effects — then the expected value calculation becomes uncertain in a way that may reduce the practical urgency of longtermist interventions.

## Cluelessness

The most philosophically serious challenge to longtermism is the "cluelessness" problem, developed by Lenman (2000: 342–370) and extended by Greaves (2016: 311–339). The argument is this: our actions have causal consequences that ripple through time, and many of those consequences are entirely unpredictable. In fact, for most of our actions, the indirect long-run effects swamp the direct short-run effects in scale — but we have no reliable way to predict what those long-run effects will be.

If we are systematically clueless about the long-run effects of our actions, then expected value calculations over long-run outcomes are unreliable. The uncertainty is not merely quantitative (we could be off by a factor of ten) but structural: we do not know which direction the effects will go, whether our current interventions will help or harm the long-run trajectory, or even whether our models of the relevant causal processes are correct.

Cluelessness does not merely reduce the expected value of long-run interventions; it undermines the expected-value framework itself as a guide to long-run decision-making. If we cannot meaningfully estimate expected values over long causal chains, we may need a different decision procedure — one based on robustness, risk-aversion, or more local causal reasoning.

## Non-Identity Applied to Longtermism

As MacAskill acknowledges (2022: 157–178), the non-identity problem applies with full force to longtermist interventions. Actions taken to improve the long-run trajectory affect which people will exist in the long run, not merely how well they will fare. A successful longtermist intervention — one that steers civilization toward a better trajectory — will typically bring into existence different people from those who would have existed in the unreformed scenario.

This means that the longtermist cannot straightforwardly claim that their interventions benefit the future people who live in the improved world, since those people would not have existed in the unreformed world. The non-identity problem threatens to undermine the person-affecting rationale for long-run interventions just as it threatens short-run environmental policy.

Longtermists typically respond by moving to an impersonal framework: what matters is the quality of the long-run future, not the welfare of specific identifiable individuals. But this response invites the Repugnant Conclusion, which impersonal frameworks must confront.

## Demandingness and Opportunity Costs

A further challenge is practical demandingness. If longtermism is correct, then the most effective use of moral resources (time, money, political capital) is their direction toward existential risk reduction and long-run trajectory improvement. This may require a significant redirection of current moral priorities — including reductions in spending on immediate welfare, poverty relief, and justice. The demandingness of the conclusion may reflect either a genuine moral truth that should motivate more radical action, or an artifact of an ethical framework that is too abstract to capture the full range of morally relevant considerations.

The practical philosopher might respond: even if longtermism is correct in principle, the institutions, character, and motivations required for a successful long-run moral project are cultivated through the same habits and practices that sustain short-run moral engagement. The redirection of moral concern from the present to the distant future may not be motivationally or institutionally sustainable.

## Assessment

Longtermism is a serious philosophical position that takes seriously the implications of egalitarian concern for all persons, across time. Its proponents have engaged honestly with its challenges and limitations. The philosophical challenges — cluelessness, non-identity, demandingness — are genuine and not fully resolved. But the core insight — that the long-run future matters, that existential risk deserves serious moral attention, that present generations have responsibilities to those who will come after — is both philosophically defensible and practically urgent. The question is not whether the future matters, but how to reason carefully about an enormously uncertain and enormously important domain.
