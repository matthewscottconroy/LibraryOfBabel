# Unit X: Time, Ethics, and Existence

---

## Unit Introduction

Every philosophical inquiry into time eventually arrives at the same threshold: the question of what time means for a life. We have studied time as a metaphysical structure, as a physical reality, as a feature of experience, and as a puzzle about identity through change. But there is a dimension of time that none of those inquiries quite captures — the dimension in which time is not merely something we *have* but something we *are*, something that shapes our choices, our responsibilities, our deaths, and our obligations to people not yet born.

This unit turns from the theoretical to what we might call the existentially urgent. That does not mean abandoning rigor; if anything, the questions here demand more of us, because the stakes are higher and the temptations to wishful thinking are stronger. We want free will to be real. We want death not to matter. We want our obligations to future generations to be manageable. Philosophy's task is not to tell us what we want to hear, but to trace the arguments wherever they lead — and then to ask, with honesty, what follows for how we should live.

Chapter 31 takes up the oldest question in this neighborhood: the problem of free will. The connection to time is not accidental. Fatalism — the view that what will happen is in some sense already settled — has been formulated in terms of the logic of future-tense sentences, the omniscience of a timeless God, and the ontological completeness of the block universe. Each formulation raises the same underlying fear: if the future is in any sense "already there," can our choices make any real difference? We trace this problem from Diodorus Cronus and Aristotle through medieval theology to contemporary debates about compatibilism and agent causation.

Chapter 32 confronts mortality directly. Epicurus famously argued that death is nothing to us — but this argument, for all its elegant economy, has never quite put the fear of death to rest, perhaps because the fear is not entirely irrational. We examine the deprivation account of death's badness, the notorious "location problem" it generates, Heidegger's phenomenological alternative, and the question of whether immortality would even be desirable.

Chapter 33 extends ethical reflection across time in both directions. We owe things to future people — but which future people, and why, and how much? Parfit's non-identity problem reveals a deep puzzle: our major decisions affect which people will come to exist, not merely how well existing people will fare. The chapter also examines the ethics of historical injustice, the question of discounting future welfare, and the longtermist thesis that the astronomical scale of the future makes it the dominant moral consideration.

These are not abstract exercises. Climate change, nuclear risk, institutional design, and daily choices about how to live all hinge on the questions examined here. Philosophy of time, at this level, is philosophy for how we inhabit our brief moment in a vast temporal order we did not choose and will not live to see resolved.
