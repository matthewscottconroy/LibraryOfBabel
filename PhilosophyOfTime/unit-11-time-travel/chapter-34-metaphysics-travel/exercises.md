# Chapter 34 Exercises: The Metaphysics of Time Travel

---

## Conceptual Exercises

**1. Lewis's Definition**
Using Lewis's definition of time travel as a discrepancy between personal and external time, classify the following cases as time travel, non-travel, or borderline, and justify your classification: (a) you age normally while on Earth, then live in a computer simulation for 1000 subjective years before exiting to find only 5 external minutes have passed; (b) you are placed in suspended animation and "wake up" 200 years later; (c) you travel at close to the speed of light for 10 personal years and return to find 100 external years have passed; (d) a magic spell transports you instantaneously to 1850. What does each case reveal about Lewis's definition?

**2. The Grandfather Paradox**
State the grandfather paradox as an argument with explicit premises. Identify which premise Lewis denies and explain his reason. Is Lewis's denial of that premise ad hoc — motivated only by the desire to avoid paradox — or does it rest on independently motivated philosophical principles? What is the philosophical price of Lewis's resolution?

**3. Context-Sensitivity of "Can"**
Lewis argues that "can" claims are context-sensitive: in one context, T can kill her grandfather; in another, she cannot. Evaluate this claim. What contextual factors determine which sense of "can" applies? Does this resolution preserve the intuitive force of the claim that T has genuine freedom of action in the past? Is there a context in which Lewis should say T "cannot" do anything during the time travel episode?

**4. Bootstrap Paradoxes**
Construct your own bootstrap paradox — one that is different from the Shakespeare/Hamlet example. (You might use a mathematical proof, an invention, or a piece of music.) Identify the explanatory gap: what question about the origins of the information is left unanswered? How does the Novikov self-consistency principle (Section 35.4) address this gap? Is it a satisfying answer?

**5. The Branching Solution**
Lewis claims that the branching timeline solution changes the subject: it does not resolve the grandfather paradox but substitutes a different scenario (inter-universe travel) for the original case (backward travel to one's own past). Evaluate this objection. Is the distinction between "traveling to one's own past" and "traveling to the past of another branch" metaphysically well-defined? If personal identity is constituted by psychological continuity (Parfit), does the distinction survive?

**6. Meeting Yourself: Endurantism vs. Perdurantism**
Explain how endurantism and perdurantism handle the case of a 30-year-old meeting her 70-year-old self. Which theory handles the case more cleanly? Are there features of the meeting-yourself case that create problems for perdurantism that are not problems for endurantism? Can psychological continuity theory adjudicate between the two approaches in this case?

**7. Self-Interaction and Consistency**
If a time traveler can interact with her past self, consider the following cases: (a) the 70-year-old gives the 30-year-old detailed investment advice that makes the 30-year-old wealthy; (b) the 70-year-old warns the 30-year-old about a dangerous decision, which the 30-year-old avoids; (c) the 30-year-old, out of curiosity, attempts to stab the 70-year-old. In each case, apply Lewis's consistency constraint to determine what must happen. Does the consistency constraint preserve or undermine the 30-year-old's freedom?

## Discussion and Essay Questions

**8. Freedom and Consistency**
Some philosophers argue that backward time travel is incompatible with genuine freedom, because the consistency constraint determines in advance what the time traveler can and cannot do. Others argue that the consistency constraint is no more freedom-eliminating than ordinary causal constraints. Which view do you find more persuasive, and why? How does this debate connect to the discussion of fatalism in Chapter 31?

**9. Science Fiction and Philosophical Clarity**
Choose a time travel narrative from fiction (film, novel, or television) and analyze it philosophically. Does the narrative use the single-timeline model or the branching model? Is it internally consistent? Does it face any of the paradoxes discussed in this chapter? What philosophical view about time does it implicitly endorse?

## Lab and Observational Exercises

**10. Time Dilation Verification**
Research the famous Hafele-Keating experiment (1971–72), in which atomic clocks on aircraft were found to lose or gain time relative to ground-based clocks, in accordance with the predictions of special and general relativity. Summarize what was done and what was found. How does this experiment demonstrate that forward time travel (in Lewis's sense) actually occurs? What would an analogous experiment for backward time travel require, and why is it physically different?

**11. Science Fiction Survey**
Examine at least three different science fiction time travel narratives and categorize their implicit philosophical positions on the following questions: (a) single timeline or branching timelines? (b) can the past be "changed"? (c) do the narratives face the grandfather paradox or bootstrap paradox? (d) how is personal identity handled when the traveler meets their past or future self? Present your analysis as a comparative table and a 300-word reflection.

**12. Consistency Puzzles**
Design your own time travel scenario that involves at least two self-consistency constraints (one avoiding a grandfather-type paradox, one involving a bootstrap-type loop). Trace through the causal structure carefully, verifying that the scenario is self-consistent on Lewis's single-timeline view. Present the scenario in diagram form (a causal graph with events and arrows) and annotate each arrow with the relevant causal relationship.
