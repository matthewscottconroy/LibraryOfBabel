# Chapter 34 Important Researchers: The Metaphysics of Time Travel

---

**David Lewis (1941–2001)**
Possibly the most technically accomplished and original analytic philosopher of the twentieth century, Lewis made foundational contributions to modal logic, possible worlds semantics, philosophy of language, metaphysics, philosophy of mind, and philosophy of science. His 1976 paper "The Paradoxes of Time Travel" is the canonical philosophical treatment of time travel and the natural starting point for any serious philosophical engagement with the subject. Lewis's definition of time travel as a discrepancy between personal and external time, his resolution of the grandfather paradox using context-sensitive ability attributions, and his four-dimensionalist framework are all indispensable tools. His broader metaphysical project — realism about possible worlds, four-dimensionalism, Humean supervenience — provides the framework within which his time travel analysis fits most naturally.

**Derek Parfit (1942–2017)**
See entries in Chapters 32 and 33. Parfit's contribution to Chapter 34 lies primarily in his theory of personal identity: the psychological continuity view, on which what matters in survival is not strict numerical identity but overlapping chains of psychological connection. Applied to time travel, this theory raises the question of what makes the 30-year-old and the 70-year-old versions of the traveler "the same person" — and whether the answer to that question matters for the metaphysics of the meeting-yourself case.

**Ted Sider (b. 1966)**
Contemporary metaphysician at Cornell University whose book *Four-Dimensionalism* (2001) is the most rigorous contemporary defense of perdurantism. Sider's work provides the technical framework within which Lewis's time travel analysis is most cleanly situated. His 2002 paper "Time Travel, Coincidences, and Counterfactuals" examines challenges to Lewis's resolution of the grandfather paradox involving cases of temporal coincidence. Sider has also contributed importantly to debates about ontology, logic, and modality.

**Nicholas J. J. Smith**
Contemporary philosopher at the University of Sydney whose work on time travel has challenged Lewis's permissive view. Smith's 1997 paper "Bananas Enough for Time Travel?" argues that causal loops are metaphysically impossible — not merely strange or explanatorily incomplete, but ruled out by a metaphysical principle about causes and effects. Smith's work provides an important counterweight to Lewis's easy acceptance of causal loop possibility. He has also written on uncertainty, probability, and vagueness.

**Ryan Wasserman**
Contemporary philosopher at Western Washington University whose book *Paradoxes of Time Travel* (2018) is the most comprehensive recent philosophical treatment of time travel paradoxes. Wasserman examines the grandfather paradox, bootstrap paradoxes, the branching solution, and related issues with great care and accessibility. He defends a broadly Lewisian approach while engaging seriously with recent challenges. The book is the best current resource for students wanting to work carefully through the philosophical issues.
