# Chapter 34: The Metaphysics of Time Travel

---

## Chapter Introduction

Time travel is one of those topics where popular imagination and serious philosophy make unexpected contact. The H. G. Wells image of a contraption that whisks you back to the Jurassic, or forward to the end of civilization, is fantasy. But behind the fantasy lies a genuine conceptual structure that rewards careful analysis. What would it mean, exactly, for someone to travel through time? What are the logical constraints on what could happen during a time travel episode? And what do these constraints reveal about the structure of time itself?

This chapter proceeds through five conceptual problems. We begin with Lewis's definition of time travel — the clearest and most influential analysis available — and establish what the concept requires. We then turn to the grandfather paradox, which has been the central puzzle in the literature since at least the 1940s, and work through Lewis's resolution with the care it deserves. We examine bootstrap paradoxes, which are in some ways more troubling than the grandfather paradox because they raise the possibility of causally self-sustaining loops with no external origin. We consider the many-worlds solution to time travel paradoxes and ask whether it genuinely resolves the problems or substitutes a different narrative. And finally we ask the personal identity question: if you meet yourself during a time travel episode, what exactly is the ontological situation?

Throughout, two themes recur. The first is the relationship between what is *logically possible* and what is *physically possible* in a world with backward time travel. Lewis's contribution was to show that backward time travel is logically consistent — no formal contradiction follows from it — and that the apparent paradoxes can be resolved. Whether it is physically possible is a different question, addressed in Chapter 35. The second theme is the tension between time travel and our ordinary intuitions about causation, agency, and the direction of time. Time travel puts these intuitions under maximum pressure and forces us to decide which to keep and which to revise.
