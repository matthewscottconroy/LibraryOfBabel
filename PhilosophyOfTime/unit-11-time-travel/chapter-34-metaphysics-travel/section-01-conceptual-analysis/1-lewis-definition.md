# 34.1.1 Lewis's Definition of Time Travel

---

## The Two Times

Lewis opens his 1976 paper with a deceptively simple observation: time travel requires a discrepancy between two ways of measuring time — *external time* and *personal time*.

*External time* is the time measured by clocks, calendars, and all the ordinary instruments we use to locate events in the temporal order of the world. It is the time coordinate of the background spacetime — the time that tells us that Tuesday comes after Monday, that 2025 comes after 2024, that the Battle of Hastings occurred before the signing of the Magna Carta. External time is, roughly, the time of physics and history.

*Personal time* is the time measured by the traveler's own processes — their biological aging, their psychological experience of duration, the elapsed time on a clock they carry with them. For ordinary people moving through ordinary spacetime, personal time and external time match closely: if you age a year, a year has passed in the external calendar. The two measures are not identical — they differ even in ordinary relativistic circumstances, when a traveler moving at high velocity ages less than a stay-at-home twin — but they are normally correlated.

Time travel, on Lewis's definition, occurs when these two times *diverge* systematically: "An object time travels iff it departs from a time and arrives at a different time, such that the duration of the journey by external time does not equal the duration by the traveler's personal time" (Lewis 1976: 145). The departure and arrival are in external time; the duration of the journey is in both. When they differ, time travel has occurred.

## Forward and Backward Time Travel

Lewis's definition accommodates two importantly different cases.

*Forward time travel* occurs when personal time runs slower than external time: the traveler ages a short subjective time while a longer external time elapses. This is precisely what relativistic time dilation produces: a traveler moving at high velocity relative to an external reference frame ages less. If you travel at close to the speed of light for what feels to you like one year, you might return to find that ten years have passed externally. You have, in Lewis's sense, traveled to the future: you departed in 2024 and arrived in 2034, while your personal clock showed only one year elapsed.

This form of time travel has been experimentally confirmed in the laboratory: particles moving at high velocity decay more slowly than stationary particles, and atomic clocks on fast aircraft run slightly slower than identical clocks on the ground (Hafele and Keating 1972). We already live in a world where forward time travel, in this technical sense, occurs.

*Backward time travel* is the more philosophically interesting case. Here personal time runs in the *same direction* (you age normally) but *in the opposite direction relative to external time*: you arrive at an external time *earlier* than you departed. You leave in 2024 and arrive in 1940. Your personal clock shows a journey of, say, five minutes; but in external time, you have moved 84 years into the past.

This case is not ruled out by Lewis's definition, and that is one of his points: the concept is coherent. Whether backward time travel is physically possible is a separate question. But it does not involve a logical contradiction in the concept itself.

## What the Definition Accomplishes

Lewis's definition accomplishes several things. First, it explains why time travel is not simply "being transported to another time." A rock sitting in the ground for a million years is in a sense "transported" from the past to the future — but it does not time travel because there is no discrepancy between its personal and external time. A time traveler must have a personal time that diverges from external time during the journey.

Second, it shows that time travel and relativistic effects are on a continuum. The relativity of simultaneity and time dilation already produce mild discrepancies between personal and external time. Backward time travel is a more dramatic version of the same structural feature.

Third, it separates the concept from any particular physical mechanism. Lewis is not committed to a specific physical account of how the discrepancy is produced — wormholes, closed timelike curves, or fictional time machines. The definition is neutral on mechanism: what matters is the discrepancy, not its source.

## Problems the Definition Leaves Open

What the definition does not settle is the physical possibility of backward time travel. Lewis uses the definition to set up his analysis of the paradoxes, arguing that if backward time travel were to occur, certain constraints would apply. But whether it can occur depends on the physics of spacetime — the subject of Chapter 35.

The definition also does not fully settle questions about personal identity. When a traveler returns to an earlier external time, there are two versions of the traveler present at that time — the one who has arrived from the future, and the one who is currently living through that period. Lewis's framework treats these as *temporal stages* of a single persisting person, connected by personal time rather than external time. This raises questions about what it means to meet your own earlier self, which we address in Section 34.5.

Finally, the definition does not by itself resolve the paradoxes of time travel. It shows that the concept is coherent — personal time and external time can diverge — but the logical constraints this places on what can happen during a time travel episode require further analysis.
