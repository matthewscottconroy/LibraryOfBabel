# 34.2.1 The Grandfather Paradox

---

## The Standard Formulation

The grandfather paradox was probably formulated in one form or another by early science fiction writers, but its philosophical canonization is largely due to Lewis's 1976 paper. Here is a clean formulation:

Suppose a time traveler (call her T) travels backward in time to a period before her grandfather (G) has conceived T's parent. T, knowing some history, locates G and kills him. G dies before siring T's parent. Therefore T's parent is never born. Therefore T is never born. Therefore T never travels back in time. Therefore G is never killed by T. But then G lives, T's parent is born, and T is eventually born, travels back in time, and kills G. Contradiction.

The argument has the logical form of a reductio: assume backward time travel is possible and that T kills G; contradiction follows; therefore at least one of the premises must be false. The most natural candidate is the premise that backward time travel is possible — hence the conclusion that it is not.

But Lewis thinks this is too quick. The reductio shows that T will not kill G; it does not show that backward time travel is impossible. The key is to distinguish between what T *can* do and what T *will* do.

## Lewis's Resolution: The Logical Constraint

Lewis's analysis starts from an ontological commitment: in a world with backward time travel, there is only one spacetime. The past is the past; there is no "duplicate" past waiting to be altered. When T arrives in the past, she is in *the* past — the same past that, from the perspective of the original timeline, is already fixed.

On this view, the question "what would happen if T killed G?" is not well-formed in the relevant sense. It is not a question about what *would* happen in a counterfactual scenario; it is a question about what *does* happen in the actual spacetime. And what does happen is that T does not kill G — because G's death would prevent T's existence, which would prevent T's time travel, which would prevent G's death. The actual history is self-consistent: T travels back, some events occur, and T fails to kill G.

The grandfather paradox does not prove that time travel is impossible. It proves only that if time travel occurs, certain things will not happen — specifically, things that would produce self-contradictions. This is a constraint on the contents of time travel episodes, not a refutation of time travel itself.

## The Metaphysical Picture

The metaphysical picture Lewis relies on is that of the block universe: the entire history of the universe is a fixed, four-dimensional structure. In this structure, either T kills G or T does not kill G. If T kills G, a contradiction follows, so T does not kill G. The actual history is self-consistent. There is no "changing the past" — the past, in the block universe, cannot be changed any more than the layout of a city can be "changed" by driving different routes through it. There is only the actual history, which either includes T's time travel or does not.

This picture has an initially vertiginous quality: if the past cannot be changed, then T's presence in the past was always part of the actual history. If T meets her grandfather as a young man during her time travel episode, that meeting was always a fact about the past — it is part of the fixed four-dimensional history. T does not "change" the past by showing up in it; she is simply occupying a portion of the spacetime that has always included her visit.
