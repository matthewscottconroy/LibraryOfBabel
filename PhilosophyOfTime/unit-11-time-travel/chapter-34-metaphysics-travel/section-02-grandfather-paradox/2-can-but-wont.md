# 34.2.2 Lewis's "Can but Won't" Analysis

---

## The Analysis Stated

The core of Lewis's resolution of the grandfather paradox is the distinction between two senses of "can." In one sense — call it the consistency-respecting sense — T cannot kill her grandfather, because if she did, a contradiction would follow. In another sense — call it the ability sense — T can try to kill her grandfather, because she has the physical ability, the knowledge, the opportunity, and the means. She has a gun, she knows where G will be, she is physically capable of pulling the trigger.

Lewis (1976: 150–152) argues that ability claims are context-sensitive. Whether we say that someone *can* do something depends on what features of the situation we hold fixed. Holding fixed only the facts immediately at hand — T's physical capacities, her knowledge, the available means — T can kill G. Holding fixed the entire causal history of the spacetime, including the fact that T exists (which entails that G was not killed before siring T's parent), T cannot kill G — because doing so would contradict a fact we are holding fixed.

Both ability attributions are correct in their contexts. There is no deep inconsistency between them. The apparent paradox arises from a failure to notice that "can" is context-sensitive and that different truth conditions can hold in different contexts of attribution.

## The Attempt Without Success

The deeper point is that the consistency constraint does not make T's time travel episode "predetermined" in some mysterious way. T will be in the presence of G, she will have the opportunity and the means to kill him, she will perhaps try — and something will go wrong. Not because of any magical force preventing her, but for ordinary causal reasons: the gun jams, she hesitates, G moves at the critical moment, a passer-by intervenes. These ordinary causal factors are sufficient to ensure that G is not killed, given the self-consistency requirement.

Lewis is emphatic that this does not require any mysterious "protection" of the timeline. The spacetime history is simply what it is. If T tries to kill G and fails, the failure has an ordinary causal explanation. The self-consistency of the spacetime is not a separate law that intervenes; it is simply a feature of the single, fixed history that exists.

This has an important implication: the constraints imposed by time travel consistency are not distinguishable, from the inside, from ordinary causal constraints. T's experience of failing to kill G does not reveal anything supernatural; she just encounters ordinary obstacles. The consistency constraint works through ordinary causal mechanisms, not through violations of them.

## Assessment: Is the Resolution Satisfying?

Lewis's resolution has been widely discussed and widely challenged. Here are the main lines of assessment.

*In favor*: The resolution is internally consistent and rests on a principled analysis of ability attributions. It shows that backward time travel does not logically imply the ability to create paradoxes; the consistency constraint is a natural consequence of the single-timeline view. It avoids postulating mysterious forces or metaphysically special "protection mechanisms."

*Against*: Several philosophers find the resolution too quick. Sider (2002: 122–131) objects that Lewis's consistency constraint seems to make time travel "fatalistic" in an unacceptable way: if T is fated to fail in her attempt to kill G, this seems like a violation of free will. T tries to do something; she has the ability (in the ordinary sense); and yet, somehow, she always fails. Doesn't this violate the principle that agents can generally succeed at things they are able and trying to do?

Lewis responds that there is no special problem here. We routinely accept that someone might try to do something and fail, for causal reasons. The time travel case is no different in principle: T fails because of some ordinary causal factor. The consistency constraint is not a new kind of causal force but a selection principle over which causal histories are possible.

A deeper worry is that the single-timeline view leaves the traveler's choices without their ordinary significance. If the past is fixed, then T's "choice" to try or not to try to kill G is already embedded in the fixed spacetime. Does she genuinely have a choice? This is the block universe / free will worry that we explored in Chapter 31, now applied to the specific case of time travel. Lewis's answer is the standard compatibilist one: freedom is about the right kind of causal relationship between deliberation and action, not about the openness of the timeline. But many readers find this unsatisfying in the time travel context in ways they do not in ordinary contexts.
