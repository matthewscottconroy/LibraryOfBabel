# 34.3.1 Causal Loops and Bootstrap Paradoxes

---

## The Structure of a Causal Loop

Consider the following scenario. A time traveler visits Shakespeare in 1600, carrying a manuscript of *Hamlet*. She shows the manuscript to Shakespeare, who copies it, publishes it, and it becomes the famous play. The traveler then acquires a copy of the published play in the twenty-first century and carries it back to 1600. Where did the play come from?

This is a bootstrap paradox. The manuscript exists — it is a real physical object, the copy of the play that the traveler carries back. Its existence is causally explained: it was published by Shakespeare, who had a copy from the traveler. But the traveler's copy was caused by the publication, which was caused by the traveler's earlier copy, which was caused by the publication, and so on. The causal chain circles back on itself without ever grounding out in a creative act. The play seems to have "created itself."

More precisely: the sequence of events forms an *ontological causal loop* — a closed causal sequence in which the later events are partially caused by the earlier ones, and the earlier events are partially caused by the later ones. The loop is logically consistent (no contradiction follows from it) but explanatorily incomplete: within the loop itself, there is no point at which the play *originates*.

## The Information Problem

The bootstrap paradox is sometimes called the "information paradox" of time travel. Information — the content of the manuscript — circulates in a closed loop without ever being created. In the standard case, when we explain how a piece of information came to exist, we trace it back to some creative act: someone wrote it, invented it, discovered it. In the bootstrap scenario, no creative act occurs within the loop. The information simply *is*, circulating self-referentially.

This seems to violate something like a principle of informational conservation or informational causation: information cannot arise from nothing; it must always have a cause that explains its content. Causal loops seem to violate this principle by allowing information to exist without any ultimate source.

The physicist and philosopher David Deutsch (see Chapter 35) has argued that this feature of causal loops — the "free creation" of information — is the deepest problem with time travel, more fundamental than the grandfather paradox. In a causal loop, the laws of physics seem to permit the existence of information that has no causal origin outside the loop, which Deutsch argues is deeply at odds with computational and informational intuitions about physical reality.

## The Novikov Self-Consistency Principle

The Russian physicist Igor Novikov proposed a self-consistency principle for spacetimes with closed timelike curves: *only self-consistent solutions to the field equations are physically realized* (Friedman et al. 1990: 1915–1922). This principle holds that the laws of physics automatically select histories that are self-consistent — histories in which nothing contradictory happens, and in which causal loops, if they exist, are genuinely consistent.

Applied to bootstrap paradoxes, the Novikov principle says: causal loops are possible (they are self-consistent) and will actually occur whenever the initial conditions permit. The information in the loop is not "uncaused" in a problematic sense; it is caused by the structure of the entire loop, which is a lawful feature of the spacetime.

This is a striking philosophical position. It implies that the explanatory model appropriate to time travel is holistic rather than linear: we do not explain events in terms of prior causes in a linear chain, but in terms of the self-consistency of the entire history. The causal loop is its own explanation, in a sense — each event within it is explained by the others.

## Are Causal Loops Metaphysically Possible?

Whether causal loops are genuinely metaphysically possible — and not merely logically consistent — is contested. Lewis argues that they are possible: a consistent spacetime history containing causal loops is a possible world, and logical consistency is sufficient for possibility. The information in the loop exists without a prior cause outside the loop, but this does not mean it exists without a cause at all: it is caused by the earlier events in the loop, which are themselves caused by the later events.

Smith (1997: 361–384) challenges this, arguing that causal loops violate a principle he calls the "Causal Principle": every event has a cause that is *not* among its effects. In a causal loop, every event is an effect of its causes, but those causes are themselves (indirectly) effects of that very event. This is not a logical contradiction, but Smith argues it is a metaphysical impossibility — the kind of thing that, while formally consistent, cannot be instantiated in any genuinely possible world.

## The Explanatory Strangeness

What most people find objectionable about bootstrap paradoxes is not that they are formally inconsistent but that they leave something unexplained. Even if the causal loop is logically and physically possible, there seems to be a question it cannot answer: why does the loop have the content it has, rather than some other content? The play could have been *Hamlet* or it could have been anything else; the causal loop selects *Hamlet* — but nothing in the loop explains *why*. The loop's content is, in a sense, a brute fact.

This explanatory strangeness may be irreducible. It might be a cost we must accept if backward time travel is possible. Or it might reflect a deep constraint — a reason to think that causal loops, however logically consistent, cannot actually be realized. Physics, so far, does not definitively settle the question; philosophy can identify what is at stake.
