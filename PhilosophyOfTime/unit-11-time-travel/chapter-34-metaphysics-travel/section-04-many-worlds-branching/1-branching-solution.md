# 34.4.1 Branching Timelines and the Resolution of Paradoxes

---

## The Branching Proposal

The basic idea is simple: when a time traveler moves backward in time, she does not arrive in her own past. Instead, a new causal branch is created (or she is transported to a pre-existing parallel branch), and she arrives in that branch's past. In this branch, she can do anything she likes — including killing "her grandfather" — without generating any contradiction, because the grandfather she kills is the grandfather of her *counterpart* in that branch, not her actual grandfather in the original branch from which she came.

This view has several sources. It can be motivated by the Everettian many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, in which each quantum measurement event generates a branching of the wavefunction into multiple branches (Everett 1957). If the universe already contains many parallel branches, perhaps time travel is a mechanism by which one branch can "branch off" further, or by which a traveler from one branch arrives in another.

It can also be motivated by science fiction convention: the "alternate history" trope, in which changes to the past produce alternate timelines rather than altering the traveler's own history. The appeal of this narrative device is precisely that it allows time travelers to act freely in the past without paradox.

## Does the Branching Solution Resolve the Grandfather Paradox?

At first glance, the branching solution seems to dissolve the grandfather paradox entirely. The paradox arose because a single timeline produces a contradiction: if T kills G, T is never born, so T never goes back to kill G, so G survives, so T is born and goes back... The contradiction follows because we are considering a single timeline in which both G's death and T's existence must find a place.

On the branching view, there is no such contradiction. In the original branch (Branch A), G survives, T is born, T uses a time machine, and departs. She arrives in Branch B, which has branched off from Branch A at the moment of arrival. In Branch B, she kills G. No contradiction: T is alive in Branch B (she arrived from Branch A, where she existed). G dies in Branch B. T never returns to Branch A; she remains in Branch B.

Everything is self-consistent. The paradox evaporates. One might object that T has now lost access to her original timeline and is stranded in a branch from which her original world cannot be recovered — but this is not a logical contradiction; it is merely a logistical inconvenience.

## The Subject-Changing Objection

Lewis (1976: 149) makes the most pointed objection: the branching solution does not resolve the grandfather paradox; it changes the subject. The question was whether *backward time travel* is possible — whether a traveler can go to *her own past* and interact with the people and events that are part of her own history. The branching solution provides something different: it allows a traveler to go to the past of a *different branch*, not her own.

But going to the past of a different branch is not the same as going to one's own past. If T arrives in Branch B, which is a branch she has no prior connection to, she is not time traveling — she is inter-universe traveling. The "past" she arrives in is not the past of her life history; it is the past of a different world. This is a different phenomenon and deserves a different name.

The grandfather paradox asks whether a traveler can go back to a time before her own birth in her own causal history and kill her grandfather there. The branching solution, on Lewis's reading, does not answer this question; it bypasses it by substituting a different scenario.

## Is "Branching Time Travel" Coherent?

Defenders of the branching approach might respond that the distinction Lewis draws — between "traveling to one's own past" and "traveling to the past of another branch" — is itself unclear. What makes a branch "one's own"? Is it the branch one originated in? Is it the branch causally connected to one's history?

On some views of personal identity — particularly Parfit's psychological continuity theory — what matters is not which causal branch one comes from but the psychological continuity of one's experiences and memories. If the branching time traveler is psychologically continuous with the person who entered the time machine in Branch A, she may have as strong a claim to being "the same person" as any other time traveler.

On this view, the branching solution does provide a form of time travel — just not the single-timeline form that Lewis analyzes. Whether this form is as interesting as the single-timeline version depends on what questions one wants time travel to help answer. If the question is "can you interact with *your* historical past?", the branching solution doesn't help. If the question is "can you interact with a historically similar past?", the branching solution works fine.

## Connections to the Many-Worlds Interpretation

The relationship between the branching time travel solution and the Everettian many-worlds interpretation (MWI) of quantum mechanics (see Chapter 27) is interesting but not straightforward. In the MWI, each quantum event generates a branching of the wavefunction, producing separate branches that do not interact after branching. Time travel, in the sense of returning to an earlier state of one's own branch, is not obviously available in the MWI — the branches diverge forward, not backward.

Some interpretations of the MWI, combined with speculative physics about closed timelike curves, might allow for inter-branch access. But this requires significant theoretical development beyond the standard MWI. The connection between many-worlds physics and branching time travel philosophy is largely a matter of suggestive analogy rather than rigorous theory.
