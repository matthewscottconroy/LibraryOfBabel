# 34.5.1 Can You Meet Yourself? Personal Identity in Time Travel

---

## The Scenario

Imagine: you are now 30 years old. Using a time machine, you travel back 40 years to a time when your 70-year-old future self visited. Your 70-year-old future self is present in the same room as your 30-year-old present self. How many people are in the room?

From the common-sense perspective, there are two people. They look different, they have different beliefs, different memories (the 70-year-old remembers being 30; the 30-year-old does not yet remember being 70), different physical properties (one has gray hair, one does not). They can carry on a conversation. They occupy different spatial locations. They are clearly numerically distinct.

But from another perspective, there is one person. The 70-year-old is the same person as the 30-year-old — she is that person, 40 years later. Personal time connects them: the 30-year-old is the earlier stage, the 70-year-old is the later stage, of a single persisting individual. Numerically, they are one person: there is only one Mary Jane Smith, and both temporal stages belong to her.

This tension — numerically one, yet spatially and qualitatively distinct — is what makes the case philosophically interesting. Different theories of personal identity handle it differently.

## Endurantism and the Problem of Coincidence

Endurantism (or three-dimensionalism) holds that persisting objects are wholly present at each time at which they exist. On this view, the 30-year-old Mary and the 70-year-old Mary are not distinct temporal stages; they are the same object, wholly present at different times.

But if both are wholly present at the same time (when the 30-year-old and the 70-year-old are in the same room), the endurantist faces the problem of spatial coincidence: the same object is wholly present in two different spatial locations simultaneously. This seems to contradict a basic principle of identity: if x = y, then they share all their properties, including their spatial location. But the 30-year-old and the 70-year-old are spatially distinct.

Endurantists have several responses. One is to relativize properties to times: the young Mary is "at location L₁ relative to the 30-year-mark on her personal timeline" and "at location L₂ relative to the 70-year-mark." Since these are different intrinsic properties, the contradiction dissolves. But critics find this response unsatisfying — it seems to turn properties into relations, changing the character of property attributions.

## Perdurantism and Temporal Parts

Perdurantism (four-dimensionalism) holds that persisting objects have distinct temporal parts — temporal stages that exist at different times. The 30-year-old and the 70-year-old are numerically distinct temporal parts of the same person-worm: the four-dimensional entity that constitutes Mary's entire existence through time.

On this view, the meeting-yourself case is clean. The room contains two distinct temporal parts of Mary: her 30-year stage and her 70-year stage. These are genuinely distinct objects, both of which are parts of the single four-dimensional person-worm. They can be spatially co-present without contradiction, because they are distinct (even if they are both parts of the same person).

The perdurantist framework handles time travel in an elegant way. A time traveler is a person-worm whose temporal parts are arranged in a non-standard order relative to external time: the 30-year stage (in external time) might come before the 70-year stage in external time, even though the 70-year stage is *later* in personal time. The worm doubles back on itself in the external time dimension. The two co-present stages are parts of the same worm, but they are distinct stages.

This is perhaps the cleanest treatment of the meeting-yourself case available. Lewis (1976: 145–146) explicitly adopts it: the time traveler is a four-dimensional entity whose temporal parts are ordered differently in personal and external time. When two stages co-exist, they are numerically distinct parts of the same person.

## Psychological Continuity and the Identity Question

On Parfit's psychological continuity theory (see Chapter 25), personal identity over time consists in overlapping chains of memory, intention, belief, and character. The 70-year-old stage and the 30-year-old stage are psychologically connected: the 70-year-old remembers being 30, shares many of the same values and personality traits, and is related by causal chains of belief and intention.

On this view, the two stages are stages of the same person — connected by the relevant chains of psychological continuity — but they are not numerically identical at a single moment. They are *stages* of the same psychological continuity sequence, present at the same external time.

Parfit's framework is compatible with both endurantism and perdurantism, though it fits more naturally with perdurantism. What it adds is an account of *why* the stages count as stages of the same person: not merely because they are parts of the same physical object, but because they are connected by the psychological relations that constitute personal identity.

## Paradoxes of Self-Interaction

The meeting-yourself case raises further puzzles when we consider self-interaction. Can the 70-year-old give information to the 30-year-old that the 30-year-old will eventually use on her journey through life? Can the 30-year-old harm the 70-year-old? If the 70-year-old kills the 30-year-old, does this produce a version of the grandfather paradox — not killing one's grandfather but killing one's past self?

These questions do not have unique answers; they depend on which theory of personal identity is correct, and on the details of the causal structure of the time travel episode. What they illustrate is that the meeting-yourself case is not merely a curiosity but a serious test case for theories of persistence, identity, and causation.

The perdurantist framework handles the interaction questions relatively cleanly: the 30-year stage and the 70-year stage are distinct objects that can causally interact, just as any two distinct objects can. But the interaction is constrained by the self-consistency requirement: nothing can happen in the time travel episode that contradicts the overall history of the person-worm. The 70-year-old cannot kill the 30-year-old, for the same reason T cannot kill her grandfather: the 70-year-old's existence entails that the 30-year-old exists, and the consistency constraint ensures that no event within the time travel episode violates this entailment.

## The Deeper Question

The meeting-yourself case ultimately asks: what is it for two things to be "the same person"? The answer, we find, depends on which metaphysical theory of persistence we accept, and different theories give different answers to the practical and existential questions that time travel raises.

What is consistent across theories is that the meeting is possible in principle — that the logical structure of time travel allows for the co-presence of temporal stages of a single individual. Whether this is metaphysically coherent in a deeper sense depends on issues about identity, constitution, and the nature of persistence that are among the most contested in metaphysics. Time travel, far from resolving these issues, makes them impossibly vivid.
