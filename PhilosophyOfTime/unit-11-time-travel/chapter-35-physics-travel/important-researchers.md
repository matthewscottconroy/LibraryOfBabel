# Chapter 35 Important Researchers: Time Travel and Physics

---

**David Deutsch (b. 1953)**
British physicist and philosopher at Oxford University whose work has ranged from the foundations of quantum mechanics to quantum computing to the philosophy of science. His 1985 proposal for a universal quantum computer is one of the foundational papers of quantum computing. His 1991 paper on quantum CTCs is the defining work on the quantum treatment of time travel, introducing the fixed-point condition and identifying the information creation implications. Deutsch is also an advocate of the Everettian many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, which he connects to his work on computation and CTC physics. His book *The Fabric of Reality* (1997) presents his philosophical worldview accessibly.

**John Friedman**
Physicist at the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee who was part of the Friedman-Morris-Novikov-Thorne collaboration that developed the Novikov self-consistency principle in its physical form. The 1990 paper by Friedman and colleagues is the canonical statement of the principle as applied to classical field theories in CTC spacetimes. Friedman has also worked on theoretical aspects of quantum gravity.

**Kurt Gödel (1906–1978)**
One of the greatest mathematicians and logicians of the twentieth century, Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) are among the most profound results in the history of mathematics. His 1949 rotating universe solution is a striking outlier in his predominantly mathematical work — a contribution to general relativistic cosmology that includes an explicit philosophical discussion of the implications for the nature of time. Gödel worked at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton alongside Einstein, with whom he had a close intellectual friendship. His philosophical work on time was part of a larger engagement with idealist philosophy, particularly Kant and Husserl.

**Stephen Hawking (1942–2018)**
British theoretical physicist whose contributions to general relativity and cosmology fundamentally shaped modern physics. His discovery of Hawking radiation (1975) — the thermal emission of particles from black holes due to quantum effects near the event horizon — established the deep connection between quantum mechanics, general relativity, and thermodynamics. His Chronology Protection Conjecture (1992) is the most influential proposal for why time machines cannot form. Hawking's popular books, particularly *A Brief History of Time* (1988), made him the most widely read scientist of his generation. Despite severe ALS from his early twenties, he remained scientifically productive throughout his life.

**Roy Kerr (b. 1934)**
New Zealand mathematician whose 1963 discovery of the Kerr metric — the exact general relativistic solution for the spacetime around a rotating black hole — is one of the landmark results of twentieth-century physics. The Kerr metric is of central astrophysical importance because real black holes rotate, and the solution has been confirmed observationally. The CTCs within the Kerr black hole's inner horizon give the solution particular relevance for the physics of time travel, though their accessibility to outside observers is precluded by the event horizons.

**Seth Lloyd (b. 1960)**
Professor of mechanical engineering and physics at MIT whose work has contributed to quantum computing, quantum information theory, and quantum gravity. His 2011 paper with Maccone and colleagues developing the post-selected quantum CTC model (P-CTCs) offers an alternative to Deutsch's approach that is grounded in quantum information theory and avoids the information creation problem. Lloyd has also made important contributions to quantum algorithms, quantum thermodynamics, and the application of information-theoretic ideas to biological systems.

**Michael Morris**
Physicist who, with Kip Thorne, published the foundational 1988 analysis of traversable wormholes and their potential as time machines. The Morris-Thorne paper is notable both for its physical rigor and for its unusual transparency about the pedagogical goals of the analysis. While Thorne has remained the more prominent figure in the broader research program, Morris's contribution to the traversable wormhole analysis was fundamental.

**Igor Novikov (b. 1935)**
Russian astrophysicist and cosmologist who has made wide-ranging contributions to general relativity, cosmology, and the physics of black holes. His self-consistency principle for CTC spacetimes — developed in collaboration with Friedman, Thorne, and others — is the central statement of the "no paradoxes from time travel" position in classical physics. Novikov has been an active advocate for taking the physics of CTCs seriously as a research program, arguing that the absence of paradoxes in self-consistent CTC spacetimes makes time travel physics well-defined and potentially realizable.

**Kip Thorne (b. 1940)**
American theoretical physicist, winner of the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics for his role in the LIGO detection of gravitational waves. Thorne has made foundational contributions to general relativity, the physics of black holes, and gravitational wave theory. His work on traversable wormholes (with Morris) and on the Novikov self-consistency principle (with Friedman, Novikov, and others) made him a central figure in the theoretical study of time travel physics. His popular book *Black Holes and Time Warps* (1994) is the best accessible account of the relevant physics. Thorne was also a scientific consultant on the film *Interstellar* (2014).
