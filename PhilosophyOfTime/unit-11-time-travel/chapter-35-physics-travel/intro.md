# Chapter 35: Time Travel and Physics

---

## Chapter Introduction

Chapter 34 established that backward time travel is logically coherent — that no formal contradiction follows from the concept. This chapter asks the harder question: is backward time travel physically possible? Does the physics of spacetime permit the kinds of trajectories that would constitute backward time travel?

The answer, from general relativity, is surprising. Several exact solutions to Einstein's field equations contain *closed timelike curves* — spacetime trajectories that curve back on themselves, so that a traveler following them would return to a previous point in their own history. These include Gödel's rotating universe (1949), the Kerr metric for rotating black holes, Tipler's infinite rotating cylinder (1974), and others. None of these solutions is thought to describe our actual universe, but their existence within general relativity shows that the physics of spacetime does not, in principle, forbid time travel.

The practical route to a time machine, in current theoretical physics, is through traversable wormholes. Morris and Thorne (1988) showed that traversable wormholes — hypothetical tunnels through spacetime connecting distant regions — require *exotic matter* (matter with negative energy density) but are otherwise compatible with general relativity. More importantly, they showed how a traversable wormhole can be converted into a time machine by accelerating one of its mouths. Whether exotic matter exists in the required quantities is an open question in physics.

Against these possibilities, Hawking proposed his Chronology Protection Conjecture (1992): quantum effects near the Cauchy horizon of a prospective time machine diverge, creating an energy density so large that it prevents the time machine from forming. This conjecture has not been proved as a theorem; it remains a conjecture supported by plausibility arguments.

The chapter closes with the quantum theory of CTCs. Deutsch (1991) showed that if closed timelike curves exist, the consistency requirement can be formulated as a fixed-point condition on density matrices — and that this formulation resolves the grandfather paradox in a quantum-mechanical setting. But the resolution has striking implications: information can apparently be "created" in Deutsch CTCs, raising deep questions about quantum information and the nature of time.
