# 35.1.1 Closed Timelike Curves: Gödel, Kerr, and Tipler

---

## General Relativity and Causal Structure

In general relativity, the *causal structure* of spacetime is determined by the metric — the mathematical object that encodes the distances between nearby spacetime points and distinguishes timelike, spacelike, and lightlike separations. A spacetime is *causally well-behaved* if there are no closed timelike curves — if you can't follow a timelike path back to where you started. In such spacetimes, there is a well-defined "before" and "after" for any pair of causally connected events.

But not all solutions to the Einstein field equations are causally well-behaved. The field equations are differential equations relating the spacetime curvature to the matter-energy content. For certain distributions of matter and energy, the solutions have exotic causal structures — including CTCs.

## Gödel's Rotating Universe (1949)

Kurt Gödel — the mathematician whose incompleteness theorems are among the most important results in the history of logic — published a paper in 1949 in *Reviews of Modern Physics* presenting an exact solution to the Einstein field equations describing a rotating universe (Gödel 1949). This solution is cosmologically unrealistic — it requires the universe to be rotating and filled with a homogeneous distribution of matter at rest in the co-rotating frame — but it is a valid solution of the equations.

In Gödel's spacetime, CTCs are not confined to extreme regions: through every point in the spacetime, there pass closed timelike curves. A sufficiently advanced civilization in a Gödel universe could, in principle, build a spaceship that follows a large enough CTC, returning to its own past. The "circles" through spacetime are large but finite; the civilization would need to travel at close to the speed of light for an extended period, then return.

Gödel drew a philosophical conclusion from his solution: since general relativity permits spacetimes with CTCs, and since CTCs would allow backward "time travel" in a physically meaningful sense, the concept of an objective global time — a universal "now" applicable to all observers — is untenable. This was a contribution to the ongoing discussion, prompted by Einstein, about the relationship between the physics of spacetime and the philosophy of time.

Our universe does not appear to be rotating in the way Gödel's solution requires; the solution is not thought to be realistic. But it demonstrates that the field equations do not forbid CTCs on purely mathematical grounds.

## The Kerr Metric and Rotating Black Holes (1963)

Roy Kerr published a solution to the Einstein field equations in 1963 describing the spacetime around a rotating black hole. The Kerr metric is astrophysically important: real black holes are believed to rotate, because they form from rotating stars. The spacetime of a Kerr black hole has several distinctive features not present in the non-rotating (Schwarzschild) case.

Most relevant for our purposes: inside the inner horizon of a Kerr black hole, the spacetime contains CTCs. A traveler who entered the Kerr black hole and passed through the outer and inner horizons would encounter CTCs in the interior region. In principle, they could follow one back to their own past.

The practical obstacles are enormous: passing through the horizons of a black hole is a one-way trip (in the sense that no information can escape), and the interior region may be unstable due to quantum effects. Nevertheless, the Kerr metric demonstrates that CTCs can arise in astrophysically realistic settings — not just in fanciful cosmological models.

## Van Stockum Dust (1937) and Tipler Cylinders (1974)

Willem Jacob van Stockum (1910–1944) found in 1937 a solution to the Einstein field equations describing an infinite cylinder of rotating dust. This solution was later shown to contain CTCs in the exterior region — the region outside the cylinder (van Stockum 1938). A traveler in the exterior region could, in principle, follow a CTC.

Frank Tipler (1974) extended this work, examining the spacetime around an infinite rotating cylinder of arbitrarily dense matter. Tipler showed that such a cylinder, if it could be constructed (requiring matter much denser than any known material), would produce CTCs in the surrounding spacetime. This is sometimes called the "Tipler cylinder" time machine. The requirement for an *infinite* cylinder was recognized as physically problematic — it requires infinite matter — and Hawking (1992) later argued that quantum effects would prevent such a machine from operating even in the finite case.

## What These Solutions Tell Us

The significance of these solutions is philosophical as well as physical. They demonstrate that:

(1) General relativity, as a physical theory, does not categorically forbid CTCs. The field equations have solutions containing CTCs.

(2) CTCs arise in some astrophysically relevant contexts (Kerr metric), not only in exotic cosmological scenarios.

(3) The causal structure of spacetime — whether CTCs exist — depends on the distribution of matter and energy. It is a dynamical feature, not a fixed background.

(4) The question of whether our universe contains CTCs is an empirical question about the actual distribution of matter and energy, not one that can be settled by pure theoretical reasoning.

The existence of these solutions makes backward time travel a subject of genuine scientific interest, not merely science fiction. It does not show that time travel is possible in our universe; it shows that the laws of physics as we understand them do not make it categorically impossible.
