# 35.2.1 Traversable Wormholes and the Time Machine Construction

---

## What Is a Wormhole?

A wormhole is a hypothetical topological feature of spacetime: a "handle" or "tunnel" connecting two distant regions of spacetime. The simplest type, the Einstein-Rosen bridge, was described by Einstein and Rosen in 1935 as a feature of the Schwarzschild black hole solution. However, Einstein-Rosen bridges are not *traversable*: they collapse too rapidly for anything to pass through them, and they connect regions behind the event horizons of black holes.

Michael Morris and Kip Thorne (*American Journal of Physics*, 1988) posed a different question: what properties would a *traversable* wormhole — one that a person or signal could pass through without being destroyed — require? Their analysis was explicitly designed as a "pedagogical" investigation, but it became a genuine research program in theoretical physics.

## Requirements for Traversability

Morris and Thorne showed that a traversable wormhole would need to satisfy several conditions:

(1) *Tidal forces must be tolerable*. A traveler passing through the wormhole must not be torn apart by the gravitational gradient. This places constraints on the geometry of the wormhole's interior.

(2) *The throat must remain open*. A wormhole that collapses before the traveler can pass through is not useful. Keeping the throat open against gravitational collapse requires matter with unusual properties.

(3) *No exotic horizons*. The traversable wormhole should not have event horizons that trap the traveler inside.

The key finding was the exotic matter requirement. To hold the throat of the wormhole open, Morris and Thorne found that it requires matter with *negative energy density* — matter that violates the *weak energy condition* (WEC). The WEC states that the energy density of matter, as measured by any observer, must be non-negative. Exotic matter violates this condition.

Is exotic matter physically possible? In classical physics, all known forms of matter satisfy the WEC. However, quantum field theory is different: the *Casimir effect* demonstrates that quantum fluctuations in a vacuum between closely spaced conducting plates produce a negative energy density in that region. This is a real, experimentally confirmed effect. Whether it can be amplified and manipulated to produce the quantities required for a traversable wormhole is an open question.

## Converting a Wormhole into a Time Machine

The most striking feature of Morris and Thorne's analysis is that it does not stop with the traversable wormhole: they describe a mechanism for converting one into a time machine.

Suppose a traversable wormhole has been constructed, with its two mouths located near each other initially. Take one mouth (call it mouth B) and accelerate it to close to the speed of light, carry it away and return it, while mouth A remains stationary. Due to special relativistic time dilation, less proper time elapses for mouth B during its journey than in the external reference frame. When B returns, it is "younger" than A — its internal time is behind A's.

Now: the wormhole tunnel still connects the two mouths. What's more, the time within the tunnel is correlated with the mouths' clocks. So if you enter through mouth A when external time is, say, year 2050, you emerge from mouth B at a time corresponding to B's internal clock — which, due to the time dilation, is some earlier external time, say 2040. You have traveled backward in external time by 10 years, while your personal time has advanced by (approximately) 10 minutes.

This is the wormhole time machine. The mechanism is physically respectable — it uses nothing but standard special relativistic time dilation and the topology of a traversable wormhole. The exotic matter required to sustain the wormhole is the physical bottleneck.

## The Light-Speed Limitation on Past Travel

An important limitation on the wormhole time machine should be noted. The earliest time a traveler can reach is the time when the wormhole was first constructed — or more precisely, when the two mouths first became out of phase in their proper times. The wormhole time machine cannot be used to travel to before the machine was built. This is sometimes called the "time machine horizon": you cannot travel to the era before the time machine existed, which is why we do not (presumably) observe visitors from the far future in our present.

This limitation is philosophically interesting: it implies that if time machines ever exist, they can only produce travel to times after the machine's construction. The familiar science fiction scenario of visiting ancient Rome or the dinosaurs would be impossible with a wormhole time machine.

## Philosophical Implications

The Morris-Thorne wormhole time machine raises all the paradoxes discussed in Chapter 34 in a physical context. The consistency constraint — Lewis's requirement that no event in the time travel episode contradict any other event — is now a requirement on the physical solutions to the field equations. The Novikov self-consistency principle (Section 35.4) addresses this specifically.

The wormhole time machine also raises a question about the *robustness* of general relativity as a physical theory: can a theory that permits time machines be the correct theory of gravity? This question motivates Hawking's Chronology Protection Conjecture (Section 35.3), which holds that quantum effects will prevent the conversion of a wormhole into a time machine.
