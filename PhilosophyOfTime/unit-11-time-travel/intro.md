# Unit XI: Time Travel

---

## Unit Introduction

Time travel occupies a peculiar position in intellectual culture: it is simultaneously a staple of popular fiction and a serious subject of inquiry in both physics and philosophy. This unit engages with it as both. The fiction is worth taking seriously because it forces us to confront, in vivid and concrete terms, questions about causation, identity, possibility, and the structure of time that more abstract treatments can obscure. The physics is worth taking seriously because it tells us that time travel — or something resembling it — is not ruled out by the laws of nature as we understand them. And the philosophy is worth taking seriously because neither the fiction nor the physics can proceed without philosophical clarity about what time travel would even mean and what its logical implications would be.

Chapter 34 focuses on the metaphysics of time travel. What is time travel, exactly? David Lewis's brilliant 1976 analysis defines it as a discrepancy between personal time (the time experienced and measured by the traveler's own processes) and external time (the time coordinate of the background spacetime). By this definition, certain forms of relativistic "time travel" — traveling fast enough to age very slowly relative to external time — already occur. What the popular imagination calls time travel — going backward in time — is a more radical case in which personal time and external time diverge in direction, not merely in rate.

The chapter then works through the philosophical puzzles that arise specifically from backward time travel. The grandfather paradox is the most famous: what prevents a time traveler from going back and killing their own grandfather? Lewis's resolution — the traveler can try but will not succeed — is elegant but raises further questions. Bootstrap paradoxes, in which the information in a causal loop has no origin, press on the concept of causation itself. The many-worlds solution dissolves some paradoxes but may change the subject. And the question of personal identity — can you meet yourself? — involves every persistence theory we have studied.

Chapter 35 brings in the physics. The Einstein field equations permit solutions with closed timelike curves (CTCs) — spacetime trajectories that loop back to their own starting point. Gödel's rotating universe, the Kerr metric for rotating black holes, and Tipler cylinders all involve CTCs. Traversable wormholes, if they can be constructed, can be converted into time machines. Hawking's Chronology Protection Conjecture holds that quantum effects prevent the formation of CTCs at the macroscopic level — but this conjecture has not been proved. The quantum treatment of CTCs by Deutsch and others raises further puzzles about information, consistency, and the relationship between quantum mechanics and spacetime structure.

By the end of this unit, time travel will appear not as a fantasy but as a serious conceptual structure that illuminates the deepest features of physical time, logical possibility, and personal identity.
