# Chapter 36 Exercises: The Unreality of Time Revisited

---

## Conceptual Exercises

**1. Three Claims**
Section 36.1 distinguished three claims that might all be packaged under the label "time is unreal": (C1) objective temporal passage is unreal; (C2) the A-series is incoherent; (C3) time altogether is unreal. For each claim, identify (a) which philosophical tradition or physical theory most directly supports it; (b) what evidence or argument supports it; (c) what it would mean for ordinary experience if it were true. Are these three claims independent, or does accepting one entail accepting the others?

**2. The Wheeler-DeWitt Equation**
The Wheeler-DeWitt equation (Ĥ|Ψ⟩ = 0) does not contain a time parameter. Explain why this is so, and what it implies for the status of time in quantum gravity. Does the absence of a time parameter in the fundamental equation mean that time does not exist? Or could it mean something more subtle — for instance, that time is an emergent feature that must be *derived* from the timeless equation? What would a derivation of time from the Wheeler-DeWitt equation look like?

**3. Time Capsules**
Evaluate Barbour's "time capsule" account of the appearance of temporal succession. The account holds that we experience time because our present configuration contains records that appear to be memories of past events. Identify three specific objections to this account. Then provide the best response Barbour could give to each objection. Do his responses succeed?

**4. Causal Sets and the Growing Block**
Causal set theory models the universe as a partially ordered set of discrete elements that grows by sequential accretion. Explain how this picture can be seen as a physical implementation of the growing block ontology. What are the disanalogies between the causal set picture and the philosophical growing block view? Does causal set theory vindicate the growing block, or does it offer a different kind of ontology?

**5. Nāgārjuna's Argument**
Reconstruct Nāgārjuna's argument from MMK XIX that time lacks intrinsic existence. What does "intrinsic existence" mean in this context? Is Nāgārjuna's argument purely logical, or does it depend on background commitments about dependent origination? How does the two-truths doctrine prevent his conclusion from collapsing into the claim that time is simply nothing?

**6. Advaita and Western Parallels**
Compare the Advaita Vedanta treatment of time with Plotinus's treatment (from Chapter 7) and McTaggart's treatment (from Chapter 13). What are the structural similarities and what are the important differences? Does the comparison illuminate something about the Western positions that was not visible before? What methodological risks attend cross-traditional philosophical comparison?

**7. Methodological Question**
Several distinct physical and philosophical arguments converge on the conclusion that time (in some sense) is not fundamental. Does this convergence provide stronger evidence for the conclusion, or could the convergence be explained by the fact that all the arguments share a common problematic assumption? Identify a possible common assumption that might explain the convergence, and assess whether it is well-grounded.

## Discussion and Essay Questions

**8. Comparison Essay**
Compare Barbour's "End of Time" view with the causal set theory view of temporal becoming. Both are serious proposals about the fundamental nature of time in quantum gravity. One eliminates time altogether; the other makes becoming fundamental. What evidence or argument could in principle decide between them? What are the philosophical stakes of the choice?

**9. The Experience of Time**
Any account of time that denies its fundamentality must explain why we experience temporal flow. Write an essay evaluating whether Barbour's time capsule account, Nāgārjuna's two-truths doctrine, and the Advaita māyā framework each provide an adequate account of the phenomenology of temporal experience. Use the materials from Chapter 28 (phenomenology of time) as your standard for what an adequate account must explain.

## Lab and Observational Exercises

**10. Primary Text Reading**
Read Nāgārjuna's MMK Chapter 19 ("Examination of Time") in Jay Garfield's translation (*The Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way*, Oxford University Press, 1995). The chapter is short (six verses). Write a two-page philosophical analysis: (a) reconstruct the argument of each verse; (b) identify the main philosophical claims; (c) assess the argument's validity; (d) compare it to one argument from the Western tradition that arrives at a similar conclusion.

**11. Barbour's Platonia**
Read Chapter 1 of Barbour's *The End of Time* (1999), which is available in many libraries and online. Write a response (400-500 words) to the following question: Does Barbour's picture of a universe of timeless Nows, with no objective temporal succession, seem coherent to you? What would need to be different about your experience for Barbour's picture to be phenomenologically accurate? What aspects of your experience resist the picture most strongly?

**12. Cross-Cultural Investigation**
Interview or research the views on time held within one non-Western philosophical or religious tradition not discussed in this chapter (suggestions: Confucian philosophy, Daoism, Yoruba temporal concepts, Hopi temporal concepts, Aztec cyclical time). Prepare a 500-word analysis comparing the tradition's view of time with the Western positions studied in this course. Focus on the following: (a) Is time regarded as fundamental or derivative? (b) Is there a distinction between conventional and ultimate accounts of time? (c) What is the relationship between temporal experience and spiritual or philosophical practice?
