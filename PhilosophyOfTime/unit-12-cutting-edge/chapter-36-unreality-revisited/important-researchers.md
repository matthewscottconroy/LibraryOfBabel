# Chapter 36 Important Researchers: The Unreality of Time Revisited

---

**Julian Barbour (b. 1937)**
Independent British physicist whose work on the foundations of classical and quantum mechanics, especially on the relational treatment of time and the timeless wavefunction of the universe, has been highly influential if controversial. His *The End of Time* (1999) is the most accessible and philosophically developed presentation of the view that time is not a fundamental feature of physical reality. Barbour works largely outside standard academic structures, having supported himself through translation work to pursue foundational research. His earlier technical work on Machian relational mechanics (*Absolute or Relative Motion?*, 1989) provides the foundation for the more popular work.

**Luca Bombelli, Joohan Lee, David Meyer, and Rafael Sorkin**
The four physicists who published the foundational paper "Space-Time as a Causal Set" in 1987, proposing causal set theory as an approach to quantum gravity. The paper is short and conceptually dense. Sorkin has been the most active and prolific developer of the theory since, and the subsequent research program is sometimes called "Sorkin's causal set program." The framework has attracted a community of researchers working on its mathematical foundations, phenomenological implications, and philosophical interpretations.

**Bryce DeWitt (1923–2004)**
American physicist who, with John Wheeler, developed the canonical quantization approach to general relativity that produces the Wheeler-DeWitt equation. DeWitt was also an early and important advocate of the Everettian many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. The Wheeler-DeWitt equation bears his name because his technical work on the quantization of gravity produced the equation whose philosophical implications — the absence of time — have been so widely discussed.

**Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE)**
The most important philosopher in the Mahāyāna Buddhist tradition, Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* develops the Madhyamaka (Middle Way) philosophy of *śūnyatā* (emptiness) and has been immensely influential in all subsequent Buddhist philosophy. His analysis of time (Chapter 19) is a canonical example of the Madhyamaka method applied to a specific category. The *MMK* has attracted major commentaries in the Tibetan and Chinese traditions, and since the 1990s has attracted increasing engagement from analytic philosophers. Garfield (1995) and Siderits and Katsura (2013) provide the most scholarly English translations with commentaries.

**Rafael Sorkin (b. 1945)**
American theoretical physicist at the Perimeter Institute and Syracuse University who is the principal developer of causal set theory. Sorkin's work combines technical physical research with sustained philosophical reflection on the implications of causal sets for the nature of time, space, and becoming. His papers on the philosophical implications of causal set dynamics — particularly on the concept of sequential growth and its relationship to temporal becoming — represent a rare integration of physical and philosophical insight. He has also contributed to foundational work on quantum measure theory and the sum-over-histories approach to quantum mechanics.

**Ādi Śaṅkara (c. 788–820 CE)**
The most important philosopher in the Advaita Vedanta tradition, Śaṅkara wrote commentaries on the *Upaniṣads*, the *Bhagavad Gītā*, and the *Brahma-Sūtras* that systematized the non-dualist interpretation of these texts. His central thesis — that Brahman alone is real, that the world of multiplicity is an appearance through māyā, and that the individual soul (*jīva*) is ultimately identical with Brahman — has been enormously influential in Indian philosophy and has shaped Hindu intellectual tradition for over twelve centuries. His work on time is embedded in his broader metaphysical system rather than being a separate topic, but the implications for temporal ontology are significant.

**John Wheeler (1911–2008)**
American theoretical physicist whose career bridged classical general relativity, quantum mechanics, and their synthesis in quantum gravity. Wheeler coined the terms "black hole," "wormhole," and "quantum foam" and developed the canonical quantization of gravity with Bryce DeWitt, leading to the Wheeler-DeWitt equation. His philosophical reflections on the nature of time, space, and quantum measurement — sometimes eccentric but always stimulating — influenced a generation of physicists and philosophers. His aphorism "it from bit" — the proposal that physical reality is fundamentally informational — anticipated themes in Chapter 38.
