# Chapter 36: The Unreality of Time Revisited

---

## Chapter Introduction

McTaggart's 1908 argument for the unreality of time was, for a long time, treated as a philosophical curiosity — brilliant in its way, but too abstract and too dependent on his peculiar metaphysical idealism to have staying power. Something has changed. Physics in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries has produced several independent lines of argument that time, or at least some central features of what we mean by time, may be unreal. These arguments come from quantum gravity, from the physics of information, from the thermodynamics of the early universe, and from a growing conviction among physicists and philosophers that the concept of time is not as fundamental as we assumed.

But "time is unreal" can mean very different things. McTaggart's conclusion was that objective temporal becoming — the movement of events from future to present to past — is contradictory and hence illusory; what we experience as time is an appearance generated by an underlying atemporal reality. Barbour's conclusion is that the fundamental ontology of the universe contains no time parameter at all — that the wavefunction of the universe is defined over a space of instantaneous configurations, not a sequence of configurations in time. The quantum gravity conclusion (if it can be stated as such) is that time, as a continuous background parameter, breaks down at the Planck scale and must be reconstituted from more fundamental, non-temporal structures.

These are different claims. Conflating them leads to confusion. This chapter unpacks them carefully, examines the evidence and arguments for each, and asks whether any or all of them represent genuine progress beyond McTaggart.

The chapter also considers non-Western perspectives. Buddhist philosophy of time and Advaita Vedanta both develop sophisticated frameworks in which time is not ultimately real — frameworks that, while developed independently from Western physics and philosophy, make contact with some of the ideas explored in this unit in philosophically productive ways.
