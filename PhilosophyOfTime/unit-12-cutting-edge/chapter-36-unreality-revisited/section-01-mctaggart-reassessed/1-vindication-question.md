# 36.1.1 Has Physics Vindicated McTaggart?

---

## Three Claims to Distinguish

McTaggart's 1908 argument proceeds in two stages: first, the A-series is more fundamental than the B-series (because temporal becoming is essential to genuine time); second, the A-series is incoherent (because of the regress argument). Together these imply that time is unreal. But this conclusion involves at least three distinguishable claims:

(C1) *Objective temporal passage is unreal.* Events do not objectively move from future to present to past; there is no mind-independent "flow" of time. This is the claim that B-theory makes against A-theory.

(C2) *The A-series is incoherent.* The A-series is not merely unreal but cannot even be coherently formulated; any attempt to describe it generates an infinite regress.

(C3) *Time is unreal altogether.* Not just passage, not just the A-series, but temporal relations themselves — earlier and later — are unreal. The B-series, as well as the A-series, is an illusion.

Modern physics and philosophy have something to say about each of these claims, but what they say is not uniform.

## What Physics Says About C1

The block universe, supported by special relativity, is a powerful argument for C1. If the simultaneity of events is relative to a reference frame, and if the "present" is what is simultaneous with now, then there is no frame-independent "present" and no objective A-series. The block universe contains all temporal positions — past, present, and future — equally and simultaneously. There is no privileged "now" that moves through the block.

This supports C1: there is no objective temporal passage in the sense of an A-series property that events objectively acquire and lose. The passage of time, if it exists at all, is not a feature of the physical world described by special relativity; it is, at most, a feature of our experience of that world.

Does this vindicate McTaggart? Partially. McTaggart argued that the A-series is necessary for genuine time, then that it is incoherent. Special relativity makes the first step — the necessity of the A-series for genuine time — look questionable, by showing that a temporally ordered world (the block universe) can exist without an objective A-series. Whether this vindicates McTaggart's conclusion (time is unreal) or merely shows that his first premise was wrong (the A-series is not necessary for time) is a question about the interpretation of the argument.

## What Quantum Gravity Says

Several approaches to quantum gravity suggest that time may not be a fundamental feature of the physical world — that it breaks down at the Planck scale and must be derived from more fundamental structures. This goes beyond C1 and approaches C3.

The Wheeler-DeWitt equation (the quantum gravity analog of the Schrödinger equation) is a time-independent equation: there is no time parameter in it. The wavefunction of the universe, in this framework, is not a function of time but a function of three-dimensional geometric configurations. How ordinary time emerges from this timeless foundation is a deep unsolved problem (the "problem of time" in quantum gravity).

Loop quantum gravity, causal set theory, and string theory each predict that the smooth, continuous time of classical physics breaks down at the Planck scale (~10⁻⁴³ seconds). At this scale, time may be discrete (as in LQG and causal sets) or may emerge from non-temporal structures (as in some string theory formulations).

This supports something like C3, but the claim is more specific: *continuous background time* is unreal as a fundamental physical parameter. Whether this is what McTaggart meant — he was primarily concerned with A-series passage, not with the Planck scale — is unclear. What is clear is that the quantum gravity program has independently arrived at a form of temporal unrealism that goes beyond what McTaggart argued.

## The Precise Distinction

Barbour (1999: 26–34) makes the point sharply: there are two ways to argue that time is unreal. One says: the A-series (passage) is illusory; the B-series (ordering) remains. This is standard B-theory. The other says: both the A-series and the B-series are derivative from a more fundamental structure that is not temporal at all. McTaggart's own view was closer to the second: he held that the apparent B-series is an appearance generated by an underlying reality (the "C-series") that is atemporal.

Physics, in the quantum gravity era, has independently arrived at something like McTaggart's second position — the view that temporal structure is derived from a non-temporal substrate. The convergence is philosophically remarkable, even if the details are different. McTaggart's non-temporal reality was a Hegelian Absolute; the quantum gravity substrate is (depending on the approach) a spin network, a causal set, or a conformal field theory on a lower-dimensional boundary.

## What McTaggart Got Right and Wrong

McTaggart was right that the appearance of temporal passage — the felt "flow" of time from future to present to past — requires explanation, and that any explanation that takes the A-series as fundamental generates the regress he described. He was right to distinguish the A-series from the B-series, and right to think that temporal ontology cannot be reduced to the B-series without losing something important.

What McTaggart arguably got wrong was the conclusion: rather than showing that *time is unreal*, his argument shows that *temporal passage, if real, generates incoherence*. This is compatible with the conclusion that temporal passage is not real — that the A-series is an appearance, not a feature of mind-independent reality — which is the B-theorist's position, not the full McTaggartian conclusion that time is altogether unreal.

The relationship between McTaggart and contemporary physics is therefore complex: he anticipated the move toward temporal unrealism at a fundamental level, but the specific arguments he gave and the specific conclusions he reached are not straightforwardly vindicated. What he gave us is a conceptual framework — the A/B distinction, the analysis of becoming and ordering — that makes the contemporary debate possible.
