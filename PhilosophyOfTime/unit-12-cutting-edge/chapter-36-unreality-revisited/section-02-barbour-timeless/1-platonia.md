# 36.2.1 Barbour's Platonia and the Timeless Universe

---

## The Wheeler-DeWitt Equation

The quantum gravity problem of time begins with the Wheeler-DeWitt equation. In standard quantum mechanics, the Schrödinger equation governs the time-evolution of quantum states: the state at any later time is determined by the state at any earlier time, and time is an external parameter that appears explicitly in the equation. But when we attempt to apply quantum mechanics to the universe as a whole — which is what a quantum theory of gravity requires — a problem arises.

General relativity is a diffeomorphism-invariant theory: there is no preferred time coordinate; different coordinate choices give equivalent physical descriptions. When quantizing general relativity using the canonical approach, this diffeomorphism invariance leads to the Wheeler-DeWitt equation:

Ĥ|Ψ⟩ = 0

where Ĥ is the Hamiltonian constraint operator and |Ψ⟩ is the wavefunction of the universe. The striking feature: the equation has *no time derivative*. The wavefunction of the universe does not evolve in time; it simply *is* — a timeless state annihilated by the Hamiltonian constraint. Time has disappeared from the fundamental equation of quantum gravity.

This is the "problem of time" in quantum gravity: how does ordinary time, with its sense of flow, present, and direction, emerge from a theory in which there is no time?

## Platonia: The Space of All Possible Nows

Barbour's answer is to take the Wheeler-DeWitt equation at face value: there is no time, and any apparent temporal succession is an emergent phenomenon, an appearance. The fundamental ontology of the universe is not a spacetime with events at different times, but a timeless space of possible instantaneous configurations of the universe, which Barbour calls *Platonia*.

Each point in Platonia is a "Now" — a complete, instantaneous three-dimensional configuration of the universe. Platonia is the space of *all possible Nows*: all the ways the three-dimensional matter-geometry of the universe could be arranged. The wavefunction of the universe, Ψ, assigns an amplitude to each point (each Now) in Platonia.

There is no temporal ordering on Platonia as such. The Nows are not arranged in a sequence from earlier to later; they are simply present — the full collection of possible instantaneous configurations, with amplitudes assigned by the wavefunction. What we experience as temporal succession — the sense that one moment follows another — is not a feature of the fundamental ontology; it is an appearance that must be explained by some other means.

## Time Capsules

If the Nows in Platonia are not arranged in temporal order, how do we explain the appearance of temporal succession? Barbour's answer is the concept of *time capsules*.

A time capsule is a Now — an instantaneous configuration — that contains records, traces, and memories of other Nows. Our own experience at this moment is a time capsule: we remember the past, we see the traces of past events in our surroundings, we have a sense of where we came from and where we are going. All of this is part of the present Now — the current configuration of our brains and surroundings. The "memories" are not actually memories of past events; they are features of the present configuration that are coherent with *as if* there had been past events.

The appearance of time comes from the fact that the wavefunction of the universe assigns high amplitude to Nows that are time capsules — to configurations that contain coherent internal records of "previous" configurations. These Nows feel like they are embedded in a temporal sequence because they contain information that points to other Nows. But there is no actual temporal sequence; there are only Nows, and some of them contain apparent memories.

This is a remarkable idea. It implies that everything we remember, everything in the historical record, every fossil and tree ring and ancient text — all of it is a feature of the present Now, not a trace of an actual past. The past, on Barbour's view, never happened — or rather, the concept of "happening" in the past is itself an illusion generated by the structure of the present Now.

## Philosophical Implications

Barbour's view is one of the most radical positions in the philosophy of time. Several philosophical implications deserve note.

*The reality of Nows*: Barbour holds that Nows are real — he is not a nihilist about the present. What he denies is that Nows are *arranged in time*. This is an unusual combination: he has a richer ontology than presentists (who hold that only the present Now is real) while having a less structured ontology (there is no temporal ordering among the Nows).

*The problem of change*: Barbour's timeless universe faces a severe version of the problem of change. If there is no time, how can anything change? Barbour's answer: genuine change is an illusion; what exists are Nows that contain apparent records of change, but nothing actually changes. This is consistent but counterintuitive to an extreme degree.

*The problem of experience*: Barbour's view faces a peculiar challenge regarding the experience of duration. We do not merely have memories of the past; we experience the *passage* of time — the sense that the present moment is moving, that earlier times are receding and later times approaching. This experience is very different from merely having a memory. Barbour must explain why the contents of a Now include not just memories but the felt experience of duration — and this requires a connection to the philosophy of temporal consciousness that he does not fully develop.
