# 36.2.2 Assessing Barbour's Timeless Universe

---

## The Strengths of the Position

Barbour's position has several genuine strengths. First, it takes seriously the Wheeler-DeWitt equation's lack of a time parameter. Rather than treating this as a problem to be solved by finding a "hidden" time, Barbour accepts it as a fundamental feature of the correct quantum cosmological description and develops a coherent ontology around it.

Second, the time capsule account of the appearance of temporal succession is genuinely creative. It shows that the appearance of time does not require actual temporal succession; it requires only that certain configurations contain coherent internal records. Whether or not this is ultimately correct, it is a substantive philosophical proposal, not a handwave.

Third, Barbour's view connects naturally to the relational approach to time associated with Leibniz and Mach (Ernst Mach's *The Science of Mechanics* [1883] argued that time is simply a convenient measure of change, not an independently existing background). If time is nothing over and above the relations between configurations of matter, and if those relations can be captured without a temporal ordering of Nows, then Barbour's view can be seen as the culmination of a venerable philosophical tradition.

## The Problem of Experienced Duration

The most serious challenge to Barbour is the phenomenology of temporal experience. We do not merely have memories of the past; we experience the duration of the present moment — the "specious present" that William James and Edmund Husserl described (see Chapter 28). This experience is not simply a matter of having memories; it is the sense of *now* as an extended, passing moment.

Barbour might respond that the time capsule structure of our brain-states accounts for this: a Now that contains records of a flowing experience is one with a high wavefunction amplitude, and that is why we experience it. But this response seems to confuse the *content* of experience (what we remember and anticipate) with the *character* of experience (the felt sense of duration). The latter is not obviously accounted for by any configuration having coherent records.

Price (1996: 13–22) makes a related point: even granting that the appearance of temporal succession can be explained by time capsules, the appearance of *temporal direction* — the sense that the past is past and the future is future, not the other way around — requires an additional explanation. Barbour must show not just that Nows can contain records of other Nows but that they contain records in the right directional structure to generate the asymmetric experience of past-as-past and future-as-future.

## The Epistemic Problem

A further challenge concerns the justification for beliefs based on apparent memories. If the past never happened — if our memories are simply features of the present Now, not traces of actual past events — then what justification do we have for beliefs based on those memories? The belief that there was a Second World War, or that I had breakfast this morning, seems to be justified by the fact that these things happened. If they didn't happen — if the world simply is the way it is now, with configurations that include apparent records of these events — then the justification for the beliefs seems to collapse.

Barbour might respond: the justification for beliefs based on apparent memories is not about what *actually* happened but about what the present configuration of my brain and the world tells me. If the configuration is the kind that reliably indicates certain states of affairs, beliefs based on it are justified. But this response seems to presuppose a reliability relation between present configurations and past states of affairs — which is exactly what Barbour denies exists.

## The Physics Questions

On the physics side, Barbour's view faces the problem of recovering ordinary temporal evolution from the timeless Wheeler-DeWitt framework. In ordinary quantum mechanics, the Schrödinger equation provides a definite temporal evolution: if you know the state now, you know the state at all other times. In Barbour's framework, there is no such evolution. The task of "deriving" ordinary time from the timeless wavefunction — of showing that an observer within the framework would experience temporal succession — is technically challenging and not fully solved.

The decoherence approach to quantum cosmology (Halliwell 1994: 2812–2831; Gell-Mann and Hartle 1993: 3345–3382) provides partial answers: decoherence processes can identify "quasi-classical" branches of the wavefunction that behave as if they are evolving in time. But whether this derivation fully recovers ordinary temporal experience is disputed.

## The View's Philosophical Place

Despite these challenges, Barbour's view represents one of the most serious attempts to derive an account of time from fundamental physics rather than assume it. Whether it succeeds or not, it clarifies what would need to be true for time to be fundamentally unreal: not merely that temporal passage is an appearance (B-theory), but that temporal ordering itself is derived from a non-temporal substrate. That is a harder and bolder claim — and assessing it requires both philosophical and physical tools that are currently at the frontier.
