# 36.4.1 Buddhist Philosophy of Time: Nāgārjuna and Abhidharma

---

## The Abhidharma Framework: Radical Impermanence

The Abhidharma tradition — the systematic philosophical analysis that developed within early Buddhism (c. 3rd century BCE to 5th century CE) — addresses temporal reality through the concept of *dharmas*: the ultimate constitutive elements of experience and reality. In the Abhidharma analysis, what ordinary persons experience as enduring objects (chairs, persons, mountains) are actually rapidly changing streams of momentary dharmas. Each dharma exists for only an instant before being replaced by a successor.

This is sometimes called the doctrine of "momentariness" (*kṣaṇikavāda* in Sanskrit). Its temporal implications are radical: nothing persists; everything is flux; the apparent stability of ordinary experience is an illusion generated by the rapid succession of momentary events. The "chair" you see is not a persisting object; it is a series of chair-dharmas, each existing for only a moment before being replaced.

This view has a surprising resonance with certain interpretations of quantum field theory, in which what we call "particles" are excitations of fields — momentary events rather than enduring substances. The parallel should not be over-stated (the underlying metaphysics is different), but it illustrates that the Abhidharma analysis is not merely a religious claim but a substantive philosophical position about the nature of temporal reality.

## Nāgārjuna on Time

Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE) was the founder of the Madhyamaka school of Buddhist philosophy, arguably the most sophisticated philosophical tradition in the entire Buddhist canon. His *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (MMK) — "Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way" — contains a chapter (Chapter 19) devoted specifically to time, which repays careful philosophical attention.

Nāgārjuna's approach is characteristic of the Madhyamaka method: he does not advance a positive metaphysical theory about the nature of time; he argues that all positive theories generate incoherence when examined with sufficient care. His argument proceeds by eliminating the candidate accounts of temporal existence.

The central argument in MMK XIX can be reconstructed as follows. Consider the present time. If the present time has intrinsic existence (exists from its own side, independently, as it is in itself), then it must exist either in dependence on the past and future, or independently of them.

(a) *Dependent existence*: If the present is what it is in dependence on past and future (it is present because the past is past and the future is future), then the present has no intrinsic, self-standing existence — its nature is constituted by its relations to other times. But then the present does not exist intrinsically; it is empty of intrinsic nature (śūnya).

(b) *Independent existence*: If the present exists independently of past and future, then the concepts of "past," "present," and "future" are mutually independent — they are not related to each other by temporal relations. But then the very meaning of "present" as a temporal concept is lost; "present" means nothing without a contrast with past and future.

Either way, the present lacks intrinsic temporal existence. The same argument applies to past and future. Nāgārjuna concludes: "Past time, future time, and present time do not exist (as intrinsically real)" (MMK XIX.1, Garfield translation).

## The Two-Truths Doctrine

Crucially, Nāgārjuna does not conclude that time is "nothing at all." He employs the Madhyamaka "two truths" doctrine: there is *conventional truth* (samvrti-satya) — the level at which time, persons, chairs, and causal relations are real and operative; and *ultimate truth* (paramārtha-satya) — the level at which these things are found to be empty of intrinsic, self-standing nature when subjected to thorough philosophical analysis.

Time is conventionally real: it operates effectively in ordinary experience and practical life. It is ultimately unreal: it has no intrinsic nature that survives rigorous philosophical analysis. This is not a contradiction; it is a distinction between levels of discourse and analysis.

The two-truths framework makes Nāgārjuna's view significantly different from McTaggart's conclusion that time is "unreal." McTaggart's conclusion is meant to apply at the level of ultimate metaphysical truth — there is no time, and what appears to be time is an appearance of something else. Nāgārjuna's conclusion is also about ultimate truth, but paired with an affirmation of conventional reality. The practitioner is expected to navigate between the two truths skillfully, neither reifying time as intrinsically real nor nihilistically denying its conventional operation.

## Implications and Comparisons

Several philosophical implications deserve note.

*The rejection of both eternalism and annihilationism*: Nāgārjuna's "middle way" (*madhyamā pratipad*) avoids the extreme of holding that things eternally persist (eternalism) and the extreme of holding that things simply vanish without any continuity (annihilationism). Applied to time, this implies rejecting both the block universe (all times equally real) and radical presentism (only the present is real, past and future are nothing).

*Comparison with McTaggart*: McTaggart argued that temporal properties are contradictory and that time is therefore unreal. Nāgārjuna argues that temporal properties lack intrinsic existence. These are different claims, though related: both involve the conclusion that time, as ordinarily conceived, does not survive philosophical analysis. The methods are different (Western analytic regress vs. Madhyamaka analysis of dependent origination), and the conclusions are differently qualified.

*Comparison with Barbour*: Barbour eliminates time from the fundamental ontology and treats the appearance of time as generated by configurations containing records. Nāgārjuna would resist the inference from "time lacks intrinsic nature" to "time is merely apparent" — the conventional reality of time is affirmed, not explained away. The parallels are suggestive but should not be overstated.
