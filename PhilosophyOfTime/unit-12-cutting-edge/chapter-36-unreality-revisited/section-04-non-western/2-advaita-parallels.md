# 36.4.2 Advaita Vedanta and the Timelessness of Brahman

---

## The Framework of Advaita Vedanta

Advaita ("non-dual") Vedanta is a school of Hindu philosophy whose most important systematizer was Ādi Śaṅkara (c. 788–820 CE). Advaita holds that ultimate reality is *Brahman* — the single, undivided, self-luminous consciousness that underlies all apparent multiplicity. The world of multiplicity — of many objects, many persons, many moments — is the appearance of Brahman through the power of *māyā* (cosmic illusion or creative power).

Within this framework, time occupies a specific position: it is a feature of the world as it appears through māyā, not a feature of Brahman as it is in itself. Brahman is *nirguna* — without qualities, without attributes, without limitation — and this includes the absence of temporal determination. Brahman does not exist *at a time*; it is not earlier or later than anything; it does not change, because change requires temporal distinction between a before-state and an after-state. Brahman is eternal in the sense of being *outside time* — not in the sense of lasting forever through time, but in the sense of being timelessly present.

## Time as a Feature of Māyā

In the Advaita framework, time (*kāla*) is one of the categories through which Brahman appears as the multiplicity of the world. The appearance of temporal succession — the sequence of moments, the flow from past to present to future — is a feature of the appearance, not of Brahman itself. Brahman, as experienced in the state of highest knowledge (*jñāna*), is recognized as timeless and unchanging.

This is not a denial that time is experienced or practically operative. At the *vyāvahārika* (conventional) level — the level of ordinary practical existence — time is real and operates effectively. We plan, we remember, we anticipate; causation operates; history unfolds. But at the *pāramārthika* (ultimate) level — the level of Brahman as it is in itself — there is no time, no distinction between before and after, no temporal multiplicity.

The structure closely parallels Nāgārjuna's two-truths doctrine, though the metaphysical framework is different. Nāgārjuna's conclusion is the emptiness of intrinsic nature (śūnyatā); Śaṅkara's conclusion is the positive identification of ultimate reality with Brahman-Consciousness. Both distinguish conventional from ultimate truth, and both identify time as a feature of the conventional rather than the ultimate.

## The *Jīva* and the Experience of Time

The individual soul (*jīva*) — what each of us ordinarily takes ourselves to be — is, in the Advaita framework, an apparent limitation of Brahman. The jīva experiences itself as a separate, temporal being: born, growing, aging, dying. This experience of temporal existence is part of the appearance generated by māyā — specifically, by *avidyā* (ignorance of the ultimate nature of reality).

The path of Advaita practice is, in part, a path of progressive recognition that the jīva's temporal experience is not ultimately real — that beneath the appearance of a temporal, finite individual is the timeless, infinite Brahman with which the jīva is ultimately identical (*Tat tvam asi* — "That thou art," *Chāndogya Upaniṣad* 6.8.7). This recognition (jñāna) is not a matter of believing a philosophical proposition but of direct experiential realization.

The relationship between this philosophical claim and ordinary temporal experience is subtle. The experience of temporal flow does not stop for the *jñānin* (one who has realized Brahman); the appearances continue. But the *attitude* toward those appearances changes: they are no longer taken to be ultimately real. This is analogous to the way a philosopher who accepts the block universe continues to experience temporal flow while knowing (intellectually) that the flow is an appearance without metaphysical foundations.

## Comparison with Western Traditions

The Advaita treatment of time invites comparison with several Western positions.

*Comparison with Plotinus*: The Neoplatonist philosopher Plotinus (c. 204–270 CE, whose work was studied in Chapter 7) holds that the highest reality — the *One* — is timeless, and that time is a feature of the lower levels of being (*Soul* and *Intellect*). This is structurally parallel to the Advaita framework: ultimate reality (Brahman/the One) is timeless, and time is a feature of the appearance of lower-level reality.

*Comparison with McTaggart*: McTaggart argued that time is unreal and that the appearance of temporal succession is generated by an underlying reality (the "C-series") that is not temporal. Advaita holds that the appearance of temporal succession is generated by Brahman's māyā-power. Both views locate the appearance of time in something that is not itself temporal.

*Comparison with Barbour*: Barbour's timeless universe is generated by the wavefunction of the universe — a mathematical object that exists on a configuration space. The appearance of time arises from configurations containing records. Advaita's timeless Brahman generates the appearance of time through the creative power of māyā. The structure is different (physicalist vs. idealist), but both involve a timeless ground and a derived temporal appearance.

## Methodological Reflections

Engaging with non-Western traditions in a philosophy of time course requires methodological care. The risk of "confirmation bias" is real: it is easy to find apparent parallels between Western physics and non-Western philosophy and to present them as mutual vindications, without doing justice to the differences. Nāgārjuna is not Barbour; Śaṅkara is not McTaggart; the convergences are real but incomplete.

What genuine cross-traditional engagement offers is not confirmation but enrichment: additional conceptual tools, different emphases, different ways of formulating questions. The Madhyamaka analysis of dependent origination provides a different route to the conclusion that time lacks intrinsic nature — a route that does not depend on Newtonian mechanics or quantum gravity but on careful analysis of concepts. The Advaita framework provides a rich account of the relationship between the timeless and the temporal that the Western tradition has not fully developed.

Both traditions deserve engagement on their own terms, with their own presuppositions and their own methods, before comparative conclusions are drawn.
