# 37.1.1 Strong and Weak Emergence

---

## The Distinction

The concept of emergence has been analyzed with increasing precision in recent philosophy of science. The most influential distinction is between *weak* and *strong* emergence (Bedau 1997: 375–399; Chalmers 2006: 244–254).

*Weak emergence* occurs when a macro-level property is in principle derivable from the micro-level description, even if the derivation is computationally complex or practically difficult. The behavior of a weather system emerges weakly from the positions and momenta of its constituent molecules: given sufficient computational resources, we could derive the weather from the molecular physics. The macro-level phenomenon is not ontologically additional — it supervenes on and is constituted by the micro-level. Weak emergence is ubiquitous in science: temperature, pressure, viscosity, and most macroscopic properties are weakly emergent from microscopic physics.

*Strong emergence* occurs when a macro-level property is not derivable from the micro-level description — even in principle, even with unlimited computational resources. The macro-level property is genuinely novel, not captured by any description of the micro-level parts and their relations. Strong emergence would imply a kind of ontological novelty at the macro-level: properties that exist over and above anything in the substrate.

Chalmers (2006) is the main philosophical advocate of strong emergence, arguing that phenomenal consciousness — the felt quality of experience — is strongly emergent from physical substrates. Most philosophers and scientists are skeptical of strong emergence, preferring to see apparent cases as arising from complex weak emergence or from limitations in our current understanding.

## Spacetime Emergence: Which Kind?

The question is: if spacetime is emergent from a non-spatiotemporal substrate (as suggested by AdS/CFT and related programs), is the emergence strong or weak?

If the emergence is weak, then spatiotemporal properties are in principle derivable from the non-spatiotemporal substrate. The smooth spacetime we observe is a high-level, coarse-grained description of something more fundamental; the derivation exists but is complex. This would be the scientifically standard case: spacetime joins temperature and other macroscopic properties as a weakly emergent feature.

If the emergence is strong, then spatiotemporal properties are not derivable from the substrate — they arise in a way that cannot be captured by any description of the underlying non-spatiotemporal reality. This would be a genuinely astonishing result: the very arena of physical existence would be ontologically additional to the substrate.

The working assumption in the theoretical physics community is that the emergence is weak — that there exist (or will be found) explicit derivations connecting the non-spatiotemporal substrate to the spatiotemporal macro-structure. The AdS/CFT correspondence provides the most detailed example we have of such a derivation (see Section 37.2), in which specific features of the bulk spacetime correspond to specific features of the boundary field theory.

## Implications for Temporal Ontology

Whether the emergence is strong or weak, the emergence thesis has significant implications for temporal ontology.

*If weak emergence*: time, as a feature of spacetime, is a high-level description that applies at the level of the emergent spacetime. Time is real — as real as temperature — but it is not fundamental. The fundamental description of reality does not include temporal predicates; temporal predicates apply only to the emergent, coarse-grained description. This is a form of temporal reductionism: time is not eliminated but is reduced to more fundamental non-temporal features.

*If strong emergence*: time would be a genuinely novel property of the emergent spacetime, not reducible to the non-temporal substrate. This would make temporal properties ontologically irreducible — something like the irreducibility of phenomenal consciousness on Chalmers's view. But as noted, strong emergence is not the working hypothesis.

In either case, the emergence thesis implies that the metaphysical questions about time examined in this course — temporal ontology, temporal direction, temporal passage — are questions about an emergent structure, not about fundamental reality. The fundamental reality is a non-spatiotemporal substrate; temporal structure supervenes on (weakly emerges from) this substrate. This is a significant revision of the background assumption of most analytic philosophy of time, which takes the spatiotemporal framework as given.
