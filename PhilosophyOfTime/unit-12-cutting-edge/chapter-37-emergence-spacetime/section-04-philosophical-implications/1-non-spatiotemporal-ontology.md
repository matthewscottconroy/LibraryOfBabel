# 37.4.1 Non-Spatiotemporal Ontologies and Spacetime Functionalism

---

## What Could the Substrate Be?

If spacetime is emergent, what is it emergent *from*? Different approaches to quantum gravity propose different answers.

*Loop quantum gravity* (Rovelli and Smolin 1988: 593–597; Ashtekar and Lewandowski 2004: R53–R152) proposes that the fundamental structure is a *spin network* — a graph whose nodes represent quanta of volume and whose edges represent quanta of area. The smooth spacetime of general relativity emerges from spin networks at large scales, much as the smooth surface of a liquid emerges from many discrete molecules. Spin networks are not spatiotemporal structures; they are combinatorial/algebraic objects — graphs with labels — from which spatial and temporal structure are derived.

*Causal dynamical triangulation* (CDT; Ambjørn, Jurkiewicz, and Loll 2004: 011301) approaches quantum gravity by summing over simplicial complexes — discrete triangulations of spacetime — with causal constraints. The path integral is dominated by configurations that give rise to a smooth, four-dimensional spacetime at large scales. The fundamental structures are simplices (generalized triangles) with a causal orientation; the smooth spacetime emerges statistically.

*String theory* does not predict a specific non-spatiotemporal substrate in the same way. Strings are extended objects living in a background spacetime. However, at energies near the Planck scale, the string perturbation expansion breaks down, and the concept of a background spacetime is no longer applicable. Some formulations of string theory (M-theory, matrix models) suggest a non-spatiotemporal structure (11-dimensional supergravity, matrix quantum mechanics) from which spacetime emerges in certain limits.

In all these cases, the substrate is a mathematical/quantum structure that is not itself a spacetime — it does not have points with coordinate positions, it does not have a metric, and its elements do not stand in spatial or temporal relations in the standard sense.

## Spacetime Functionalism

How should we think about the ontological status of spacetime if it is emergent from a non-spatiotemporal substrate? Huggett and Wüthrich (2018: 30–42) propose an answer: *spacetime functionalism*.

Spacetime functionalism draws an analogy with functional theories of mind. Functionalism in philosophy of mind holds that mental states are defined by their functional roles — by the causal-computational relations they stand in to inputs, outputs, and other mental states — rather than by their physical constitution. Pain is whatever plays the pain-role: it is caused by tissue damage, it causes avoidance behavior and distress, it interacts with other mental states in characteristic ways. Whatever physical or non-physical substrate realizes this functional role counts as pain.

Similarly, spacetime functionalism holds that spatiotemporal structure is whatever plays the spacetime role: it is what stands in spatiotemporal relations, what provides the arena for physical processes, what satisfies the laws that govern spatial and temporal behavior. Whatever in the fundamental physics plays this role — whether it is a spin network, a matrix model, or an entanglement structure — counts as spacetime.

On this view, the question "is there spacetime?" does not require a specific physical substrate. It requires only that there be some structure in the fundamental physics that plays the spacetime role — that can be interpreted as providing a spatiotemporal framework when appropriately coarse-grained. If the spin network or the entanglement structure plays this role (as the physics suggests), then there is spacetime — it is just that the identity of what counts as spacetime is more flexible than we thought.

## Implications for Temporal Ontology

Spacetime functionalism has interesting implications for the questions that have occupied us throughout this course. If temporal structure is whatever plays the time-role — providing temporal ordering, temporal direction, temporal passage (or its appearance) — then the metaphysical questions about time are questions about which physical structures play this role and how.

This is a somewhat deflationary approach: it does not answer the question of whether the block universe is true, or whether temporal passage is real, by appeal to the fundamental physics directly. Instead, it says: whatever the fundamental physics provides that plays the relevant role, that is time. If the fundamental physics provides only a B-series-like ordering, then B-theory is vindicated. If it provides something that plays an A-series role (like causal set dynamics), A-theory is vindicated. The physics adjudicates by determining which role is played.

The limitation of spacetime functionalism is that it defers rather than resolves some of the hardest questions. Specifying the "spacetime role" precisely, and determining whether a given physical structure plays it, is a non-trivial philosophical task. The analogy with functionalism about mind is suggestive but not fully worked out.

## The Stakes

The emergence of spacetime program represents the deepest current challenge to the ordinary assumptions of the philosophy of time. If time — as a feature of spacetime — is emergent from a non-spatiotemporal substrate, then the foundational questions of the discipline are not about fundamental physics but about how a certain kind of emergent structure arises from that physics, and about the significance of that emergent structure for experience, knowledge, and value.

This does not make the philosophy of time less important; it makes it more important, and harder. The harder questions are: what kind of existence does emergent temporal structure have? Can emergent time ground the A-series, or only the B-series? Can we make sense of temporal becoming if time is emergent? Can we maintain the ethical significance of temporal facts (obligations to future generations, the badness of death) if time is emergent? These questions have no settled answers, but they are the right questions to be asking.
