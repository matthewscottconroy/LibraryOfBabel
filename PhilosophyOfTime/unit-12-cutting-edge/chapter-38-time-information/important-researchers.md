# Chapter 38 Important Researchers: Time and Information

---

**Claude Shannon (1916–2001)**
American mathematician and electrical engineer at Bell Laboratories and MIT, whose 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" founded information theory. Shannon introduced the concept of information entropy (*H* = −Σ *p*ᵢ log₂ *p*ᵢ), defined the "bit" as the fundamental unit of information, established the channel capacity theorem, and created the mathematical framework that underlies modern telecommunications and computing. The formal identity between Shannon entropy and Boltzmann-Gibbs thermodynamic entropy has been one of the most fertile and contested connections in twentieth-century physics, provoking deep questions about whether information is physically real. Shannon was notoriously reluctant to speculate about philosophical implications of his work, but those implications have been worked out by others ever since.

**Rolf Landauer (1927–1999)**
German-American physicist at IBM Research, best known for Landauer's principle (1961): erasing one bit of information necessarily generates at least *k_B T* ln 2 of heat. This result demonstrated that information is not merely abstract — it has irreducible thermodynamic consequences — and connected information processing to the second law of thermodynamics. Landauer's principle resolved the Maxwell's demon paradox by showing that the demon's act of erasing its memory generates entropy that compensates the entropy it reduces through sorting. Landauer's broader philosophical position was that "information is physical": information cannot exist without a physical substrate, and operations on information have physical consequences. The principle has been experimentally confirmed in multiple laboratories.

**Stephen Hawking (1942–2018)**
British theoretical physicist and cosmologist at Cambridge University whose contributions to black hole physics and cosmology were transformative. His 1975 calculation of Hawking radiation — the thermal radiation emitted by black holes as a quantum effect — is among the most important results in theoretical physics, combining quantum field theory and general relativity. Hawking's original position was that information is lost in black hole evaporation, violating the unitarity of quantum mechanics; he later conceded (2004) that information is probably preserved. The black hole information paradox he identified remains among the most important unsolved problems in theoretical physics.

**Don Page (1948–)**
Canadian theoretical physicist at the University of Alberta whose 1993 analysis of the entanglement entropy of Hawking radiation showed that — if the evolution is unitary — the entropy should follow a characteristic curve (the Page curve): rising to a maximum at the "Page time" (when roughly half the black hole's entropy has been radiated), then falling back to zero as the black hole fully evaporates. The Page curve became the benchmark against which any purported resolution of the information paradox must be measured. Page has also made important contributions to quantum cosmology, Bayesian reasoning in physics, and the anthropic principle.

**Ahmed Almheiri (1987–)**
Emirati theoretical physicist at the Institute for Advanced Study who led the development of the island formula — one of the most important recent advances on the black hole information paradox. The island formula (Almheiri et al. 2019–2020) provides a prescription for calculating the entanglement entropy of Hawking radiation that reproduces the Page curve, strongly suggesting that information is preserved in black hole evaporation. Almheiri's work represents a synthesis of quantum information theory, semiclassical gravity, and holography, and has generated an enormous amount of follow-up research.

**Edwin Jaynes (1922–1998)**
American physicist and statistician at Washington University who developed the maximum entropy approach to statistical mechanics, arguing that thermodynamic entropy is a measure of missing information and that statistical mechanics is a branch of Bayesian inference rather than a separate physical theory. Jaynes's approach — that the equilibrium distribution is the maximum-entropy distribution consistent with macroscopic constraints — provides the clearest statement of the "information is physical" thesis in classical statistical mechanics and directly connects thermodynamic entropy to Shannon entropy.

**Leo Szilard (1898–1964)**
Hungarian-American physicist who in 1929 published the first rigorous analysis of Maxwell's demon, showing that the demon's acquisition of information about individual molecules must generate entropy that compensates the entropy reduction achieved by sorting. Szilard's 1929 paper is the founding document of the information-theoretic approach to thermodynamics and anticipated Landauer's principle by three decades. Szilard also made foundational contributions to nuclear physics (he conceived the neutron chain reaction in 1933) and worked on the Manhattan Project. His analysis of Maxwell's demon established for the first time that measurement and information acquisition have irreducible thermodynamic consequences.

**Stephen Wolfram (1959–)**
British-American physicist and computer scientist who developed *A New Kind of Science* (2002), arguing that cellular automata and other simple computational rules can generate the full complexity of natural phenomena. His 2020 "Wolfram Physics Project" proposes that the universe is a hypergraph evolving according to simple rewriting rules, with time corresponding to the sequential application of those rules. Wolfram's computational approach to time is the most developed contemporary version of digital physics, though it remains controversial in both physics and philosophy. Wolfram also created the *Mathematica* computer algebra system.
