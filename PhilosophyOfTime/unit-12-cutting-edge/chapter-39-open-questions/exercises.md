# Chapter 39 Exercises: Open Questions and the Future of the Philosophy of Time

---

## Comprehension and Analysis

**1. The Passage Problem**
Distinguish the *phenomenological* passage problem from the *metaphysical* passage problem. The phenomenological problem asks why we experience time as flowing; the metaphysical problem asks whether there is anything in the objective structure of the world that corresponds to this experience. Why might a satisfactory answer to one problem fail to answer the other? Which problem do you find more tractable, and why?

**2. Presentism and Special Relativity**
Outline the standard argument that presentism is incompatible with special relativity (the Putnam-Rietdijk argument). Then evaluate the three main presentist responses: neo-Lorentzianism, relativist presentism, and the growing block response. Which response is most defensible? What would each response need to concede or establish in order to succeed?

**3. The Problem of Time in Quantum Gravity**
The Wheeler-DeWitt equation contains no time variable. What are the philosophical implications of this for the reality of time? Distinguish Rovelli's "thermal time" response (time is emergent from thermodynamics) from the view that the absence of time in the fundamental equations shows that time is unreal. Are these different views, or does the second follow from the first?

**4. The Arrow of Time and Initial Conditions**
Why does the thermodynamic arrow of time depend on the low-entropy initial conditions of the universe? Why can't the second law be explained by the laws of physics alone (without an asymmetric initial condition)? Evaluate Penrose's claim that the low entropy of the initial conditions requires explanation. Is this an empirical question, a metaphysical question, or both?

**5. The Hard Problem of Temporal Experience**
State the hard problem of consciousness, then explain what makes temporal experience a particularly clear instance of the hard problem. What does the neuroscience of interval timing (dopaminergic mechanisms, the internal clock) explain, and what does it fail to explain about the phenomenology of temporal experience? Could a complete neuroscience of time perception in principle solve the hard problem of temporal experience? Defend your answer.

**6. The Specious Present**
Husserl argued that the experience of a melody as a melody — rather than as a series of isolated sounds — requires a temporal structure of retention (of the just-past) and protention (of the immediately forthcoming). Explain this analysis. What philosophical problem does it solve? What problems does it create? How does the specious present relate to the mathematical "instant" of physical time?

**7. Longtermism and Fanaticism**
The longtermist argument depends on the expected value of long-term outcomes vastly outweighing the expected value of near-term outcomes when future populations are taken into account. A critic argues that this reasoning is "fanatical" — that it would justify any short-term sacrifice for uncertain long-term gains. Reconstruct the fanaticism objection precisely and evaluate it. Does MacAskill's focus on existential risk reduction escape this objection?

## Short Essay Questions

**8. The Open Future and Free Will**
The ontological status of the future — whether future events are already real (eternalism) or not yet real (presentism, growing block) — seems relevant to questions about free will. If future events are already real, is it possible to act in a way that changes them? If future events are not yet real, does this open a space for genuine agency? Write a 600-word essay developing the connection (or lack of connection) between temporal ontology and agency.

**9. Temporal Justice**
The concept of temporal justice extends distributive justice from space (who gets what) to time (who gets when, and for how long). Identify three distinct dimensions of temporal justice: obligations to future generations, reparations for historical injustice, and the distribution of free time within contemporary societies. For each dimension, what philosophical question is most pressing, and what considerations are most relevant to answering it?

**10. The Methodological Division**
Some philosophers of time argue that the metaphysics should be constrained by physics — that a metaphysical position that is incompatible with our best physical theories is disqualified. Others argue that metaphysics is autonomous — that it asks a different kind of question from physics and cannot be adjudicated by physical theory. Develop and evaluate both sides of this methodological debate. What role should physics play in constraining metaphysical claims about time?

**11. Cross-Cultural Philosophy of Time**
Section 39.5 argues that cross-cultural engagement in the philosophy of time is both possible and valuable, but that it requires care to avoid appropriation or exoticization. Using the examples of Nāgārjuna (36.4.1) and Śaṅkara (36.4.2), illustrate what genuine cross-cultural philosophical engagement looks like. What does each tradition contribute that Western analytic philosophy of time does not? Where are the limits of cross-cultural comparison?

## Observational and Laboratory Exercises

**12. Time Perception and Altered States**
The internal clock (interval timing) is affected by attention, arousal, and pharmacological agents. Conduct a series of prospective duration estimation experiments on yourself (with appropriate ethical constraints — no pharmacological agents). Estimate durations of 10 seconds, 30 seconds, and 1 minute under conditions of: (a) focused attention on time; (b) distraction by a demanding cognitive task; (c) mild exercise; (d) boredom (unstimulating waiting). Record your estimates and compare them to the actual durations. What do the results suggest about the mechanisms of interval timing? How do they relate to the philosophical claim that temporal experience reflects an internal clock?

**13. Researching Temporal Justice**
Access time-use survey data from the American Time Use Survey (ATUS, published annually by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics) or equivalent data for another country. Identify three measures of temporal inequality: (a) average weekly hours of paid labor by income quintile; (b) average hours of unpaid care work by gender; (c) commuting time by residential location (as a proxy for income). Construct a brief empirical analysis (two to three pages) of the temporal inequalities you find. What philosophical framework — from the readings in Chapters 33 and 39 — is most adequate for evaluating these inequalities as matters of justice?
