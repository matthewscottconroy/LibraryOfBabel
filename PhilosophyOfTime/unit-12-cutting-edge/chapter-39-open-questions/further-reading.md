## Annotated Entries

**Callender, Craig. 2017. *What Makes Time Special?* Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
The most comprehensive recent philosophical treatment of the arrow of time, temporal passage, and the relationship between physical time and manifest (experienced) time. Callender argues that much of the apparent mystery of time arises from conflating different questions and that careful attention to what physics actually says dissolves many apparent puzzles. The book is technically demanding in places but consistently illuminating; it represents the state of the art in philosophically rigorous engagement with the science of time. Essential reading for anyone pursuing the field seriously.

**Maudlin, Tim. 2007. *The Metaphysics Within Physics*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
A collection of essays defending the view that metaphysics of time should be grounded in physics — that what time is depends on what our best physical theories say, not on conceptual analysis conducted independently of science. Chapter 4, "On the Passing of Time," defends primitivism about temporal directionality — the view that temporal passage is fundamental and cannot be derived from entropy or any other more basic feature — against deflationary and eliminativist accounts. Challenging but rewarding.

**Prosser, Simon. 2016. *Experiencing Time*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
A careful philosophical treatment of the phenomenology and epistemology of temporal experience, arguing for illusionism about the experience of passage: while time seems to flow, this experience does not accurately represent an objective feature of the world. Prosser distinguishes carefully between different claims that might be meant by "time flows," engages with both the philosophical literature and the empirical literature on temporal perception, and provides the most thorough current defense of the illusionist position.

**Skow, Bradford. 2015. *Objective Becoming*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
A defense of objective temporal passage against illusionist and eliminativist accounts. Skow defends the "moving spotlight" theory — the view that there is an objective, moving present that is genuinely more real or metaphysically privileged than other times — and responds to the main philosophical objections to this view. An essential complement to Prosser's illusionism; reading both together gives a comprehensive picture of the current state of the debate about passage.

**Ismael, Jenann T. 2016. *How Physics Makes Us Free*. Oxford: Oxford University Press.**
An exploration of the relationship between the determinism of physics and the freedom of persons, with extensive discussion of the temporal dimensions of agency and consciousness. Ismael argues that the situated perspective of an agent — located at a particular time, with a particular past and an anticipated future — is genuinely compatible with the timeless perspective of physics, and that this reconciliation illuminates both the nature of time and the nature of freedom. Beautifully written and philosophically rigorous.

**MacAskill, William. 2022. *What We Owe the Future*. New York: Basic Books.**
The principal philosophical statement of longtermism: the view that improving the long-run future of humanity is among the most morally important things we can do. MacAskill develops the expected value argument for longtermism with care, addresses the main objections, and translates the philosophical argument into concrete recommendations. The book is accessible and well-written. Read alongside Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (Part IV) for the philosophical foundations of intergenerational ethics, and with critical attention to the non-identity problem and the ethics of imposing risks on future people.

**Huggett, Nick, and Christian Wüthrich, eds. 2018. *Beyond Spacetime: The Foundations of Quantum Gravity*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.**
The definitive collection of philosophical essays on the foundations of quantum gravity and the emergence of spacetime. The introduction by Huggett and Wüthrich provides an accessible overview of the philosophical issues. The essays cover the problem of time, emergent spacetime, spacetime functionalism, causal set theory, loop quantum gravity, and string theory/holography. This is where the frontier of philosophy of physics and the philosophy of time most productively intersect.

**Thomas, Emily. 2023. *Time: A History of a Big Idea*. Glasgow: William Collins.**
A wide-ranging survey of philosophical thinking about time across cultures and centuries. Thomas covers Western philosophy from the pre-Socratics to the present, with attention to non-Western traditions and to the historical context in which philosophical ideas about time developed. Accessible to general readers while remaining philosophically substantive. Valuable for situating the technical debates of academic philosophy of time in their broader intellectual history.

**Prior, A.N. 1967. *Past, Present and Future*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.**
The foundational text of tense logic, in which Prior develops the formal system of temporal operators and explores their metaphysical implications. Prior defends a broadly A-theoretic approach (temporal passage is real) using formal logical tools. The book requires some familiarity with modal and tense logic but is accessible to readers with philosophical training. Understanding tense logic as developed here is essential for following much of the formal literature on the metaphysics of time.
