# Chapter 39 Important Researchers: Open Questions and the Future of the Philosophy of Time

---

**Craig Callender (1967–)**
American philosopher at the University of California, San Diego, whose work spans the philosophy of physics, the philosophy of time, and the foundations of thermodynamics. His book *What Makes Time Special?* (2017) is the most comprehensive recent philosophical treatment of the arrow of time, temporal passage, and the relationship between physical and manifest time. Callender has been an important voice arguing that analytic metaphysics of time must engage seriously with physics and that much traditional metaphysics of time is "physics-free." His work on the Past Hypothesis and the thermodynamic arrow, on the conventionality of the second law, and on the pragmatics of temporal concepts is philosophically rigorous and scientifically informed.

**Tim Maudlin (1958–)**
American philosopher at New York University, one of the most influential contemporary philosophers of physics and physics-oriented philosophers of time. His books *The Metaphysics Within Physics* (2007) and *Philosophy of Physics: Space and Time* (2012) argue that the metaphysics of time should be grounded in what physics says, not in intuition or conceptual analysis conducted independently of physics. Maudlin defends a primitivist view about the direction of time — temporal directionality is a fundamental feature of reality, not derived from entropy or any other factor. His work on causation, laws of nature, and the direction of time is philosophically foundational.

**Jenann Ismael (1968–)**
American philosopher at Columbia University whose work bridges philosophy of physics, philosophy of mind, and metaphysics. Her books *The Situated Self* (2007) and *How Physics Makes Us Free* (2016) explore the relationship between objective time (the time of physics) and the time of experience (the time of persons who are situated in a particular moment, with a particular history and anticipated future). Ismael argues that the distinction between physical time and experiential time is less problematic than it appears once we understand how a self-representing subject is embedded in a physical world. Her work is notable for integrating physics, philosophy of mind, and practical philosophy.

**David Wallace (1976–)**
British philosopher of physics at the University of Southern California, whose work on the Everett interpretation of quantum mechanics, the foundations of statistical mechanics, and the emergence of the arrow of time is among the most technically sophisticated in contemporary philosophy of physics. His book *The Emergent Multiverse* (2012) provides the most thorough philosophical defense of the many-worlds interpretation. His work on the arrow of time, including the relationship between the Past Hypothesis and the emergence of the macroscopic world from quantum mechanics, is central to contemporary debates.

**William MacAskill (1987–)**
British philosopher at Oxford University, co-founder of the effective altruism movement and principal philosophical developer of longtermism. His book *What We Owe the Future* (2022) is the most systematic philosophical treatment of longtermism — the view that improving the long-run future of humanity is among the most morally important things we can do. MacAskill develops the philosophical foundations of longtermism, addresses objections (including the non-identity problem and epistemic limits on long-run planning), and argues for specific practical priorities (reducing existential risk, improving global governance, ensuring safe AI development). His work has been both widely praised and sharply criticized.

**Bradford Skow (1978–)**
American philosopher at MIT whose work on the metaphysics of time has been influential, particularly his defense of the "moving spotlight" theory — a form of presentism that holds that time passes in the sense that the present moves through a block universe. His book *Objective Becoming* (2015) defends the view that temporal passage is objective — that there is a genuine, mind-independent moving present — against the view that passage is illusory. Skow's work represents the most sophisticated contemporary defense of passage-realism within an analytic framework.

**Simon Prosser**
British philosopher at the University of St. Andrews whose work on the philosophy of time focuses on the experience of temporal passage and whether that experience is veridical. His book *Experiencing Time* (2016) defends a form of illusionism about temporal passage: while we experience time as flowing, this experience does not accurately represent an objective feature of the world. The experience of passage is a cognitive construction, not a perception of an objective flow. Prosser's careful engagement with both philosophical and empirical arguments about temporal experience makes his work a valuable contribution to the field.

**Jonardon Ganeri (1964–)**
British-Indian philosopher at New York University whose work bridges analytic philosophy and classical Indian philosophy, with significant contributions to the philosophy of mind, the philosophy of logic, and comparative philosophy. His books *The Concealed Art of the Soul* (2007) and *The Self: Naturalism, Consciousness, and the First-Person Stance* (2012) demonstrate how classical Indian philosophy (particularly Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika realism and Buddhist anti-realism about the self and time) can inform and enrich analytic debates. Ganeri's work represents the most philosophically rigorous and textually careful engagement with Indian philosophy of time in the analytic tradition.

**Emily Thomas (1985–)**
British philosopher at Durham University whose work on the history of philosophy of space and time has recovered important figures and arguments from the early modern and Victorian periods. Her book *The Meaning of Travel* (2020) uses the history of travel philosophy to illuminate questions about the experience of space and time. Her more recent *Time* (2023) is a wide-ranging historical and philosophical survey of the philosophy of time across cultures and centuries, including non-Western traditions. Thomas's work demonstrates the value of historical and cross-cultural approaches to the philosophy of time.

**Nick Beckstead**
American philosopher and AI safety researcher, whose Oxford doctoral dissertation *On the Overwhelming Importance of Shaping the Far Future* (2013) provided the first systematic philosophical development of longtermism as a moral view. Beckstead's dissertation develops the expected value argument for longtermism with rigor, addresses objections from moral theory and population ethics, and connects the philosophical argument to concrete claims about what actions would most effectively shape the long-run future. Beckstead later became a research director at the Open Philanthropy Project, connecting philosophical work on longtermism to philanthropic practice.
