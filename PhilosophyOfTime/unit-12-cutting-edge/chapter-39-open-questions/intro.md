# Chapter 39: Open Questions and the Future of the Philosophy of Time

---

## Chapter Introduction

A comprehensive tour of the philosophy of time should end not with conclusions but with questions. This is not a failure of the discipline; it is its nature. Philosophy of time is genuinely hard. Its hardest questions are not merely technically demanding — requiring more mathematics or more empirical data — but conceptually difficult in ways that more mathematics or data will not straightforwardly resolve. They require new ways of thinking, not just new inputs to existing ways of thinking.

This closing chapter surveys the terrain of genuinely unsettled questions across the philosophy of time, identifies productive research directions, and invites the reader to take up the inquiry as a participant rather than a spectator. Section 39.1 revisits the unsolved problems in the metaphysics of time: the passage problem, the presentism-relativity impasse, and the ontological status of the future. Section 39.2 surveys the frontiers where physics and philosophy are most intensely in dialogue: quantum gravity's implications for temporal ontology, the cosmological arrow, and the measurement problem. Section 39.3 turns to time, consciousness, and neuroscience, and the hard problem of phenomenal temporal experience. Section 39.4 examines ethical and social dimensions of open temporal questions: longtermism, historical injustice, and temporal justice. Section 39.5 reflects on methodological questions: what roles remain for formal logic, analytic metaphysics, and cross-cultural comparison in the study of time.

This is an invitation rather than a survey. The questions examined here have occupied some of the most acute philosophical minds in history — Augustine, Aristotle, Kant, McTaggart, Einstein, Husserl — and remain genuinely open. If this course has done its work, you are now better equipped to think about them than you were when you began. What you do with that equipment is up to you.
