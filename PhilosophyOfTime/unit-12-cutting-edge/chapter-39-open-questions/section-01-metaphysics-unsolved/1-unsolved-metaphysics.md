# 39.1.1 The Deepest Unsolved Problems in the Metaphysics of Time

---

## The Passage Problem

No problem in the philosophy of time is more persistent or more resistant to satisfying solution than the problem of temporal passage. The problem has a simple statement: time seems to pass, to flow from past to future, to move. This is among the most vivid and universal features of our experience of time. Yet philosophers and physicists have found it extraordinarily difficult to give a coherent account of what this passage consists in.

The problem has two aspects. The first is the *rate problem*: at what rate does time pass? The natural answer — "at one second per second" — is trivially uninformative. A genuine rate requires two independent units: velocity is measured in meters per second, not meters per meter. "One second per second" measures time's passage in terms of itself, which gives no information. If time flows, it flows at a rate specified by some other temporal scale — but there is only one time, and the second dimension that a genuine rate requires is missing.

The second is the *metaphysical problem*: what is it for the present moment to change, to be replaced by a later moment? The block universe picture of four-dimensional spacetime represents all temporal positions as equally real, with no privileged "now." On this picture, there is no passage: all times exist simultaneously in the eternal block, and the "flow" of time is an appearance generated by the way conscious beings are situated within the block. But if this is right, the phenomenology of passage requires explanation. Why does it seem like time flows if it does not?

Several responses have been developed, none of them fully satisfying. *Primitivism* about passage (Maudlin 2007; Skow 2015) holds that passage is a primitive, fundamental feature of time that cannot be reduced to anything more basic. This preserves the phenomenological datum but at the cost of postulating a temporal feature that has no role in physics. *Illusionism* (Barbour 1999; Prosser 2016) holds that the experience of passage is a cognitive illusion — a feature of how we process and represent temporal information, not a feature of time itself. This fits with the block universe but requires explaining why the illusion is so universal and vivid. *Relationalism about passage* holds that time passes relative to consciousness or cognition — that the moving present is a feature of our mental lives, not of mind-independent reality.

The problem remains unsolved. No position is free of serious objections, and no consensus has emerged. This is not a stable resting place; it is an invitation to further work.

## Presentism and Relativity

Presentism — the view that only the present exists, that past things no longer exist and future things do not yet exist — is among the most intuitively compelling positions in the metaphysics of time. It captures the folk sense that the world changes by gaining new things and losing old ones, and that the present is the locus of reality.

Special relativity appears to pose a fatal challenge to presentism. Relativity abolishes absolute simultaneity: whether two spatially separated events are simultaneous depends on the reference frame in which they are assessed. There is no fact of the matter about which events are "happening now" that is independent of a choice of reference frame. If presentism requires that "the present" picks out a unique, frame-independent set of simultaneously existing things, then presentism is incompatible with special relativity: there is no such set.

The responses to this challenge constitute one of the most active areas in contemporary philosophy of time. *Neo-Lorentzian presentism* posits a privileged reference frame (perhaps determined by the cosmic microwave background, or by quantum gravity) in which the present is defined, while allowing that this frame is empirically undetectable in a Lorentz-invariant world. *Relativized presentism* holds that "existence" is a relation rather than a monadic property — things exist *relative to* a reference frame — so that the present exists relative to each frame. *Possibilist presentism* (Crisp 2007) appeals to metaphysically possible worlds to ground temporal claims. *Eliminativist presentism* (Stein 1991) abandons the claim that there is a unique objective present, accepting that "now" is indexical and frame-relative.

None of these responses is fully satisfying. Neo-Lorentzian presentism postulates metaphysical structure that physics does not endorse. Relativized presentism changes the meaning of "exists" in a way that many find objectionable. Eliminativist presentism abandons the distinctive claim that makes presentism interesting. The impasse remains. What would a genuinely satisfying reconciliation of presentism and relativity look like? This is a major open question.

## The Ontological Status of the Future

A third major open problem concerns the reality of the future. The growing block theory holds that the past and present are real but the future is not yet real. Presentism holds that only the present is real. The block universe holds that past, present, and future are equally real. Each of these positions faces its own difficulties.

The growing block faces the *epistemological problem*: if the growing block is correct, then from the inside — from within the block — we cannot tell whether we are at the "leading edge" of the block (the objective present) or at some earlier point. We always experience ourselves as being at the present, but if the block grows, there are earlier observers who also experienced themselves as being at the present but are now in the past. The growing block cannot explain why we are at the present rather than at some earlier time (Braddon-Mitchell 2004).

The open future — the view that the future is genuinely open and indeterminate — connects to questions about free will, the metaphysics of possibility, and the semantics of future-tensed statements. Aristotle's sea battle argument (*De Interpretatione* 9) raised the question of whether statements about the future have determinate truth values. Prior's tense logic provides formal tools for representing the open future, but the metaphysical basis for the openness remains contested.

What grounds the asymmetry between the fixed past and the open future? The causal-physical answer — that the past fixes the present, which fixes the future, through the laws of nature — is challenged by quantum mechanics, which introduces genuine indeterminism (though whether quantum indeterminism grounds the openness of the future or merely represents our epistemic limitations is contested). The metaphysical answer — that the past is real, the future is not, and this is a primitive metaphysical fact — is defensible but explanatorily unsatisfying.

## What Progress Looks Like

These three problems — passage, presentism-relativity, and the openness of the future — have resisted resolution for different reasons. The passage problem may require rethinking the relationship between phenomenology and metaphysics, and asking why our experiential evidence for passage should be taken as evidence for an objective feature of the world. The presentism-relativity problem may require either revising our commitment to the Lorentz symmetry of fundamental physics or revising our commitment to the claim that existence is an absolute rather than a relational property. The openness of the future may require a more careful account of the relationship between determinism, indeterminism, and metaphysical possibility.

Progress in philosophy does not always look like solutions. It sometimes looks like clarification: showing that a problem that seemed to have a simple solution is actually harder than it appeared, or showing that two problems that seemed distinct are actually the same problem in different guise. The three problems surveyed here have been substantially clarified in the last century. Whether they will be solved — and what a solution would look like — remains open.
