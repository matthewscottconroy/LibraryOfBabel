# 39.3.1 Time, Consciousness, and the Neuroscience Frontier

---

## The Neural Basis of the Specious Present

The specious present — the brief interval of time that feels like the "now," neither past memory nor anticipated future — was identified by psychologists in the late nineteenth century (James 1890; Stern 1897) and has been the subject of intense philosophical and empirical investigation ever since. Its duration varies with attention, arousal, and context, typically falling in the range of two to three seconds for most cognitive tasks.

Neuroscience has made significant progress in understanding the neural mechanisms underlying temporal perception, but the specious present remains philosophically puzzling. Several neural mechanisms contribute to temporal experience: the basal ganglia appear to be involved in timing intervals in the seconds range (scalar timing theory; Gibbon, Church, and Meck 1984); the cerebellum is involved in fine motor timing in the milliseconds range; circadian rhythms (driven by the suprachiasmatic nucleus) provide time-of-day information. But the question of how these mechanisms give rise to the *experience* of temporal flow — to the phenomenal quality of the present moment — is not answered by identifying the mechanisms.

The specious present raises a specific philosophical problem: the present, as physics understands it, is a durationless instant. But the experienced present has duration — it is a span of time, not a point. How can an extended temporal experience be present? Husserl's account of retention and protention (Chapter 26) addresses this by arguing that the experienced present always involves a "just past" (retention) and an "about to occur" (protention), so that duration is constitutive of the present rather than a departure from it. Neuroscience can study the mechanisms of retention and protention, but whether it can explain the phenomenal character of these temporal phenomena — the felt quality of "just now" versus "right now" — is less clear.

## The Hard Problem and Temporal Experience

The hard problem of consciousness (Chalmers 1995) — the problem of explaining why physical processes give rise to phenomenal experience at all, rather than merely to information processing without any subjective feel — applies with special force to temporal experience. Not only do we need to explain why there is something it is like to process temporal information, but we need to explain why that experience has the specific character it has: the felt flow of time, the distinction between memory and anticipation, the peculiar sense in which the present is "here" in a way that no other moment is.

Physicalist approaches to consciousness (functionalism, higher-order theories, global workspace theory, integrated information theory) face the challenge of explaining temporal phenomenology in physical or functional terms. Each approach generates specific predictions about the neural correlates of temporal experience that can in principle be tested. But even if the neural correlates are identified, the hard problem remains: why should any neural state give rise to the *experience* of temporal flow rather than merely the functional role of temporal processing?

Some philosophers have proposed that the hard problem is especially tractable for temporal experience. Ismael (2016) argues that the self's temporal character — its being located at a particular time, with a particular history and a particular anticipated future — is constitutive of what it is to be a self. On this view, temporal experience is not an add-on to an otherwise atemporal self but part of the essential structure of selfhood. This might explain why temporal experience is so fundamental and pervasive: to be a subject at all is to be temporally located.

## Disorders of Temporal Perception

Neurological and psychiatric disorders that affect temporal perception provide a natural experiment in the relationship between neural processes and temporal experience. Patients with damage to the basal ganglia (as in Parkinson's disease) have impaired interval timing: they tend to underestimate durations, producing movements and speech that are too fast. Patients with damage to the cerebellum have impaired millisecond timing, affecting motor coordination and the perception of rhythm. Patients with certain kinds of damage to the prefrontal cortex show impaired prospective memory — difficulty mentally projecting themselves into the future.

These disorders reveal the neural systems that contribute to temporal experience, but they also raise philosophical questions. If time perception is implemented by multiple specialized neural systems, is there a unified sense in which there is a single "experience of time"? Or is temporal experience composed of multiple distinct components that happen to be integrated in ordinary experience? The question echoes debates about the unity of consciousness more generally.

More radically: if time perception can be disrupted by neural damage, what does this tell us about the relationship between phenomenal temporal experience and objective time? One interpretation is that temporal experience is constructed by the brain, in which case what we experience as "the present" is a neural representation with a specific construction latency (estimated at roughly 80–100ms) rather than direct access to objective present time. If this is right, then "the present" as experienced is always slightly in the past, and the experience of real-time perception is partly confabulated.

## What Neuroscience Can and Cannot Settle

Neuroscience can identify the neural mechanisms that underlie temporal processing, the frequency with which different temporal experiences occur, and the conditions (pharmacological, developmental, pathological) under which these experiences are distorted. It can establish that temporal experience is dependent on neural processes and that different aspects of temporal experience are implemented by different neural systems.

What neuroscience cannot settle — at least, not without philosophical work — are the following questions: (1) Why do any neural processes give rise to phenomenal temporal experience? (2) Is the phenomenal quality of temporal experience veridical — does it accurately represent objective temporal features of the world, or is it a reliable but indirect representation? (3) What is the relationship between the experienced present and the objective present? (4) Can there be genuine temporal experience without a subject — without a system that has phenomenal consciousness?

These questions require philosophical analysis in addition to empirical research. The philosophy of time and the philosophy of mind are not separate disciplines; at the level of temporal consciousness, they converge.
