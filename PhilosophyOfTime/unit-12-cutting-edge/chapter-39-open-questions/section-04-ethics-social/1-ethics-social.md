## Longtermism and Our Obligations to the Deep Future

Longtermism is the view that the most important moral priority for individual and collective action is improving the long-run future of humanity (and potentially other sentient beings). The view has been developed most systematically by William MacAskill (*What We Owe the Future*, 2022) and Nick Beckstead (*On the Overwhelming Importance of Shaping the Far Future*, 2013) and is associated with the effective altruism movement.

The philosophical basis of longtermism rests on several claims. The *scale argument*: the number of possible future people vastly exceeds the current population; if the future extends for millions or billions of years, future people may outnumber current people by factors of billions or trillions. The *tractability argument*: certain interventions (reducing existential risk, improving governance, ensuring that advanced AI is developed safely) can significantly improve the probability of a good long-run future. The *expected value argument*: even small improvements in the probability of a good long-run future generate enormous expected moral value, given the vast number of future lives at stake.

Longtermism has been challenged on several philosophical grounds. The *non-identity problem* (Parfit 1984): the particular future people whose interests longtermist actions are meant to serve would not have existed if those actions had not been taken — their very existence depends on the actions we take. This makes it difficult to claim that we are benefiting specific individuals by securing a good future. The *discounting argument*: many moral and economic frameworks discount future welfare, giving less weight to outcomes that are temporally distant. Longtermists typically reject pure time preference (giving less weight to welfare merely because it occurs in the future), but the question of how to weight near and far future welfare is genuinely difficult. The *epistemic argument*: the future is deeply uncertain; we cannot reliably predict the consequences of actions over long time horizons, and attempts to shape the far future may have unpredictable and possibly negative effects.

These challenges do not refute longtermism, but they reveal the philosophical complexity of reasoning about our obligations to the future. The metaphysics of time enters here: if the future is genuinely open and indeterminate, what grounds our obligations to it? If the block universe is correct and future people are just as real as present people, does this strengthen or weaken the longtermist case?

## Memory, Forgiveness, and Political Time

Political communities have complex temporal relationships to their own pasts. Nations must decide how to remember historical injustices — slavery, colonialism, genocide — and whether and how to respond to them in the present. These questions involve philosophical problems about collective memory, responsibility across time, and the ethics of historical interpretation.

The philosophy of memory and forgiveness connects to temporal ontology in specific ways. Jacques Derrida (2001) and Paul Ricoeur (2004) have argued that forgiveness involves a complex temporal structure: it is not the erasure of the past but a transformation of the relationship to the past. Forgiveness acknowledges what happened — it does not pretend the past was different — but it refuses to let the past determine the present in the same way. The past is released: not forgotten, but no longer binding in the same way.

This temporal structure of forgiveness has political implications. Walter Benjamin (1940) argued that the past is not dead and gone but carries with it a "weak messianic power" — a claim on the present to redress what was not redeemed. Benjamin's image of the "angel of history" (facing backward, seeing only the wreckage of the past even as it is blown forward into the future) is one of the most striking philosophical images of the relationship between past and present. It raises the question: what is owed to the past, and by whom, and for how long?

Political reconciliation processes — truth commissions, reparations programs, monument controversies — embody specific views about the proper relationship between past and present. They presuppose that the past is available for revision (through reinterpretation, symbolic acknowledgment, or material compensation) without being available for undoing. Philosophy of time can illuminate the conceptual structure of these political practices.

## Temporal Justice and Intergenerational Institutions

Temporal justice is an emerging framework for thinking about the just distribution of goods, burdens, and opportunities across time. It extends standard frameworks of distributive justice — which focus on distribution across persons alive at the same time — to encompass distribution across generations.

Several philosophical frameworks bear on temporal justice. John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971) includes a "just savings principle" that requires each generation to preserve gains of culture and civilization and maintain the justice of institutions. But Rawls's framework was primarily designed for individuals living in the same society and has difficulty handling the radical temporal separation between current actors and distant future beneficiaries or victims. Contractarian frameworks more generally face challenges with intergenerational justice: future generations cannot participate in the contract that is supposed to generate obligations.

The concept of intergenerational justice connects directly to metaphysics of time. If the future is not yet real (presentism, growing block), in what sense can present people have obligations to future people who do not yet exist? If the future is just as real as the present (block universe), does this strengthen the case for treating future welfare on a par with present welfare? The metaphysics of temporal ontology is not only of theoretical interest; it shapes what obligations we have and how we reason about them.

The climate crisis makes these questions urgent. Current generations are making decisions about greenhouse gas emissions whose primary consequences will fall on people who are not yet born or who are children today. The philosophical analysis of these obligations — their scope, their strength, and their grounding — requires both metaphysical clarity about the status of future persons and ethical clarity about the basis of intergenerational obligation.
