# Unit XII: Cutting-Edge Questions and Open Problems

---

## Unit Introduction

We have traveled a long way. Beginning with Augustine's confession that time is familiar until someone asks him about it, we have worked through ancient and medieval philosophy, classical physics and relativity, phenomenology and analytic metaphysics, personal identity, ethics, and time travel. We have traced the A-series and the B-series, watched the block universe congeal and the growing block grow, felt the pull of presentism and the vertigo of timeless eternalism. We have worried about mortality, about free will, and about our obligations to people not yet born.

Now we arrive at the frontier — the questions that have not been answered, the problems that are not solved, the territories that current physics and philosophy have barely begun to map. This is not a failure of the discipline; it is its most honest and most exciting moment. The questions that remain open are the ones that matter most.

Chapter 36 revisits McTaggart's thesis that time is unreal, asking what a century of physics and philosophy has done to it. The block universe, quantum gravity, Barbour's timeless Platonia, and non-Western frameworks of temporal unreality all bear on the question — but they bear on it differently, and distinguishing their claims carefully is philosophically essential.

Chapter 37 takes up the emergence of spacetime from a non-spatiotemporal substrate — one of the most active and philosophically consequential frontiers in contemporary physics. The AdS/CFT correspondence, the ER=EPR conjecture, and the general program of deriving spacetime from quantum information raise questions that no philosopher of time can ignore: if spacetime itself is emergent, then time — as a feature of spacetime — is emergent too. What is it emergent *from*? And what does "emergent" mean when the substrate is not spatial or temporal?

Chapter 38 explores the relationship between time and information. The thermodynamic arrow of time as an informational phenomenon, the black hole information paradox, Landauer's principle, and digital physics all illuminate connections between temporal structure and the structure of information. These connections may not be accidental; they may reveal something deep about what time *is*.

Chapter 39 is an honest inventory: what remains unsolved, what is the current state of the disciplines involved, and how should a student of the philosophy of time approach the years of work ahead? Open questions are not failures; they are invitations.
