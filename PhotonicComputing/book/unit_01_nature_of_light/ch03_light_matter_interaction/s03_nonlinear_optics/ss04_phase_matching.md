# 3.3.4 — Phase Matching

## Why Phase Matching Matters

Phase matching is the condition under which light generated by a nonlinear process accumulates constructively over a long propagation distance. Without phase matching, the generated light oscillates between the forward and backward directions, limiting conversion to the coherence length $L_c$. With phase matching, conversion efficiency grows as $L^2$.

For a general $\chi^{(2)}$ or $\chi^{(3)}$ process, the phase-matching condition is momentum conservation for the photons:

$$\mathbf{k}_\text{output} = \sum_i \mathbf{k}_i^\text{input}$$

For SHG: $k_{2\omega} = 2k_\omega$ → $n_{2\omega} = n_\omega$. For degenerate FWM: $k_4 = 2k_1 - k_2$.

Since the refractive index of any dispersive material varies with frequency ($n_{2\omega} \neq n_\omega$ in general), exact phase matching must be engineered.

## Birefringence Phase Matching

In birefringent crystals, the ordinary (o) and extraordinary (e) polarizations have different refractive indices. By choosing appropriate polarizations and crystal orientation, the phase-matching condition $n_{2\omega} = n_\omega$ can be satisfied for specific angles. Two types:

**Type I**: both pump photons have the same polarization (e.g., both ordinary); SHG output has orthogonal polarization (extraordinary). Phase matching at angle $\theta$ where $n_e(2\omega, \theta) = n_o(\omega)$.

**Type II**: the two pump photons have orthogonal polarizations; SHG output polarization depends on crystal. Used in BBO, KTP.

**Limitation**: the phase-matched interaction involves different polarizations traveling at different group velocities (*group velocity mismatch*, GVM). This limits the bandwidth and pulse duration that can be phase-matched. For pulsed operation, GVM-compensated designs are required.

## Quasi-Phase Matching (QPM)

In quasi-phase matching, the phase mismatch is compensated by *periodic inversion* of the nonlinear coefficient. In periodically poled LiNbO₃ (PPLN), the crystal's spontaneous polarization is reversed periodically with period $\Lambda_\text{QPM}$. The effective nonlinear coefficient acquires a Fourier series of spatial harmonics:

$$d_\text{eff}(z) = \frac{2d_{33}}{\pi}\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) e^{i G z} + \text{higher harmonics}$$

where $G = 2\pi/\Lambda_\text{QPM}$ is the quasi-phase matching grating vector. The first-order QPM condition: $\Delta k - G = 0$, i.e., $\Lambda_\text{QPM} = 2\pi/\Delta k = \lambda_0/(2|n_{2\omega} - n_\omega|)$.

For PPLN at 1550 nm → 775 nm: $\Lambda_\text{QPM} \approx 18$ μm. With periodic poling periods down to 5–30 μm, PPLN can phase-match any SHG, OPA, or SPDC process in the transparency range of LiNbO₃.

**Advantages of QPM over birefringence PM**:
1. Can use the largest nonlinear coefficient ($d_{33} = 27$ pm/V for LiNbO₃, vs. $d_{15} \approx 4$ pm/V accessible with birefringence PM).
2. Can phase-match at room temperature without special crystal orientations.
3. Can be designed for any wavelength within the transparency window by adjusting $\Lambda_\text{QPM}$.
4. Group velocity matching can be engineered simultaneously.

PPLN waveguides (periodic poling of thin-film LiNbO₃ waveguides) achieve extremely high efficiency: normalized SHG efficiency $> 100\%/W/cm²$ has been demonstrated [1].

## Waveguide Dispersion Engineering

In photonic integrated circuits, the dispersion can be controlled by the waveguide geometry — waveguide dispersion adds to (or subtracts from) material dispersion. By adjusting the waveguide cross-section, the phase-matching condition for FWM in silicon nitride can be satisfied for specific pump/signal/idler wavelength combinations.

This is the basis of **on-chip optical parametric amplification and frequency conversion**: a pump at 1064 nm in a silicon nitride waveguide with engineered anomalous dispersion can phase-match an OPA process that amplifies signals at 1310 nm while generating an idler at 900 nm. By controlling the waveguide dimensions (width and height), the phase-matching wavelengths can be tuned across the telecom range.

**Microresonator-based frequency combs (Kerr combs)**: In a high-Q microresonator (ring resonator), the Kerr nonlinearity, combined with carefully engineered anomalous dispersion, can generate a *frequency comb*: an equally spaced set of spectral lines spanning hundreds of nm. The comb spacing is the FSR of the resonator. Kerr combs in Si₃N₄ microresonators are now commercially produced and used for WDM communications and photonic computing as multi-wavelength sources [2].

## Summary

- Phase matching: momentum conservation for the interacting photons; $\Delta k = 0$ required for efficient conversion.
- Birefringence PM: use polarization and angle to satisfy $n_{2\omega} = n_\omega$; limited by GVM.
- Quasi-phase matching (QPM): periodic poling of $d_\text{eff}$; can use largest $d_{33}$; tunable by $\Lambda_\text{QPM}$.
- Waveguide dispersion engineering: control phase matching in integrated circuits by adjusting waveguide geometry.
- Kerr combs in microresonators: FWM + anomalous dispersion → multi-wavelength source for WDM and photonic computing.
