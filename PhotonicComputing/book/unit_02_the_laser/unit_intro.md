## What This Unit Is About

Everything in photonic computing depends on two things being possible: converting electrical signals into light, and converting light back into electrical signals. Without these two transductions, optical computation would be a closed loop with no input and no output — a beautiful physics experiment but not a computing system. The laser is the primary device that performs the first conversion. The photodetector performs the second.

But to describe the laser as merely an "electrical-to-optical converter" is to miss almost everything interesting about it. The laser is one of the most precisely engineered quantum systems in existence. Its output light has properties — coherence, narrow linewidth, directionality, high intensity — that are not possessed by any other light source. These properties are not accidents; they are direct consequences of the physics of stimulated emission and optical resonators, developed in Chapter 3 and now brought to full engineering fruition here.

Similarly, the photodetector is not merely a "light-to-electrical converter." Its performance limits — sensitivity, speed, noise, dynamic range — determine the fundamental signal-to-noise ratio of any photonic computing system. Understanding the photodetector means understanding shot noise, Johnson noise, the quantum efficiency of photon-to-electron conversion, and the bandwidth-noise tradeoff that governs every optical receiver ever designed.

This unit is where the physics of Unit I meets the engineering realities of actual photonic systems. The material here is, in a sense, the most immediately practical in the book: every photonic computing experiment ever reported uses lasers and photodetectors, and their characteristics set hard limits on what is achievable.

## Two Chapters, Two Essential Questions

**Chapter 4: Laser Physics** asks: *How is coherent light produced, and what determines its quality?*

We develop the complete theory of laser operation, starting from the population inversion physics of Chapter 3 and adding the essential ingredient: optical feedback from a resonant cavity. We treat rate equations, gain saturation, threshold behavior, single-mode vs. multimode operation, laser noise (the Schawlow-Townes linewidth formula), and the specific properties of semiconductor diode lasers that make them the universal choice for integrated photonic systems. We give special attention to distributed feedback (DFB) lasers, vertical-cavity surface-emitting lasers (VCSELs), and external-cavity tunable lasers — the specific laser architectures that appear in real photonic computing systems.

**Chapter 5: Photodetectors** asks: *How is light detected, and what limits the sensitivity and speed?*

We develop the physics of photodetection from the photoelectric effect through modern p-i-n photodiodes and avalanche photodetectors (APDs). We treat the noise sources in detail — shot noise from photon discreteness, Johnson noise from resistance, dark current — and derive the fundamental signal-to-noise ratio of an optical receiver. We analyze the bandwidth-responsivity tradeoff, the figure of merit for high-speed photonic computing receivers, and the specific characteristics of germanium-on-silicon photodetectors that are the standard integrated-photonics detection element. We conclude with single-photon detection (SPADs, SNSPDs) for quantum photonic applications.

## The Indispensable Partnership

The laser and the photodetector are not independent components. They are coupled at the level of fundamental physics. The shot noise of a photodetector — the irreducible quantum noise floor of optical measurement — originates in the discrete nature of photons, which are themselves generated by the quantum processes (stimulated and spontaneous emission) that make a laser work. The Schawlow-Townes linewidth of a laser — its minimum spectral width — arises from the same spontaneous emission that limits the noise figure of optical amplifiers.

Understanding this partnership — grasping that the noise floor of a photonic computing system is set by the quantum mechanics of light-matter interaction — is one of the central goals of this unit. Photonic computing is sometimes described as if it operates "without quantum noise," in contrast to electronic computing's thermal noise. This is false: every photonic measurement disturbs the quantum state of the field. The question is not whether quantum noise exists, but whether its magnitude is tolerable for the computation at hand. This unit provides the framework for answering that question quantitatively.

## Connections to the Larger Book

The laser and photodetector appear in every subsequent unit, either directly or through their noise and performance limits. In Unit V (classical photonic computing), the laser linewidth determines the coherence length available for interference-based matrix multiplication, and the photodetector noise determines the precision of each matrix element. In Unit VII (quantum photonics), the photodetector is the measurement apparatus that collapses quantum states — and whether that collapse is projective or weak determines what kind of quantum computation is possible. In Unit X (frontiers), the quest for on-chip laser sources in silicon photonics — one of the great unsolved engineering challenges of the field — is understood only against the background established here.

## The Historical Setting

The laser was invented in 1960. Theodore Maiman demonstrated the first operating laser — a pulsed ruby laser — on May 16, 1960, at Hughes Research Laboratories in Malibu, California [1]. This occurred despite Maiman's grant application having been rejected by the Air Force on the grounds that the scientific panel reviewing it did not believe a three-level system could achieve threshold inversion at room temperature. They were wrong.

The theoretical foundations had been laid in the years immediately preceding: Townes and Schawlow's proposal for an "optical maser" in 1958 [2], and Prokhorov's independent similar proposal in the same year [3]. Basov and Prokhorov had earlier developed maser theory, and the three shared the 1964 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. Maiman, inexplicably, was never awarded the Nobel Prize.

The semiconductor diode laser — the form of laser used in almost all modern photonic systems, including photonic computing chips — was demonstrated in 1962, within two years of Maiman's ruby laser, by three independent groups [4,5,6]. The development of the double-heterostructure diode laser by Alferov and Kroemer in the late 1960s, for which they shared the 2000 Nobel Prize, made room-temperature continuous-wave operation of semiconductor lasers possible and laid the foundation for the fiber-optic revolution.

The photodetector's history is older: Einstein's explanation of the photoelectric effect in 1905 [7] was the first quantitative theory of photon absorption in a solid, and it earned him his only Nobel Prize. Modern p-i-n photodiodes and avalanche photodetectors are the industrialized descendants of this physics.
