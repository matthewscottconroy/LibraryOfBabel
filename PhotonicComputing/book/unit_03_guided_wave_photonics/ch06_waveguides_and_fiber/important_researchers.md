# Important Researchers: Chapter 6 — Waveguides and Fiber

**Charles K. Kao (1933–2018)**: Proposed in 1966 that silica fiber could achieve < 20 dB/km loss if purified to remove transition metal impurities. This was dismissed by most as physically unrealistic at the time; within four years Corning demonstrated 20 dB/km. Kao received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2009 "for groundbreaking achievements concerning the transmission of light in fibers for optical communication."

**Elias Snitzer (1925–2012)**: Demonstrated the first optical fiber waveguide in 1961, showed that glass fibers could support guided modes matching the electromagnetic theory, and later invented the erbium-doped fiber amplifier concept in 1988. Snitzer's contributions span from the founding theory of fiber optics to the amplifier technology that made long-haul WDM possible.

**Emanuel Desurvire (b. 1955) and David Payne (b. 1944)**: Independently demonstrated the first erbium-doped fiber amplifiers with practical gain in 1987 (Payne's group at Southampton) and 1989 (Desurvire at Bell Labs) [1,2]. Their demonstrations transformed optical communication by eliminating the need for electronic regeneration in long-haul systems.

**Tatsuo Miya and collaborators (NTT Japan)**: Demonstrated the first fiber with < 0.2 dB/km loss in 1979 [3], establishing 1550 nm as the operating wavelength for long-haul transmission and showing that the silica loss minimum had been achieved.

**Robert Stolen and Akira Tomita (Bell Labs)**: Pioneered the study of nonlinear effects in optical fibers in the early 1970s–1980s, including SRS, SBS, SPM, and FWM. Their experimental work established the nonlinear coefficients and thresholds that govern fiber transmission limits.
