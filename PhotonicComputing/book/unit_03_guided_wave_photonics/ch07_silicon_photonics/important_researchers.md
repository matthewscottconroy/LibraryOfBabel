# Chapter 7: Important Researchers

---

## Richard Soref (1931–)

Richard Soref worked at the Air Force Cambridge Research Laboratories and later GTE/Sylvania before joining the Air Force Research Laboratory, where he spent most of his career. His 1986 paper proposing silicon as a platform for photonic integrated circuits [Soref & Lorenzo, *IEEE Journal of Quantum Electronics*, 1986] and his 1987 paper with Bennett establishing the quantitative relations between carrier density and refractive index in silicon [Soref & Bennett, *IEEE Journal of Quantum Electronics*, 1987] are the intellectual foundations of the entire silicon photonics field. The "Soref-Bennett equations" remain the design standard for silicon electro-optic modulators four decades later. Soref also recognized early that silicon's compatibility with CMOS manufacturing was its decisive strategic advantage, a view that took the photonics community another decade to fully absorb. He is sometimes called the "Father of Silicon Photonics."

---

## Graham Reed (1960–)

Graham Reed, based at the University of Surrey and later Southampton, led the first experimental demonstrations of silicon optical modulators operating at useful speeds. His group produced the first silicon modulator operating above 1 GHz [Png et al., 2004] and has contributed foundational work on silicon rib waveguides, modulator optimization, and silicon photonics device physics. Reed's 2010 review paper in *Nature Photonics* ["Silicon optical modulators"] remains the canonical reference for the field. He co-founded Silicon Photonics Ltd. and has been instrumental in the transfer of academic silicon photonics research to commercial foundries.

---

## Michal Lipson (1971–)

Michal Lipson (Cornell University, later Columbia University) is arguably the most prolific and influential experimentalist in silicon photonics. Her group at Cornell produced a cascade of landmark results: the first silicon electrooptic modulator operating at 1 Gbps (2004), the first microring resonator modulator (Xu et al., *Nature*, 2005), the first demonstration of optical parametric oscillation in silicon (Absil et al.), silicon Raman lasers, and numerous innovations in silicon photonic device physics. Her approach — rigorous physics combined with aggressive device engineering — defined the style of the field. She received the MacArthur Fellowship ("genius award") in 2010 for this work.

---

## Mario Paniccia (1968–) and the Intel Silicon Photonics Team

Mario Paniccia led Intel's Silicon Photonics group from its founding in 2000 until the group was eventually absorbed into Intel's datacenter interconnects business. The Intel group produced the first silicon Raman laser (2004), the first GHz-bandwidth silicon modulator (Liu et al., *Nature*, 2004, based on a MOS capacitor geometry), and the first hybrid silicon laser using bonded InP on SOI (2006). Perhaps more importantly, the Intel group demonstrated that silicon photonics was not merely an academic curiosity but a manufacturable technology — a position the rest of the industry was skeptical of until the mid-2000s. The Intel silicon photonics product line now ships hundreds of thousands of optical transceivers per year.

---

## Marko Lončar (1971–)

Marko Lončar's group at Harvard has defined the thin-film lithium niobate on insulator (LNOI) platform as it is known today. His group's 2018 paper in *Nature* ["Integrated lithium niobate electro-optic modulators operating at CMOS-compatible voltages," Wang et al.] demonstrated the combination of $V_\pi L = 2.2$ V·cm and >100 GHz bandwidth that established LNOI as a serious contender to silicon photonics for high-performance applications. His group also demonstrated record-efficiency SHG and electro-optic frequency combs on LNOI. Lončar has made the LNOI process available to the community through collaborations and through HyperLight, a startup he co-founded.

---

## Tobias Kippenberg (1975–)

Tobias Kippenberg (EPFL, Lausanne) is the leading figure in microresonator frequency combs and the photonic Damascene process for ultra-low-loss Si₃N₄ waveguides. His group demonstrated the first Kerr frequency comb in a microresonator (2004), the first dissipative Kerr soliton (DKS) comb (Herr et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2014), and the photonic Damascene process enabling Si₃N₄ waveguide losses < 0.01 dB/cm (Pfeiffer et al., *Optica*, 2016). The DKS comb is now the standard coherent multi-wavelength source for WDM photonic computing demonstrations. His lab co-founded Ligentec, which commercializes Si₃N₄ photonics.

---

## Harish Bhaskaran and Wolfram Pernice

Harish Bhaskaran (Oxford) and Wolfram Pernice (Münster) lead the two groups that have most advanced phase-change material photonic devices. Bhaskaran's group (with collaborators) produced the first integrated PCM optical memory (Rios et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2015), the first all-optical synaptic PCM device (Feldmann et al., *Nature*, 2019), and the first integrated PCM photonic tensor core (Feldmann et al., *Nature*, 2021). Pernice's group developed many of the device geometries and fabrication techniques for GST on silicon photonic waveguides. Together, these two groups have defined the experimental state-of-art for non-volatile photonic computing and in-memory optical computation.

---

## Naomi Halas and Peter Nordlander

Naomi Halas (Rice University) and Peter Nordlander (Rice University) are the leading figures in nanophotonics and plasmonics at the metal-dielectric interface. Their work on nanoshells, nanoparticles, and Fano resonances has illuminated the physics of optical phenomena at dimensions far below the diffraction limit. While not focused on photonic computing per se, their contributions to understanding how metal nanostructures interact with light — the foundational science behind plasmonic interconnects and nanoscale optical concentrators that may eventually appear in photonic computing systems — make their names essential in any treatment of the physics of light at the nanoscale.
