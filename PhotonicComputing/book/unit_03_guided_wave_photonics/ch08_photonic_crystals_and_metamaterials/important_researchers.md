# Chapter 8: Important Researchers

---

## Eli Yablonovitch (1946–)

Eli Yablonovitch's 1987 paper proposing that a periodic dielectric structure could create a photonic bandgap analogous to the electronic bandgap in semiconductors [*Physical Review Letters*, 58, 2059] is one of the founding documents of the field. His motivation was practical: an atom inside a photonic bandgap material cannot emit spontaneously (there are no modes to emit into), which would enable dramatically more efficient lasers by eliminating unwanted spontaneous emission. Yablonovitch coined the term "photonic bandgap" and created the first experimental realization of a 3D photonic crystal at microwave frequencies (yablonovite, 1991). He has continued to make foundational contributions to photovoltaics, semiconductor physics, and photonic crystals throughout his career at UCLA.

---

## Sajeev John (1957–)

Sajeev John's 1987 paper, published simultaneously with Yablonovitch's, approached the photonic crystal problem from the perspective of localization theory: in a disordered periodic structure, photons can undergo Anderson localization — trapped in space by multiple scattering in the same way electrons are localized in disordered electronic systems [*Physical Review Letters*, 58, 2486]. John's work established the connection between photonic crystals and condensed matter physics and inspired the mathematical treatment of photonic band structure using the formalism of electronic band theory. He has been at the University of Toronto throughout his career and is the co-recipient of the Harvey Prize and Buckley Prize for this work.

---

## John Joannopoulos (1947–) and Steven Johnson

John Joannopoulos at MIT has been the most influential theorist in the development of photonic crystals, producing both foundational theory and the practical computational tools that made the field quantitative. His textbook *Photonic Crystals: Molding the Flow of Light* (with Johnson, Winn, and Meade, Princeton University Press) is the standard reference. His student Steven Johnson developed the MIT Photonic Bands (MPB) package and the FDTD solver MEEP, which together are the most widely used tools for photonic crystal simulation. Johnson has also made major contributions to the theory of slow light, disorder in photonic crystals, and computational electromagnetics.

---

## Norio Noda (1959–)

Norio Noda at Kyoto University has made the most significant experimental advances in 2D photonic crystal slab fabrication and device demonstration. His group produced the first photonic crystal nanocavity with $Q > 10^6$ (2003), the first photonic crystal laser, and the first demonstration of slow-light-enhanced electro-optic modulation. Noda's work is characterized by extraordinary fabrication precision — his e-beam lithography achieves ~1-nm hole placement accuracy — which is why his devices achieve quality factors an order of magnitude better than most other groups. His contributions established photonic crystal cavities as practical devices rather than theoretical curiosities.

---

## Federico Capasso (1949–)

Federico Capasso at Harvard is best known for inventing the quantum cascade laser (at Bell Labs, 1994), but his work on metasurfaces since 2011 has defined a second field. His group's 2011 paper introducing the generalized Snell's law via phase-gradient metasurfaces [*Science*, 334, 333] launched the modern metasurface era. Subsequent work from his group (often in collaboration with Marko Lončar and Devlin/Khorasaninejad) produced the first high-efficiency dielectric metalens (TiO₂, visible wavelengths), established the PB phase mechanism for broad metasurfaces, and demonstrated that flat optics could match the performance of traditional lenses. His lab co-founded Metalenz, a startup commercializing metasurface optics.

---

## Thomas Ebbesen (1954–)

Thomas Ebbesen's 1998 paper demonstrating extraordinary optical transmission through arrays of sub-wavelength holes in a gold film [*Nature*, 391, 667] was a shock to the optics community. The accepted wisdom was that a hole smaller than $\lambda/2$ transmits negligibly. Ebbesen showed that a periodic array of such holes transmits orders of magnitude more light than expected — mediated by surface plasmon polaritons resonating with the array lattice. This result launched plasmonics as a major subfield of photonics. Ebbesen, based at the University of Strasbourg, has continued to contribute to the field including fundamental work on vacuum field fluctuations in plasmonic cavities.

---

## Stefan Maier (1975–)

Stefan Maier, at Imperial College London and later Ludwig Maximilian University Munich, is one of the leading experimental and theoretical figures in plasmonics for photonic applications. His textbook *Plasmonics: Fundamentals and Applications* (Springer, 2007) is the standard graduate-level reference. His group has made major contributions to plasmonic waveguiding, near-field energy transfer, and the integration of plasmonic structures with silicon photonics. He has been particularly effective at characterizing and explaining the fundamental loss limits of plasmonics, providing the field with an honest accounting of what is and is not possible.

---

## Jürg Leuthold (1966–)

Jürg Leuthold at ETH Zürich leads the group that has most successfully applied plasmonics to practical photonic systems. His group's development of organic electro-optic (OEO) materials integrated in plasmonic slot waveguides has produced the highest bandwidth-per-footprint electro-optic modulators reported: >200 GHz bandwidth in a 10–20 μm device. These "plasmonic modulators" represent the most compelling engineering case for plasmonics — a regime where the ohmic loss is acceptable because the device is so short that very little power is dissipated. Leuthold's work demonstrates that plasmonics is not dead; it is finding its appropriate niche.
