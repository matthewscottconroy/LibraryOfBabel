# Chapter 11: Important Researchers

## Joseph Goodman (Stanford University)

Joseph Goodman is the author of *Introduction to Fourier Optics* (1968, now in its 4th edition), the definitive textbook on the subject. His derivations of the lens Fourier transform property, the coherent and incoherent imaging systems, and the theory of coherence are the standard reference for an entire generation of optical physicists and engineers. Goodman also developed the Joint Transform Correlator (with Weaver, 1966) and made foundational contributions to speckle theory, holography, and statistical optics. Every researcher in photonic computing who works with coherent light is working in a framework Goodman helped create.

## Adolf Lohmann (University of Erlangen-Nuremberg)

Adolf Lohmann was one of the early pioneers of optical information processing and optical computing. He introduced the concept of the space-bandwidth product as a measure of computational capacity for optical systems, developed computer-generated holograms (CGHs) for arbitrary optical pattern generation, and contributed to the theory of optical logic gates in the 1980s. Lohmann's perspective — that optical computing's advantage lies in its parallelism and its natural affinity for Fourier operations — has proven more prescient than the digital optical logic programs he participated in.

## Bahram Jalali (UCLA)

Bahram Jalali is the inventor of the photonic time-stretch ADC and a prolific contributor to silicon photonics, nonlinear fiber optics, and photonic computing. His work on the time-stretch ADC demonstrated that photonics can achieve sampling rates and resolutions inaccessible to electronic ADCs for wideband RF signals. Jalali's group has also contributed to Raman amplification in silicon, broadband photonic analog-to-digital conversion, and the application of compressive sensing to photonic ADC systems. He has been consistently willing to compare photonic performance honestly against electronic alternatives, a rare and valuable quality in the field.

## José Capmany (Universitat Politècnica de València)

José Capmany and his group at UPV are the primary contributors to the field of integrated microwave photonics — the effort to move the bulky fiber-based microwave photonic systems of the 1990s onto silicon photonic chips. Capmany co-authored the 2007 *Nature Photonics* review paper that established microwave photonics as a distinct subfield and has led international programs on photonic RF processing for defense and communications. His work on microwave photonic filters, beamforming networks, and channelizers is the foundation of the field.

## Arthur VanderLugt (University of Michigan)

Arthur VanderLugt developed the matched filter in optics (the VanderLugt filter) in 1964, providing the theoretical and experimental basis for coherent optical pattern recognition. Before digital image processing existed, the optical correlator he developed was a practically useful system for character recognition and target identification. The mathematical framework he established (the relationship between matched filtering and the Fourier transform) is still the basis for understanding what a 4f processor computes.

## David Miller (Stanford) (see also Chapter 10)

David Miller's contribution to optical logic (in addition to his optical interconnect work) is important in a different way: his 2010 editorial "Are optical transistors the logical next step?" is one of the most clear-headed assessments ever written of why optical logic is fundamentally disadvantaged compared to CMOS. By explaining carefully why photons are the wrong tool for Boolean logic — and what they are good for — Miller helped redirect the field away from a futile goal toward the analog computing applications where photonics has genuine advantages.
