# Subsection 11.3.1: Why Optical Logic Is Hard

## Orientation

A transistor performs switching: a small control signal (gate voltage) controls a large power supply (drain-to-source current). The energy required to flip a transistor state is $C_{gate} V_{DD}^2 \approx 10$–$100$ aJ — attojoules. The switching time is $\tau \approx C_{gate} V_{DD} / I_{DS} \approx 1$–$10$ ps. The energy-delay product (a figure of merit for logic) is $E \cdot \tau \approx 10^{-31}$–$10^{-31}$ J·s.

To build an optical logic gate, we need a physical mechanism that makes the optical output depend nonlinearly on the optical input — specifically, a mechanism that produces either ON (high output power) or OFF (low output power) based on whether the input exceeds a threshold. And crucially, the mechanism must be *cascadable*: the output of one gate must be strong enough to drive the input of the next gate.

---

## 11.3.1.1 The Cascadability Requirement

### Gain and Loss

In electronics, transistors have power gain: the output power ($I_{DS} \times V_{DS}$) exceeds the control power ($I_G \times V_G$). This gain allows a chain of $N$ logic gates to operate without signal degradation: each gate restores the signal level.

For optical logic, the analogous requirement is *optical power gain*: the output optical power must exceed the input optical power, accounting for all insertion losses. If it does not, the signal degrades with each gate stage, and the fan-out (number of gates one gate can drive) is less than 1.

**The gain problem**: Passive optical elements (waveguides, beam splitters, ring resonators) always have loss. Achieving net optical gain requires either:
(a) An active amplification element (semiconductor optical amplifier, EDFA), which adds noise and requires external pumping, or
(b) Strong nonlinearity where a small control power controls a large signal power — the parametric gain case.

Neither is as clean as the transistor, which achieves gain passively (from a DC power supply) with negligible added noise (shot noise only, not amplified spontaneous emission).

### Optical Bistability: The Threshold Device

The most straightforward route to optical logic is *optical bistability* — a device that switches between two stable output states depending on the input power. The canonical optical bistable device is a Fabry-Perot cavity containing a nonlinear medium (Kerr effect, Section 3.3.4):

The round-trip phase in the cavity includes the Kerr phase shift $\Delta\phi_{\text{NL}} = (2\pi/\lambda) n_2 I \cdot L$. The transmitted intensity $I_T$ as a function of input intensity $I_{\text{in}}$:

At low finesse $\mathcal{F}$, the Airy function is smooth (single-valued). At high finesse, there is a range of $I_{\text{in}}$ for which the Airy function becomes multi-valued (S-shaped), giving two stable states (high and low transmission) — bistability.

**Switching energy**: For a resonator with mode volume $V_{\text{mode}}$ and Kerr coefficient $n_2$:

$$\Delta\phi_{\text{NL}} = \frac{2\pi n_2}{\lambda} \cdot \frac{U_{\text{cavity}}}{V_{\text{mode}}} \cdot L$$

To achieve $\Delta\phi_{\text{NL}} = \pi$ (switching the resonance by one linewidth):

$$U_{\text{switch}} = \frac{\pi \lambda V_{\text{mode}}}{2 n_2 L}$$

For silicon (which has the largest Kerr coefficient among common waveguide materials, $n_2 = 6\times10^{-18}$ m²/W) and a microring with $V_{\text{mode}} = (500 \text{ nm})^3 \times 2\pi R \approx 10^{-17}$ m³:

$$U_{\text{switch}} = \frac{\pi \times 1550\times10^{-9} \times 10^{-17}}{2 \times 6\times10^{-18} \times 2\pi \times 10^{-5}} \approx \frac{4.9\times10^{-23}}{3.8\times10^{-21}} \approx 13 \text{ aJ}$$

This is competitive with CMOS (10–100 aJ). The problem is not the switching energy in principle — it is everything else.

## 11.3.1.2 Two-Photon Absorption: The Silicon Killer

Silicon's Kerr effect is large, but silicon also has strong two-photon absorption (TPA) at 1550 nm. As derived in Section 3.3.4, the TPA coefficient $\beta_{\text{TPA}} = 0.5$ cm/GW for silicon. For an optical field with intensity $I$ (W/m²):

$$\frac{dI}{dz} = -\alpha_0 I - \beta_{\text{TPA}} I^2$$

At the optical power levels needed for Kerr bistability (peak intensities $\sim 1$ GW/cm² in a silicon waveguide), TPA removes more power than the Kerr effect can shift:

**TPA figure of merit**: $\text{FOM} = n_2 / (\lambda \beta_{\text{TPA}})$

For silicon at 1550 nm: $\text{FOM} = 6\times10^{-18} / (1550\times10^{-9} \times 0.5\times10^{-2}) = 6\times10^{-18} / 7.75\times10^{-12} = 7.7\times10^{-7}$.

For a Kerr switch to work before TPA destroys the pulse, we need $\text{FOM} > 1/(4\pi) \approx 0.08$ [1]. Silicon's FOM of $7.7\times10^{-7}$ is six orders of magnitude below this threshold. TPA makes silicon completely impractical for Kerr switching.

**Free-carrier generation by TPA** makes things worse: the carriers generated by TPA absorb more light (free-carrier absorption, Section 7.2) and shift the refractive index. The lifetime of these carriers in silicon is nanoseconds (unless swept out by a p-n junction, which adds design complexity). The cumulative effect: optical bistability in silicon requires power levels that immediately saturate in TPA and free-carrier loss, never reaching the Kerr threshold cleanly.

### Alternative Materials for Kerr Switching

| Material | $n_2$ (m²/W) | $\beta_{\text{TPA}}$ (cm/GW) | FOM |
|----------|-------------|--------------------------|-----|
| Si (1550 nm) | $6\times10^{-18}$ | 0.5 | $7.7\times10^{-7}$ |
| AlGaAs (1550 nm, bandgap-engineered) | $1.5\times10^{-17}$ | 0.02 | 6.5 |
| Si₃N₄ (1550 nm) | $2.4\times10^{-19}$ | ~0 | ∞ (but $n_2$ small) |
| Chalcogenide glass (As₂S₃) | $3\times10^{-18}$ | 0.001 | 300 |
| LiNbO₃ | $1.9\times10^{-19}$ | ~0 | — |

AlGaAs and chalcogenide glasses have FOM > 1, making Kerr switching physically possible. But both have smaller refractive indices than silicon (smaller confinement → larger mode volume → higher switching energy), higher propagation loss (2–3 dB/cm), and less mature fabrication. The "best-case" switching energy is still ~10–100 aJ, with switching speeds limited by material recovery times ($> 1$ ps).

## 11.3.1.3 The Fundamental Energy-Latency Comparison

Let us make the comparison explicit. For a Kerr optical switch in an optimized platform (AlGaAs microring, FOM = 6.5, $n_2 = 1.5\times10^{-17}$ m²/W):

- Switching energy: ~50 aJ (estimated)
- Switching latency: $\sim$ cavity photon lifetime $= Q/\omega_0 \approx 10{,}000 / (2\pi \times 193 \text{ THz}) \approx 8$ ps
- Energy-delay product: $50\times10^{-18} \times 8\times10^{-12} = 4\times10^{-28}$ J·s

For a 5 nm CMOS NAND gate:
- Switching energy: 20–50 aJ
- Switching latency: 10–30 ps (for a standard cell)
- Energy-delay product: $50\times10^{-18} \times 20\times10^{-12} = 10^{-27}$ J·s

The optical Kerr switch is comparable to CMOS at the device level — not dramatically better. And the Kerr switch requires:
- A continuous-wave (or pulsed) light source
- Optical waveguides and coupling elements
- Optical isolation (to prevent backward-propagating signals)
- Optical-to-electrical conversion to interface with any digital system

All of these overhead costs are absent in a CMOS transistor. When the total system energy is counted, optical logic loses to CMOS by at least 1–2 orders of magnitude for digital operations.

**The lesson**: Optical logic cannot compete with CMOS for Boolean computation because (1) optical nonlinearities require more energy per bit than electronic nonlinearities, or (2) when optical nonlinearities are made comparable, the system overhead (laser, isolator, O/E conversion) swamps the device advantage. This is not a temporary engineering limitation — it reflects fundamental physics.
