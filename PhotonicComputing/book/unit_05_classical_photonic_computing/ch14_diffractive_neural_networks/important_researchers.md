# Chapter 14: Important Researchers

## Aydogan Ozcan (University of California, Los Angeles)

Ozcan is the architect of the diffractive deep neural network and the corresponding author of nearly every foundational paper in this chapter. A Chancellor's Professor of electrical and computer engineering and bioengineering at UCLA, he built his group's reputation on computational imaging, holography, and lens-free microscopy before turning that expertise on optical machine learning: the 2018 *Science* paper that introduced the D2NN came out of his laboratory, as did the analyses of network capacity, misalignment resilience, differential detection, broadband operation, and terahertz pulse shaping that define the field's frontier. His central insight — that free-space diffraction can be treated as a trainable, all-optical neural layer — organizes the entire chapter.

## Xing Lin (Tsinghua University)

Lin is the first author of "All-optical machine learning using diffractive deep neural networks" (2018), the experiment that launched the field, which he carried out as a member of Ozcan's group. Now on the faculty at Tsinghua University's Department of Electronic Engineering, he has continued at the center of diffractive computing, contributing to the diffractive optical-logic work (Qian et al. 2020) and to the large-scale reconfigurable diffractive processing unit (Zhou et al. 2021). His work bridges the founding UCLA demonstrations and the large-scale optoelectronic systems built in China.

## Deniz Mengu (University of California, Los Angeles)

Mengu is the analyst of the diffractive-network program. Working in Ozcan's group, he authored the systematic study of how diffractive networks scale with depth and neuron count and how they integrate with electronic networks (2019), the misalignment-resilient "vaccination" training method (2020), and — with Jingxi Li — the class-specific differential detection that restores signed outputs (2019). Where Lin built the first device, Mengu quantified what such devices can and cannot do; Section 14.4 rests heavily on his results.

## Mona Jarrahi (University of California, Los Angeles)

Jarrahi is the terahertz engineer whose sources and detectors made the original D2NN experiments physically possible. A professor of electrical and computer engineering at UCLA, she is a leading figure in terahertz photonics, particularly plasmonic photoconductive emitters and receivers that deliver bright, well-characterized terahertz beams. Her hardware appears as a co-authored contribution across the terahertz diffractive demonstrations — Lin 2018, Luo 2019, Mengu 2020, Veli 2021 — and it is precisely the long terahertz wavelength her instruments exploit that makes those millimeter-scale, 3D-printed networks buildable.

## Qionghai Dai (Tsinghua University)

Dai leads the large-scale optoelectronic-computing effort at Tsinghua, where he is a professor in the Department of Automation working on computational photography, brain-inspired computing, and gigapixel imaging. He is the senior author of the reconfigurable diffractive processing unit (Zhou et al. 2021, *Nature Photonics*), which reimagined the fixed diffractive stack as an electronically programmable, million-neuron computing engine capable of switching between tasks — the demonstration that carried diffractive computing from a printed curiosity toward a general-purpose accelerator.

## Lu Fang (Tsinghua University)

Fang, a professor in Tsinghua University's Department of Electronic Engineering, co-led the reconfigurable diffractive processing unit with Dai. Her research in computational imaging and large-scale visual computing supplied much of the systems engineering — the programmable optoelectronic layers, the electronic nonlinearity between them, and the training pipeline — that let the DPU scale far beyond the hand-built terahertz networks. Her work exemplifies the reconfigurable branch of the field set against the fixed printed-layer branch.

## Demetri Psaltis (École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne)

Psaltis is the historical conscience of optical neural computing. Beginning in the 1980s and 1990s, largely at Caltech, he pioneered optical neural networks, holographic associative memories, and volume-holographic information processing — the intellectual ancestors of today's diffractive networks. Now director of the Optics Laboratory at EPFL, he remains active in optical computing and co-authored the 2020 *Nature* perspective on deep optics and photonics. He is included here to make clear that the D2NN did not appear from nowhere: it revives, with modern training, ideas he helped establish decades earlier.

## Gordon Wetzstein (Stanford University)

Wetzstein directs the computational imaging program at Stanford, where he is a professor of electrical engineering. His work on "deep optics" — jointly optimizing physical optics and neural networks end-to-end — is a sibling discipline to diffractive networks, and he is the lead author of the 2020 *Nature* review "Inference in artificial intelligence with deep optics and photonics," the standard survey that frames where diffractive computing sits within optics-for-AI. His perspective supplies the broad context for this chapter's place in the field.

## Hongsheng Chen (Zhejiang University)

Chen is a professor at Zhejiang University's College of Information Science and Electronic Engineering, working on electromagnetics, metamaterials, and wave manipulation. He is the senior author of "Performing optical logic operations by a diffractive neural network" (Qian et al. 2020), which showed that a diffractive network can be trained to implement Boolean logic on optical inputs. His contribution extends the diffractive paradigm from pattern classification toward general optical computation, and connects the D2NN to the metamaterials community.
