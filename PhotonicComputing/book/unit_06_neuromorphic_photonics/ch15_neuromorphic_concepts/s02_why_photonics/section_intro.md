# Section 15.2: Why Photonics for Neuromorphic Computing?

Section 15.1 abstracted the neuron into a small set of operations: integrate weighted inputs, leak, fire a stereotyped spike, and adjust weights by spike timing. Any physical substrate that implements these can compute in a brain-inspired way. This section asks what specifically light offers — why photonics, rather than yet another electronic implementation, is worth the trouble.

Three advantages recur throughout Unit VI. The first is **speed**: an optical spike can be picoseconds wide, several orders of magnitude faster than a biological action potential, so a photonic network can traverse many layers in nanoseconds (§15.2.1). The second is **connectivity**: an optical signal fans out passively by splitting, and wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) lets many independent signals share a single waveguide, each on its own wavelength — a natural fit for the dense, all-to-all wiring a neural network demands (§15.2.2). The third is **event-driven energy**: like a biological neuron, a photonic neuron need dissipate power only when it spikes, and non-volatile weights can hold their values with no standby cost (§15.2.3).

Against these stands the central challenge that frames the whole field: **energy per spike**. The advantages above are decisive only if each optical spike — and each weighting and conversion it undergoes — costs little enough that the total, spikes multiplied by energy-per-spike, beats an equivalent digital accelerator. Present photonic neurons operate in the femtojoule-to-picojoule range, with a target below one femtojoule per spike. The three subsections that follow quantify each advantage in turn and set up the device chapter (Chapter 16) that pursues that target.
