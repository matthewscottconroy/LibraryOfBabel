# 16.4.2 Surrogate-Gradient Training of Photonic SNNs

## Why backpropagation stalls at the spike

Training a network by gradient descent requires differentiating its output with respect to every weight. In a spiking neuron the output is generated by a threshold: the neuron emits a spike when its membrane potential $V$ crosses $V_\text{th}$,
$$S=\Theta(V-V_\text{th}),\qquad \Theta(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x\ge0\\0,&x<0,\end{cases}$$
where $\Theta$ is the Heaviside step. Its derivative is zero everywhere except at $V=V_\text{th}$, where it is a Dirac delta:
$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}=\delta(V-V_\text{th}).$$
Backpropagation-through-time (BPTT), which unrolls the recurrent membrane dynamics over the time-steps and applies the chain rule, therefore fails: almost every gradient it tries to propagate is multiplied by zero, and the single non-zero point is an infinite spike. The learning signal cannot flow back through the spike.

## The surrogate-gradient trick

The surrogate-gradient method (Neftci et al. 2019) resolves this with a deliberate asymmetry between the forward and backward passes. **The forward pass keeps the true hard spike** $S=\Theta(V-V_\text{th})$ — the network still computes with genuine all-or-nothing events, so its dynamics are unchanged. **The backward pass replaces the ill-behaved $\partial S/\partial V$ with a smooth surrogate** $\sigma'(V-V_\text{th})$, a bump function peaked at threshold:
$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\ \longrightarrow\ \sigma'(V-V_\text{th}).$$
A common choice is the derivative of a fast sigmoid (Zenke & Ganguli 2018, "SuperSpike"),
$$\sigma'(x)=\frac{1}{\left(1+\beta\,|x|\right)^{2}},$$
with steepness $\beta$; triangular or box-shaped surrogates work similarly. The surrogate lets a non-zero gradient flow whenever $V$ is *near* threshold — even for a neuron that did not actually fire but came close — so BPTT can once again assign credit across the whole unrolled network. Intuitively, the surrogate treats the spike as a steep-but-smooth nonlinearity for the purpose of credit assignment alone: a neuron sitting just below threshold still receives pressure to move its weights toward, or away from, firing, in proportion to how close it came. The forward computation stays exact; only the gradient is approximated, and the steepness $\beta$ tunes how sharply that gradient concentrates around threshold.

### Worked Example: one neuron, one gradient step

Consider a neuron with threshold $V_\text{th}=1.0$ driven, at the time-step of interest, by a single weighted input $V=w\,x$ with $w=1.00$ and $x=0.8$, so $V=0.8$.

*Forward pass.* $V-V_\text{th}=0.8-1.0=-0.2<0$, so $S=\Theta(-0.2)=0$: the neuron does **not** spike, and the forward network uses this hard 0.

*Backward pass.* Take the fast-sigmoid surrogate with $\beta=5$:
$$\sigma'(V-V_\text{th})=\frac{1}{\left(1+5\,|{-}0.2|\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{(1+1)^{2}}=\frac{1}{4}=0.25.$$
Although no spike fired, the surrogate reports a slope of $0.25$ — the neuron was close to threshold, so it is eligible for a weight update.

*Gradient and update.* Suppose BPTT has delivered an error signal $\delta\equiv\partial L/\partial S=0.5$ at this neuron's output. By the chain rule,
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=\delta\cdot\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\cdot\frac{\partial V}{\partial w}=\delta\,\sigma'(V-V_\text{th})\,x=0.5\times0.25\times0.8=0.10.$$
A gradient-descent step with learning rate $\eta=0.1$ then updates
$$w\leftarrow w-\eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=1.00-0.1\times0.10=0.99.$$
The weight moves by $-0.01$. Had we used the true Heaviside derivative, $\partial S/\partial V=0$ here, the gradient — and the update — would have been exactly zero, and the neuron could never have learned to adjust its firing. (In a full SNN this single-step calculation is repeated at every time-step of the BPTT unroll and summed over time, but the mechanism is exactly the one shown.)

## Train offline, then deploy — and the sim-to-hardware gap

Surrogate-gradient training runs in software: a differentiable model of the LIF/excitable dynamics is trained on labelled data, and the learned weights are then programmed into the photonic chip — as microring drop settings or PCM crystalline fractions (§16.2, §16.3). The danger is the **sim-to-hardware gap**: photonic weights are analog and imperfect. Detector shot noise, thermal drift of ring resonances, PCM programming stochasticity and transmission drift, and limited weight resolution all mean the chip does not implement the trained weights exactly, and accuracy can collapse on deployment.

The standard remedy is to make the training model *itself* non-ideal — injecting the hardware's noise and quantization into the forward pass during training, so the optimizer finds weights that are robust to them. This is **noise-aware / hardware-in-the-loop training**, developed for classical optical neural networks in Unit V (§13.3); the same machinery applies to spiking photonic networks, with the surrogate gradient handling the additional non-differentiability of the spike. Shastri et al. (2021) survey these training-and-deployment strategies in the neuromorphic-photonic setting.

The contrast with STDP (§16.4.1) is now sharp. STDP learns locally and online but is hard to aim at a specified task; surrogate-gradient BPTT is global, supervised, and task-optimal, at the cost of an offline differentiable model and a careful, noise-aware transfer of the trained weights to hardware. In practice the two are often combined — offline surrogate-gradient training to reach a task-competent weight set, with local plasticity for on-chip adaptation afterward.
