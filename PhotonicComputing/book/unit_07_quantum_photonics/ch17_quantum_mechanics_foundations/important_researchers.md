# Chapter 17: Important Researchers

---

## Paul Dirac (1902–1984)

Dirac supplied the language of this entire unit. His bra-ket notation (introduced 1939, building on the *transformation theory* he and Jordan developed in 1927) is the $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ formalism of Section 17.1.1, and his 1930 *Principles of Quantum Mechanics* codified the postulates in the operator form used here. More consequentially for photonics, his 1927 paper "The quantum theory of the emission and absorption of radiation" performed the first quantization of the electromagnetic field — expanding the field in modes and quantizing each as an oscillator — which is precisely the construction of Section 17.3.1. The photon-as-mode-excitation picture, and the creation and annihilation operators that build it, are Dirac's. He shared the 1933 Nobel Prize with Schrödinger.

---

## Erwin Schrödinger (1887–1961)

Schrödinger's wave equation is the time-evolution law of Section 17.1.4, and his name attaches to the unit's other central theme. In a trio of 1935 papers responding to Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen, he coined the term *Verschränkung* — entanglement — and declared it "not one but rather *the* characteristic trait of quantum mechanics, the one that enforces its entire departure from classical lines of thought." The same papers gave us the cat. Everything in Section 17.4 elaborates the phenomenon he named. He shared the 1933 Nobel Prize with Dirac.

---

## John von Neumann (1903–1957)

Von Neumann's 1932 *Mathematische Grundlagen der Quantenmechanik* set quantum theory on its Hilbert-space foundations and introduced three tools this chapter cannot do without: the *density matrix* $\rho$ for mixed states and subsystems (Section 17.1.1), the projective *measurement postulate* that collapses the state (Section 17.1.3), and the *von Neumann entropy* $S(\rho) = -\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\ln\rho)$ that quantifies entanglement of a bipartite pure state through its reduced density matrix (Section 17.4.4). The distinction between a coherent superposition and a classical mixture — the thing decoherence destroys — is his to state precisely.

---

## Eugene Wigner (1902–1995)

Wigner introduced his eponymous quasi-probability distribution in 1932, seeking quantum corrections to classical statistical mechanics. The Wigner function of Section 17.3.4 — a real distribution on phase space that returns the correct marginals for every quadrature yet can go *negative* — became quantum optics' portrait of a state and, through the Hudson and Mari-Eisert theorems, the operational boundary between classically simulable Gaussian optics and genuinely quantum resources (a distinction Chapter 21 turns into an architecture question). Wigner received the 1963 Nobel Prize for his work on symmetry principles.

---

## Roy Glauber (1925–2018)

Glauber built the quantum theory of optical coherence, for which he received half of the 2005 Nobel Prize. His 1963 papers defined the *coherent states* $|\alpha\rangle$ of Section 17.3.2 — the eigenstates of the annihilation operator, the quantum description of ideal laser light, with Poissonian statistics and minimum symmetric noise — and the hierarchy of correlation functions $g^{(n)}$ that distinguish laser, thermal, and single-photon light. The coherent-state formalism, the $P$-representation, and the $g^{(2)}$ function are Glauber's; they open Chapter 18 and never leave this unit.

---

## John Stewart Bell (1928–1990)

Bell asked whether the EPR "elements of reality" could be completed by local hidden variables, and in his 1964 paper "On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen paradox" proved they could not: any local theory obeys an inequality that quantum mechanics violates. The CHSH form of that inequality and its Tsirelson-bounded quantum maximum $2\sqrt{2}$ are the subject of Section 17.4.3. Bell converted a philosophical dispute into an experimental question — and, decades later, into the security proofs of device-independent QKD (Chapter 22). He died in 1990, the year he was reportedly nominated for the Nobel Prize.

---

## John Clauser (1942–) and Alain Aspect (1947–)

Clauser, with Horne, Shimony, and Holt, cast Bell's inequality into the experimentally testable CHSH form (1969) and, with Freedman (1972), performed the first test — an early violation with photon pairs. Aspect's Orsay experiments of 1981–82 sharpened the case decisively, culminating in the version with *time-varying* analyzers (Aspect, Dalibard, and Roger, 1982) that closed the locality loophole by switching measurement settings faster than light could cross the apparatus. Their photon-pair Bell tests are the experimental spine of Section 17.4.3. Both shared the 2022 Nobel Prize.

---

## Anton Zeilinger (1945–)

Zeilinger (also a 2022 Nobel laureate) turned entanglement into a laboratory technology. His group introduced the three-particle GHZ states (with Greenberger and Horne) that sharpen Bell's argument to a single measurement, demonstrated quantum teleportation (1997) and entanglement swapping (1998), and — with the Vienna and NIST/Delft groups — closed the remaining loopholes in the 2015 Bell tests. The maximally entangled Bell states of Section 17.4.2 are, in his hands, the resource that Chapters 20 and 22 consume for fusion measurements, teleportation, and quantum communication.

---

## William Wootters (1951–) and Wojciech Zurek (1951–)

Wootters and Zurek proved the *no-cloning theorem* in a celebrated 1982 note ("A single quantum cannot be cloned"): linearity forbids any device that copies an unknown quantum state. Presented in this chapter as a corollary of the measurement and evolution postulates (Section 17.1), it is the load-bearing fact of quantum information — no quantum amplifiers, hence no repeater-by-amplification, hence the exponential distance wall and the security of QKD (Chapter 22). Wootters later gave the *concurrence* measure of two-qubit entanglement (Section 17.4.4); Zurek built the theory of decoherence and einselection that explains why the quantum-classical boundary looks the way it does.
