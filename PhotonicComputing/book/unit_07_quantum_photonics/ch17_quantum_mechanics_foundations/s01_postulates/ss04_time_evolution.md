# 17.1.4 Time Evolution

## The Fourth Postulate

**Postulate 4.** *Between measurements, the state of a closed quantum system evolves according to the Schrödinger equation*

$$i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{H}(t)\,|\psi(t)\rangle$$

*where $\hat{H}$, the Hamiltonian, is the Hermitian operator representing the system's total energy.*

For a time-independent Hamiltonian the solution is

$$|\psi(t)\rangle = \hat{U}(t)\,|\psi(0)\rangle, \qquad \hat{U}(t) = e^{-i\hat{H}t/\hbar}$$

The exponential of an operator is defined by its power series (or, more usefully, by acting on the eigenbasis of $\hat{H}$). Because $\hat{H}$ is Hermitian, $\hat{U}$ is **unitary**: $\hat{U}^\dagger\hat{U} = \mathbb{1}$. Unitarity is conservation of probability — $\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|\hat{U}^\dagger\hat{U}|\psi(0)\rangle = 1$ for all time — and it makes closed-system evolution *reversible* and *linear*. Density matrices evolve as $\rho(t) = \hat{U}\rho(0)\hat{U}^\dagger$ (the von Neumann equation $i\hbar\,\dot{\rho} = [\hat{H}, \rho]$ in differential form).

## Stationary States and Relative Phase

Expand $|\psi(0)\rangle$ in energy eigenstates $\hat{H}|E_n\rangle = E_n|E_n\rangle$:

$$|\psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n\, e^{-iE_n t/\hbar}\,|E_n\rangle$$

Each eigenstate just accumulates phase at its own rate $E_n/\hbar$ — energy eigenstates are **stationary states** whose measurement statistics never change. All dynamics lives in the *relative* phases between components of a superposition. This is the quantum root of every interference phenomenon in this book: dynamics = phase accumulation, and computation-by-interference = engineered phase accumulation.

**Worked example (two-level beating).** Let $|\psi(0)\rangle = (|E_1\rangle + |E_2\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$. Then $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}(|E_1\rangle + e^{-i(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar}|E_2\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$. Any observable with off-diagonal elements between the two eigenstates oscillates at the **transition frequency** $\omega_{21} = (E_2 - E_1)/\hbar$. For an atom's ground and excited states this oscillating dipole is the microscopic origin of emission at $\omega_{21}$; for a photon split between two waveguide modes with propagation constants $\beta_1, \beta_2$, the same mathematics gives the directional-coupler power oscillation of Chapter 6, with $z$ playing the role of $t$.

## Photonic Unitaries: Optics as Scheduled Evolution

In quantum photonics we rarely solve the Schrödinger equation in real time; instead we use fixed optical elements, each of which *implements* the unitary $\hat{U} = e^{-i\hat{H}\tau/\hbar}$ generated by its interaction Hamiltonian over the transit time $\tau$. The dictionary:

| Element | Generator (Hamiltonian) | Unitary action |
|---|---|---|
| Phase shifter on mode $a$ | $\hat{H} \propto \hat{n}_a = \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$ | $\hat{U}(\phi) = e^{i\phi\hat{n}_a}$; $|n\rangle \to e^{in\phi}|n\rangle$ |
| Beam splitter / coupler on modes $a,b$ | $\hat{H} \propto \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b} + \hat{b}^\dagger\hat{a}$ | SU(2) rotation mixing the modes (Section 18.2.1) |
| Wave plate on $H,V$ | $\hat{H} \propto \hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n}}$ | Polarization rotation on the Poincaré sphere |
| Squeezer (pumped $\chi^{(2)}$) | $\hat{H} \propto i(\hat{a}^{\dagger 2} - \hat{a}^2)$ | $\hat{S}(r)$, Section 17.3.3 |

A lossless linear-optical circuit on $N$ modes — any mesh of beam splitters and phase shifters — implements an $N \times N$ unitary matrix on the mode operators. This is the same Reck/Clements decomposition used for classical MZI-mesh processors (Chapters 2 and 11); the quantum upgrade is that the *same* hardware now acts on superpositions and entangled states of photons. A single photon in a dual-rail qubit acted on by an MZI undergoes exactly the qubit rotation $e^{-i\theta\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n}}/2}$: linear optics gives all single-qubit gates for free (Chapter 20). What linear optics does *not* give is any interaction between photons — the Hamiltonians above are all quadratic in mode operators, hence they map single-photon states to single-photon states and never generate the conditional phases a two-qubit gate needs. That absence is the central obstacle of photonic quantum computing, stated here as a property of the available generators.

## The Heisenberg Picture

The Schrödinger picture evolves states and keeps operators fixed. The **Heisenberg picture** does the opposite: states are frozen at $t=0$ and observables carry the dynamics,

$$\hat{A}_H(t) = \hat{U}^\dagger(t)\,\hat{A}\,\hat{U}(t), \qquad \frac{d\hat{A}_H}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}, \hat{A}_H]$$

All predictions — expectation values, probabilities, correlations — are identical: $\langle\psi(t)|\hat{A}|\psi(t)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|\hat{A}_H(t)|\psi(0)\rangle$. Quantum optics lives mostly in the Heisenberg picture, for a practical reason: field states like coherent or squeezed states are unwieldy, while mode operators transform *simply* through optical elements. "The beam splitter maps $\hat{a} \to (\hat{a} + i\hat{b})/\sqrt{2}$" is one line in the Heisenberg picture (Section 18.2.1); the equivalent Schrödinger statement acts on an infinite-dimensional state space. Correlation functions such as $g^{(2)}(\tau) $ (Section 18.1) are defined directly as Heisenberg-picture operator products at two times.

## Closed versus Open: Where Unitarity Ends

Postulate 4 governs *closed* systems. A photon coupled to the outside world — a lossy waveguide, an absorbing filter, a detector — is an *open* system: the photon plus environment evolve unitarily, but the photon alone does not. Tracing out the environment (Section 17.4.1) turns pure states into mixed ones and coherences decay: **decoherence**. Photons are exceptional among qubit platforms in that their dominant decoherence channel is not dephasing but outright *loss* — the photon leaves, taking its quantum state with it. Loss per element can be made small ($\sim 0.1$ dB) but never zero, and unlike unitary errors it cannot be undone by any compensating unitary; it must be *detected* (dual-rail encoding makes loss an erasure) and *corrected* (Chapter 20). The clean world of this section — reversible, norm-preserving evolution punctuated by projective measurements — is the design ideal that real photonic engineering approaches asymptotically, one decibel at a time.
