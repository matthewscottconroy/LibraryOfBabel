# Section 17.4: Quantum Entanglement

Everything so far concerned one system at a time — one qubit, one mode. Quantum mechanics saves its deepest surprise for *composite* systems. When two quantum systems are described jointly, the tensor-product structure of Hilbert space permits states that cannot be factored into "system A is in this state, system B is in that state" — states in which neither part has any definite state of its own, while the whole is perfectly definite. Schrödinger named the phenomenon *Verschränkung* — entanglement — and called it "not one but rather the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics, the one that enforces its entire departure from classical lines of thought."

This section builds entanglement from the ground up: the tensor-product formalism and the partial trace (17.4.1); the four Bell states, the maximally entangled two-qubit states that photonic sources mass-produce (17.4.2); Bell's theorem and the CHSH inequality, which turn the EPR philosophical dispute into a laboratory measurement — one that photons have decided, conclusively, against local realism (17.4.3); and the quantitative theory of entanglement measures, which treats entanglement as a resource to be counted, distilled, and spent (17.4.4).

For photonic computing, entanglement is not exotic background; it is the working medium. Cluster states (Chapter 20), entangled-pair distribution (Chapter 22), and the correlations that make measurement-induced gates possible are all applications of the machinery assembled here.
