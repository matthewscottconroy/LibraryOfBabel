# Chapter 18: Important Researchers

---

## Roy J. Glauber (1925–2018)

Glauber (Harvard; Nobel Prize 2005) built the quantum theory of optical coherence. In two 1963 papers he introduced the coherent states as the quantum description of laser light and defined the hierarchy of normally ordered correlation functions $g^{(n)}$ that this entire chapter is organized around. His framework gave precise meaning to "classical" versus "non-classical" light — the Glauber-Sudarshan $P$ representation and the criterion that antibunching (Section 18.1.3) requires a $P$ function that is not a probability density. It was partly the Hanbury Brown-Twiss controversy that demanded such a theory, and Glauber supplied it.

---

## Robert Hanbury Brown (1916–2002) & Richard Q. Twiss (1920–2005)

The radio astronomer Hanbury Brown and the mathematical physicist Twiss invented intensity interferometry (Section 18.1.2). Correlating the *intensities* rather than the amplitudes from two detectors, they built a stellar interferometer immune to atmospheric phase noise and, in 1956, measured the angular diameter of Sirius. Their laboratory demonstration of photon bunching in thermal light — two detectors after a beam splitter clicking together more than chance allows — provoked a foundational controversy and, in its resolution, launched modern quantum optics. Every $g^{(2)}$ measurement in this book uses their apparatus.

---

## Leonard Mandel (1927–2001)

Mandel (Rochester) was the presiding figure of experimental quantum optics for a generation, and his name is attached to two pillars of this chapter. With Kimble and Dagenais he reported the first observation of photon antibunching in 1977 (Section 18.1.3), and with Hong and Ou he discovered the two-photon interference effect of 1987 (Section 18.2.2) that now bears all three names. The Mandel $Q$ parameter (Section 18.1.1) is his. His work turned photon statistics from theory into a measurable, decisive signature of the quantum nature of light.

---

## H. Jeff Kimble (1949–)

Kimble (Caltech) appears throughout the quantum-photonics unit. In this chapter he is co-author of the 1977 antibunching experiment (Section 18.1.3), the first light ever shown to be more orderly than a laser, and of the 1986 generation of squeezed light by parametric down-conversion with Wu, Hall, and Wu (Section 18.3.2), which established the sub-threshold OPO as the standard squeezing source. The same 1998 collaboration with Furusawa produced the first unconditional continuous-variable quantum teleportation, the bridge to Chapter 21.

---

## Alain Aspect (1947–)

Aspect (Institut d'Optique; Nobel Prize 2022) is best known for the 1980s Bell-inequality tests, but the experiment most relevant here is the 1986 work with Grangier and Roger (Section 18.1.3): a single photon sent onto a beam splitter, shown never to trigger both detectors at once. Their anticorrelation parameter fell far below the classical bound of unity, giving the cleanest possible demonstration that light is delivered in indivisible quanta — and, in the same apparatus, that the very same photon rebuilds interference fringes when the paths are recombined.

---

## Carlton M. Caves (1950–)

Caves (New Mexico) supplied the theoretical insight that turned squeezing into a metrological tool. His 1981 analysis traced the shot-noise limit of an interferometer to vacuum fluctuations entering the *unused* port, and proposed injecting squeezed vacuum there to beat the standard quantum limit (Section 18.3.3). The idea sat mostly dormant for three decades until the loss engineering caught up; it is now permanent infrastructure in every gravitational-wave detector. Caves also formalized much of the quantum limits on amplification and measurement that recur across this unit.

---

## Roman Schnabel

Schnabel (Universität Hamburg; formerly Albert Einstein Institute, Hannover) leads the group that holds the squeezing records this chapter quotes. His team's steady campaign against optical loss produced the directly detected **15 dB** squeezed states of Vahlbruch et al. (2016) at 1064 nm (Section 18.3.2), and developed the squeezed-light sources engineered for gravitational-wave interferometry, including the GEO600 squeezer (Section 18.3.3). His work is the practical embodiment of the loss-fragility formula: fifteen decibels is what a decade of hunting sub-percent losses buys.

---

## Akira Furusawa (1961–)

Furusawa (University of Tokyo) is a leader of continuous-variable quantum information. His 1998 experiment with Kimble, Braunstein, and colleagues achieved the first unconditional quantum teleportation, using two-mode squeezed light (Section 18.3.1) as the entangled resource — the CV counterpart to discrete-variable teleportation. His group has since pushed squeezing generation, large-scale CV cluster states, and time-multiplexed CV processing, directly seeding the continuous-variable quantum computing of Chapter 21.

---

## Rainer Weiss (1932–), Barry Barish (1936–) & Kip Thorne (1940–)

The 2017 Nobel laureates built LIGO, the instrument in which the squeezing of this chapter now operates (Section 18.3.3). Weiss conceived the kilometer-scale laser interferometer and its noise budget; Thorne developed the theory of gravitational-wave sources and signals; Barish transformed the project into the collaboration that delivered GW150914, the first direct detection, in 2015. Their interferometer is where Caves' 1981 proposal became working astronomy — the standard-quantum-limited measurement that squeezed vacuum was invented to surpass.
