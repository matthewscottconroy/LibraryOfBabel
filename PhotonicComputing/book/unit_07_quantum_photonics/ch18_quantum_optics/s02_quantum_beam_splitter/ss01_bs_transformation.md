# 18.2.1 The Quantum Beam Splitter Transformation

## A Beam Splitter Is a Two-Mode Unitary

A lossless beam splitter takes two input modes, $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{b}$, and returns two output modes, $\hat{c}$ and $\hat{d}$. Because the device is linear and lossless, the outputs are a linear combination of the inputs:

$$\hat{c} = t\,\hat{a} + r\,\hat{b}, \qquad \hat{d} = r\,\hat{a} + t\,\hat{b},$$

with complex transmission and reflection amplitudes $t$ and $r$. This is the whole device — a $2\times 2$ matrix acting on mode operators. Everything quantum follows from one demand: the outputs must be legitimate bosonic modes, obeying $[\hat{c},\hat{c}^\dagger] = [\hat{d},\hat{d}^\dagger] = 1$ and $[\hat{c},\hat{d}^\dagger]=0$, just like the inputs. Imposing these commutators on the transformation forces

$$|r|^2 + |t|^2 = 1, \qquad r^*t + r\,t^* = 0.$$

The first is energy conservation; the second is a phase (reciprocity) condition that ties the reflection phase to the transmission phase. The transformation matrix must be unitary — which is exactly the statement that a lossless linear-optical element implements a unitary on modes, the thread that runs from the Reck-Clements meshes of classical photonics to the dual-rail gates of Chapter 20.

The symmetric **50/50** choice, used throughout this chapter, is

$$t = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \qquad r = \frac{i}{\sqrt2},$$

where the factor $i$ carries the $\pi/2$ phase that reflection picks up relative to transmission. One checks the reciprocity condition immediately: $r^*t + rt^* = (-i/\sqrt2)(1/\sqrt2) + (i/\sqrt2)(1/\sqrt2) = 0$. Equivalently, in terms of building output *states* from input photons, the creation operators map as

$$\hat{a}^\dagger \to \frac{\hat{c}^\dagger + i\,\hat{d}^\dagger}{\sqrt2}, \qquad \hat{b}^\dagger \to \frac{i\,\hat{c}^\dagger + \hat{d}^\dagger}{\sqrt2}.$$

We will use this creation-operator form for every Fock-state calculation.

## The Vacuum Port Is Not Optional

Here is the single most important quantum lesson of the beam splitter, and the one classical intuition gets wrong. Suppose you send light into port $a$ only and leave port $b$ "empty." It is tempting to forget $b$ and write $\hat{c} = \hat{a}/\sqrt2$ — the transmitted field. But then

$$[\hat{c},\hat{c}^\dagger] = \tfrac{1}{2}[\hat{a},\hat{a}^\dagger] = \tfrac{1}{2} \neq 1,$$

and $\hat{c}$ is *not a valid mode*. The commutator is rescued only by keeping the second input: $\hat{c} = (\hat{a} + i\hat{b})/\sqrt2$ restores $[\hat{c},\hat{c}^\dagger] = \tfrac12(1+1) = 1$. The "empty" port must be filled — with vacuum, at least — and the vacuum's fluctuations *enter the output whether you like it or not*. There is no such thing as an unused port on a beam splitter; there is only a port carrying vacuum. This is the same mechanism by which loss un-squeezes light (a lossy element is a beam splitter tapping signal against vacuum, Section 17.3.3) and the same port through which squeezed vacuum is deliberately injected into an interferometer's dark fringe (Section 18.3.3). Ignore the vacuum port and you violate the uncertainty principle.

## A Single Photon Delocalizes — It Does Not Split

**Worked example.** *One photon on a 50/50 beam splitter.*

Send a single photon into port $a$, vacuum into port $b$: the input is $|1,0\rangle = \hat{a}^\dagger|0,0\rangle$. Apply the creation-operator map:

$$\hat{a}^\dagger|0,0\rangle \to \frac{\hat{c}^\dagger + i\,\hat{d}^\dagger}{\sqrt2}\,|0,0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|1,0\rangle + i\,|0,1\rangle\big).$$

Read this carefully. The output is *one photon* in a coherent superposition of "in port $c$" and "in port $d$" — a **delocalized single photon**, not two half-photons. If you place detectors at both outputs, exactly one fires; the probability is $1/2$ each, and the joint (coincidence) probability is *zero*. That last fact is the Grangier-Roger-Aspect anticorrelation of Section 18.1.3: a photon is indivisible, and the beam splitter routes it probabilistically, it does not cleave it. The relative phase $i$ between the two branches is physical and interferes if the paths are later recombined.

This delocalized single-photon state *is* the dual-rail qubit of Chapter 20: identify $|1,0\rangle \equiv |0\rangle_L$ and $|0,1\rangle \equiv |1\rangle_L$, and the 50/50 beam splitter is a Hadamard-like single-qubit gate, with phase shifters supplying the rest of the Bloch sphere. Every single-qubit operation in linear optical quantum computing is an interferometer built from exactly this transformation.

## Classical Light Just Divides

For contrast, feed a coherent state (Section 17.3.2) into port $a$ and vacuum into $b$. Because $\hat{D}(\alpha)$ is generated by $\hat{a}^\dagger$ and the map is linear, the output is a *product* of coherent states:

$$|\alpha\rangle_a\,|0\rangle_b \to \left|\tfrac{\alpha}{\sqrt2}\right\rangle_c\,\left|\tfrac{i\alpha}{\sqrt2}\right\rangle_d.$$

A bright classical beam simply divides its amplitude between the outputs, with no entanglement and no surprises — half the power to each port, exactly the classical result. The difference between this factorized output and the path-entangled single-photon state above is the entire difference between classical and quantum optics at a beam splitter: brightness divides, but single quanta delocalize. Push a *second* photon into the other port and the two behaviors collide — which is the Hong-Ou-Mandel effect.

## From One Splitter to a Universal Mesh

The single $2\times2$ unitary is the building block of arbitrarily large linear-optical circuits. A phase shifter is the diagonal unitary $\mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\phi})$ on two modes; sandwiching it between two beam splitters makes a Mach-Zehnder interferometer whose split ratio is tunable through $\phi$ — a *variable* beam splitter, and, on the dual-rail qubit, an arbitrary single-qubit rotation. Cascading such elements across $N$ modes realizes *any* $N\times N$ unitary on the mode operators, by the Reck and Clements decompositions that also govern classical photonic processors (Units 3 and 5). The quantum content is only that the amplitudes being transformed are now probability amplitudes for photons: the very same mesh that routes classical power implements, photon by photon, the linear-optical networks of Chapter 20. Note also that the placement of the reflection phase is a convention — some texts assign $i$ to transmission, or split the phase symmetrically as $e^{i\pi/2}$ on each reflected arm — and physical predictions (click probabilities, HOM visibility) are independent of the choice, provided one is consistent, because they depend only on the unitarity of the transformation.

## Why It Matters

The beam splitter transformation is the atom of linear optics: unitary on modes, mandatory vacuum port, single photons delocalized rather than split. These three facts are the grammar of Chapters 20 through 22. The dual-rail qubit, the loss model of squeezed-light systems, the injection of squeezing into LIGO, and the two-photon interference of the next subsection all reduce to this one $2\times 2$ unitary and the vacuum that necessarily accompanies it.
