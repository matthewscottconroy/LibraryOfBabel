# 18.2.3 HOM as the Primitive of Linear Optical Quantum Computing

## The Only Interaction in Town

Linear optics has a fundamental problem for computing: photons do not interact. A beam splitter mixes modes, a phase shifter rotates a phase, but nothing in a passive linear network lets the state of one photon depend on the presence of another. Yet a universal quantum computer needs a two-qubit entangling gate — a CNOT or CZ — whose entire job is to make one qubit's evolution conditional on another's. For most of the 1990s this looked like a proof that linear optical quantum computing was impossible.

The Hong-Ou-Mandel effect is the loophole. Two indistinguishable photons at a beam splitter produce an output — $\frac{i}{\sqrt2}(|2,0\rangle + |0,2\rangle)$ — that is entangled in the output paths, generated by no interaction whatsoever, purely by the interference of two-photon amplitudes (Section 18.2.2). HOM is the *only* nonlinearity-like resource linear optics possesses, and it is not even a nonlinearity: it is quantum statistics. Knill, Laflamme, and Milburn (2001) showed how to convert it into a genuine, if probabilistic, two-qubit gate by adding ancilla photons and **measurement** — detection is the nonlinear step, and HOM interference is what makes the measured outcome depend jointly on both computational photons. Every entangling operation in Chapter 20, from the KLM CZ gate to the fusion gates of measurement-based architectures, is at its core one or more HOM interferences read out by photon counting.

## Bunching Is Entanglement

It is worth seeing the entanglement directly. The HOM output $|2,0\rangle$ and $|0,2\rangle$ superposed is a path-entangled two-photon state — the $N=2$ member of the NOON family $(|N,0\rangle + |0,N\rangle)/\sqrt2$. In a **fusion gate** (Chapter 20), two photons drawn from two separate entangled resource states are interfered on a beam splitter and detected; the HOM bunching statistics project the *remaining* photons onto an entangled state, fusing two small cluster states into a larger one. Success and failure of the fusion are read from which output ports bunch. The computation is nothing but a choreography of HOM interferences, each one stitching entanglement between photons that never met before the beam splitter.

## Distinguishability Is Gate Error

Because the gate *is* the interference, anything that degrades the HOM visibility degrades the gate. If the two interfering photons have indistinguishability $M = |\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle|^2 < 1$, a fraction $1-M$ of the two-photon amplitude fails to interfere, leaks into the wrong output configuration, and corrupts the post-selected state. To an excellent approximation the entangling-gate infidelity inherits this shortfall linearly,

$$\varepsilon_{\text{gate}} \sim (1 - M),$$

up to an $O(1)$ factor set by the specific gate. Distinguishability in *any* degree of freedom counts: a spectral offset, a timing jitter comparable to the coherence time, a polarization misalignment, a transverse-mode mismatch. Each subtracts from $M$ and adds to the error budget. This is why Chapter 19 treats indistinguishability as a make-or-break source specification, and why the field measures it obsessively with the HOM dip.

**Worked example.** *From 1% spectral mismatch to a gate-error budget.*

Model each photon as a normalized Gaussian wavepacket in frequency, of rms bandwidth $\sigma$, with center frequencies offset by $\Delta\omega$. The overlap integral gives

$$M = |\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle|^2 = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta\omega^2}{4\sigma^2}\right).$$

Suppose the two sources are mismatched by a center-frequency offset equal to $20\%$ of their bandwidth, $\Delta\omega/\sigma = 0.20$. Then

$$M = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(0.20)^2}{4}\right) = e^{-0.010} \approx 0.990,$$

a $1\%$ distinguishability, hence a gate error of order $\varepsilon_{\text{gate}}\sim 10^{-2}$. That already sits at or above the fault-tolerance threshold of typical quantum error-correcting codes — a single mistuned source can put the machine below threshold. Turn the requirement around: fault-tolerant linear optical computing demands photon indistinguishability error below $\sim 10^{-3}$, i.e. HOM visibility

$$V = M > 99.9\%.$$

Achieving $1 - M < 10^{-3}$ requires

$$\frac{\Delta\omega}{\sigma} < \sqrt{-4\ln(0.999)} \approx 0.063,$$

so the two photons' center frequencies must be matched to about $6\%$ of their bandwidth — and simultaneously their arrival times to a small fraction of the coherence time, their polarizations to a fraction of a degree, and their spatial modes to near-unity overlap. The exponential sensitivity of $M$ to mismatch is why source uniformity (Section 19.1) is the central unsolved problem of scalable photonics: no two quantum emitters are born identical, and a $99.9\%$ specification tolerates almost no spread.

## The Scaling Consequence

Distinguishability compounds with the other photonic error, loss. A fault-tolerant photonic architecture must deliver, per photon, both loss and distinguishability errors in the $10^{-3}$ range, and it must do so across the $10^{10}$–$10^{12}$ photons per second that a useful machine consumes (Chapter 20). The HOM effect gives linear optics its entangling power for free; the price is that this power is only as good as the photons are identical. Two-photon interference is simultaneously the enabling physics of photonic quantum computing and the tightest constraint on its hardware.

## Independent Sources, Not Just Delayed Copies

There is a hard version of the requirement that the numbers above understate. It is comparatively easy to make a photon interfere with a *delayed copy of itself* from the same source — the wavepackets are guaranteed identical. A scalable computer instead demands HOM interference between photons from *different* sources, or from the same source at widely separated times, which must be identical without any shared origin to enforce it. This is why source *uniformity* and *stability*, not merely single-source purity, dominate the photonic-hardware agenda: a mesh interfering thousands of photons per logical operation needs every emitter matched in center frequency, bandwidth, and temporal profile to the $\sim6\%$-of-bandwidth tolerance derived above. Fusion-based architectures compound this: each fusion consumes a fresh pair of photons that have never met, so the distinguishability error enters *per fusion*, and a machine performing $10^{9}$ fusions per second cannot tolerate a per-photon error above $\sim10^{-3}$ without error correction. The HOM effect gives the interaction for free; identical photons at scale is the bill.

## Why It Matters

Everything in Chapter 20 rests on this subsection. The dual-rail qubit (Section 18.2.1) supplies the single-qubit gates; HOM interference supplies the entangling gates; and measurement converts probabilistic interference into deterministic-enough logic. The KLM protocol, cluster-state and fusion-based architectures, and even boson sampling are all elaborations of two-photon interference at beam splitters. Master the HOM effect and its indistinguishability requirement, and the architecture of the next chapter follows as engineering.
