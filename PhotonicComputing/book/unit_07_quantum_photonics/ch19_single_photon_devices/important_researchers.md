# Chapter 19: Important Researchers

---

## H. Jeff Kimble (1949–)

Jeff Kimble (Caltech) defined experimental optical cavity QED. His group produced the first observation of photon antibunching in resonance fluorescence (1977, with Dagenais and Mandel — the founding experiment of single-emitter quantum optics), the first optical strong coupling with single atoms (Thompson, Rempe & Kimble, 1992), photon blockade with one trapped atom (Birnbaum et al., 2005), and, with Duan, the cavity-assisted photon gate protocol that underlies deterministic photonic logic. His 2008 "quantum internet" manifesto framed the network vision that Chapter 22 pursues. Much of Section 19.3 is, directly or by descent, Kimble-group physics.

---

## Serge Haroche (1944–)

Serge Haroche (ENS/Collège de France; Nobel Prize 2012, shared with David Wineland) built microwave cavity QED into a precision laboratory for the Jaynes-Cummings model: vacuum Rabi oscillations, collapse-and-revival with circular Rydberg atoms, quantum nondemolition counting of photons one by one, and the watched decoherence of cavity "cat" states. His experiments transformed the dressed-state ladder of Section 19.3.1 from formalism into measured spectra, and his textbook with Jean-Michel Raimond (*Exploring the Quantum*) is the definitive treatment.

---

## Pascale Senellart (1972–)

Pascale Senellart (CNRS/C2N Paris) leads the group that turned quantum-dot single-photon sources into engineered, reproducible devices. Her in-situ optical lithography technique deterministically centers a chosen dot in a micropillar cavity to ~10 nm precision, solving the spatial-alignment half of the Purcell problem; her electrically contacted, charge-stabilized devices (Somaschi et al., 2016) delivered the "near-optimal" combination of purity, indistinguishability, and brightness. She co-founded Quandela, one of the first companies to sell single-photon sources — and photonic quantum computers built on them.

---

## Peter Michler (1961–)

Peter Michler (Stuttgart) performed the founding experiment of the quantum-dot source field: the single-photon turnstile (Michler et al., *Science*, 2000), demonstrating triggered antibunched emission from a single InAs dot. His group has remained at the forefront of resonant excitation techniques, two-photon (biexciton) pumping for entangled-pair generation, and telecom-wavelength quantum dots — the excitation-scheme toolbox of Section 19.1.2 is substantially his group's legacy.

---

## Paul Kwiat (1963–)

Paul Kwiat (Illinois; earlier with Zeilinger in Innsbruck and at Los Alamos) built the sources that powered a generation of quantum optics: the bright type-II SPDC polarization-entangled source (1995) and the "ultrabright" two-crystal type-I source (1999) remain the standard laboratory designs. His group pioneered hyperentanglement (entanglement in multiple degrees of freedom simultaneously) and time-multiplexed quasi-deterministic single-photon sources — the multiplexing concept of Section 19.1.4 in experimental form.

---

## Gregory Gol'tsman (1946–)

Gregory Gol'tsman (Moscow State Pedagogical University) led the team that invented the SNSPD (2001): a NbN nanowire that clicked on single optical photons with picosecond-scale response. What began as a hot-electron detector experiment became the enabling detector technology for essentially all frontier quantum photonics — from loophole-free Bell tests to boson sampling to deep-space optical communication.

---

## Sae Woo Nam (1965–)

Sae Woo Nam (NIST Boulder) built the world's best photon counters twice over: the near-unity-efficiency, photon-number-resolving transition-edge sensors (Lita et al., 2008) and, with Verma, Marsili, Mirin, and Shaw, the amorphous-material (WSi, MoSi) SNSPDs that first exceeded 90% and then 98% system detection efficiency. NIST detectors sit inside the loophole-free Bell tests, the Jiuzhang and Borealis boson samplers, and half the quantum optics labs on Earth.

---

## Robert Hadfield (1976–)

Robert Hadfield (Glasgow) is the field's cartographer and a leading SNSPD experimentalist: his 2009 *Nature Photonics* review defined the figure-of-merit framework by which all single-photon detectors are compared, and his group has advanced practical SNSPD systems — miniaturized cryogenics, mid-infrared detection, array readout — that moved superconducting detectors from dilution-refrigerator physics experiments toward deployable instruments.

---

## Jelena Vučković (1971–)

Jelena Vučković (Stanford) connects nanophotonic design to quantum emitters. Her group demonstrated some of the earliest photonic-crystal-cavity quantum dot sources and strong-coupling devices, co-authored the indistinguishable-photon milestone (Santori et al., 2002), and later pioneered inverse design — computational optimization of photonic structures — now applied to couplers, cavities, and emitter interfaces across the field. Her work embodies the chapter's central engineering claim: the emitter is only as good as the electromagnetic environment built around it.

---

## Wolfram Pernice (1972–) and Val Zwiller (1971–)

Wolfram Pernice (Münster/Heidelberg) demonstrated the waveguide-integrated traveling-wave SNSPD (2012) and has driven the co-integration of superconducting detectors with photonic circuits — including the PCM-photonic neuromorphic platforms of Unit 6. Val Zwiller (KTH Stockholm) advanced both halves of the chapter: quantum-dot nanowire sources and high-performance SNSPD systems, co-founding Single Quantum, one of the first commercial SNSPD suppliers. Together they represent the integration thrust — sources, detectors, and circuits on one chip — on which scalable photonic quantum hardware depends.
