# Chapter 20: Important Researchers

---

## Emanuel Knill, Raymond Laflamme (1960–), and Gerard Milburn

The authors of the 2001 *Nature* paper that founded the field. Their insight — that photodetection supplies the nonlinearity linear optics lacks — turned "linear optical quantum computing" from an oxymoron into a research program, complete with the NS gate, the nondeterministic CZ, and teleportation-based boosting of Section 20.2. Each brought deep prior standing: Knill and Laflamme had already co-authored the Knill-Laflamme conditions and early quantum error-correcting codes (the intellectual roots of Section 20.5), and Knill's 2005 analysis of realistically noisy devices set the fault-tolerance framing the whole chapter inherits. Milburn is among the most prolific theorists of quantum optics and continuous-variable systems (Chapter 21). The paper's lasting legacy is not the specific circuit — long since superseded — but the working vocabulary it bequeathed: heralding, ancilla factories, feed-forward, and teleported gates, the very terms in which every later section of this chapter is written.

---

## Robert Raussendorf and Hans Briegel

Inventors of the one-way quantum computer (2001) and the cluster states it runs on (Section 20.3). Briegel and Raussendorf's "persistent entanglement" paper defined the resource; their 2003 collaboration with Daniel Browne supplied the full measurement-pattern formalism — byproduct operators, feed-forward, universality — used in Section 20.3.2. Raussendorf went on, with Harrington and Goyal (2007), to foliate topological codes into 3D cluster states, the RHG lattice that underpins photonic fault tolerance (Section 20.5.2). Briegel also co-invented the quantum repeater, connecting this chapter to the long-distance networks of Chapter 22.

---

## Daniel Browne and Terry Rudolph

Authors of the 2005 fusion-gate paper (Section 20.3.1) that made photonic cluster states resource-efficient, and, with Kieling and Eisert, of the percolation picture of cluster growth (Section 20.3.3). Rudolph — a relentless advocate for the silicon-photonic route, and, incidentally, Erwin Schrödinger's grandson — co-founded PsiQuantum and co-authored the fusion-based-quantum-computation blueprint (Bartolucci et al., 2023) of Sections 20.3.3 and 20.5.3. Browne (UCL) remained an academic leader in measurement-based models and fault tolerance. Their loss-tolerance work with Varnava (2006) established the tree-cluster codes of Section 20.5.2.

---

## Scott Aaronson (1981–) and Alex Arkhipov

Authors of "The Computational Complexity of Linear Optics" (2011), the paper that gave photonics its clearest claim to quantum advantage (Section 20.4.1). Aaronson, a complexity theorist at UT Austin, framed boson sampling as a minimal, physically realizable task whose classical simulation would collapse the polynomial hierarchy, and articulated the permanent-of-Gaussians and anti-concentration conjectures on which the argument rests. Arkhipov, then his student at MIT, co-developed the analysis. Their work reoriented the entire experimental program of Section 20.4 and defined the validation problem that still shadows every advantage claim.

---

## Jian-Wei Pan (1970–) and Chao-Yang Lu

Leaders of the USTC group that built the Jiuzhang boson samplers (Section 20.4.3): the 76-photon 2020 demonstration and the 113-photon phase-programmable 2021 follow-up, the first photonic claims of quantum computational advantage. Pan is the central figure of Chinese quantum science — his multiphoton-entanglement and satellite-QKD work also anchors Chapter 22 — and Lu drives the group's multiphoton experiments and quantum-dot source development (Chapter 19). Their samplers turned the Aaronson-Arkhipov thought experiment into headline-grabbing hardware, and became the prime targets of the classical-simulation counterattack.

---

## Jeremy O'Brien and Mark Thompson

Pioneers of integrated linear-optical quantum computing at Bristol. O'Brien led the first experimental photonic CNOT (2003) and, with Politi and Thompson, the silica-on-silicon waveguide gates (2008) that launched integrated quantum photonics — the hardware lineage behind every chip in this chapter. Thompson drove silicon-photonic quantum circuits toward manufacturability. In 2016 the two co-founded PsiQuantum (with Rudolph and Shadbolt), betting the fault-tolerant roadmap of Section 20.5.3 on foundry-scale silicon photonics — the wager that a useful machine must be *manufactured*, not assembled. The company's subsequent partnership with GlobalFoundries and its 2025 manufacturability demonstration are the direct continuation of the integrated-photonics program these two began in Bristol in the mid-2000s.

---

## Mercedes Gimeno-Segovia and Pete Shadbolt

Authors, with Browne and Rudolph, of the 2015 "ballistic" result (Section 20.3.3) showing that three-photon GHZ states and boosted fusion percolate into a universal cluster with no feed-forward — the theoretical unlock that made a purely photonic fault-tolerant architecture credible. Both trained in the Bristol/Imperial orbit of integrated photonics (Shadbolt's thesis work on programmable photonic chips is a standard reference) and both became architects at PsiQuantum, carrying the ballistic-percolation and fusion-network ideas from *Physical Review Letters* into the company's engineering roadmap.
