# 20.4.3 Experimental Demonstrations

## From Three Photons to Quantum Advantage

The experimental history of boson sampling is a decade-long scaling race. It opened in 2013 with four independent proof-of-principle demonstrations — Broome et al. and Spring et al. in *Science*, Tillmann et al. and Crespi et al. in *Nature Photonics* — each sending three or four photons through small integrated or bulk interferometers of a handful of modes. These experiments verified the permanent-governed statistics on a scale a laptop could check; their purpose was to establish the physics, not to outrun classical computers.

## Why Photonics Reached This First

It is no accident that the earliest advantage claims were photonic rather than superconducting. Boson sampling needs no qubits, no gates, no error correction, and no memory — only bright squeezed or single-photon sources, a low-loss interferometer, and fast photon-number or threshold detectors, all room-temperature-friendly except the cryogenic SNSPDs (Chapter 19). Jiuzhang built its $100$-mode network in bulk optics fed by $25$ two-mode squeezers, holding interferometric phase stable to the nanometer; Borealis discarded the spatial network entirely for a single squeezer and fiber delay loops, so that "adding a mode" costs a time bin rather than a physical port. That hardware minimalism is precisely the point of the Aaronson-Arkhipov proposal: it targets the cheapest quantum device that is still classically hard, which is why photonics, not the gate-model platforms, produced the first credible demonstrations.

The leap to the intractable regime came from USTC. **Jiuzhang 1.0** (Zhong et al., 2020) implemented Gaussian boson sampling with $50$ squeezed-light sources interfered across a $100$-mode bulk-optical network, detecting up to $76$ photons in a single run with SNSPDs. The group estimated that reproducing its samples would take the fastest classical supercomputers of the day around $2.5$ billion years — a claimed speedup of order $10^{14}$, and the first photonic claim of quantum computational advantage. **Jiuzhang 2.0** (Zhong et al., 2021) added phase programmability and pushed to $113$ detected photons across $144$ modes. Xanadu's **Borealis** (Madsen et al., 2022) took a different, more scalable road: a single squeezer and a *time-multiplexed* loop architecture, in which pulses circulate through fiber delay lines and programmable couplers to synthesize a $216$-mode GBS instance with a fully programmable gate sequence — the first advantage-scale sampler that was reconfigurable rather than hard-wired.

## The Classical Counterattack

None of these claims stood still, because "quantum advantage" is a moving target defined against the *best* classical algorithm, and the best classical algorithm keeps improving. Three lines of attack narrowed the early margins. First, better exact-simulation algorithms exploited the fact that real devices are **lossy and imperfectly squeezed**: photon loss and mode distinguishability make the effective state lower-rank and more classically compressible than the ideal, and tensor-network and phase-space methods leveraged this to slash estimated classical runtimes by many orders of magnitude. Second came **classical spoofers** — algorithms that never simulate the true distribution at all, but reproduce the low-order marginals and correlation functions that experiments actually use for validation, thereby passing the same tests as the quantum device. Third, the very metrics improved: as validation tightened, some early speedup figures were revised sharply downward. The lasting lesson is that a single-number "speedup" is provisional; advantage is a contested boundary, and each side has repeatedly moved it.

This exposes the field's structural difficulty, the **validation problem**. Aaronson and Arkhipov's hardness is precisely the statement that one *cannot* efficiently compute the output distribution — which is also what makes it hard to *verify* that a machine samples from the right one. Experiments fall back on necessary-but-insufficient checks: confirming bosonic bunching (two-photon correlations exceeding the classical, distinguishable-particle baseline), matching low-order marginals, and cross-entropy or Bayesian tests against classical mock-ups. Passing them rules out simple classical impostors; it does not prove the device sampled the true, uncomputable distribution. What the demonstrations robustly establish is narrower than "a useful quantum computer" and broader than "nothing": a specialized photonic device produced, in seconds, samples that no known classical method reproduces in feasible time — strong evidence that quantum dynamics can outpace classical simulation, delivered without a single logical qubit or error-corrected gate.

## Worked Example: Where a $10^{14}$ Speedup Comes From

Take Jiuzhang 1.0's headline at face value and reconstruct the arithmetic. The device ran in the laboratory for a time of order $T_Q \sim 200$ s to accumulate its sample set. The quoted classical cost to reproduce that set on a leading supercomputer was

$$T_C \approx 2.5\times 10^{9}\ \text{yr} \times \big(3.15\times 10^{7}\ \text{s/yr}\big) \approx 8\times 10^{16}\ \text{s}.$$

The claimed speedup is then simply the ratio

$$\frac{T_C}{T_Q} \approx \frac{8\times 10^{16}}{2\times 10^{2}} \approx 4\times 10^{14},$$

consistent with the $\sim 10^{14}$ figure. One can see where the classical wall comes from independently: each detected pattern's probability is a Hafnian (or Torontonian) of an $N\times N$ matrix costing $\sim 2^{N/2}$ operations, and at $N = 70$ photons that is $2^{35} \approx 3\times 10^{10}$ operations *per candidate pattern* — before accounting for the many candidates a sampler must weigh. At an optimistic $10^{18}$ floating-point operations per second, a single such evaluation is $\sim 30$ ns, but the full sampling task multiplies this by astronomically many evaluations and by memory-bandwidth penalties the flop count ignores, which is how nanoseconds-per-Hafnian inflates to billions of years for the complete run. The estimate is exactly as strong as its assumptions — a fixed classical algorithm on fixed hardware — and it was precisely those assumptions that the classical counterattack later revised, which is why every such number should be read as a snapshot of a contest, not a settled verdict.
