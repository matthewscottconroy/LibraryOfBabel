# 21.1.2 — Gaussian States and Operations

## States as Means and Covariances

An $N$-mode field is described by the quadrature vector $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = (\hat{x}_1, \hat{p}_1, \ldots, \hat{x}_N, \hat{p}_N)^T$, with commutators summarized as

$$[\hat{r}_j, \hat{r}_k] = i\,\Omega_{jk}, \qquad \Omega = \bigoplus_{k=1}^{N}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

where $\Omega$ is the *symplectic form*. A state is *Gaussian* if its Wigner function — the quasi-probability distribution over phase space — is a Gaussian:

$$W(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{r}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T \sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{r}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right]}{(2\pi)^N \sqrt{\det\sigma}}$$

A Gaussian state is therefore *completely characterized* by its first moments $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \langle\hat{\mathbf{r}}\rangle$ (a $2N$-vector) and its covariance matrix

$$\sigma_{jk} = \frac{1}{2}\langle \hat{r}_j\hat{r}_k + \hat{r}_k\hat{r}_j \rangle - \langle\hat{r}_j\rangle\langle\hat{r}_k\rangle$$

(a $2N \times 2N$ real symmetric matrix). This is an enormous compression: instead of a wavefunction over $\mathbb{R}^N$, just $2N + N(2N+1)$ real numbers. The uncertainty principle becomes the matrix inequality $\sigma + \frac{i}{2}\Omega \geq 0$; for a single mode this reduces to $\det\sigma \geq 1/4$.

The states of Section 21.1.1 in this language (single mode, ordering $(x,p)$):

- **Vacuum / coherent state:** $\sigma = \frac{1}{2}I$; coherent states have $\boldsymbol{\mu} \neq 0$.
- **Squeezed vacuum:** $\sigma = \frac{1}{2}\,\text{diag}(e^{-2r}, e^{+2r})$.
- **Thermal state** ($\bar{n}$ photons): $\sigma = (\bar{n} + \frac{1}{2})I$ — the only single-mode Gaussian state that is mixed and isotropic.

## Operations as Symplectic Matrices

A *Gaussian unitary* is one generated by a Hamiltonian at most quadratic in $\hat{a}, \hat{a}^\dagger$. In the Heisenberg picture it acts affinely on the quadratures,

$$\hat{\mathbf{r}} \;\to\; S\,\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \mathbf{d}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\mu} \to S\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{d}, \qquad \sigma \to S\,\sigma\,S^T$$

where the real matrix $S$ must preserve the commutators: $S\,\Omega\,S^T = \Omega$. Such matrices form the *symplectic group* $\text{Sp}(2N, \mathbb{R})$. Gaussian operations map Gaussian states to Gaussian states. The elementary generators, with their optical hardware:

| Operation | Generator | Symplectic action | Hardware |
|---|---|---|---|
| Displacement $D(\alpha)$ | linear in $\hat{x}, \hat{p}$ | $\mathbf{d} \neq 0$, $S = I$ | modulator + strong beam |
| Phase rotation $R(\theta)$ | $\hat{n} = \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}$ | rotation matrix in $(x,p)$ | path length / phase shifter |
| Squeezing $S(r)$ | $\hat{a}^2 - \hat{a}^{\dagger 2}$ | $\text{diag}(e^{-r}, e^{+r})$ | optical parametric amplifier |
| Beam splitter $B(\tau)$ | $\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b} - \hat{a}\hat{b}^\dagger$ | orthogonal mixing of two modes | directional coupler / BS |

A beam splitter of transmissivity $\tau$ acts on two modes' quadratures as the block matrix $\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\tau}\,I & \sqrt{1-\tau}\,I \\ -\sqrt{1-\tau}\,I & \sqrt{\tau}\,I\end{pmatrix}$ — an orthogonal (and hence symplectic) rotation of phase space. The *Bloch-Messiah decomposition* states that any Gaussian unitary factors as: passive linear optics (an interferometer) → single-mode squeezers → passive linear optics. Every Gaussian circuit is thus buildable from beam splitters, phase shifters, and squeezers. *Gaussian measurements* complete the toolbox: homodyne and heterodyne detection both map Gaussian states to Gaussian post-measurement states, with outcome statistics computable from $(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \sigma)$ by Gaussian marginalization and conditioning — exactly the update rules of a Kalman filter.

**Worked example: two-mode squeezing and EPR entanglement.** Non-degenerate parametric down-conversion applies the two-mode squeezer $\hat{S}_2(r) = \exp[r(\hat{a}\hat{b} - \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}^\dagger)]$, producing (with ordering $(x_a, p_a, x_b, p_b)$):

$$\sigma = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} \cosh 2r \, I & \sinh 2r \, Z \\ \sinh 2r \, Z & \cosh 2r \, I \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z = \text{diag}(1,-1)$$

Each mode alone looks thermal (variance $\frac{1}{2}\cosh 2r$ — noisy!), but the joint quadratures are correlated: $\text{Var}(\hat{x}_a - \hat{x}_b) = \text{Var}(\hat{p}_a + \hat{p}_b) = e^{-2r}$. As $r \to \infty$ this approaches the idealized Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen state of 1935: perfectly correlated positions, perfectly anticorrelated momenta. At 10 dB of two-mode squeezing ($e^{-2r} = 0.1$), measuring $\hat{x}_a$ predicts $\hat{x}_b$ ten times better than the vacuum limit allows for independent modes. This EPR entanglement, produced deterministically, is the resource behind CV teleportation (first achieved by Furusawa et al. in 1998 [4]) and CV quantum key distribution (Chapter 22).

## CV Cluster States: Entanglement at Scale

The measurement-based computing model of Chapter 20 carries over to CV. A *CV cluster state* on a graph $G$ is (ideally) prepared by taking a zero-momentum eigenstate at every vertex and applying the entangling gate $C_Z = e^{i\hat{x}_j\hat{x}_k}$ along every edge; its stabilizer-like *nullifiers* $\hat{p}_j - \sum_{k \in N(j)}\hat{x}_k \to 0$ have variance $e^{-2r}$ for finite squeezing $r$. Computation proceeds by measuring rotated quadratures node by node; homodyne measurements enact arbitrary *Gaussian* operations, and a single non-Gaussian measurement type upgrades the model to universality (Menicucci et al., 2006 [5]).

Because squeezed states are deterministic and beam splitters suffice for the entangling step, CV cluster states scale spectacularly. Using *time-domain multiplexing* — one squeezer, one delay line, one beam splitter, with consecutive pulses of the same beam serving as distinct modes — the Furusawa group entangled more than **one million** temporal modes into a 1D cluster state in 2016 [6]. In 2019, two groups back to back generated *two-dimensional* cluster states, the topology required for universal one-way computation: $5 \times 1200$ modes (Asavanant et al. [7]) and $24 \times 12500$ modes (Larsen et al. [8]). No qubit platform has entangled more than a few hundred physical systems; the CV records are three to four orders of magnitude larger. The catch: finite squeezing means every measurement injects noise $\propto e^{-2r}$, which accumulates through the computation — 2D cluster states are a universal *substrate*, but fault tolerance requires the GKP encoding of the next subsection.

## The No-Go Theorem: Gaussian Is Classical

Here is the field's most important negative result. **If a circuit consists of (i) Gaussian input states, (ii) Gaussian unitaries and Gaussian channels, and (iii) Gaussian measurements, then its outcome statistics can be sampled efficiently on a classical computer** (Bartlett, Sanders, Braunstein & Nemoto, 2002 [9]). The proof is implicit in everything above: propagate $(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \sigma)$ through the symplectic maps — polynomial cost in $N$ — then sample the Gaussian outcome distribution. The Wigner function remains positive throughout and functions as an honest classical probability distribution being updated by classical rules. This is the precise CV analogue of the Gottesman-Knill theorem for stabilizer circuits, with the correspondence (Gaussian ↔ stabilizer, symplectic ↔ Clifford, homodyne ↔ Pauli measurement).

Consequently, quantum advantage requires breaking the Gaussian world somewhere:

- **Non-Gaussian measurement:** photon-number-resolving detection of Gaussian states — this is exactly Gaussian boson sampling (Chapter 20; Borealis, Section 21.2.1).
- **Non-Gaussian gate:** a Hamiltonian of cubic or higher order, e.g., the cubic phase gate $V(\gamma) = e^{i\gamma\hat{x}^3}$; with it, commutators generate polynomials of arbitrary order, giving universality over CV (Lloyd & Braunstein, 1999 [10]).
- **Non-Gaussian state:** inject a resource state (a GKP state, a cubic-phase state, or a photon-subtracted squeezed state) into an otherwise Gaussian circuit via teleportation-style gadgets.

Negativity of the Wigner function is the resource-theoretic marker: states and operations with nonnegative Wigner representation are classically simulable in this sense (Mari & Eisert, 2012 [11]). All three routes are actively pursued; all are hard, because non-Gaussian states are exactly the ones that decohere fastest under loss.

## Summary

- Gaussian states are fully specified by first moments $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance matrix $\sigma$; uncertainty reads $\sigma + \frac{i}{2}\Omega \geq 0$.
- Gaussian unitaries act as symplectic matrices, $\sigma \to S\sigma S^T$; Bloch-Messiah: interferometer + squeezers + interferometer.
- Two-mode squeezing yields EPR entanglement with correlation variances $e^{-2r}$ — deterministic entanglement on demand.
- CV cluster states reach millions of modes (1D) and $\sim 10^5$ modes (2D) via time multiplexing — record entangled-state sizes on any platform.
- All-Gaussian circuits are efficiently classically simulable (CV Gottesman-Knill); quantum advantage demands a non-Gaussian state, gate, or measurement.
