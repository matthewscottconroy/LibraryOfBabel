# Section 22.2: Quantum Repeaters

Classical fiber networks are possible only because of one device: the amplifier. Every ~80 km, an erbium-doped fiber amplifier absorbs a fading bit stream and re-emits it strong, and the process repeats across oceans. Section 22.1 ended at the wall this trick cannot climb: quantum signals die exponentially with distance ($10^{-20}$ transmission at 1,000 km), the PLOB bound makes the death sentence protocol-independent — and the amplifier is forbidden. The prohibition is not technological but theorematic: an unknown quantum state cannot be copied, so it cannot be regenerated, cloned, or noiselessly amplified. Four lines of linear algebra (Subsection 22.2.1) close the classical escape route.

The quantum repeater (Briegel, Dür, Cirac & Zoller, 1998) is the field's answer, and it is a fundamentally different machine from its classical namesake. It does not amplify the signal — it never touches the payload at all. Instead it changes *what is transmitted*: distribute **entanglement** over short segments where loss is survivable, *store* each segment's entanglement in a quantum memory until its neighbor segment also succeeds, then fuse adjacent segments by **entanglement swapping** — a Bell measurement at the shared node that leaves the two outer ends entangled though they never interacted. Repeat hierarchically, and end-to-end entanglement spans the continent while every photon only ever flew one segment. Because segments succeed or fail *independently* and heraldedly, the resource cost grows polynomially with distance instead of exponentially — provided the memories can hold entanglement long enough for classical heralding signals to crawl between nodes, and provided **entanglement purification** can scrub the accumulated noise.

Once Alice and Bob share a high-fidelity Bell pair, everything else follows: teleportation moves any qubit between them, QKD keys drop out of measuring the pair, and distributed quantum computing becomes a matter of bandwidth. The repeater is thus the enabling machine of the entire quantum internet — and its critical component, the quantum memory, is the least mature technology in this book.
