# Section 22.3: The Quantum Internet

Section 22.1 delivered a working application — cryptographic keys — and Section 22.2 delivered the machine that would free it from the distance limit: the repeater. The quantum internet is the vision that subsumes both. Its fundamental service is not the delivery of bits but the *delivery of entanglement*: a fresh, high-fidelity Bell pair supplied on demand between any two nodes on Earth, from which key distribution, teleportation of arbitrary qubits, and a wider catalogue of tasks follow as special cases. Kimble's 2008 "quantum internet" manifesto named the goal; the photonics of the preceding sections is the substrate; this section is the systems view that assembles components into a network.

Why reach past QKD? Because shared entanglement is a strictly more powerful primitive than a shared key. Two parties holding a Bell pair can distil a secret bit — but they can also teleport a qubit between quantum processors, so that several modest machines cooperate as one larger computer (distributed quantum computing); run *blind* computation, in which a client delegates a calculation to a remote server that learns neither the data nor the algorithm; execute secure multiparty computation and anonymous transmission; and correlate distant apparatus — atomic clocks, telescopes, interferometers — at the standard quantum limit, extending baselines and stabilities no local instrument can reach. None of these reduces to classical networking or to key distribution alone; each consumes live entanglement as a resource.

The organizing insight is that "quantum internet" names not one technology but a *ladder of capabilities*, each rung defined by what the end nodes can do with the entanglement they are handed — from merely measuring it (QKD) to storing and processing it (distributed computing). Subsection 22.3.1 climbs that ladder; 22.3.2 examines the architecture and protocol stack that must run on every rung.

## Subsections

- **22.3.1 — The Quantum Internet Roadmap**: The six-stage capability ladder of Wehner, Elkouss, and Hanson — trusted-repeater, prepare-and-measure, entanglement-distribution, quantum-memory, few-qubit fault-tolerant, and full quantum-computing networks — and the demonstrated systems that occupy each rung today.
- **22.3.2 — Network Architecture and the Protocol Stack**: Entanglement as the network resource; the physical/link/network/transport layering of Dahlberg et al.; entanglement routing and swap-based path selection; end-node platform choices; and the Delft multi-node network as the first integrated demonstrator.
