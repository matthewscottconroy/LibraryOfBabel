# Chapter 23: Important Researchers

---

## John Bowers (1954–)

John Bowers (UC Santa Barbara) is the central figure of heterogeneous III-V-on-silicon integration. His group, with Intel collaboration, demonstrated the first electrically pumped hybrid silicon evanescent laser (Fang et al., *Optics Express*, 2006), embodying the decisive insight that an *unpatterned* bonded III-V film lets the laser cavity be defined by silicon-side lithography — converting an impossible assembly-alignment problem into a routine lithography problem. The Bowers group went on to build the full heterogeneous device suite (SOAs, modulators, photodetectors, mode-locked and narrow-linewidth lasers) and became a driving force behind quantum-dot lasers grown epitaxially on silicon, the leading candidate for fully monolithic light sources. Much of the commercial silicon photonics industry's laser integration descends directly from this program.

---

## Roel Baets (1957–)

Roel Baets built the Ghent University–IMEC silicon photonics program into Europe's reference platform and, through ePIXfab and its successors, pioneered the multi-project wafer model for photonics — the access mechanism this chapter presents as the field's economic foundation. His group's contributions span the platform itself (the 193 nm lithography processes whose uniformity Selvaraja et al. quantified), grating couplers and fiber interfaces, III-V-on-silicon adhesive bonding with DVS-BCB, and, through students including Wim Bogaerts and Günther Roelkens, much of the field's design-automation and heterogeneous-integration culture.

---

## Michael Hochberg (1979–) and Tom Baehr-Jones

Hochberg and Baehr-Jones, working as a designer-duo across Luxtera, the University of Washington, Delaware, and the OpSIS multi-project-wafer service they founded, articulated and operationalized "fabless silicon photonics" (*Nature Photonics*, 2010): standardized foundry processes, shared runs, portable PDKs, and design methodology imported from electronics. OpSIS (with IME Singapore) gave a generation of academic groups their first full active-flow chips, and the textbook Hochberg co-authored with Chrostowski codified the approach for students.

---

## Lukas Chrostowski (1974–)

Lukas Chrostowski (University of British Columbia) leads the SiEPIC program, which turned photonic design education and rapid prototyping into shared community infrastructure: open e-beam-fabrication shuttle runs, the SiEPIC-EBeam PDK and KLayout-based open tools, design-for-test layout conventions (standardized grating-coupler arrays, automated probing), and the *Silicon Photonics Design* textbook with Hochberg. His group's emphasis on fabrication-variability-aware design — corner models, monitor structures, calibration methodology — directly underlies Section 23.2.3's treatment of variation as a first-class design input.

---

## Bahram Jalali (1961–)

Bahram Jalali (UCLA) did foundational silicon photonics work in the 1990s and 2000s when the field was still a contrarian bet, including early SOI waveguide technology and the demonstration of Raman amplification and lasing in silicon — the first laser action in silicon of any kind, achieved by side-stepping the indirect bandgap with a nonlinear process. His reviews (with Fathpour) helped define the platform's identity and roadmap during its formative years.

---

## Günther Roelkens (1979–)

Günther Roelkens (Ghent/IMEC) leads the III-V-on-silicon integration effort within the Ghent program: adhesive DVS-BCB bonding processes, taper designs coupling bonded III-V devices to silicon waveguides, and, more recently, micro-transfer printing of III-V (and other material) coupons onto photonic wafers — the approach that combines wafer-scale parallelism with known-good-die economics and sparse use of expensive III-V material.

---

## Peter O'Brien

Peter O'Brien leads the photonic packaging group at Tyndall National Institute (Cork), the laboratory most responsible for treating photonic packaging as a scientific discipline rather than an assembly afterthought: fiber-array attach and micro-optics, electrical and thermal co-integration, standardized packaging design kits, and European pilot-line infrastructure. The Carroll et al. review from his group is the standard reference behind this chapter's cost and process analyses of packaging.

---

## Christian Koos (1977–)

Christian Koos (Karlsruhe Institute of Technology) introduced photonic wire bonding — free-form polymer waveguides written between chips by two-photon polymerization after loose die placement — reframing optical packaging's central problem (mechanical alignment) as a lithographic post-correction. His group's hybrid multi-chip systems (silicon photonics + InP lasers + polymer interconnects) demonstrate the modular, mixed-platform integration style that many photonic computing systems are likely to adopt.
