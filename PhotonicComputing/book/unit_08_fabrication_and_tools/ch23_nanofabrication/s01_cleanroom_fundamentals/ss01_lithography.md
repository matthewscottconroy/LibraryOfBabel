# 23.1.1 Lithography: Photolithography, DUV, EUV, and Electron-Beam

## The Cleanroom and the Wafer

Nanofabrication happens in cleanrooms because the patterns are smaller than dust. Cleanrooms are classified by airborne particle count (ISO 14644-1): an ISO 5 room (the older "Class 100") permits at most ~3,500 particles ≥ 0.5 μm per cubic meter, versus hundreds of millions in ordinary indoor air. Lithography bays run at ISO 4–5; ordinary lab space around the tools may be ISO 7–8. People are the dominant particle source — hence the bunny suits.

The substrate is a single-crystal silicon wafer, grown by the **Czochralski method**: a seed crystal is dipped into molten silicon and slowly pulled and rotated, growing a boule that is then sawn and polished into wafers (200 mm and 300 mm diameters dominate). Photonics needs **silicon-on-insulator (SOI)**: a thin single-crystal device layer (typically 220 nm) atop a buried oxide (BOX, 2–3 μm) on a silicon handle wafer. Commercial SOI is made by the Smart Cut™ process: implant hydrogen into an oxidized donor wafer, bond it face-down to a handle wafer, split it along the hydrogen-damaged plane with a thermal treatment, and polish the transferred film. The device-layer thickness uniformity of this process — a few nanometers across a wafer, sub-nanometer within a die — directly sets the resonance-wavelength uniformity of every ring resonator in Chapter 7 (we quantify this in Section 23.2.3).

## Photolithography

Photolithography transfers a mask pattern into a photosensitive polymer (**photoresist**) spun onto the wafer:

1. **Coat**: spin resist to a controlled thickness (tens of nm to a few μm); soft-bake.
2. **Expose**: project the mask image onto the resist. Modern tools are *steppers/scanners* that image one reticle field (~26 × 33 mm) at a time, stepping across the wafer, with the mask pattern typically at 4× scale.
3. **Develop**: dissolve the exposed (positive resist) or unexposed (negative resist) regions, typically in aqueous TMAH. Deep-UV resists are *chemically amplified*: a photo-generated acid catalyzes deprotection during a post-exposure bake, multiplying sensitivity.

The resolution of projection lithography is governed by the **Rayleigh criterion**:

$$CD = k_1 \frac{\lambda}{NA}$$

where $CD$ is the minimum printable feature ("critical dimension"), $\lambda$ the exposure wavelength, $NA$ the numerical aperture of the projection optics, and $k_1$ a process factor: $k_1 \approx 0.6\text{–}0.8$ for a simple process, pushed to $k_1 \approx 0.25\text{–}0.4$ with resolution-enhancement tricks (off-axis illumination, phase-shift masks, optical proximity correction). A companion Rayleigh relation gives the **depth of focus**, $DOF = k_2 \lambda / NA^2$ — the price of high NA is a focus budget of only a few hundred nanometers, which is why wafers must be planarized (Section 23.1.3).

**Worked example.** A 193 nm ("DUV") scanner with dry optics, $NA = 0.75$ and $k_1 = 0.4$:

$$CD = 0.4 \times \frac{193\ \text{nm}}{0.75} \approx 103\ \text{nm}$$

With water-immersion optics ($NA = 1.35$, since the water raises the effective aperture) and $k_1 = 0.3$: $CD \approx 43$ nm. These two numbers bracket what silicon photonics uses today. A 450 nm waveguide is easy; a 100 nm directional-coupler gap is near the edge for dry 193 nm and comfortable for immersion.

The generations relevant to photonics: **i-line** (365 nm, mercury lamp; MEMS and legacy processes), **KrF DUV** (248 nm; older photonic processes), **ArF DUV** (193 nm dry and immersion; the workhorse of every major photonics foundry — IMEC, AIM Photonics, AMF, GlobalFoundries), and **EUV** (13.5 nm).

## Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV)

EUV lithography exposes at $\lambda = 13.5$ nm, generated by firing a high-power CO₂ laser at falling molten tin droplets to create a plasma. At this wavelength *everything* absorbs, so the entire optical train — collector, illuminator, projection optics, and even the mask — must be reflective, built from Mo/Si multilayer Bragg mirrors (~70% reflectivity each; after ~10 reflections only a few percent of the source power reaches the wafer), and the whole system operates in vacuum. With $NA = 0.33$ and $k_1 \approx 0.3$–0.4, single-exposure EUV prints ~13–16 nm features; "high-NA" ($NA = 0.55$) tools push below 10 nm [Wagner & Harned, *Nature Photonics*, 2010].

Photonic devices, with 100–500 nm features, do not need EUV, and no photonic foundry uses it today — an EUV scanner costs well over $100M and its throughput is precious. EUV matters to this book for a different reason: monolithic electronic-photonic integration. If photonics is to live on the same die as sub-7 nm logic (the direction pointed by co-packaged optics and by monolithic demonstrations like Atabaki et al., *Nature*, 2018), the photonic layers must coexist with a process flow built around EUV patterning of the transistors.

## Electron-Beam Lithography

E-beam lithography dispenses with masks entirely: a focused electron beam (typically 100 keV, spot size of a few nm) writes the pattern directly into an electron-sensitive resist (PMMA or ZEP, positive; HSQ, negative), shot by shot. Because electron wavelengths at 100 keV are picometers, resolution is limited not by diffraction but by electron scattering in the resist and substrate: practical dense-feature resolution is ~5–10 nm.

E-beam's virtues: resolution, and *zero mask cost* — every write can be a new design, which makes it the tool of research prototyping and of photonic-crystal and inverse-designed devices with deep-subwavelength features. Its vices:

- **Throughput.** Writing is serial. The write time for area $A$ at areal dose $D$ with beam current $I$ is $t = DA/I$. A 5 × 5 mm photonic layer with 30% pattern density in ZEP ($D \approx 250$ μC/cm²) at $I = 10$ nA takes $t = 250\,\mu\text{C/cm}^2 \times 0.075\ \text{cm}^2 / 10\ \text{nA} \approx 1900$ s — half an hour. High-resolution HSQ at ~1000+ μC/cm² and lower current stretches this to many hours; a full wafer is out of the question. (Chip-scale e-beam services such as Applied Nanotools' SOI process turn designs around in weeks rather than months precisely because of this masklessness.)
- **Proximity effect.** Electrons backscatter from the substrate over tens of micrometers, pre-exposing neighboring features; doses must be corrected computationally (PEC).
- **Field stitching.** The beam deflects accurately only over a write field (~100–1000 μm); the stage then moves and fields are stitched with 10–30 nm placement errors. A waveguide crossing a field boundary acquires a small offset — a scattering point and phase defect. Layouts for e-beam deliberately route waveguides to minimize boundary crossings, or use multi-pass writing to average the error.

E-beam also writes the *photomasks* used by optical lithography — so every process is ultimately e-beam-patterned at one remove.

## Choosing a Lithography, as a Photonics Designer

| | i-line / KrF | ArF DUV (dry/immersion) | EUV | E-beam |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Resolution (dense) | 350 / 150 nm | ~100 / ~40 nm | < 16 nm | 5–10 nm |
| Throughput | >100 wafers/hr | >100 wafers/hr | ~100+ wafers/hr | ~1 chip/hr–day |
| Mask cost per layer | $ | $$–$$$ | $$$$ | none |
| Photonics role | legacy, thick features | **foundry standard** | future monolithic EPIC | research, rapid prototyping |

Two lithography properties propagate directly into photonic performance. **Line-edge roughness (LER)** of the resist — a few nm RMS — transfers into waveguide-sidewall roughness and thence into scattering loss (Section 23.1.2). And **CD uniformity** — how consistently a drawn 500 nm line prints as 500 nm across a die, a wafer, and a lot — becomes the effective-index and resonance-wavelength statistics that Section 23.2.3 turns into tuning-power budgets. When a foundry advertises "±5 nm CD control," that sentence, more than any optics, determines how many rings your photonic tensor core can hold without recalibration.
