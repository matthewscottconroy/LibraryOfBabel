# Section 25.1: The Electronic-Photonic Interface

Ask a newcomer where the power goes in a photonic accelerator and they will point at the laser. Ask a practitioner and they will point at everything else.

A photonic matrix core computes with signals that are analog, optical, and fast. The world that feeds it computes with signals that are digital, electronic, and — by photonic standards — slow. Between the two sits a conversion layer that appears on no architecture diagram's glamour shot but dominates every honest power budget: digital-to-analog converters that turn stored weights and activations into drive voltages; modulator drivers that swing those voltages across real capacitances; transimpedance amplifiers that turn microamp photocurrents back into volts; analog-to-digital converters that re-digitize the result; and the clock distribution network that keeps thousands of gigahertz-rate converters phase-aligned. Each element has a physics-limited energy floor and a precision ceiling, and together they set the terms on which any photonic core must compete.

This section develops the interface in two passes.

**Subsection 25.1.1 — DACs, ADCs, and TIAs: the conversion tax.** We quantify each interface component: the energy per conversion of high-speed data converters (from the Walden and Schreier figures of merit and Murmann's long-running ADC survey), the aperture-jitter limit that caps the resolution of any converter running at tens of gigasamples per second, the noise and power of transimpedance amplification, and the often-forgotten cost of clocking. The output of the subsection is a table of realistic per-operation energies — the numbers that will be reused throughout Sections 25.2 and 25.3 — and a first statement of the amortization principle: interface energy scales with the *edge* of a photonic array ($O(N)$ converters) while computation scales with its *area* ($O(N^2)$ MACs), so the tax per MAC falls as $1/N$.

**Subsection 25.1.2 — Hybrid integration and co-design methodology.** Physics sets the energy of a conversion; packaging sets how many conversions you need and how much parasitic capacitance each one drives. We compare the three ways of marrying an electronic die to a photonic one — 2.5D side-by-side on an interposer, 3D stacking with microbumps or hybrid bonds, and monolithic fabrication in a single process — and quantify why the parasitics of each (nanohenries of wirebond, tens of femtofarads of microbump, femtofarads of monolithic via) translate directly into picojoules or femtojoules per conversion. We then step up one level of abstraction to the co-design methodology itself: start from the algorithm, partition the computation by physical comparative advantage (photonics for linear algebra and transport; electronics for nonlinearity, memory, and control), and iterate across the device/circuit/architecture/algorithm stack. The worked example is the canonical one: a neural-network layer realized as photonic matrix-vector multiply, electronic accumulation, and digital activation.

A warning about mindset before we begin. It is tempting to treat the interface as a temporary nuisance — something that better photonics will eventually abolish. It will not. As long as the systems that generate data (memories, networks, sensors with digital outputs) and the systems that consume results (CPUs, storage, humans) remain electronic and digital, every analog optical computer is bounded by conversions at its edges. The interface can be amortized, integrated, and co-designed; it cannot be wished away. The entire economic case for photonic computing lives or dies in this layer, which is why this chapter puts it first — before the metrics, before the benchmarks, and before any discussion of where photonics wins.
