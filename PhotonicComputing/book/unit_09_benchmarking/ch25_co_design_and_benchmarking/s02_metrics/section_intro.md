# Section 25.2: Metrics and Benchmarking

A field earns the right to be called engineering when its claims become checkable. This section builds the checking apparatus for photonic computing.

The difficulty is not a shortage of numbers — the literature overflows with them — but a shortage of *commensurable* numbers. One paper reports the energy of its photonic core alone; another includes lasers but not converters; a third quotes operations at 4-bit precision against a GPU's 16-bit figure; a fourth reports peak throughput that no workload can sustain and omits the accuracy its analog noise actually delivers. None of these papers is necessarily dishonest. Each has simply drawn its measurement boundary in a different place, and the reader who does not track boundaries will come away believing whatever the abstract wished.

The section proceeds in three subsections, from definitions to comparisons.

**Subsection 25.2.1 — Throughput and energy efficiency.** Defines the standard metrics — MACs and OPs (and the factor of two between them), GMAC/s and TOPS, peak versus delivered throughput, latency versus throughput, energy per MAC and TOPS/W, and reconfiguration rate. The centerpiece is a complete worked example: an end-to-end wall-plug energy budget for a 64×64 photonic matrix engine at 10 GHz, itemizing laser (through its wall-plug efficiency), modulators, conversion chain, static weight-holding power, and control. The result — tens of femtojoules to a picojoule per MAC depending on assumptions, with the photonic core itself a minor line item — is the single most instructive calculation in the chapter.

**Subsection 25.2.2 — Precision and SNR.** Analog computing does not have a bit width; it has a signal-to-noise ratio. We convert between the two (ENOB), inventory the noise sources — shot noise and its photon-budget consequence $N_{\text{ph}} \gtrsim 2^{2b}$ per detected symbol, laser RIN, TIA thermal noise, crosstalk, phase drift, and component-error accumulation in large meshes — and connect hardware SNR to what actually matters: task accuracy, and the noise-aware training that partially recovers it.

**Subsection 25.2.3 — Fair comparison with GPUs and TPUs.** Establishes the electronic reference points (A100, H100, TPU generations, edge NPUs, analog-electronic in-memory accelerators), presents the MLPerf-style methodology of accuracy-constrained system benchmarking, and then does the field's least glamorous and most necessary work: a catalog of benchmarking pitfalls, an audit checklist, and case-study readings of prominent published photonic-accelerator claims — what each headline number means, what sits outside its boundary, and what a skeptical buyer should ask next.

One orienting principle governs all three subsections. A benchmark is an *experiment on a claim*, and like any experiment it needs controlled variables: same task, same accuracy target, same measurement boundary (the wall plug), same operation-counting convention. Where the photonic literature meets that standard, its best results are genuinely impressive. Where it does not, this section provides the tools to say precisely what is missing — which is a more useful skill than either cheerleading or dismissal.
