# Section 25.3: Where Photonics Wins — and Where It Doesn't

After two sections of taxes and audits, it is fair to ask what remains. The answer is: a real territory, with defensible borders — smaller than the press releases claim, larger than the skeptics allow, and mapped precisely by the physics developed in this book.

The map follows from three facts established in Sections 25.1 and 25.2. First, the conversion tax is paid at the *edges* of an optical computation, so photonics favors workloads that maximize optical work per edge crossing: large dense linear operations, long-resident weights, and — best of all — signals that enter or leave the system as light and never pay the tax at all. Second, the analog noise floor caps effective precision at roughly 4–8 bits, so photonics favors workloads that tolerate or are trained for low precision. Third, an optical path computes in transit-time — tens of picoseconds, with no instruction fetch, no scheduling, no memory stalls — so photonics is strongest where latency, not just throughput, carries economic weight.

**Subsection 25.3.1 — Fixed-weight inference at low latency.** The strongest case: a static matrix held in the optical fabric (ideally in non-volatile elements), streaming inputs, batch-size-1 latency measured in nanoseconds end-to-end. We work the latency budget, examine the demonstrations, and identify the applications — real-time control, RF/radar pipelines, financial and scientific streaming inference — where constant-time optics beats scheduled electronics.

**Subsection 25.3.2 — Bandwidth-limited and optics-native tasks.** Where WDM parallelism multiplies one physical structure into dozens of parallel processors, and where the signal is already optical or too fast for electronics to digest — front-ends for sensing, LIDAR and radar, communications, and analog preprocessing that compresses a signal *before* the ADC so that a cheaper converter suffices. Here photonics does not compete with the ADC; it rescues it. The already-won market of optical interconnect is the anchor case.

**Subsection 25.3.3 — Where electronics wins (today).** The complement, stated without flinching: guaranteed precision and error-corrected arithmetic; cheap, abundant nonlinearity; memory — the absence of any photonic SRAM/DRAM equivalent is arguably the field's single deepest limitation; integration density four to five orders of magnitude beyond photonics; reconfiguration; general-purpose control; and a manufacturing ecosystem refined by fifty years and trillions of dollars. The conclusion is not defeat but role definition: photonics as a *domain accelerator and interconnect fabric* inside electronic systems, not a replacement for them.

Read this section as the payoff of the chapter's discipline: with honest accounting in hand, "where does photonics win?" stops being a matter of enthusiasm and becomes a calculation — one you will be equipped to redo yourself as the underlying numbers move.
