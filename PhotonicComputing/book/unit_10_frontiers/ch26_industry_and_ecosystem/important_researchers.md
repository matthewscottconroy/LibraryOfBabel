# Chapter 26: Important Researchers

*The people profiled here are chosen for their roles in building the industry and ecosystem — founders, platform-makers, and system architects — complementing the device and theory researchers profiled in earlier chapters.*

---

## Nicholas Harris

Nicholas Harris did his PhD in Dirk Englund's group at MIT, building the programmable nanophotonic processors that underlie both quantum photonic simulation [Harris et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2017] and the coherent optical neural network [Shen et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2017]. In 2017 he co-founded Lightmatter with Darius Bunandar and Thomas Graham and led it as CEO through the photonic tensor-core era (Envise) and the strategic turn to the Passage photonic interposer — the sector's defining compute-to-interconnect pivot. Harris exemplifies the modern founder-physicist: the company's roadmap is legible as a sequence of decisions about which parts of his thesis physics could be sold, and in what order.

---

## Yichen Shen

Yichen Shen was the first author, from Marin Soljačić's MIT group, of the 2017 coherent nanophotonic neural network paper and founded Lightelligence the same year. Under his leadership the company produced PACE (2021) — the 64×64-scale photonic-electronic computing engine demonstrated on recurrent Ising workloads — and then the Hummingbird optical network-on-chip, tracing independently the same compute-to-interconnect arc as its sibling startup. Shen's career is a reminder that a single landmark paper can seed multiple, competing interpretations of its own commercial meaning.

---

## Jeremy O'Brien and Terry Rudolph

Jeremy O'Brien (formerly Bristol, where he built one of the world's leading integrated quantum photonics groups) and Terry Rudolph (formerly Imperial College, a theorist of linear-optical quantum computing) co-founded PsiQuantum in 2016 with Pete Shadbolt and Mark Thompson. O'Brien, as CEO, set the company's two refusals — no NISQ machines, no laboratory fabrication — and Rudolph articulated the intellectual case publicly [Rudolph, *APL Photonics*, 2017]. Together they converted the academic lineage of Chapters 19–22 into the largest-capitalized bet in photonic quantum computing, including its 300 mm foundry partnership with GlobalFoundries and the 2024 government-backed utility-scale projects in Brisbane and Chicago.

---

## Christian Weedbrook

Christian Weedbrook, a continuous-variable quantum information theorist by training, founded Xanadu in Toronto in 2016. The company under his leadership produced the X8 programmable squeezed-light chip [Arrazola et al., *Nature*, 2021], the Borealis GBS advantage experiment [Madsen et al., *Nature*, 2022], the Aurora modular networked prototype [Aghaee Rad et al., *Nature*, 2025], and — strategically as important — PennyLane, one of the most widely adopted open-source quantum software frameworks. Weedbrook's dual hardware-software strategy gave the CV route both an experimental record and a community.

---

## Pascale Senellart

Pascale Senellart (CNRS, C2N Paris-Saclay) is the leading figure in deterministic solid-state single-photon sources: her group's quantum-dot-in-micropillar devices achieved near-optimal brightness and indistinguishability [Somaschi et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2016]. In 2017 she co-founded Quandela, which grew from selling sources into a full-stack photonic quantum computing company (Perceval software, Ascella-class cloud processors [Maring et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2024]). Senellart demonstrates the "component wedge" route from laboratory physics to company: own the hardest part first.

---

## The Ayar Labs Founding Lineage: Vladimir Stojanović, Rajeev Ram, Miloš Popović, Mark Wade, Chen Sun

Ayar Labs (2015) productized a decade of academic work on "zero-change" electronic-photonic integration — building photonics in unmodified CMOS logic processes — carried out between MIT, Berkeley, and CU Boulder by Stojanović, Ram, and Popović and their students, including Mark Wade and Chen Sun, who became company founders and executives. The lineage's landmark results — the first light-communicating microprocessor [Sun et al., *Nature*, 2015] and monolithic 45 nm photonics [Atabaki et al., *Nature*, 2018] — became TeraPHY, the in-package optical I/O chiplet [Wade et al., *IEEE Micro*, 2020]. This is the cleanest existing example of a university research program becoming a product category (optical I/O) without changing its technical thesis.

---

## Keren Bergman

Keren Bergman (Columbia University) is the principal academic architect of optically interconnected computing systems: photonic networks-on-chip, silicon photonic links for high-performance computing, and the energy-bandwidth analyses that underpin the interconnect-first thesis [Cheng et al., *Optica*, 2018]. Long before the industry's pivot, her group's system-level work — spanning device, network, and architecture — argued that photonics' first decisive role inside computers would be communication. The mid-2020s product landscape is, in effect, the Bergman research agenda commercialized.

---

## Michael Hochberg

Michael Hochberg co-built the institutional plumbing this chapter depends on: he co-founded Luxtera-lineage silicon photonics efforts, led foundry-accessible photonics at multiple companies (including Elenion), and co-authored, with Lukas Chrostowski, *Silicon Photonics Design* — the textbook that standardized PDK-based photonic design for a generation of students. His career is a reminder that ecosystems are built deliberately: MPW access, design kits, and design education are inventions with inventors.

---

## Yoshihisa Yamamoto

Yoshihisa Yamamoto (Stanford emeritus; director of NTT Research's PHI Lab) connects this chapter to the next: architect of the coherent Ising machine program (Section 27.1) and of the NTT-Stanford collaboration that scaled it to 100,000 spins, he embodies the industrial basic-research model — corporate laboratories pursuing decade-horizon physics — that produced much of the frontier surveyed in Chapter 27.
