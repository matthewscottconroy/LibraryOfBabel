# Chapter 27: Important Researchers

*The people profiled here are the scientists of the frontier — the physicists and engineers whose laboratory results define the directions this chapter surveys — complementing the founders and system-builders profiled in Chapter 26. Each is placed by a single representative result drawn from this chapter's reference list; none of these lists is complete, and many of these researchers appear in one another's citations. The coherent Ising machine program, in particular, grew from Yoshihisa Yamamoto's group (profiled in Chapter 26), whose students recur below.*

---

## Peter L. McMahon

Peter McMahon (Cornell), trained in Yamamoto's Stanford group, co-built the fully programmable 100-spin coherent Ising machine with all-to-all measurement-feedback coupling [McMahon et al., *Science*, 2016] — the demonstration that turned the CIM from a fixed-topology curiosity into a reprogrammable device. He is equally important as the field's most rigorous critic: his review "The physics of optical computing" [McMahon, *Nature Reviews Physics*, 2023] supplies much of this chapter's evaluative discipline, and he co-authored the standard Ising-machine survey [Mohseni, McMahon & Byrnes, *Nature Reviews Physics*, 2022] and the sub-photon-per-multiplication optical network [Wang et al., *Nature Communications*, 2022]. He exemplifies the frontier scientist who both builds the machine and writes its honest audit.

---

## Alireza Marandi

Alireza Marandi (Caltech), as a student in Yamamoto's group, was first author of the time-multiplexed optical-parametric-oscillator network that realized a coherent Ising machine [Marandi et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2014] — the experiment that launched the measurement-feedback CIM line and established the OPO pulse as a clean binary spin. His independent program in ultrafast nonlinear photonics, integrated OPOs, and on-chip frequency combs keeps a foot in both the Ising and comb frontiers of this chapter, and illustrates how a single thesis experiment can seed a decade of a research group's identity.

---

## Tobias J. Kippenberg

Tobias Kippenberg (EPFL) is the central figure in the physics of the dissipative Kerr soliton. His group established the formation of temporal solitons in optical microresonators [Herr et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2014] and co-authored the definitive account that named and organized the phenomenon [Kippenberg et al., *Science*, 2018], converting the microcomb from a spectroscopic curiosity into an engineered, reproducible light source. The soliton microcomb is the enabling component behind the wavelength-parallel processors of Section 27.2, and much of what a system designer can assume about comb stability traces to this body of work.

---

## Michal Lipson and Alexander L. Gaeta

Michal Lipson and Alexander Gaeta (both Columbia, following long careers at Cornell) brought the optical frequency comb onto a chip. Lipson's engineering of ultra-low-loss integrated silicon-nitride resonators and Gaeta's nonlinear optics of four-wave mixing together made the integrated microcomb a manufacturable platform rather than a tabletop experiment; their joint review with Kippenberg is the standard map of the photonic-chip-based comb field [Gaeta, Lipson & Kippenberg, *Nature Photonics*, 2019]. Their partnership is a model of the device-plus-physics collaboration that integrated photonics repeatedly rewards.

---

## Shanhui Fan

Shanhui Fan (Stanford) is the theorist whose work defined the synthetic-dimension program: the idea that a resonator's frequency modes, coupled by modulation, can serve as the sites of an engineered lattice [Yuan et al., *Optica*, 2018], realized experimentally in a single cavity carrying two independent synthetic dimensions [Dutt et al., *Science*, 2020]. His broader contributions span nonreciprocal and topological photonics and the fundamental limits of light-matter interaction. The synthetic-dimension framework is what reframes the frequency comb of Section 27.2 from a bank of parallel channels into a programmable many-body lattice.

---

## Marin Soljačić

Marin Soljačić (MIT), with John Joannopoulos, demonstrated the first photonic analog of the quantum Hall effect — unidirectional, backscattering-immune electromagnetic edge states in a magneto-optic photonic crystal [Wang et al., *Nature*, 2009] — and co-authored the founding review that organized the field [Lu, Joannopoulos & Soljačić, *Nature Photonics*, 2014]. His group's later coherent nanophotonic neural network seeded the photonic-AI companies of Chapter 26, so that Soljačić personally links the topological-physics frontier of this chapter to the industry of the last — a reminder that the two are one research community.

---

## Mordechai Segev and Mercedeh Khajavikhan

Mordechai Segev (Technion) and Mercedeh Khajavikhan (then at CREOL, University of Central Florida) carried topological ideas into all-dielectric and active photonics. Segev's group demonstrated photonic Floquet topological insulators in arrays of helically curved waveguides [Rechtsman et al., *Nature*, 2013], bringing protected edge transport to platforms with no magnetic field; together with Khajavikhan and Miguel Bandres they then realized the topological insulator laser, in which a topologically protected edge mode enforces robust single-mode lasing [Bandres et al., *Science*, 2018]. Their collaboration is the clearest case in this chapter of a topological principle translating into a concrete device advantage.

---

## Aydogan Ozcan

Aydogan Ozcan (UCLA) originated the diffractive deep neural network (D²NN): a cascade of trained, 3D-fabricated diffractive layers that performs all-optical inference at the speed of light propagation, consuming no power beyond the illumination itself [Lin et al., *Science*, 2018]. The D²NN is the canonical fixed-function free-space computer of Section 27.3 and the reference point for the fixed-versus-reconfigurable trade — a design whose radical virtue (zero reconfiguration cost) is inseparable from its radical limitation (one network per fabrication).

---

## Sylvain Gigan

Sylvain Gigan (Sorbonne Université and the Laboratoire Kastler Brossel, Paris) is a leader in imaging and computing *through* complex, multiply scattering media. His group turned a scattering medium into a large-scale optical reservoir capable of predicting spatiotemporal chaotic dynamics [Rafayelyan et al., *Physical Review X*, 2020], demonstrating that optical "disorder" is not noise to be fought but a computational resource — a high-dimensional random projection available for free. This is the state of the art in scattering-based free-space reservoir computing and a touchstone for the cross-cutting reservoir frontier.

---

## Andrea C. Ferrari

Andrea Ferrari (Cambridge) is a central figure in graphene photonics and optoelectronics. His review with Bonaccorso set the research agenda for the field a decade before it matured [Bonaccorso et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2010], and he co-authored the roadmap for graphene-based integrated photonics in datacom and telecom that frames the integration model of Section 27.5.1 [Romagnoli et al., *Nature Reviews Materials*, 2018]. His work articulates the 2D-materials case as it actually stands — a case about interface devices and manufacturability, argued in the language of both device physics and process engineering.

---

## David J. Moss

David Moss (Swinburne University of Technology), with Arnan Mitchell and their collaborators, demonstrated a microcomb-driven photonic convolutional accelerator reaching many tera-operations per second by using the soliton comb's lines as parallel WDM channels feeding a time-multiplexed matrix engine [Xu et al., *Nature*, 2021] — a companion, on the microcomb side, to the phase-change photonic tensor core [Feldmann et al., *Nature*, 2021]. His work is the concrete embodiment of the comb-as-laser-bank thesis of Section 27.2, and a useful specimen for the end-to-end energy audits this chapter's exercises demand.
