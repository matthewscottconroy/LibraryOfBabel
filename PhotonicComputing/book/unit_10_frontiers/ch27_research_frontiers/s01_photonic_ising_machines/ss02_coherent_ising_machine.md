# 27.1.2 The Coherent Ising Machine

The coherent Ising machine (CIM) is the most developed photonic optimizer and the cleanest realization of the generic recipe of Section 27.1.1. Its spin is the binary phase of a degenerate optical parametric oscillator; its annealing schedule is a pump ramp; its coupling fabric is — in every large-scale demonstration to date — a measurement-and-feedback loop that is frankly electronic. That last fact is the honest crux of the section, and we build toward it deliberately.

## The OPO as a Spin

A degenerate optical parametric oscillator (OPO) is pumped at frequency $2\omega$ and radiates at the subharmonic $\omega$. Phase-sensitive parametric gain distinguishes exactly two signal phases, $0$ and $\pi$. Below the oscillation threshold the cavity holds squeezed vacuum with no definite phase; as the pump crosses threshold the field snaps into one of the two phase states, chosen by noise, and thereafter is *phase-bistable*. Identify $0 \leftrightarrow \sigma_i = +1$ and $\pi \leftrightarrow \sigma_i = -1$ and the OPO is a spin whose "up/down" is written in optical phase rather than amplitude — a robust encoding, since the two states are exchanged by the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ symmetry the parametric process itself respects. The proposal to build an Ising machine from a *network* of such oscillators is due to Wang and co-workers [Wang et al., *Physical Review A*, 2013], with the operating principle later framed as an optical neural network running at the quantum-noise limit [Yamamoto et al., *npj Quantum Information*, 2017].

The computational content lives in *how the network selects a global phase pattern*. If many OPOs share a pump and are coupled, the collective mode that reaches threshold first is the one with the largest net parametric gain — equivalently, the smallest loss — and for suitably engineered couplings that mode's phase pattern is the one minimizing the Ising energy. The machine is thus a *minimum-gain* selector: physics finds the low-loss configuration, and we arrange for low loss to mean low $H$.

## Time-Multiplexing: One Ring, Many Spins

Building $N$ physical resonators and wiring $N^2$ couplings does not scale. The enabling trick is *time-multiplexing*: a single long fiber-ring cavity containing a nonlinear crystal (periodically poled lithium niobate) is pumped by a train of pulses, so that each circulating pulse in its own time slot is an independent OPO [Marandi et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2014]. A ring supporting thousands of slots is thereby a network of thousands of nominally identical spins sharing one gain medium, one pump, and one cavity — a decisive simplification, at the cost of turning all spin–spin coupling into an operation that must act *between time slots on successive round trips*.

## Measurement Feedback: Where the Coupling Actually Happens

Marandi's early networks coupled pulses with optical delay lines, which fixes the connectivity topology in hardware. Arbitrary, all-to-all $J_{ij}$ demanded a different idea, realized independently in 2016: *measurement feedback* (MFB) [McMahon et al., *Science*, 2016; Inagaki et al., *Science*, 2016]. On each round trip a weak tap sends every pulse to a balanced homodyne detector, which reads out the in-phase amplitude $c_i$ — a soft, signed spin value. Those measurements are digitized and streamed to an FPGA, which computes, for every slot $i$, the feedback term

$$f_i = \sum_j J_{ij}\, c_j,$$

converts it back to analog, and imprints it on a fresh pump-synchronous pulse injected into slot $i$ through an amplitude/phase modulator. Each spin is thereby pushed by the field the rest of the network *would* have exerted, with an interaction matrix $J_{ij}$ that is a number in the FPGA's memory and can be reprogrammed at will — including the dense, all-to-all, signed couplings that no fixed optical delay network could supply.

This is the honest subtlety the section exists to make. The celebrated "optical" Ising machine performs its defining operation — the matrix–vector product $J\mathbf{c}$ that constitutes the coupling — *in electronics*, not in light. The optics contributes excellent bistable spins and a low-noise, naturally parallel gain medium; the computation of the interactions is a digital multiply-accumulate on an FPGA, wrapped in analog-to-digital and digital-to-analog conversion. Watch the interfaces: the energy and latency budget of an MFB-CIM is dominated by homodyne detection, data conversion, and the FPGA's $O(N^2)$ accumulation per round trip, not by the parametric gain that gives the machine its name. We take up the consequences in Section 27.1.3; here the point is architectural honesty — the MFB-CIM is a hybrid opto-electronic system, not an all-optical solver.

## The Annealing Schedule and the Scaling Record

Annealing is a pump ramp. Starting below threshold, the OPO amplitudes are small and continuous — *soft spins* that explore a smooth landscape in which many configurations coexist. As the pump is raised through threshold the amplitudes grow and the bistability sharpens; the soft-spin cost the network minimizes deforms continuously into the discrete Ising energy, and the phase pattern freezes. Ramping slowly is the optical analogue of lowering temperature slowly in simulated annealing, giving the system time to settle into a deep minimum rather than the nearest one.

The demonstrated scale is genuinely striking. The 2016 MFB machines reached $N = 100$ with full all-to-all connectivity [McMahon et al., *Science*, 2016] and $N = 2000$ nodes [Inagaki et al., *Science*, 2016]. By exploiting the enormous slot count a fast fiber ring can hold, later work reached a $100{,}000$-spin CIM [Honjo et al., *Science Advances*, 2021] — a spin count no superconducting quantum annealer approaches. Whether that spin count translates into a *computational* advantage is a separate question, deferred to 27.1.3; as a demonstration of scalable analog optical dynamics it stands impressively on its own terms.

## Opto-Electronic and Spatial-Photonic Variants

Once one accepts an electronic feedback loop, the OPO is no longer sacred, and a family of simpler machines follows. The *opto-electronic oscillator* CIM replaces the parametric process with an electro-optically modulated light field whose nonlinearity and bistability are engineered in the feedback electronics — "a poor man's coherent Ising machine" that reproduces much of the CIM's behavior with off-the-shelf telecom components and no phase-sensitive gain medium [Böhm et al., *Nature Communications*, 2019]. It makes explicit what the MFB-CIM implies: the essential ingredients are bistable nodes and a programmable coupling loop, and those can be assembled without an OPO at all.

A different lineage spatializes rather than time-multiplexes. The *spatial-photonic Ising machine* encodes each spin in the phase of a pixel on a spatial light modulator (SLM); a single free-space propagation and a lens compute an interference pattern whose measured intensity plays the role of the Ising energy, and feedback updates the SLM phases toward lower energy [Pierangeli et al., *Physical Review Letters*, 2019]. Its appeal is massive parallelism — an SLM offers $10^5$–$10^6$ pixels — but its native coupling is fixed by optical propagation (effectively a rank-constrained, Mattis-type $J_{ij} = \xi_i \xi_j$ interaction), so encoding arbitrary dense couplings requires additional structure. It is the Ising-machine member of the free-space computing family developed in Section 27.3.

Across all these variants the same failure mode recurs and has a common cure. Analog spins do not have equal amplitudes; an oscillator that happens to grow large can dominate the dynamics and pin the system in a spurious minimum. *Amplitude-heterogeneity correction* — introducing auxiliary error variables that dynamically rescale the effective couplings to suppress amplitude inequality — measurably destabilizes local minima and improves solution quality [Leleu et al., *Physical Review Letters*, 2019]. That an *algorithmic* correction, implemented in the feedback, is what most improves the machine is itself a preview of 27.1.3's theme: in these systems the optics supplies the substrate, but the feedback loop increasingly supplies the intelligence.
