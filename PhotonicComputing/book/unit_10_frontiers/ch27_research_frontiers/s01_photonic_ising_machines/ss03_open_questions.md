# 27.1.3 Open Questions: Benchmarks, Bottlenecks, and What Would Settle Them

Section 27.1.2 built a machine that is beautiful physics and a real engineering achievement. This section asks the only question that ultimately matters for *computing*: does it beat a competent classical solver on the same instances? The honest answer, as of roughly 2025, is that the case is unproven — and the discipline of asking the question properly is worth more than any single reported speedup.

## The Benchmark Question

The temptation is to report that a CIM found good MaxCut solutions in microseconds and stop there. The Chapter 25 disciplines forbid it. A photonic Ising machine is interesting only relative to *the best classical heuristic on the same problem instances*, measured on metrics that account for everything.

Two comparisons anchor the literature. First, CIM versus quantum annealer: a careful head-to-head against a D-Wave machine found the two technologies to have *complementary* strengths — the MFB-CIM performed better on dense graphs with all-to-all couplings, while the annealer did well on sparse graphs matching its native hardware topology — with no decisive, across-the-board winner [Hamerly et al., *Science Advances*, 2019]. Neither, notably, ran away from strong classical software. Second, and more sobering, is the classical baseline that the CIM's own physics inspired. Toshiba's *simulated bifurcation* machine takes the classical mechanical analogue of a network of parametric oscillators — a system of nonlinear Hamiltonian equations undergoing adiabatic bifurcation — and simply *integrates those equations numerically*, in massively parallel form, on FPGAs and GPUs [Goto et al., *Science Advances*, 2019]. It is a purely digital algorithm; it exploits the same soft-spin dynamics that make the CIM work; and it runs on commodity hardware that improves every year with no optics involved. It is, in short, a brutal baseline — and its existence means that "our dynamics solve Ising problems well" is an argument *for a classical algorithm* as much as for a photonic machine.

The comparative review by Mohseni, McMahon, and Byrnes [*Nature Reviews Physics*, 2022] is the field's honest ledger, and its verdict is consistent: across Ising-machine technologies, well-implemented classical heuristics — simulated bifurcation, parallel tempering, breakout local search — are very hard to beat, and reported advantages have a way of evaporating once the baseline is competent and tuned.

A further clarification sharpens what is even being claimed, because two very different advantages get conflated. A *constant-factor* speedup — the machine solves the same size of problem some fixed multiple faster or cheaper because thousands of soft spins evolve in parallel — is plausible and, for the right workload, valuable; but it competes directly with the parallelism a GPU or an FPGA also brings, and with next year's transistors. An *asymptotic* advantage — a genuinely better scaling of time-to-solution with problem size $N$ — would be far more consequential, and it is the thing no photonic Ising machine has demonstrated against a strong classical baseline. Keeping the two apart is essential, because many headline results are constant-factor claims dressed in the language of scaling.

## Bottlenecks: Where the Time and Energy Actually Go

If a CIM is to win, it must win somewhere specific. Enumerating the bottlenecks honestly shows why that is hard.

**(a) The feedback loop, not the optics, is the bottleneck.** As Section 27.1.2 stressed, the coupling in every large CIM is a digital matrix–vector product wrapped in homodyne detection, ADC, and DAC. The optical round trip is fast; the electronics are not, and they set both the clock and the wall-plug power. Any energy or speed claim that meters only the parametric gain and ignores the converters and the FPGA is measuring the wrong system.

**(b) Coupling precision is limited and worsens under mapping.** Analog couplings carry perhaps 4–8 bits of effective precision (Chapter 25). Many problems of interest need constraints encoded as penalty terms (Section 27.1.1), and those penalties inflate the dynamic range $J_{ij}$ must span — pushing the required precision past what the analog hardware and its converters deliver, so that the machine solves a *distorted* version of the intended problem.

**(c) The $O(N^2)$ interaction step does not vanish.** All-to-all connectivity is the CIM's selling point over topology-limited annealers, but the feedback must compute $\sum_j J_{ij} c_j$ for all $i$ every round trip — an $O(N^2)$ operation per iteration on the FPGA. For dense problems this digital MVM is precisely the work a classical solver also does; the photonic machine has not removed it, only relocated the spins into light. Sparse-coupling machines dodge the $O(N^2)$ cost but forfeit the generality that motivated the architecture.

**(d) The metric must be time-to-solution *and* energy-to-solution.** A fair figure of merit is the wall-clock time (or the joules) to reach a target solution quality with a target probability, amortizing the repetitions a heuristic needs — not the raw round-trip rate, and not the best-of-many-runs quality quoted without its retry budget. Time-to-solution and energy-to-solution can rank technologies differently, and both must be reported.

## What Would Settle It

The question is answerable; it simply requires adversarial benchmarking. What the field needs, and has begun to converge on, is: standardized instance classes spanning sparse to dense with controlled hardness; reported *distributions* of time- and energy-to-solution rather than best cases; and — decisively — a *classical baseline tuned by someone motivated to win*, running current simulated-bifurcation or local-search code on current hardware. A photonic advantage that survives that test on a well-defined problem family would be real and worth building products around; one that appears only against a hobbled baseline is an artifact. The asymmetry is deliberate: the burden of proof is on the new machine.

## Verdict

Stated plainly, and without embarrassment on either side: as of roughly 2025 the coherent Ising machine and its relatives are a superb physics platform — clean binary spins, genuine large-scale analog dynamics, a $100{,}000$-spin demonstration — and a *plausible* special-purpose accelerator for specific dense-coupling optimization workloads. They have *not* established a durable, general computational advantage over the best classical heuristics run on modern hardware. Both halves are true. The frontier here is not a shortage of impressive demonstrations; it is the benchmarking discipline that would convert a demonstration into an advantage, and the interface engineering that would let the optics, rather than the feedback electronics, carry the load.
