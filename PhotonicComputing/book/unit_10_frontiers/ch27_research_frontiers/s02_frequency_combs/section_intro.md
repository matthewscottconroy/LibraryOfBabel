# Section 27.2: Integrated Frequency Combs for Computing

Every photonic processor that computes in parallel across wavelengths — the broadcast-and-weight networks of Unit VI, the WDM tensor cores of Unit V — needs a supply of many precise, mutually coherent optical carriers. The brute-force way to provide them is a rack of individually stabilized diode lasers, one per wavelength, each with its own control loop and its own drift. The elegant way is to generate all of them at once from a single continuous-wave pump and a single nonlinear microresonator. A *dissipative Kerr soliton microcomb* does precisely this: through the Kerr nonlinearity it converts one CW laser into a spectrum of hundreds of exactly equally spaced, phase-locked optical lines — a self-organized optical frequency comb on a chip, the integrated descendant of the frequency-comb metrology that reshaped precision measurement.

The comb's relevance to computing is twofold, and this section treats each promise separately because they stand on very different evidentiary footing. The first is *substitution*: a microcomb is a compact, low-part-count replacement for a bank of lasers, and it is the natural light source for any wavelength-parallel architecture — a proposition now backed by real photonic-accelerator demonstrations. The second, more speculative, is that the comb is not merely a source but a *computational medium in its own right* — that the frequency axis can host lattices, gauge fields, and linear-algebraic transforms (the program of *synthetic frequency dimensions*). Throughout, we hold to the chapter's disciplines: separate the demonstrated substitution win from the still-open medium claim, compare against the laser bank the comb is meant to replace rather than against nothing, and keep the energy accounting whole — a comb that displaces $N$ lasers must still be weighed, pump and amplifier included, against those $N$ lasers.
