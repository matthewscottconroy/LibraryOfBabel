# 27.2.2 The Microcomb as a Multi-Wavelength Source for Computing

## The Value Proposition

Wavelength-division multiplexing is the photonic processor's cheapest form of parallelism: encode different data or weights on different colors, send them down the same waveguide, and let a single physical path carry many independent computations at once. The broadcast-and-weight neuromorphic networks of Unit VI and the WDM tensor cores of Unit V both live or die by the number of clean, evenly spaced, mutually coherent carriers available. Supplying $N$ carriers from $N$ discrete DFB lasers means $N$ packages, $N$ bias and temperature controllers, and $N$ independent wavelength drifts to police. A microcomb collapses that to one pump laser and one resonator (Section 27.2.1): the comb returns $N$ lines whose spacing is fixed by geometry to be *exactly* uniform and whose relative phases are locked by the physics of soliton formation. For coherent architectures the fixed inter-line phase relationship is not a convenience but a prerequisite, and it is one a bank of free-running lasers cannot provide at any price. This is a real architectural simplification, and the demonstrations bear it out.

It helps to be concrete about how the lines get used. In a WDM tensor core each wavelength carries one element — or one row — of the data; a spectrally selective weight bank imposes a per-line transmission; and a single photodetector sums the weighted lines by simple photocurrent addition, so $N$ comb lines execute an $N$-element multiply-accumulate in one detection event, and a bank of such detectors executes a matrix–vector product. The comb's exactly uniform spacing keeps every channel on its demultiplexer grid, and its mutual coherence is what permits coherent (signed, phase-sensitive) weighting rather than intensity-only weighting. The value delivered by a single source therefore scales with precisely this usable line count — which is why so much effort goes into maximizing it.

## The Demonstrations

Two 2021 results established the microcomb as a working light source for photonic computing. Feldmann and co-workers drove an integrated photonic tensor core with a microcomb, using the comb lines as parallel WDM channels through a crossbar of phase-change-material weights to perform convolutions and matrix operations across wavelengths simultaneously [Feldmann et al., *Nature*, 2021]. In parallel, Xu and co-workers built a convolutional accelerator around a *soliton-crystal* microcomb — a state in which many solitons pack the ring, yielding a flatter comb with higher per-line power and, importantly, far more robust and repeatable generation than a lone single soliton — and reported an operation rate of roughly $11$ TOPS using time- and wavelength-interleaving [Xu et al., *Nature*, 2021]. The soliton-crystal choice is itself an honest lesson: the "hard to generate, low power per line" problems of Section 27.2.1 pushed the accelerator designers toward the comb state that ameliorated them.

## Lessons from the Communications Cousin

Wavelength-parallel *computing* is the young sibling of wavelength-parallel *communication*, where microcombs matured first and where the same engineering truths were learned earlier. Marin-Palomo and co-workers used DKS combs as both the transmitter carriers and the receiver local oscillators for massively parallel coherent transmission, exceeding $50$ Tbit/s [Marin-Palomo et al., *Nature*, 2017]; Corcoran and co-workers pushed a single soliton-crystal chip source to $44.2$ Tbit/s over installed standard fiber [Corcoran et al., *Nature Communications*, 2020]. These are not computing results, but they are directly load-bearing: they prove the comb can carry hundreds of independent, coherently detected channels at scale, and they surfaced the practical issues — line flatness, per-line noise, local-oscillator coherence — that any computing use inherits.

## The Caveats: What the Comb Does and Does Not Buy

The disciplines of Chapter 25 require metering the whole system, and there the substitution is less lopsided than "one laser replaces $N$" suggests.

*Per-line relative intensity noise.* Comb lines inherit the pump's intensity noise and add soliton amplitude noise; a single tooth can be noisier than a clean, dedicated DFB. Because analog optical computing floors its SNR on carrier relative intensity noise (RIN) (Chapter 25), this directly caps achievable precision — a cost a discrete-laser bank does not pay in the same way.

*Comb flattening wastes power.* A soliton comb's $\mathrm{sech}^2$ envelope gives strong center lines and weak edge lines. Using them as equal WDM carriers means passing the comb through a programmable spectral leveler (a waveshaper) that *attenuates the strong lines down to the weak ones* — deliberately discarding optical power to buy uniformity. Soliton crystals and dispersion engineering soften this, but do not remove it.

*Wall-plug efficiency of comb generation.* The single-soliton conversion efficiency is only a few percent, and the CW pump usually needs an amplifier to reach the parametric threshold. The honest ledger therefore compares one pump-plus-amplifier-plus-resonator against $N$ diode lasers — and at low channel counts the comb can actually *lose* on wall-plug power. The comb wins on component count, footprint, and coherence; whether it also wins on energy depends on $N$ and on the pump chain, and must be computed, not assumed.

*The comb replaces only the source.* Most decisively, the microcomb displaces the laser bank and nothing else. The modulators that encode data onto each line, the weight elements, the photodetectors and transimpedance amplifiers, and the DACs and ADCs at the boundary all remain — and those interfaces, not the light source, dominate the energy and set the speed of a photonic accelerator (Chapter 25). Watch the interfaces: swapping $N$ lasers for one comb improves the part of the budget that was never the bottleneck.

## Net Assessment

The microcomb is a genuine and demonstrated architectural simplification for wavelength-parallel photonic computing — fewer sources to build and stabilize, and a coherence that discrete lasers cannot match. It is not, by itself, an automatic energy win, and it does nothing to relieve the modulator, detector, and converter interfaces that govern system performance. The correct summary is the one this chapter keeps returning to: the comb is a real advance at the source, and its system-level payoff must still be accounted end-to-end, against a laser bank of equal line count and against the full interface budget it leaves untouched.
