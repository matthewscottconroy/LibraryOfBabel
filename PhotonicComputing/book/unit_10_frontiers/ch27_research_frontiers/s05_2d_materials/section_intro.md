# Section 27.5: Photonic Computing with 2D Materials

Every architecture in this chapter — the Ising machine, the comb-driven tensor core, the free-space processor, the topological device — is gated in the end not by its celebrated core physics but by the humble components that get light on and off the chip: the modulators that encode data, the detectors that read results, and the sources that supply the carrier. Chapter 25 made the accounting explicit; this chapter's introduction made it a discipline (*watch the interfaces*). Two-dimensional materials are the frontier's most direct assault on that interface layer. Graphene, the transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) such as MoS₂ and WSe₂, and the van der Waals heterostructures assembled by stacking them are atomically thin crystals whose optoelectronic response is strong, broadband, and gate-tunable — and, because they adhere to a surface by van der Waals forces rather than by epitaxy, in principle transferable onto any photonic platform, silicon and silicon nitride included. They promise the active device layer that passive dielectric photonics has lacked for a decade: an electro-optic and optoelectronic material that is CMOS-compatible in placement, if not yet in manufacture.

The register of this section is deliberately sober, because the field's excitement and its honest status diverge more here than almost anywhere else in the book. The device physics is spectacular and, in many cases, thoroughly *demonstrated*: micron-scale graphene electro-absorption modulators, graphene photodetectors past 100 GHz, atomically thin light emitters and single-photon sources. What remains *open* is the manufacturing question that governs whether any of it reaches a computing system — wafer-scale, uniform, low-defect, low-contact-resistance integration of materials still largely grown, transferred, and contacted by artisanal means. The summary carried throughout is therefore blunt: 2D materials attack the *interfaces* of photonic computing, the modulators and detectors and emitters that dominate its energy and speed budgets, and not the *arithmetic* itself. Their near-term value is as interface and emitter devices, and as an open materials frontier, not as a proven computing substrate.
