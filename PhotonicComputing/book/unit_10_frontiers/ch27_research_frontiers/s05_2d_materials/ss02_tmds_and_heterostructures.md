# 27.5.2 TMDs and van der Waals Heterostructures

## From Indirect to Direct: The Monolayer Bandgap

If graphene's defining feature is the *absence* of a bandgap, the transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) supply the complementary asset: a real, sizeable, and — in the monolayer limit — *direct* semiconductor gap. A TMD is a layered crystal of formula MX₂, with a plane of transition-metal atoms (Mo, W) sandwiched between two planes of chalcogen (S, Se, Te). Bulk MoS₂ is an indirect-gap semiconductor near 1.2 eV; thinned to a single molecular layer, its gap becomes *direct* at the $K$ points of the Brillouin zone and widens to roughly 1.8–1.9 eV, accompanied by a jump in photoluminescence yield of orders of magnitude [Mak et al., *Physical Review Letters*, 2010]. The same crossover occurs, with material-specific energies in the visible to near-infrared, across the family (MoS₂, WSe₂, WS₂, MoSe₂), which the foundational review of TMD electronics and optoelectronics surveys as a coherent class of materials [Wang et al., *Nature Nanotechnology*, 2012]. Where graphene offers a broadband absorber and detector, monolayer TMDs offer what silicon has always lacked: an efficient, atomically thin *emitter*.

## Excitons and Valleys

Two features make TMD optics distinctive beyond the mere existence of a direct gap, and both are central to any device argument. The first is the dominance of excitons. In a monolayer, dielectric screening is weak (the field lines of a bound electron-hole pair leave the sheet) and confinement is extreme, so exciton binding energies reach *hundreds* of meV — one to two orders of magnitude larger than in bulk III–V semiconductors. Excitons therefore govern the optical response even at room temperature, with a non-hydrogenic Rydberg series and rich many-body structure [Wang et al., *Reviews of Modern Physics*, 2018]. The optical spectrum of a monolayer TMD is, to first approximation, an excitonic spectrum, and any modulator or emitter built from it operates on excitonic resonances rather than on free-carrier interband transitions.

The second feature is the valley degree of freedom. Broken inversion symmetry in the monolayer, combined with strong spin-orbit coupling, makes the two inequivalent band extrema — the $K$ and $K'$ valleys — couple to opposite helicities of circularly polarized light, an optical selection rule with no counterpart in silicon or graphene [Mak & Shan, *Nature Photonics*, 2016]. This valley polarization behaves as an addressable internal label on the carriers and is the basis of "valleytronics": a candidate information-carrying degree of freedom in which the valley index, rather than charge or spin, encodes the bit. Whether valley coherence can survive long enough, and be read out cheaply enough, to matter for computing is an open question; as physics it is thoroughly established.

## Device Potential: Modulators, Emitters, and Single-Photon Sources

The device case for TMDs runs along the same interface axis as graphene's, but weighted toward *sources and resonant modulators* rather than broadband detection. Because the optical response is sharp and excitonic, small perturbations near the exciton resonance — an applied field via the quantum-confined Stark effect, or electrostatic band-filling that bleaches the transition — produce large changes in absorption and refractive index, enabling compact electro-absorption and electro-optic modulators. The comparative review of optical modulators built from 2D layered materials places the TMD, graphene, and black-phosphorus options side by side and makes clear that each targets the modulation interface, not the computation [Sun et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2016]. On the emission side, the direct gap enables electrically driven monolayer light-emitting diodes and cavity-coupled emitters — an on-chip light source in a material that can be transferred onto a silicon or silicon-nitride photonic circuit. Finally, localized excitons trapped at strain sites or defects in monolayer WSe₂ act as *single-photon emitters* exhibiting antibunched emission, a direct bridge to the deterministic quantum-photonic sources of Unit VII. The register throughout is the same: these are demonstrated device physics, situated squarely at the interface and source layer of a photonic system.

## Van der Waals Heterostructures and Moiré Physics

The deeper frontier opens when 2D layers are *stacked*. Because adjacent layers are held only by van der Waals forces, dissimilar materials can be assembled with atomic-layer precision and *without* the lattice-matching constraint that governs conventional epitaxy — a graphene conductor, a hexagonal-boron-nitride (hBN) insulator, and a TMD semiconductor can be combined in essentially arbitrary order. Geim and Grigorieva articulated the program and its "atomic Lego" metaphor [Geim & Grigorieva, *Nature*, 2013], and Novoselov and colleagues surveyed its maturation into a general method for building designer materials layer by layer [Novoselov et al., *Science*, 2016]. From these building blocks one assembles p–n junctions, tunnel devices, encapsulated high-mobility channels, and vertically integrated photodetectors and LEDs, each engineered by choice of layers rather than by growth chemistry — with hBN serving as the near-ideal dielectric and encapsulant that protects the fragile monolayers.

Stacking two layers with a small twist angle or lattice mismatch produces a *moiré superlattice*: a long-period beat pattern that imposes a new, tunable periodic potential on the electrons and excitons. The moiré potential flattens electronic minibands, and flat bands mean strong correlations; optically, it can trap arrays of moiré excitons in a periodic lattice and support moiré exciton-polaritons. This is where a genuinely new frontier for *photonic* computing might lie — engineered arrays of quantum emitters, and correlated moiré-polariton systems as a candidate platform for quantum many-body and correlated-photon simulation. It must be flagged honestly as *open*: the correlated moiré-photon program is an active research direction as of roughly 2025, not a demonstrated computing capability, and the assembly of moiré structures remains a low-throughput, exquisitely manual art.

## The Computing Case: Interfaces and a Materials Frontier, Not (Yet) a Substrate

The overall assessment mirrors Section 27.5.1's, with the emphasis shifted. As device physics, monolayer TMDs and van der Waals heterostructures are among the richest systems in condensed-matter optics, and much of the catalog above is convincingly *demonstrated*: the indirect-to-direct crossover, giant excitonic effects, valley-selective optics, compact modulators, monolayer emitters, single-photon sources, and heterostructure photodiodes. What is *open* is the computing case. Nothing here yet constitutes a proven computing substrate; the value to photonic computing is, once again, primarily as *interface and emitter devices* — modulators and on-chip light sources for the systems built in Units V–VII — and secondarily as a *materials frontier* whose moiré-correlated physics may one day supply a quantum-simulation platform. Between the physics and any system stands the same manufacturability gap that governs graphene, only steeper: monolayers must be grown or exfoliated with wafer-scale uniformity, stacked with controlled twist angle, contacted without Fermi-level pinning, and encapsulated for stability — none of which is, as of roughly 2025, a high-yield industrial process. Read every TMD or heterostructure computing claim by separating the device demonstration, which is often superb, from the integration claim, which is usually still pending. Watch the manufacturability gap.
