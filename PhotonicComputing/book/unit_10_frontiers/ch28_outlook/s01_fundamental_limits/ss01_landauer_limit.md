# 28.1.1 Energy per Operation and the Landauer Limit

## Landauer's Principle

Landauer's principle sets the deepest floor. Erasing one bit of information — resetting a two-state system to a known state, discarding which state it was in — dissipates at least $k_B T\ln 2$ of heat into the environment [Landauer, *IBM Journal of Research and Development*, 1961]. At $T=300$ K,

$$k_B T\ln 2 \approx (1.38\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K})(300\ \mathrm{K})(0.693)\approx 2.9\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{J},$$

about 18 meV, of order the room-temperature thermal energy $k_B T\approx 26$ meV. The bound is not a statement about switches or wires; it is statistical mechanics linking logical irreversibility to physical entropy. Erasing a bit collapses the logical state space by a factor of two, and the second law demands that the associated entropy $k_B\ln 2$ be exported to the environment as heat. Half a century after Landauer proposed it, the principle was confirmed in direct measurement, tracking the heat released as a colloidal particle in a double-well optical trap was reset to a known well [Bérut et al., *Nature*, 2012]. Information erasure has a thermodynamic price, and the price is exactly Landauer's.

## Reversible Computing and the Erasure Caveat

The bound applies to *irreversible* operations only. A logically reversible computation — one whose outputs uniquely determine its inputs, so that no information is discarded — can in principle be carried out with arbitrarily little dissipation, the central insight of the reversible-computing tradition. Optics is in fact a natural host for reversibility: a lossless linear-optical network implements a unitary transformation, and unitary maps are reversible by construction (Chapter 11). Two facts, however, keep Landauer's floor out of reach in any real machine. First, useful computation eventually erases: an inference reads out a decision, a matrix engine writes a result and clears its accumulators, and every such step discards state. Second — and decisively — the actual dissipation of a photonic processor is set not by erased bits but by the analog machinery that surrounds the optics, and that machinery operates many orders of magnitude above Landauer regardless of the logical reversibility of the arithmetic.

## Where Real Multiply-Accumulates Sit

Put numbers on the multiply-accumulate (MAC), the atomic operation of the linear algebra that dominates machine-learning workloads. A photonic MAC today costs on the order of $0.1$–$1$ pJ — $10^{-13}$ to $10^{-12}$ J — once the full optoelectronic path is counted [Nahmias et al., *IEEE JSTQE*, 2020; Tait, *Physical Review Applied*, 2022]. A digital CMOS MAC in an advanced node is comparable, of order $0.1$ pJ and falling — energy per operation in digital accelerators has improved by roughly 40% per year over the past decade, a moving target the reader should never forget when a photonic advantage is quoted against a frozen electronic baseline. Both numbers sit seven to nine orders of magnitude above the Landauer floor of $2.9\times10^{-21}$ J. The conclusion is worth stating without hedging: **Landauer is not the binding constraint on photonic computing, and will not become one in any foreseeable device.** A machine that dissipated even a thousand times the thermodynamic minimum — a few attojoules per operation — would be a revolution; today's machines dissipate closer to a hundred million times it. The floor is real, and the field is nowhere near it.

## What Actually Binds: The Optical Energy Ledger

If not thermodynamics, then what? The binding constraints are the mundane, systematic energy costs of getting information into light, through the optics, and back into electronics — the ledger that Chapter 25 insists on. In rough order of importance for an integrated photonic accelerator:

- **Laser wall-plug efficiency.** The optical power budget begins at the laser, whose electrical-to-optical conversion efficiency is often only 10–30%; every downstream optical loss is ultimately paid for in laser power at this penalty.
- **Optical propagation and coupling loss.** Waveguide attenuation, insertion loss through each modulator and coupler, and fiber-to-chip coupling all deplete the signal the detector must finally register above noise.
- **Modulator drive energy.** Encoding an electronic operand onto light costs energy to charge the modulator; the sub-femtojoule modulator is a long-standing target, not a solved problem.
- **Photodetector and transimpedance-amplifier energy.** Converting light back to a usable electrical signal, and amplifying it, costs energy set by detector capacitance and the required bandwidth.
- **Data-converter energy.** The DACs that load operands and the ADCs that read results are frequently the single largest line item, and their cost climbs steeply with bit depth — the hinge on which Section 28.1.2 turns.

The systematic accounting of these terms is the substance of the attojoule-optoelectronics program [Miller, *Journal of Lightwave Technology*, 2017] and of the power-modeling literature specific to photonic neural networks [Nahmias et al., *IEEE JSTQE*, 2020; Tait, *Physical Review Applied*, 2022]. Its lesson is uniform: the celebrated "free" optical multiplication is a small fraction of the total energy, and the interfaces dominate.

## Amortization: Why Optical MAC Energy Falls as $1/N$

One structural feature of optical linear algebra genuinely helps, and it must be stated precisely so as not to overclaim. In broadcast-and-weight and coherent-detection architectures, a single optical signal fans out to drive many multiplications in parallel, and a single detection event accumulates their sum. The optical energy required to read one dot product to a fixed precision is therefore *shared* across its $N$ terms, so the optical energy **per MAC** falls as $1/N$ with the vector length [Hamerly et al., *Physical Review X*, 2019]. For the large matrices of real networks — $N$ in the hundreds or thousands — this amortization drives the *optical* component of the per-MAC energy toward the attojoule scale, below the digital MAC and, in the limit, toward the single-photon regime that Section 28.1.2 examines. The crucial caveat, and the reason this is not a free lunch, is that the amortization applies only to the optical term; the fixed per-detection cost of the ADC and its amplifier does not shrink with $N$, and for small matrices it dominates. The advantage is real, asymptotic, and problem-size-dependent — exactly the kind of claim to be checked against the full ledger rather than asserted.

## The Engineering Ceiling

The picture that emerges is a hierarchy of limits separated by a vast gulf. Far below sits the Landauer floor at $\sim10^{-21}$ J, untouchable and, for photonics, irrelevant. Far above it sits an *engineering ceiling* — the practical energy per operation set by loss, converters, and laser efficiency — presently at the picojoule scale and descending toward the femtojoule scale as modulators shrink, detectors integrate monolithically with their amplifiers, and lasers grow more efficient. The entire game of energy-efficient photonic computing is played in the many orders of magnitude between that ceiling and the floor, and it is won or lost on the interfaces, not on the thermodynamics. This is the physical reading of optical computing that the modern literature has converged on [McMahon, *Nature Reviews Physics*, 2023]: the optics is cheap and the conversions are dear, and a field that forgets this will keep quoting the wrong limit.
