# Section 28.2: The Great Open Problems

Section 28.1 established the *floors* — Landauer, the precision-energy trade-off, the quantum noise limit — below which no photonic computer can go, regardless of how clever the engineering. This section concerns the *walls*: the concrete, stubborn problems that stand between today's laboratory demonstrations and a photonic computer worth building at scale. Unlike the fundamental limits, these are not laws of physics. They are unsolved problems — which is to say, research opportunities — and the honest way to enter the field is to know precisely which wall you propose to climb.

Most of the field's day-to-day difficulty lives in four recurring engineering challenges that surface in every architecture of Units V–VII. **Thermal management and crosstalk**: the thermo-optic phase shifter, the workhorse tuning element of silicon photonics, dumps milliwatts of heat per element into a substrate whose neighbors are themselves heat-sensitive, so integration density and thermal stability fight each other directly. **Calibration and drift**: an MZI mesh (Chapter 11) encodes its matrix in analog phases that wander with temperature, aging, and fabrication error, forcing continuous closed-loop control whose overhead is charged to the system, not the physics (Chapter 25). **The electronic-photonic bottleneck**: every optical computation is bracketed by digital-to-analog and analog-to-digital conversion, and those converters are frequently larger, hotter, and slower than the optics they serve. **Scalable sources**: quantum photonics needs deterministic, indistinguishable single photons at high rate, while classical WDM computing needs many stable wavelengths — both still gated by source technology (Unit VII; the microcombs of Chapter 27). These challenges are serious, but they are tractable; progress on them is incremental and ongoing.

Beneath them sit three problems of a different order — deep enough that a genuine solution to any one would reshape the field, and old enough that their very persistence is a result. We give each its own subsection. The first asks whether optics can ever possess a true logic device. The second asks what a photonic computer actually costs once every electron is counted. The third asks where a photonic computer keeps its data.

- **28.2.1** — *Is There a Photonic Transistor?* The sixty-year search for a cascadable, low-energy, photon-controlled photon switch, and why its absence quietly shaped the entire field.
- **28.2.2** — *End-to-End Energy Efficiency*: the accounting problem — whether the optical core's energy advantage survives contact with lasers, converters, and calibration.
- **28.2.3** — *The Photonic Memory Problem*: light does not sit still, and there is no optical RAM — the unsolved constraint that makes every photonic computer a hybrid.
