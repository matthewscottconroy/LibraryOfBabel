# 28.3.1 Convergence of Classical and Quantum Photonics

## Two Fields, One Substrate

Today, classical photonic computing (matrix engines, neuromorphic photonics, Ising machines) and quantum photonic computing (linear-optical and continuous-variable quantum processors) are largely separate fields — separate conferences, separate figures of merit, and, for the most part, separate people. What they already share is the *substrate*: both are being built on the same integrated-photonics platforms — silicon-on-insulator, silicon nitride, thin-film lithium niobate — using the same foundries (Chapter 26), the same modulators and interferometers, the same integrated detectors, and increasingly the same design tools. This shared physical layer is the seed of a convergence thesis worth taking seriously, precisely because it is so cheap to state and so demanding to realize.

## The Shared-Platform Reality

The convergence is not speculative at the hardware level; it is already a fact of the foundry. A programmable Mach-Zehnder interferometer mesh (Chapter 11) is the *same device* whether you call its unitary a classical linear-algebra operator or a quantum interferometer: the mesh that implements a matrix-vector product for a photonic neural network is, reconfigured, the multiport interferometer at the heart of a boson-sampling or Gaussian-boson-sampling experiment (Unit VII; the quantum processors of Chapter 26) — a point the programmable-photonics literature makes explicitly [Bogaerts et al., *Nature*, 2020]. The supporting cast is shared too: integrated superconducting-nanowire single-photon detectors, heralded and quantum-dot single-photon sources, ultra-low-loss waveguides and delay lines, and hybrid integration of III-V gain and quantum emitters onto silicon are all infrastructure serving both agendas [Elshaari et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2020; Moody et al., *Journal of Physics: Photonics*, 2022]. When the integrated-quantum-photonics roadmap [Moody et al., 2022] lists its priorities — better sources, lower loss, integrated detectors, scalable programmable circuits — it is, item for item, the same list a classical photonic-accelerator engineer would write.

The divergence is in the scoring, not the parts. A classical mesh is judged on insertion loss, analog precision, and energy per MAC; a quantum mesh on absolute loss measured against a fault-tolerance threshold (Unit VII). A single low-loss-waveguide or higher-efficiency-detector improvement therefore advances both agendas at once, even though each grades it on a different curve and against a different failure mode. That a foundry multi-project wafer can now carry a neural-network mesh and a quantum interferometer side by side, fabricated in one identical process, is the most concrete evidence that the substrates have already merged while the communities that use them have not.

## The Hybrid Thesis

The interesting claim is not that the two fields share parts but that they might share *computations*. The convergence thesis, stated structurally, is that the natural architecture is a **hybrid pipeline**: classical optical or analog processing on the front end, a quantum-coherent processor in the middle, and classical processing on the back end — each stage doing what it does best, on one integrated platform, with light as the common signal. Concretely, one can imagine classical optical or analog **feature extraction and dimensionality reduction** (a linear-optical transform, a reservoir, a matrix engine) preparing the input to a **quantum sampling core** (a GBS or interferometric circuit whose output distribution is classically hard to produce, Unit VII), whose samples are then **read out and post-processed classically** — potentially on the very chip that prepared them. The quantum stage supplies a distribution or correlation no classical stage can cheaply generate; the classical stages supply the pre- and post-processing that a bare quantum sampler cannot do for itself. This is the photonic expression of the broader "quantum co-processor" picture, and photonics is unusually well placed to realize it, because the classical and quantum layers speak the same physical language — photons in waveguides — rather than requiring a transduction between, say, superconducting qubits and a room-temperature host.

## Requirements and Open Issues

Two things must become true for the hybrid thesis to be engineering rather than aspiration, and both are open. First, the **quantum-classical interface must not destroy the quantum advantage**: every place where the pipeline measures, digitizes, and re-encodes is a place where coherence is lost and where the conversion costs of Section 28.2.2 reappear — now charged against a far scarcer resource. Keeping the quantum core's fragile state intact while coupling it to lossy classical optics is a genuine, unsolved design problem; loss, the master variable of Unit VII, does not become less punishing because a classical processor sits next door. Second, and more fundamentally, it is an **open question whether the combination buys anything at all**: whether a near-term, noisy photonic quantum processor wrapped in classical photonic pre- and post-processing can solve a useful problem that neither a classical photonic processor nor a classical digital computer could solve as well. This must be stated without hype. There is a structural *argument* for optimism — the layers compose cheaply on a shared substrate — but no demonstrated *result* that the composite outperforms the best classical approach on a problem anyone needs solved. It is exactly the kind of claim Chapter 25's discipline exists to adjudicate, and it has not yet been adjudicated in photonics' favor.

## Two Quantum Bets, One Convergence

The convergence will also be shaped by which quantum-photonic architecture matures, and here the field is running the two publicly stated bets of Chapter 26 in parallel, as of circa 2025. The **discrete-variable** program (single photons, fusion-based computation toward fault tolerance — the approach publicly associated with PsiQuantum) and the **continuous-variable** program (squeezed light, Gaussian boson sampling, bosonic codes — the approach publicly associated with Xanadu) present different convergence surfaces. The CV route's Gaussian operations and homodyne readout sit closer to the analog signal processing of classical photonic computing; the DV route's photon-counting and resource-state machinery sit further from it. Which substrate — if either, at scale — ultimately hosts hybrid classical-quantum photonic systems is undetermined, and a student entering now is entering before the selection is made. The honest framing, carried from Chapter 26: these are aspirations with quantifiable critical paths (loss budgets, squeezing levels, source rates), not scheduled outcomes, and the convergence thesis itself is a hypothesis about architecture, not a promise about a product [Shastri et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2021].
