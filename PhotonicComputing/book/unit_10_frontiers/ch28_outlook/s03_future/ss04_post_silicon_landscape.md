# 28.3.2 The Post-Silicon Landscape and a Realistic Long View

## The End of Scaling, and Its Heirs

For half a century, computing improved by making the silicon transistor smaller, and that engine is running out of physics. Feature sizes have reached the near-atomic scale, where gate control fails as electrons **tunnel** through barriers only a few atoms thick; **heat** density has capped practical clock frequencies since Dennard scaling ended in the mid-2000s; and the economic return of each new process node has thinned. Silicon CMOS is not disappearing — it will remain the substrate of computing for the foreseeable future — but the era in which a single, uniform technology delivered exponential improvement on its own is over. What replaces it is not a successor transistor but an *architecture*: **heterogeneous integration**, in which specialized technologies are co-packaged, each contributing what its physics does best [Berggren et al., *Nanotechnology*, 2021].

## A Portfolio, Not a Winner

The candidate post-silicon technologies — photonics, spintronics and magnetic memory, 2D and novel-channel materials, superconducting and quantum devices, and analog and neuromorphic circuits — are best understood not as competitors for a single throne but as members of a portfolio [Berggren et al., *Nanotechnology*, 2021; Marković et al., *Nature Reviews Physics*, 2020]. The defining move of the coming period is *integration*: chiplets and interposers, 3D stacking, and co-packaging that let a system route each operation to the substrate that runs it most efficiently. In that framing, the question "will photonics replace electronics?" is simply the wrong question. Electronics will keep doing the logic, control, and memory it already does superbly; the right question is *which jobs migrate to light*, and that has a structurally defensible answer.

## Photonics' Realistic Role, Argued from Physics

Three roles for photonics survive honest scrutiny, in decreasing order of near-term certainty.

1. **Interconnect — the role photonics has already won.** Photons beat electrons at *communication* by a margin that widens with distance and bandwidth, because an optical waveguide carries enormous bandwidth at distance-independent loss while a copper trace does neither. This is the interconnect-first thesis of Chapter 26 rendered structural: co-packaged optics, optical I/O chiplets (Ayar Labs and its peers), and photonic interposers (Lightmatter's Passage) are photonics moving *inward* — from between computers, to between racks, to inside the package. It is winning first because it is where the physics is least ambiguous and where the electronic-photonic conversion (Section 28.2.2) is the product rather than the overhead.
2. **Specific compute kernels.** For a narrow set of operations, optics maps onto the computation so naturally that it can win even against relentlessly improving digital hardware: large **linear-algebra and broadcast operations**, where the $1/N$ amortization of Section 28.2.2 bites; **analog signal front-ends** such as FMCW LIDAR and optical-coherence-tomography processing, and direct **RF and microwave photonic** filtering, where the signal is already optical or analog and digitizing it first is the expensive step; and the domain-specific, physics-matched processors of Chapter 27. The unifying principle is *minimal conversion*: photonics wins a kernel when the data is already light, or when the arithmetic done per conversion is high enough to amortize the interfaces.
3. **Quantum input/output and networking.** Whatever substrate ultimately wins quantum computing, photons are the only carrier that moves quantum information between modules and across distance at room temperature — which makes photonic interconnect the likely *nervous system* of quantum machines and networks (Unit VII), independent of whether photonic quantum *computing* wins on its own.

These three are not equally certain, and an honest presentation says so: the first is happening now, the second is demonstrated for particular kernels but open at the system level, and the third is a well-motivated structural bet [Shastri et al., *Nature Photonics*, 2021; McMahon, *Nature Reviews Physics*, 2023].

## A Realistic Long View

Step back far enough and a pattern comes into focus. Light won long-haul **communication** decades ago — optical fiber has carried the world's telecommunications since the 1970s, a historical fact and not a forecast — and it has been moving steadily *inward* ever since: from between continents to within data centers, from between racks to between chips, and now from between packages toward inside them. The trajectory of photonics in computing is this same centripetal motion, from the network toward the processor, and its pace is set by the loss, energy, and integration walls this chapter has catalogued — not by any single anticipated breakthrough. Read that way, the durable questions of the field are not "can we build it," since the physics is largely known, but "**what should we build, for whom, and why**": which computations genuinely want to be done in light, and which are being forced there against their nature.

## Advice for Researchers Entering Now

For a student or engineer entering the field today, the argument of this book condenses into a short, unglamorous, and durable set of instructions.

- **Master the end-to-end system view.** The decisive number is never the optical-core figure of merit; it is the whole-system energy, latency, and precision with converters, lasers, and calibration all included (Chapter 25). Learn to draw the system boundary correctly, because most inflated claims come from drawing it too small.
- **Learn both the physics and the electronic context.** Photonics competes with, and lives beside, CMOS. A researcher who understands modulators but not DACs, or interferometers but not the digital baseline they must beat, cannot tell a real advantage from an artifact of an unfair comparison.
- **Benchmark against competent baselines.** A photonic result is meaningful only relative to a well-implemented classical or electronic alternative *on the same task* (Chapters 25 and 27). Beating a strawman is the field's most common self-inflicted wound.
- **Distinguish physics results from computing claims.** Much of the best work in this book is superb physics whose *computational* advantage is unestablished — and saying so is a strength, not a hedge. The two claims live on different axes and must never be conflated.
- **Choose problems where the physics maps to the computation with minimal conversion.** The most promising photonic computing problems are those where the data is already optical or analog, where the operation is natively linear and large, or where the alternative is an even more expensive digital step. Find the computation that *wants* to be done in light.

The field does not need more demonstrations that light can, in principle, multiply two numbers. It needs a clear-eyed accounting of where photonic physics delivers a decisive, system-level advantage, and disciplined engineering to capture it. Light has already won communication and is moving toward the processor; the task of the next generation is to meet it there — honestly, at the boundary where photons stop being a promise and become the cheapest way to do a specific, valuable computation. That is the whole argument of this book, handed to the people who will decide whether photonic computing becomes a permanent layer of the computing stack or a recurring and beautiful footnote.
