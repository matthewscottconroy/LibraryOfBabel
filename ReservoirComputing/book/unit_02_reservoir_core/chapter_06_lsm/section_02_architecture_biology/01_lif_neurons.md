# Section 2.1: Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neurons

## 2.1 The Cell as a Resistor-Capacitor Circuit

Every neuron in the brain is enclosed by a lipid bilayer membrane: a thin, insulating sheet that separates the intracellular fluid from the extracellular fluid. This membrane has two key electrical properties.

**Capacitance.** The membrane is a thin insulator between two conducting fluids. Like a parallel plate capacitor, it stores charge. The membrane capacitance $C_m$ (in farads, or typically picofarads for a neuron) relates the charge stored on the membrane $Q$ to the voltage across it $V$: $Q = C_m V$.

**Resistance (via ion channels).** The membrane is not a perfect insulator: it is studded with ion channels — protein pores that allow specific ions (Na$^+$, K$^+$, Ca$^{2+}$, Cl$^-$) to flow across it down their electrochemical gradients. These channels create a nonzero conductance, which can be modeled as a resistance $R_m$. In the absence of a signal, the channels maintain the membrane at the **resting potential** $V_{rest} \approx -65$ mV.

**The RC circuit model.** Taken together, these two properties give the membrane the electrical behavior of an RC circuit: a capacitor $C_m$ in parallel with a resistor $R_m$. This circuit is driven by the synaptic input current $I(t)$, which represents the flow of charge through synaptic ion channels in response to neurotransmitter binding.

Applying Kirchhoff's current law to this circuit: the current $I(t)$ is split between the capacitive current (which charges the membrane) and the resistive current (which flows through the ion channels back to resting potential):

$$I(t) = C_m \frac{dV}{dt} + \frac{V - V_{rest}}{R_m} \tag{2.1}$$

Rearranging:

$$\boxed{C_m \frac{dV}{dt} = -\frac{V - V_{rest}}{R_m} + I(t)} \tag{2.2}$$

This is the **leaky integrator** differential equation for the membrane potential. The term $-(V - V_{rest})/R_m$ is the **leak** current: it drives $V$ back toward the resting potential $V_{rest}$ at a rate proportional to the deviation $V - V_{rest}$.

**The time constant.** Dividing both sides by $C_m$:

$$\frac{dV}{dt} = -\frac{V - V_{rest}}{\tau_m} + \frac{I(t)}{C_m} \tag{2.3}$$

where $\tau_m = R_m C_m$ is the **membrane time constant** (units: seconds). With no input ($I = 0$), the membrane potential decays exponentially to $V_{rest}$: $V(t) = V_{rest} + (V_0 - V_{rest})e^{-t/\tau_m}$. For typical cortical neurons, $\tau_m \approx 10$-$30$ ms.

## 2.2 Adding the Spike: Threshold and Reset

The RC circuit model describes the **subthreshold** dynamics — what happens while the membrane is building up toward the threshold. When the membrane reaches the threshold potential $V_{th}$ (typically around $-55$ mV, or about $+10$ mV above rest), a dramatic nonlinear event occurs: the **action potential** (spike).

In the full Hodgkin-Huxley model, the spike is generated by the rapid opening of voltage-gated Na$^+$ channels (depolarization) followed by their inactivation and the opening of K$^+$ channels (repolarization). The result is a brief, stereotyped voltage pulse that propagates along the axon.

The LIF model simplifies this: it declares that when $V(t)$ reaches $V_{th}$, a spike is emitted and the membrane potential is immediately reset to the reset potential $V_{reset}$ (often equal to $V_{rest}$):

$$\text{If } V(t) = V_{th}: \quad \text{emit spike}, \quad V(t^+) = V_{reset} \tag{2.4}$$

Additionally, there is a brief **refractory period** $\tau_{ref}$ (typically 2-5 ms) during which the membrane is held at $V_{reset}$ regardless of input, modeling the sodium channel inactivation that prevents immediate re-firing.

**The complete LIF model:**

$$C_m \frac{dV}{dt} = -\frac{V - V_{rest}}{R_m} + I(t), \quad V < V_{th} \tag{2.5a}$$

$$\text{When } V = V_{th}: \quad \text{spike at time } t_f, \quad V \to V_{reset} \text{ for } t \in [t_f, t_f + \tau_{ref}] \tag{2.5b}$$

The output of the neuron is the **spike train**: a sequence of delta functions at the spike times $t_1, t_2, \ldots$:

$$s(t) = \sum_k \delta(t - t_k) \tag{2.6}$$

## 2.3 The f-I Curve

A key characterization of a neuron is its **f-I curve**: the steady-state firing rate $f$ as a function of constant injected current $I$.

For a constant input $I$, the LIF neuron integrates toward threshold. With $V_{rest} = 0$ and $V_{reset} = 0$ for simplicity, and with $I = const$:

$$\tau_m \frac{dV}{dt} = -V + R_m I$$

The steady state is $V_\infty = R_m I$ (not necessarily reachable if $V_\infty < V_{th}$). If $R_m I > V_{th}$, the neuron fires repeatedly. The time to reach threshold from reset is:

$$V(t) = R_m I\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_m}\right)$$

Setting $V(t^*) = V_{th}$: $t^* = \tau_m \ln\!\left(\frac{R_m I}{R_m I - V_{th}}\right)$

The interspike interval is $ISI = t^* + \tau_{ref}$, giving the firing rate:

$$f(I) = \frac{1}{ISI} = \left[\tau_{ref} + \tau_m \ln\!\left(\frac{R_m I}{R_m I - V_{th}}\right)\right]^{-1} \tag{2.7}$$

valid for $I > I_{th} \equiv V_{th}/R_m$ (the rheobase current). For $I \leq I_{th}$, $f = 0$.

The f-I curve is:
- Zero for $I \leq I_{th}$ (sub-threshold; the neuron is silent).
- Increasing for $I > I_{th}$ (the neuron fires at higher rates for stronger input).
- Asymptotically approaching $1/\tau_{ref}$ for $I \to \infty$ (limited by the refractory period).

For large $I$: $\tau_m \ln(R_m I/(R_m I - V_{th})) \approx \tau_m V_{th}/(R_m I) \ll \tau_{ref}$, so $f \to 1/\tau_{ref}$.

**The gain of the f-I curve** near threshold:

$$\frac{df}{dI}\bigg|_{I \to I_{th}^+} = \frac{1}{\tau_m} \cdot \frac{R_m}{V_{th} - R_m I_{th}} \cdot \frac{1}{[\ln(\ldots)]^2}$$

which diverges as $I \to I_{th}^+$ (the neuron is infinitely sensitive near threshold). This logarithmic divergence is a distinctive feature of the LIF model and is consistent with experimental measurements of cortical neurons near their firing threshold.

## 2.4 Relationship to Hodgkin-Huxley

The Hodgkin-Huxley (HH) model [HodgkinHuxley1952] is the complete, biophysically detailed description of action potential generation. It models the membrane potential dynamics as:

$$C_m \frac{dV}{dt} = -g_{Na} m^3 h (V - E_{Na}) - g_K n^4 (V - E_K) - g_L (V - E_L) + I(t) \tag{2.8}$$

where $m, h, n \in [0,1]$ are gating variables for the sodium channel (activation $m$, inactivation $h$) and potassium channel (activation $n$), each obeying their own first-order ODEs. $g_{Na}, g_K, g_L$ are maximum conductances; $E_{Na}, E_K, E_L$ are reversal potentials.

The HH model accurately reproduces the shape of the action potential, refractory dynamics, and phenomena like spike frequency adaptation. It has four state variables per neuron.

**What the LIF preserves:**
- The subthreshold RC dynamics (equation 2.2).
- The threshold-and-reset mechanism (a severe simplification of the full Na$^+$/K$^+$ dynamics).
- The refractory period (modeled phenomenologically rather than mechanistically).
- The correct qualitative relationship between input current and firing rate (f-I curve).

**What the LIF loses:**
- The shape of the action potential (LIF produces an instantaneous spike, not a $\sim$1 mV, 1 ms pulse).
- The nonlinear subthreshold dynamics below threshold (voltage-gated Na$^+$ channels begin to open below threshold, creating a nonlinear amplification not captured by the RC model).
- Spike frequency adaptation (the progressive slowing of firing rate under sustained input, due to slow K$^+$ channels).
- Bursting dynamics (alternating periods of rapid firing and silence).
- The detailed time course of the after-hyperpolarization (AHP) following a spike.

For liquid state machine computation, what matters is primarily: (a) the timing of spikes (which encodes information via the spike train), (b) the membrane time constant (which sets the integration timescale), and (c) the f-I curve (which sets the operating point). The LIF captures all three. The additional complexity of HH is computationally expensive and, for the purposes of the LSM, does not add significant computational power.

More realistic models (like the **adaptive exponential integrate-and-fire** model, AdEx [Brette2005], or the **Izhikevich** model [Izhikevich2003]) incorporate some of the missing HH features (adaptation, bursting, resonance) at moderate computational cost, and can be used in more biologically faithful LSM simulations.

## 2.5 The LIF Network: Dynamics in a Liquid

In the LSM, we have a network of $N$ LIF neurons indexed $i = 1, \ldots, N$. Each neuron $i$ has membrane potential $V_i(t)$, and the synaptic input current $I_i(t)$ is the sum of contributions from all presynaptic neurons $j$ that have recently spiked, plus any external input.

The synaptic current from neuron $j$ to neuron $i$ due to a spike at time $t_j$ is modeled as an exponential decay:

$$I_{ij}(t) = w_{ij} \sum_{t_j \in \text{spikes}_j} e^{-(t - t_j)/\tau_{syn}} \cdot \mathbf{1}[t > t_j] \tag{2.9}$$

where $w_{ij}$ is the synaptic weight (positive for excitatory, negative for inhibitory, zero if not connected) and $\tau_{syn}$ is the synaptic time constant.

The full LIF network equations are:

$$C_m \frac{dV_i}{dt} = -\frac{V_i - V_{rest}}{R_m} + \sum_{j=1}^{N} I_{ij}(t) + I^{ext}_i(t) \tag{2.10a}$$

$$\text{If } V_i = V_{th}: \quad \text{spike at } t_i^{(k)}, \quad V_i \to V_{reset} \text{ for } \tau_{ref} \text{ ms} \tag{2.10b}$$

This system is continuous in time, unlike the discrete ESN update. Simulation requires numerical integration (e.g., Euler's method with small step size $\Delta t \ll \min(\tau_m, \tau_{syn})$) or event-driven simulation (only updating states at spike events).

The liquid state at time $t$ can be defined in several ways:
- The vector of membrane potentials $[V_1(t), \ldots, V_N(t)]$.
- The vector of filtered spike trains $[z_1(t), \ldots, z_N(t)]$ where $z_i(t) = \sum_{t_i^{(k)}} e^{-(t-t_i^{(k)})/\tau_{filt}}$.
- The vector of recent spike counts in a time window $[t-\Delta, t]$.

For practical readout computation, filtered spike trains are most common, as they provide a continuous, differentiable representation of the network's activity.
