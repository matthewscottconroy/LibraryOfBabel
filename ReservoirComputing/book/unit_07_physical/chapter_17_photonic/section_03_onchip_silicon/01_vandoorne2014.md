# 17.3.1 Vandoorne et al. 2014: On-Chip Silicon Photonic Reservoir

## Motivation: From Table-Top to Chip

The Appeltant experiment used a table-top system occupying several square meters of optical bench, built from discrete components. For practical applications — embedded computing, neuromorphic hardware, telecommunications — a chip-scale implementation is essential. Vandoorne et al. [Vandoorne2014] achieved this in 2014, implementing a 16-node passive reservoir on a silicon photonic chip.

The key word is **passive**: unlike the Appeltant system (which requires active driving of an MZI modulator), the Vandoorne chip uses only passive optical elements — waveguides and micro-ring resonators — whose nonlinear dynamics arise from the optical physics itself rather than from external electronics. This enables extremely low power consumption (in principle, only the optical input power is needed) and the potential for all-optical processing at terahertz speeds.

## Silicon Photonics Basics

Silicon photonic chips use silicon-on-insulator (SOI) waveguides to guide light at 1550 nm. The key nonlinear effects exploited in the Vandoorne reservoir are:

**Two-photon absorption (TPA).** When two photons are simultaneously absorbed by silicon, a conduction band electron is excited. TPA is proportional to the square of the intensity:

$$\frac{dI}{dz} = -\alpha_{TPA} I^2$$

where $\alpha_{TPA}$ is the two-photon absorption coefficient. TPA provides a nonlinear loss that saturates at high intensities — the essential nonlinearity for reservoir computing.

**Free-carrier dispersion (FCD).** The electrons generated by TPA change the refractive index of the silicon through the plasma effect:

$$\Delta n \approx -\frac{e^2 \lambda^2}{8\pi^2 c^2 \varepsilon_0 n_0}\left(\frac{\Delta N_e}{m_e^*} + \frac{\Delta N_h}{m_h^*}\right)$$

where $\Delta N_{e,h}$ are the electron and hole densities generated by TPA, and $m_{e,h}^*$ are effective masses. FCD introduces a dynamic phase shift that depends on the optical intensity history — providing temporal memory.

**Free-carrier absorption (FCA).** The generated carriers also absorb light, providing additional nonlinear loss.

## Micro-Ring Resonators and Coupled Mode Theory

The basic unit of the Vandoorne reservoir is the **micro-ring resonator** (MRR): a loop waveguide that couples evanescently to a straight bus waveguide. Light at a resonant wavelength is trapped in the ring and circulates, while off-resonant light passes through.

The coupled mode equations for a single ring resonator are:

$$\frac{da}{dt} = \left(-\frac{1}{\tau_e} - \frac{1}{\tau_0} + i\omega_0\right)a + \kappa s_{in}$$

$$s_{out} = s_{in} - \kappa^* a$$

where:
- $a$ is the complex field amplitude in the ring (normalized so $|a|^2$ = energy)
- $\tau_e$ is the external coupling time constant (from coupling to the bus waveguide)
- $\tau_0$ is the intrinsic loss time constant (absorption and scattering)
- $\omega_0$ is the resonant frequency of the ring
- $\kappa$ is the coupling coefficient
- $s_{in}$, $s_{out}$ are the input and output field amplitudes

The nonlinear dynamics arise from the dependence of $\omega_0$ on the intensity $|a|^2$ through TPA and FCD:

$$\omega_0(|a|^2) = \omega_{0,0} - \frac{\partial\omega_0}{\partial N_c} \cdot \alpha_{TPA}|a|^4 / (\hbar\omega_0)$$

This creates a **bistable** or **self-pulsing** response at high input powers — the silicon ring analogue of the Ikeda oscillator.

## The 16-Node Reservoir Architecture

Vandoorne et al. fabricated a chip containing **16 micro-ring resonators** coupled to each other through a mesh of waveguides. The coupling topology is a $4 \times 4$ grid: rings are coupled to their nearest neighbors on the grid. The coupling strength between rings $i$ and $j$ is controlled by the fabricated waveguide coupling length and gap.

**Light propagation through the reservoir:**
1. Input light (modulated with the task's input signal) enters from an input waveguide.
2. The input couples to the first row of rings.
3. Light propagates through the ring network, with each ring's output coupling to its neighbors.
4. At each ring, the TPA and FCD nonlinearities create input-dependent dynamics.
5. After propagating through all 16 rings, the light exits at 16 output ports.
6. Photodetectors at each output port measure the intensity $|s_{out,k}|^2$ for ring $k$. This is the reservoir state.

**Key numbers:**
- Chip area: approximately $250 \times 800$ µm²
- Ring radius: 5 µm (circumference $\sim 31$ µm; free spectral range $\sim 14$ nm at 1550 nm)
- Input power: 0.1–1 mW
- Processing speed: determined by the carrier lifetime ($\sim$ 0.5 ns) and ring photon lifetime ($\sim$ 3 ps). Potential speed: up to 200 GHz.

## Experimental Results

Vandoorne et al. evaluated the chip on two benchmarks:

**Header recognition.** A pattern recognition task from optical packet switching: identify a specific 3-bit pattern in a 12-bit optical header. This is a temporal classification task with memory depth 3. The silicon photonic reservoir achieved pattern recognition with error rate $< 10^{-3}$.

**Boolean logic.** The chip implemented XOR operations on binary inputs — a nonlinear, non-linearly-separable function. The reservoir resolved the XOR problem by exploiting the TPA nonlinearity, demonstrating that the passive chip has genuine nonlinear computational power.

**Comparison with simulation:** Vandoorne et al. compared the physical chip to a software simulation of the same network. The simulation and experiment agree well for low input powers (where TPA is weak and the coupling model is accurate), but diverge at high powers where multi-photon effects become important.

## Speed Advantages

The theoretical maximum speed of photonic reservoir computing is set by the photon lifetime in the resonator, which is of order picoseconds for a typical silicon ring. In practice, the carrier lifetime ($\sim 0.5$ ns) currently limits the speed, since the free carriers generated by TPA must recombine before the next input sample arrives.

| System | Speed | Power | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPU-based ESN (N=1000) | $\sim 10^6$ sym/s | 100 W | Standard implementation |
| Optoelectronic (Appeltant) | $\sim 10^7$ sym/s | 1–10 W | Includes electronic components |
| Silicon photonic (Vandoorne) | $\sim 10^9$ sym/s | $\sim 10$ mW | Carrier-lifetime limited |
| Theoretical all-optical | $\sim 10^{11}$ sym/s | $\sim 10$ µW | Photon-lifetime limited |

The speed advantage grows from $10^3$ (optoelectronic vs. GPU) to a potential $10^5$ (all-optical vs. GPU). Even at the current carrier-limited speed of $10^9$ sym/s, the silicon photonic chip is $10^3 \times$ faster than GPU simulation at $10^4 \times$ lower power.

**The remaining challenge** is scalability: the Vandoorne chip has only 16 nodes. A practical reservoir for difficult tasks needs $N = 100$–$1000$ nodes. Silicon photonic fabrication at this scale is achievable with current technology, but the coupling topologies become more complex and the alignment between designed and fabricated parameters becomes harder to control.
