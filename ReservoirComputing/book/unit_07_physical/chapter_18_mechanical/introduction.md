# Chapter 18: Mechanical and Soft-Body Reservoirs

## Introduction

There is something philosophically striking about the claim that a robot's body can think. Not metaphorically, not loosely — but in a precise, computationally meaningful sense. This chapter develops that claim rigorously, showing how the physical dynamics of compliant mechanical systems constitute genuine computational substrates, and how the theoretical framework of reservoir computing provides the language needed to make that idea precise.

The conventional picture of embodied robotics treats the body as a passive carrier of sensors and actuators, with all intelligence residing in a centralized controller. Morphological computation inverts this picture [PfeiferIida2004]. It proposes that the body's physical structure — its compliance, its inertia, its geometry — itself performs computations that would otherwise burden the controller. The reservoir computing framework formalizes exactly what that means: a body that possesses fading memory and a nonlinear projection of its history into a rich state space is, functionally, a reservoir.

This is not merely a theoretical curiosity. The practical consequences are substantial. A compliant robot arm, when driven by a periodic input signal, generates a high-dimensional state trajectory at its joints and contact points. That trajectory encodes a filtered history of the input in a form that a simple linear readout can exploit [HauserEtAl2011]. The arm need not be redesigned or optimized for computation; its passive mechanical properties suffice. The computation is, in the most literal sense, embodied.

### Chapter Overview

We begin in Section 18.1 by situating mechanical reservoirs within the broader theory of morphological computation, tracing the intellectual lineage from Rolf Pfeifer's embodied cognition program through Fumiya Iida's experimental work to the formal reservoir computing analysis by Hauser, Ijspeert, Füchslin, Pfeifer, and Maass. Section 18.2 examines the compliant robot arm experiment in detail — the most carefully characterized physical reservoir computing demonstration in the literature — and extracts the general principles it illustrates. Section 18.3 extends the analysis to tensegrity structures, where the global connectivity of tension and compression elements creates a particularly rich state space. Section 18.4 considers granular media, a more exotic substrate whose collective dynamics under forcing generate high-dimensional nonlinear responses. Throughout, we maintain contact with the mathematical framework developed in earlier chapters, translating physical phenomena into the language of state-space dynamics, echo state properties, and linear separability.

### A Note on Physical Reservoirs Generally

Physical reservoir computing — the use of real physical systems rather than simulated neural networks as reservoirs — has emerged as a significant research direction for reasons that go beyond the mechanical case. Optoelectronic delay systems, soft materials, chemical reaction networks, and electronic analogs all share the same functional logic: exploit the natural dynamics of a physical system, drive it with an input signal, observe its high-dimensional response, and train only a linear readout. Chapter 17 addressed optoelectronic systems; this chapter focuses on the mechanical domain. The unifying insight is that computation need not be designed into a substrate — it can be harvested from the dynamics that already exist.

The fading memory property, central to the theoretical guarantees of reservoir computing (Chapter 4), has a particularly natural interpretation in mechanical systems. Viscous damping, elastic restoring forces, and inertia together produce a system whose current state depends on its input history with exponentially decaying weighting — precisely the fading memory structure required. The challenge, as we shall see, is not whether mechanical systems possess fading memory, but whether their particular form of memory is rich enough to support the computations of interest.
