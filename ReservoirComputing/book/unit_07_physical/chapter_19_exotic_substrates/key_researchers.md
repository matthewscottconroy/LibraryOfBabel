# Chapter 19: Key Researchers

## Julie Grollier (CNRS / Thales Physics Unit, Paris)

Julie Grollier leads the Nanoelectronics group at the CNRS/Thales Physics Unit and is the most influential figure in spintronic neuromorphic computing. Her group produced the first experimental demonstration of STNO-based spoken digit recognition [TorrejonEtAl2017] and has authored the comprehensive review of spintronic neuromorphic computing [GrollierEtAl2020] that defines the field's agenda. Grollier's approach combines deep expertise in spintronics with a systems-level perspective on neuromorphic computing, making her group uniquely positioned to bridge materials science and machine learning.

**Representative works**: [TorrejonEtAl2017], [GrollierEtAl2020], [RiouEtAl2019]

## Damien Querlioz (CNRS, Orsay)

Damien Querlioz has made important contributions to the theoretical foundations of memristive computing and stochastic neuromorphic systems. His work on probabilistic synapses and the connection between memristive noise and Bayesian computation has opened new theoretical directions. Querlioz collaborated with Grollier on multiple spintronic RC papers.

**Representative works**: [QuerliozEtAl2013], [QuerliozEtAl2015]

## Leon Chua (University of California, Berkeley)

Leon Chua predicted the memristor in 1971 [Chua1971] on the basis of circuit-theoretic symmetry arguments — one of the most remarkable theoretical predictions in electronics history, vindicated 37 years later. His broader theoretical contributions to nonlinear circuit theory provide the mathematical foundations for memristive computing. Chua's subsequent work has clarified the conditions under which physical devices qualify as memristors (versus memristive systems or other related classes).

**Representative works**: [Chua1971], [ChuaKang1976], [ChuaEtAl2012]

## Dmitri Strukov (University of California, Santa Barbara)

Dmitri Strukov was the lead author of the HP Labs paper [StrukoveEtAl2008] that first reported the physical realization of a memristive device. His subsequent research has focused on memristive hardware for neuromorphic computing, including experimental demonstrations of pattern recognition in memristive crossbar arrays. Strukov's group develops both device physics and systems-level architectures for memristive computing.

**Representative works**: [StrukoveEtAl2008], [StrukoveEtAl2019]

## Kohei Nakajima (University of Tokyo)

In addition to his work on mechanical reservoirs (Chapter 18), Nakajima co-authored the foundational paper on quantum reservoir computing [FujiiNakajima2017] with Keisuke Fujii. This work established the theoretical framework for QRC: formalized the input encoding, reservoir dynamics, and measurement protocol; proved conditions for the echo state property in open quantum systems; and provided initial performance benchmarks.

**Representative works**: [FujiiNakajima2017], [NakajimaEtAl2019QRC]

## Keisuke Fujii (Osaka University)

Keisuke Fujii is a quantum information theorist who co-developed the quantum reservoir computing framework with Nakajima [FujiiNakajima2017]. His background in quantum error correction and quantum complexity theory informs the analysis of whether quantum reservoirs offer genuine advantages over classical alternatives — a question that remains open and contested.

**Representative works**: [FujiiNakajima2017], [FujiiEtAl2020]

## Wilfried Haensch (IBM Research)

Wilfried Haensch has led research on analog hardware for neural networks at IBM, including memristive and phase-change materials for in-memory computing. IBM's development of PCM (phase-change memory) arrays as neuromorphic hardware is complementary to the memristive reservoir approach and has produced important experimental benchmarks.

**Representative works**: [HaenschEtAl2018], [SebastianoEtAl2020]

## Shunsuke Fukami (Tohoku University)

Shunsuke Fukami is a leading experimentalist in spin-orbit torque devices and domain wall motion, areas closely related to skyrmion computing. His group has demonstrated controlled skyrmion nucleation and manipulation at room temperature, an important step toward experimental skyrmion reservoirs.

**Representative works**: [FukamiEtAl2016], [FukamiEtAl2020]

## Andrew Kent (New York University)

Andrew Kent has made foundational contributions to the theory and experiment of spin-transfer torque switching, including the first demonstration of spin-torque switching in a nanopillar device. His theoretical work on the stochastic switching dynamics of STNOs provides the noise models needed to assess STNO reservoir performance under realistic conditions.

**Representative works**: [KentEtAl2003], [KentOlson2015]
