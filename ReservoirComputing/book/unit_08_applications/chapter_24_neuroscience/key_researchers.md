# Chapter 24 — Key Researchers

## David Sussillo

**Affiliation:** Google Brain / DeepMind (formerly Stanford University, Shenoy Lab)

**Contributions:** Sussillo is a central figure in the application of RNN and reservoir-like models to neuroscience. His 2009 paper with Abbott [Sussillo2009] demonstrated FORCE learning — training only the readout of a random RNN — and showed that the resulting network generates coherent output patterns resembling motor commands. His 2015 *Nature Neuroscience* paper [Sussillo2015] (with Churchland, Kaufman, and Shenoy) made the connection to real neural data explicit, showing that the model's internal dynamics match motor cortex population dynamics quantitatively. He has also developed interpretability methods for trained RNNs (fixed point analysis, linear approximation) that are widely used in computational neuroscience.

**Selected publications:**
- Sussillo, D. & Abbott, L.F. (2009). Generating coherent patterns of activity from chaotic neural networks. *Neuron*, 63(4), 544–557.
- Sussillo, D., Churchland, M.M., Kaufman, M.T., & Shenoy, K.V. (2015). A neural network that finds a naturalistic solution for the production of muscle activity. *Nature Neuroscience*, 18(7), 1025–1033.

## Mark M. Churchland

**Affiliation:** Columbia University, Department of Neuroscience

**Contributions:** Churchland's 2012 *Nature* paper [Churchland2012] describing rotational dynamics in motor cortex population activity is one of the most cited papers in systems neuroscience of the last 15 years. The finding — that motor cortex activity rotates in state space during reaching — has reshaped how the field thinks about motor cortex, shifting focus from single-neuron tuning curves to population-level dynamics. Churchland has continued to develop the dynamical systems approach to motor cortex, including analysis of preparatory activity, null space constraints, and the geometry of movement-related neural manifolds.

**Selected publications:**
- Churchland, M.M. et al. (2012). Neural population dynamics during reaching. *Nature*, 487(7405), 51–56.
- Kaufman, M.T., Churchland, M.M. et al. (2014). Cortical activity in the null space. *Nature Neuroscience*, 17, 440–448.

## Krishna V. Shenoy

**Affiliation:** Stanford University, Departments of Electrical Engineering, Neurobiology, and Bioengineering (Howard Hughes Medical Institute Investigator; 1968–2023)

**Contributions:** Shenoy's lab pioneered large-scale neural population recordings in motor cortex and developed the brain-machine interface (BMI) applications that use motor cortex activity to control prosthetic limbs and computer cursors. His collaboration with Churchland and Sussillo on dynamical systems models of motor cortex [Churchland2012, Sussillo2015] provided the experimental grounding for the computational modeling work. Shenoy was also a founder of the BrainGate clinical BMI program.

## Surya Ganguli

**Affiliation:** Stanford University, Department of Applied Physics

**Contributions:** Ganguli has made foundational contributions to the theory of neural computation, including the analysis of the geometry of neural population codes, the role of network connectivity in shaping computational capacity, and the relationship between network structure and dynamics. His work on "compressed sensing" of neural population activity [Ganguli2012] provides theoretical grounding for why high-dimensional neural activity can encode low-dimensional task variables. His collaboration with Shenoy and others connects the dynamical systems and information-theoretic perspectives on cortical computation.

**Selected publications:**
- Ganguli, S. & Sompolinsky, H. (2012). Compressed sensing, sparsity, and dimensionality. *Annual Review of Neuroscience*, 35, 485–508.
- Ganguli, S. et al. (2008). Memory traces in dynamical systems. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 105(48), 18970–18975.

## Dean V. Buonomano

**Affiliation:** University of California, Los Angeles, Department of Neurobiology

**Contributions:** Buonomano has worked at the intersection of reservoir computing and neuroscience for two decades. His 2009 review with Maass [Buonomano2009] is an excellent synthesis of the state-dependent computation / reservoir framework in neuroscience. His own experimental work focuses on timing and temporal processing in cortical circuits, providing direct empirical tests of the state-dependent computation hypothesis.

**Selected publications:**
- Buonomano, D.V. & Maass, W. (2009). State-dependent computations: Spatiotemporal processing in cortical networks. *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, 10(2), 113–125.
- Buonomano, D.V. & Merzenich, M.M. (1995). Temporal information transformed into a spatial code by a neural network with realistic properties. *Science*, 267(5200), 1028–1030.

## Wolfgang Maass

**(See also Chapter 5 and Chapter 13)**

**Contributions to this chapter:** Maass is the inventor of the liquid state machine [Maass2002], the spiking-neuron precursor to ESN that is the primary theoretical model connecting reservoir computing to neuroscience. His advocacy for the reservoir/LSM framework as a model of cortical computation has been highly influential. His extensive subsequent work on the computational principles of spiking neural networks, probabilistic inference in cortical circuits, and the role of variability in neural computation continue to shape the field.

## David Marr

**Affiliation:** Massachusetts Institute of Technology (1945–1980)

**Contributions (historical):** Marr's 1969 theory of the cerebellum [Marr1969] proposed that the cerebellum learns associations between mossy fiber inputs (context) and climbing fiber inputs (desired output), using a Hebbian-like learning rule at parallel fiber → Purkinje cell synapses. This is the first computational theory of cerebellar learning and remains, with Albus's refinements, the dominant theoretical framework. Marr's broader contributions to visual neuroscience and his three-level framework for understanding neural computation (computational, algorithmic, implementational) are foundational to the entire field.

**Selected publications:**
- Marr, D. (1969). A theory of cerebellar cortex. *Journal of Physiology*, 202(2), 437–470.
- Marr, D. (1982). *Vision: A Computational Investigation into the Human Representation and Processing of Visual Information*. MIT Press. (Posthumous.)
