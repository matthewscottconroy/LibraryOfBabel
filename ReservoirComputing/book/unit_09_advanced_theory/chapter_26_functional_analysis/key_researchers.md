# Chapter 26: Key Researchers

**Karl Weierstrass (1815–1897).** German mathematician who proved the foundational polynomial approximation theorem in 1885, showing that every continuous function on a closed interval can be uniformly approximated by polynomials. His 1885 paper "Über die analytische Darstellbarkeit sogenannter willkürlicher Functionen einer reellen Veränderlichen" established this result using a convolution/smoothing argument. Weierstrass is also known for constructing the first example of a continuous, nowhere-differentiable function, demonstrating the subtlety of continuity.

**Marshall Harvey Stone (1903–1989).** American mathematician who generalized Weierstrass's theorem to arbitrary compact Hausdorff spaces in 1937 ("Applications of the Theory of Boolean Rings to General Topology"). Stone's theorem shows that the key ingredients are the algebraic structure (subalgebra) and two separation conditions (separates points, contains constants), not the specific structure of the real line. Stone made foundational contributions to spectral theory, Boolean algebras, and functional analysis.

**Leon O. Chua (1936–).** Electrical engineer at UC Berkeley, inventor of the Chua circuit (the first physical implementation of a chaotic circuit), and co-author with Stephen Boyd of the seminal 1985 paper establishing universal approximation for fading-memory systems [BoydChua1985]. Chua's work spans nonlinear circuit theory, chaos, and the foundations of computing.

**Stephen P. Boyd (1959–).** Professor at Stanford, co-author of the Boyd-Chua theorem [BoydChua1985], and author of the widely used textbook *Convex Optimization* (with Vandenberghe, 2004). Boyd's early work established the functional-analytic foundations of nonlinear system approximation; his later work focused on convex optimization and its applications in signal processing and machine learning.

**George Cybenko (1952–).** Author of the landmark 1989 paper "Approximations by Superpositions of a Sigmoidal Function" [Cybenko1989], which proved that single-hidden-layer neural networks with sigmoidal activations are universal approximators. Cybenko's proof used a functional-analytic argument (Hahn-Banach separation) to show that the only measure annihilating all network outputs is the zero measure. Cybenko is a professor at Dartmouth.

**Kurt Hornik (1957–).** Proved the universal approximation theorem for neural networks using a different approach from Cybenko, establishing density results for a broader class of activation functions [Hornik1991]. Hornik's work clarified that the multilayer structure, not the specific activation function, is what enables universality.

**Moshe Leshno, Vladimir Ya. Lin, Allan Pinkus, Shimon Schocken.** Authors of the 1993 paper "Multilayer feedforward networks with a nonpolynomial activation function can approximate any function" [Leshno1993], which gave the cleanest characterization: neural networks are universal approximators if and only if the activation function is non-polynomial. Their proof uses distribution theory (the activation function must not be a polynomial in the distributional sense).

**Ali Rahimi and Benjamin Recht.** Authors of the influential 2007 paper "Random Features for Large-Scale Kernel Machines" [Rahimi2007], which introduced the random feature perspective on kernel methods. Their framework directly connects to reservoir computing: a random reservoir implements random features for the kernel induced by the reservoir dynamics. Rahimi's famous 2017 NeurIPS "Test of Time" talk raised concerns about the theoretical foundations of deep learning that remain relevant.

**Lukas Gonon.** Researcher who has proven some of the tightest approximation and generalization bounds for echo state networks [Gonon2020, Gonon2021], establishing explicit rates for functionals in Sobolev-type spaces and deriving the first non-asymptotic generalization bounds for reservoir computing. Gonon's work bridges the gap between the existence results of Boyd-Chua and the rate theory needed for practical guarantees.

**Sergei Natanovich Bernstein (1880–1968).** Russian/Soviet mathematician who introduced the Bernstein polynomial approximation scheme in his 1912 proof of the Weierstrass theorem, giving the first fully probabilistic proof of an approximation result. Bernstein polynomials converge uniformly but slowly; they preserve shape properties (positivity, monotonicity, convexity) of the function being approximated, making them useful in computer-aided geometric design as well as in analysis.
