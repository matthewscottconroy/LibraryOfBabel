# Chapter 31: Key Researchers

**Goran Lindblad (1940–).** Swedish mathematical physicist who derived the general form of the Markovian quantum master equation in 1976 [Lindblad1976], now universally called the Lindblad equation. His paper "On the Generators of Quantum Dynamical Semigroups" provided the rigorous mathematical basis for open quantum systems dynamics. The Lindblad equation is the quantum analogue of the Fokker-Planck equation and the foundational tool for quantum reservoir computing.

**Vittorio Gorini, Andrzej Kossakowski, and E.C.G. Sudarshan.** Independently derived the same master equation as Lindblad in 1976 [GoriniKossakowski1976], for the finite-dimensional case. Their paper "Completely Positive Dynamical Semigroups of N-Level Systems" appeared simultaneously with Lindblad's work; the equation is sometimes called the GKSL equation in recognition of all four authors.

**John Preskill (1953–).** Theoretical physicist at Caltech who coined the term "NISQ" in 2018 [Preskill2018] and has been a major voice for calibrated assessment of quantum computing prospects. His lecture notes on quantum computation (available freely online) are the standard reference for quantum information theory.

**Keisuke Fujii and Kohei Nakajima.** Authors of the influential 2017 paper "Harnessing Disordered-Ensemble Quantum Dynamics for Machine Learning" [FujiiNakajima2017], one of the first systematic theoretical studies of quantum reservoir computing. They showed that quantum systems with many-body interactions can implement rich nonlinear computations and derived capacity bounds.

**Kohei Nakajima.** A leading researcher in quantum and physical reservoir computing. Nakajima and collaborators have published extensively on quantum RC theory and experiment, including studies of the role of entanglement, the memory capacity of quantum reservoirs, and comparisons with classical reservoirs [NakajimaTanaka2021].

**Sneha Ghosh and colleagues.** Authors of experimental NISQ quantum reservoir computing papers demonstrating the framework on real IBM quantum hardware [Ghosh2021]. Their work provided early empirical evidence that quantum RC is feasible on available devices, while also honestly assessing the current limitations.

**Maria Schuld and Francesco Petruccione.** Authors of *Machine Learning with Quantum Computers* [SchuldPetruccione2021], which provides the most comprehensive theoretical treatment of quantum machine learning, including quantum kernels. Their quantum kernel perspective is relevant to understanding why quantum reservoirs might provide advantage.

**Scott Aaronson (1981–).** Theoretical computer scientist at UT Austin, who has been one of the most careful and skeptical voices on quantum advantage claims. His blog (Shtetl-Optimized) and papers [AaronsonChen2017] provide rigorous frameworks for distinguishing genuine quantum advantage from artifacts of the comparison. His work is essential reading for understanding the simulation argument of Section 31.1.2.

**Lukas Coolidge, Ilya Drozdov, and colleagues at IBM Quantum.** The engineering teams at IBM, Google, IonQ, and other companies who have built and operate the NISQ hardware described in Section 31.3. Their technical papers on device characterization, error rates, and system calibration are the primary sources for hardware specifications.

**Manuel Matthiessen and colleagues.** Recent contributors to photonic quantum reservoir computing, exploiting the temporal modes of quantum optical systems. Photonic platforms offer natural time-multiplexing for quantum reservoirs, analogous to the optical delay-line reservoirs of Chapter 12.
