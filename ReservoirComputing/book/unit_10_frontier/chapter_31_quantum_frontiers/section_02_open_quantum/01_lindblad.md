# Section 31.2: Open Quantum Systems as Reservoirs

## 31.2.1 Closed vs. Open Quantum Systems

A *closed* quantum system evolves unitarily: $\rho(t) = U(t)\rho(0)U(t)^\dagger$ for a unitary $U(t) = e^{-iHt/\hbar}$. Unitary evolution is reversible: the state at time 0 can always be recovered from the state at time $t$. This is problematic for reservoir computing, which requires the reservoir to *forget* its initial state (ESP). A unitary quantum system is the opposite of fading memory — it perfectly preserves all information.

An *open* quantum system is coupled to an environment (bath). The coupling causes decoherence — the loss of quantum coherence to the environment — and dissipation. Open quantum systems can forget initial conditions, which is precisely what we need for reservoir computing.

**Quantum analogue of fading memory.** For a quantum reservoir, fading memory means that the density matrix $\rho(t)$ converges to a steady state $\rho_{\text{ss}}$ independent of the initial state $\rho(0)$ as $t \to \infty$, when driven by a constant input. This is the quantum analogue of the echo state property.

## 31.2.2 The Lindblad Master Equation

The Lindblad (or GKSL) master equation [Lindblad1976, GoriniKossakowski1976] is the most general form of the equation of motion for the density matrix of an open quantum system:

$$\frac{d\rho}{dt} = \mathcal{L}(\rho) = -\frac{i}{\hbar}[H, \rho] + \sum_{k}\left(L_k \rho L_k^\dagger - \frac{1}{2}\{L_k^\dagger L_k, \rho\}\right),$$

where:
- $H$ is the Hamiltonian (Hermitian), governing the unitary part of the dynamics.
- $\{L_k\}$ are the *Lindblad operators* (or jump operators), describing the coupling to the environment.
- $[A, B] = AB - BA$ is the commutator.
- $\{A, B\} = AB + BA$ is the anti-commutator.

The Lindblad equation preserves: (1) Hermiticity of $\rho$, (2) $\text{tr}(\rho) = 1$, (3) positive semidefiniteness of $\rho$ (i.e., $\rho$ remains a valid density matrix). These three conditions are the quantum analogue of a probability distribution being non-negative and summing to 1.

**Physical interpretation of each term:**
- $-\frac{i}{\hbar}[H, \rho]$: Unitary (coherent) evolution generated by $H$. This term alone gives the von Neumann equation for closed systems.
- $L_k \rho L_k^\dagger$: "Jump" term — the system emits a quantum of energy/information to the environment through channel $k$, corresponding to a quantum jump described by $L_k$. This is the quantum analogue of a stochastic transition.
- $-\frac{1}{2}\{L_k^\dagger L_k, \rho\} = -\frac{1}{2}(L_k^\dagger L_k \rho + \rho L_k^\dagger L_k)$: Decay term — ensures trace preservation by balancing the "in-flow" (jump terms).

**Example: Two-level system (qubit) with decay.** For a qubit with $H = \omega_0 \sigma_z / 2$ (energy splitting) and a single jump operator $L = \sqrt{\gamma} \sigma_-$ (decay from excited to ground state at rate $\gamma$):
$$\frac{d\rho}{dt} = -\frac{i\omega_0}{2}[\sigma_z, \rho] + \gamma\left(\sigma_- \rho \sigma_+ - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_+\sigma_- \rho - \frac{1}{2}\rho \sigma_+\sigma_-\right).$$

The steady state is $\rho_{\text{ss}} = |0\rangle\langle 0|$ (ground state), regardless of initial conditions. This is fading memory: the qubit forgets its initial state.

## 31.2.3 Decoherence as Fading Memory

Decoherence — the decay of quantum coherences (off-diagonal elements of $\rho$ in a preferred basis) — is the quantum mechanism of fading memory. Physically, decoherence occurs because the quantum system is entangled with the environment, and tracing out the environment suppresses the coherences.

**Dephasing.** Consider a qubit with $L = \sqrt{\gamma_\phi} \sigma_z$ (dephasing channel):
$$\frac{d\rho}{dt} = -\frac{i}{\hbar}[H, \rho] + \gamma_\phi\left(\sigma_z \rho \sigma_z - \rho\right).$$

This preserves the diagonal elements of $\rho$ (populations) but decays the off-diagonal elements (coherences): $\rho_{01}(t) = \rho_{01}(0) e^{-2\gamma_\phi t}$. The coherences decay exponentially with rate $2\gamma_\phi$, regardless of the initial state. This is fading memory for the coherences.

**General decoherence.** For a general Lindblad equation with multiple jump operators, the density matrix converges to a steady state $\rho_{\text{ss}}$ at a rate determined by the *Liouvillian gap* — the spectral gap of the superoperator $\mathcal{L}$. The Liouvillian gap plays the role of the contraction rate in the classical ESP:

$$\|\rho(t) - \rho_{\text{ss}}\|_1 \leq \|\rho(0) - \rho_{\text{ss}}\|_1 \cdot e^{-\Delta_{\mathcal{L}} t},$$

where $\Delta_{\mathcal{L}} = -\text{Re}(\lambda_1) > 0$ is the gap between the zero eigenvalue (corresponding to $\rho_{\text{ss}}$) and the next eigenvalue of $\mathcal{L}$.

**Quantum ESP.** A quantum reservoir with input-dependent Lindblad equation $\mathcal{L}_u$ has the *quantum echo state property* if for every input sequence $(u(0), u(-1), \ldots)$ satisfying a fading-memory condition, there is a unique density matrix $\rho^*$ (the quantum echo state response) such that $\rho(t) \to \rho^*$ regardless of initial conditions.

## 31.2.4 The Quantum Reservoir as a CPTP Map

For discrete-time quantum reservoir computing, the input-driven evolution over one time step is described by a CPTP map $\mathcal{E}_u$:

$$\rho(t+1) = \mathcal{E}_{u(t)}(\rho(t)),$$

obtained by discretizing the Lindblad equation or by directly specifying the quantum channel.

**Universal quantum reservoir.** A quantum reservoir is *universal* if, for any fading-memory functional $H: U^{\mathbb{Z}_{-}} \to \mathbb{R}$, there exists a quantum reservoir and linear readout that approximates $H$. Quantum universality theorems analogous to Boyd-Chua have been proved [GharbiNakajima2021]:

**Theorem 31.2.1 (Quantum Universality, informal).** *A quantum reservoir with sufficient qubits and a generic Hamiltonian coupling to the input achieves universal approximation of fading-memory functionals, in the sense of the Boyd-Chua theorem.*

The proof follows the classical argument: the observables $\{\text{tr}(O_k \rho)\}$ (for Pauli observables $O_k$) are polynomial functionals of the input (via the input-dependence of $\rho$), they separate points (since Pauli operators span the full operator algebra), and they form an algebra under products (products of observables are observables). Apply Stone-Weierstrass.

## 31.2.5 Measurement as Readout

The readout in quantum reservoir computing is the measurement of observables on the quantum state $\rho(t)$:
$$y_k(t) = \text{tr}(O_k \rho(t)), \quad k = 1, \ldots, m.$$

For a quantum system with $N$ qubits, the natural observables are tensor products of Pauli matrices: $O_{ijk\ldots} = \sigma_{i_1} \otimes \sigma_{i_2} \otimes \cdots \otimes \sigma_{i_N}$ where $\sigma_{i_j} \in \{I, X, Y, Z\}$. There are $4^N$ such operators, forming a basis for the full operator space.

In practice, quantum measurements are projective and destructive: measuring $O$ on $\rho$ produces outcome $\lambda$ with probability $\text{tr}(P_\lambda \rho)$ and collapses $\rho$ to $P_\lambda \rho P_\lambda / \text{tr}(P_\lambda \rho)$. This is fundamentally different from classical readout (which is non-destructive). To extract multiple observables, one must either:
1. Prepare many copies of $\rho$ and measure each copy once (quantum state tomography — expensive: $O(4^N)$ measurements for full tomography).
2. Use ancilla qubits and indirect measurements.
3. Measure only a small set of observables (most practical).

**Practical readout.** In NISQ implementations (Section 31.3), the readout consists of measuring each qubit in the computational basis, giving a binary string $b \in \{0,1\}^N$. The expectation value $\langle Z_i \rangle = \text{tr}(Z_i \rho)$ is estimated by averaging over $M$ measurements:
$$\hat{y}_i = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M (-1)^{b_i^{(m)}}.$$
This gives $N$ expectation values with standard error $O(1/\sqrt{M})$. For $N = 10$ qubits and $M = 1000$ measurements per time step, the readout provides 10 features with accuracy $\approx 3\%$ — comparable to what is achievable with $N = 10$ classical neurons.

## 31.2.6 Noise as Feature vs. Noise as Bug

In classical reservoir computing, noise in the reservoir dynamics is generally detrimental: it perturbs the echo state response and reduces accuracy. In quantum reservoir computing, decoherence (the quantum analogue of noise) plays a dual role:

**Noise as Feature (beneficial)**:
- Decoherence provides the fading memory: without dissipation, the reservoir would be a closed unitary system and would not satisfy the ESP.
- The combination of unitary evolution (coherent dynamics, generating complex nonlinear correlations) and decoherence (dissipation, implementing fading memory) is precisely what a good reservoir needs.
- Optimal quantum reservoir performance may require tuning the decoherence rate — too little and the system does not forget; too much and the reservoir state is too noisy.

**Noise as Bug (detrimental)**:
- In NISQ devices (Section 31.3), uncontrolled noise (gate errors, readout errors, crosstalk) degrades reservoir states in ways that are not the useful fading-memory kind.
- Noise accumulates over many time steps, eventually dominating the reservoir dynamics.
- Current NISQ devices have gate error rates of $\sim 0.1-1\%$, limiting useful circuit depth to $O(100)$ gates.

The optimal quantum reservoir may be one where the decoherence is *controlled* (implemented via intentional Lindblad operators with chosen jump rates) rather than uncontrolled (NISQ noise). This requires either quantum error correction (not yet available at scale) or specially designed open quantum systems with tunable dissipation.
