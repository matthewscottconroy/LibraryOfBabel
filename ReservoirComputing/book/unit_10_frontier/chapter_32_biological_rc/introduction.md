# Chapter 32: Biological Reservoir Computing

## Introduction

Reservoir computing was partly inspired by neuroscience — the hypothesis that the cerebellum and other recurrent neural circuits act as biological reservoirs, with fixed recurrent dynamics and trained readout layers [Mauk1997, JaegerHaas2004]. In recent years, this relationship has become reciprocal: researchers have begun using actual biological neurons as the physical substrate of reservoir computers.

This chapter examines biological reservoir computing at multiple levels: theoretical, experimental, and philosophical. The theoretical question is what we can learn from biology about reservoir design. The experimental question is what specific demonstrations have achieved, assessed as honestly as possible. The philosophical question is what the use of living neural tissue for computation implies ethically.

We focus on three topics:

**Section 32.3** covers the DishBrain experiments [Kagan2022], in which cortical neurons grown on multi-electrode arrays were connected to a Pong-like game and appeared to learn to keep the ball in play. This is the most widely discussed experiment in biological RC and requires careful analysis: what was actually demonstrated, what the authors claim, and what is contested.

**Section 32.4** addresses the ethical and philosophical implications. When biological neurons are used for computation, questions about sentience, moral status, and obligation arise. These questions do not have settled answers. We present the main philosophical positions and the relevant neuroscience, and we argue that uncertainty itself generates moral obligations.

**Section 32.5** draws lessons from biological implementations for reservoir design: heterogeneity, noise, plasticity, and energy efficiency.

A note on tone: this chapter is the most contested in the book, scientifically and philosophically. We aim to be accurate about what is known, honest about what is uncertain, and appropriately cautious about claims in both directions. Where the science is contested, we say so. Where the philosophy is unresolved, we say so. The reader who approaches this material with intellectual humility will come away with a richer understanding of both reservoir computing and the nature of biological intelligence.
