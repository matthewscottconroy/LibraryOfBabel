# Chapter 32: Key Concepts

**Multi-Electrode Array (MEA).** A chip containing arrays of electrodes (typically 64–512) that can both record extracellular potentials from nearby neurons and deliver electrical stimulation. The bidirectional interface used in the DishBrain experiment. MEAs allow real-time closed-loop interaction with biological neural networks.

**In Vitro Neurons.** Neurons cultured outside the body in a controlled environment. Can be derived from primary tissue (e.g., embryonic mouse cortex) or from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). Maintain basic electrophysiological properties of neurons but lack the full architectural context of an intact brain.

**Cortical Organoid.** A 3D brain-like structure grown from iPSCs that self-organizes into layers resembling early cortical development. Can reach 2-4mm diameter and exhibit coordinated spontaneous electrical activity. The current frontier of in vitro neural complexity, raising active ethical debates about moral status.

**DishBrain.** The system described in Kagan et al. (2022, *Neuron*) in which cortical neurons on MEAs were connected to a simplified Pong game via bidirectional stimulation and readout. The neurons exhibited above-chance performance that improved over sessions, interpreted by the authors as evidence for goal-directed behavior under the free energy principle.

**Free Energy Principle.** A theoretical framework from Karl Friston [Friston2010] in which biological systems minimize "free energy" (an information-theoretic quantity related to prediction error) as a unified explanation of perception, action, and learning. Used by the DishBrain authors to interpret the neurons' behavioral improvement. The framework is influential but controversial in its claims of universality.

**Hard Problem of Consciousness.** The philosophical problem, articulated by David Chalmers [Chalmers1995], of explaining why there is subjective experience at all — why physical processes in the brain feel like something from the inside. Distinguished from the "easy problems" of explaining cognitive functions. No scientific or philosophical consensus on how to resolve it.

**Biological Naturalism.** The philosophical position (Searle) that consciousness is a biological phenomenon produced by specific causal mechanisms in neural tissue, not reducible to computation or information processing. Under this view, the biological substrate is necessary but not sufficient for consciousness.

**Functionalism.** The philosophical position that mental states are defined by their functional role — their causal relations to inputs, outputs, and other mental states — not by their physical substrate. Under functionalism, any system that implements the right functional organization is conscious, regardless of whether it is made of neurons or silicon.

**Integrated Information Theory (IIT).** A theory of consciousness (Tononi et al.) identifying consciousness with integrated information $\Phi$ — a measure of how much information is generated by a system above and beyond its parts. A system is conscious to the degree that it has high $\Phi$. Makes specific predictions about which physical systems are conscious. [Tononi2016]

**Precautionary Principle (bioethics).** Under moral uncertainty about whether a system has morally relevant properties, err on the side of caution and treat the system with appropriate moral consideration. Applied to biological RC: if there is non-trivial probability that neuronal cultures experience something, obligations arise to minimize potential distress.

**Heterogeneity (reservoir design).** A reservoir property where neurons differ in their time constants, thresholds, or activation functions. Biological neurons are highly heterogeneous. Heterogeneous reservoirs can represent a wider range of temporal features than homogeneous ones.

**Short-Term Plasticity.** Rapid (~ms to ~s timescale) changes in synaptic efficacy based on recent activity. Short-term depression: synaptic weight decreases with repeated activation (high-pass filtering). Short-term facilitation: synaptic weight increases with repeated activation (low-pass filtering). Implementing short-term plasticity in reservoir models expands the temporal feature set.

**Homeostatic Plasticity.** Slow (~hours to days) adaptation of neuronal excitability that maintains overall activity within functional bounds. Prevents both runaway excitation and quiescence. In reservoir computing, homeostatic adaptation could self-tune the reservoir to operate near the edge of chaos.

**Small-World Network.** A network topology with high local clustering (cliques) and short average path lengths between nodes (efficient global communication), achieved by a small fraction of long-range connections. Associated with efficient information transmission. The Watts-Strogatz model interpolates between ring lattices and random graphs.

**Neuromorphic Computing.** Computing hardware designed to mimic the structure and dynamics of biological neural systems: event-driven (spiking), massively parallel, analog-digital mixed-signal, energy-efficient. Examples: Intel Loihi, IBM TrueNorth, BrainScaleS. Directly implements reservoir computing principles in hardware with orders-of-magnitude better energy efficiency than GPUs for appropriate tasks.

**Sparse Coding.** The principle that at any given moment, only a small fraction of neurons are active. Reduces metabolic cost, reduces correlations between neural representations, and is thought to maximize information capacity per unit energy. Motivates sparse reservoirs and sparse activation functions.
