# Chapter 32: Key Researchers

**Brett Kagan and colleagues at Cortical Labs (2022).** Lead author of the DishBrain paper [Kagan2022] and CEO of Cortical Labs, the company commercializing biological computing using neurons on chips. The DishBrain work brought biological reservoir computing to wide public attention. Kagan's group continues to develop the OrganoID platform for neural organoid computing.

**Karl Friston (1959–).** Neuroscientist at University College London, developer of the free energy principle and active inference framework. The DishBrain paper used Friston's framework to interpret the neurons' behavior. Friston is among the most-cited neuroscientists in the world, and his free energy framework is influential in computational neuroscience, though it is not universally accepted.

**David Chalmers (1966–).** Philosopher at New York University, author of the hard problem of consciousness [Chalmers1995] and *The Conscious Mind* (1996). Chalmers' framing of the hard problem is essential background for the philosophical discussion in Section 32.4. Chalmers has also written on AI consciousness and has engaged directly with questions about the moral status of synthetic and biological neural systems.

**Giulio Tononi (1960–).** Neuroscientist and psychiatrist at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, developer of Integrated Information Theory (IIT) [Tononi2016]. IIT provides one of the few quantitative frameworks for assessing consciousness in physical systems, including in vitro neurons. Tononi's work is controversial in philosophy of mind but has stimulated important empirical and theoretical debate.

**Alysson Muotri (1975–).** Neuroscientist at UC San Diego who has developed some of the most complex brain organoids, including organoids connected to robotic arms and organoids with spontaneous electrophysiological activity resembling pre-term infant EEG [Trujillo2019]. His work has been at the forefront of both the science and the ethics of brain organoid research.

**Wolfgang Maass (1950–).** Professor at TU Graz, co-developer (with Henry Markram and Thomas Natschläger) of Liquid State Machines [MaassNatschläger2002], one of the two foundational frameworks of reservoir computing. Maass's LSM framework was explicitly inspired by biological neural circuits, especially the mammalian neocortex. His work connecting computational neuroscience and machine learning has been foundational.

**Thomas Natschläger.** Co-author with Maass on the original LSM paper and on subsequent work developing biologically realistic reservoir models, including models with short-term synaptic plasticity. His work on the computational consequences of short-term plasticity (Section 32.5.3) directly motivates biological-inspired reservoir designs.

**Henry Markram (1962–).** Neuroscientist at EPFL, co-developer of the Blue Brain Project and a founding contributor to Liquid State Machines. Markram's large-scale reconstruction of cortical microcircuits provides detailed biological templates for reservoir architectures.

**Carver Mead (1934–).** Pioneer of neuromorphic engineering — the design of silicon chips that mimic biological neural computation. His 1990 paper "Neuromorphic Electronic Systems" [Mead1990] initiated the field. Mead's insight that analog CMOS circuits can efficiently implement the dynamics of biological neurons is the foundation of neuromorphic computing hardware.

**Giacomo Indiveri.** Head of the neuromorphic cognitive systems group at ETH Zurich and INI Zurich. A leading developer of neuromorphic chips and circuits for spike-based computing. His work on the BrainScaleS system and other neuromorphic platforms demonstrates the practical viability of biological-inspired hardware for reservoir computing.
