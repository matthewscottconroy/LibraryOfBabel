# Chapter 1 — Further Reading

## Primary sources

**Shannon, C. E. (1948). "A Mathematical Theory of Communication." *Bell System
Technical Journal* 27(3): 379–423 and 27(4): 623–656.**
Read at least the first six pages. The diagram discussed in §1.2 is on the second
page, and the sentence about semantics being "irrelevant to the engineering
problem" is on the first. The mathematics gets demanding around section 12, but
the prose framing of the problem is accessible to anyone who has finished this
chapter, and it is worth seeing how carefully Shannon states what he is and is not
claiming. Freely available from Bell Labs and widely mirrored.

**Nyquist, H. (1924). "Certain Factors Affecting Telegraph Speed." *Bell System
Technical Journal* 3(2): 324–346.**
The bandwidth–symbol-rate relationship, twenty-four years before Shannon. Short,
and notable for how much of it is about the practical business of telegraph
apparatus rather than theory.

**Hartley, R. V. L. (1928). "Transmission of Information." *Bell System Technical
Journal* 7(3): 535–563.**
The logarithmic measure of information, pre-entropy. Reading Hartley and then
Shannon shows precisely what Shannon added: the probabilities.

**Thomson, W. (1855). "On the Theory of the Electric Telegraph." *Proceedings of
the Royal Society of London* 7: 382–399.**
The law of squares. Heavy going, but it is the first quantitative treatment of a
transmission line as something other than a wire, and the moment cable
engineering became a science.

## Books

**Standage, T. (1998). *The Victorian Internet.* Walker & Company.**
The history of the electric telegraph told as a communications-network story:
the codes, the operators, the fraud, the romances conducted over the wire, the
regulatory panics. Short, extremely readable, and structurally useful — nearly
every social phenomenon attributed to the Internet appeared first on the
telegraph, which is a good corrective to the field's chronic presentism.

**Gleick, J. (2011). *The Information: A History, a Theory, a Flood.* Pantheon.**
The best general-audience treatment of what information *is*, running from African
talking drums through Babbage and Shannon to modern data. The Shannon chapters are
excellent and require no mathematics.

**Soni, J. & Goodman, R. (2017). *A Mind at Play: How Claude Shannon Invented the
Information Age.* Simon & Schuster.**
A biography that takes both halves of Shannon seriously — the theorist and the
man who built a mechanical mouse. Good on the intellectual context at Bell Labs.

**Gordon, J. S. (2002). *A Thread Across the Ocean: The Heroic Story of the
Transatlantic Cable.* Walker & Company.**
The full 1858/1866 story, including Whitehouse, Thomson, and the astonishing
logistics of laying 2,500 nautical miles of cable from a converted warship.

**Kurose, J. F. & Ross, K. W. *Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach.*
Pearson, 8th edition (2020).**
The standard university text, and the principal alternative to the approach taken
here — it works downward from applications rather than upward from signals. Worth
owning as a second perspective; its chapter 1 covers the same ground as this one
with a very different emphasis. Where this book and Kurose–Ross disagree about
*order*, they rarely disagree about *fact*.

**Tanenbaum, A. S., Feamster, N. & Wetherall, D. *Computer Networks.* Pearson,
6th edition (2021).**
The other standard text, bottom-up like this book and considerably more detailed
on the physical layer. Chapter 1's treatment of network hardware taxonomy is
particularly good.

## Standards and reference documents

**RFC 1122, *Requirements for Internet Hosts — Communication Layers* (1989).**
Not readable end to end at this stage, but skim §1.1.3 for the clearest official
statement of what the Internet architecture assumes about hosts. We will return to
it in Unit V.

**ITU-T Recommendation G.652, *Characteristics of a single-mode optical fibre and
cable*.**
Where the refractive index figures used in §1.1 come from. You do not need to read
it; you should know that numbers like "204 km/ms" have a document behind them
rather than being folklore.

## For the certification-minded

**CompTIA Network+ N10-009 Exam Objectives (free download from CompTIA).**
Read the objectives document once now, early, and then ignore it until Unit XIII.
Reading it now tells you the shape of the destination; reading it repeatedly
before you understand the mechanisms is the failure mode this book was written to
avoid. Appendix D maps every objective to a chapter.
