# Chapter 1 — The People

**Claude Elwood Shannon (1916–2001).** American mathematician and engineer.
His 1937 MIT master's thesis, *A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching
Circuits*, showed that Boolean algebra could describe electrical switching
circuits — arguably the single most useful master's thesis ever written, and the
foundation of all digital logic design. His 1948 *A Mathematical Theory of
Communication*, published in the *Bell System Technical Journal*, created
information theory in essentially finished form: the bit as a unit, entropy as a
measure, the separation of source coding from channel coding, and the capacity
theorem that Chapter 4 develops. He worked at Bell Labs from 1941 to 1956 and
then at MIT. He also built a juggling machine, a rocket-powered frisbee, a
flame-throwing trumpet, and a mechanical mouse that solved mazes, and rode a
unicycle down the Bell Labs corridors. The combination of the two halves of that
biography is not incidental to how he thought.

**William Thomson, Lord Kelvin (1824–1907).** Scots-Irish mathematical physicist,
professor at Glasgow from the age of twenty-two. His analysis of signal
propagation in submarine cables — the "law of squares," published in 1855 —
correctly predicted that signalling rate would fall as the square of cable length
due to capacitance, and was the first quantitative treatment of what we now call
intersymbol interference. He invented the mirror galvanometer and later the siphon
recorder, receivers sensitive enough to read the faint, smeared signals that
Whitehouse's brute-force approach could not. He was aboard for the cable-laying
expeditions and was knighted for the 1866 cable's success. He is also the reason
your thermometer has an absolute scale.

**Wildman Whitehouse (1816–1890).** English surgeon who appointed himself chief
electrician of the Atlantic Telegraph Company. He rejected Thomson's mathematics
in favour of his own experiments, applied approximately 2,000 volts to the 1858
cable in an attempt to force signals through faster, and destroyed its insulation
within three weeks. The subsequent inquiry ended his career. He appears in this
book not as a villain but as a permanent category: the confident practitioner
who mistakes a signal-integrity problem for a power problem. Chapter 42 meets him
again with a Wi-Fi antenna.

**Claude Chappe (1763–1805).** French engineer who built the first practical
long-distance telecommunications network — an optical semaphore system whose first
line, Paris to Lille, opened in 1794 and carried news of a military victory in
about half an hour. At its height the French network ran 556 stations over
4,800 km. Chappe coined the word *télégraphe*. He died by suicide in 1805, in part
over disputes about priority for the invention. His system is the earliest
engineering example of a relay network with an explicitly designed symbol
alphabet, error-detection conventions, and a codebook — that is, a protocol stack.

**Samuel Morse (1791–1872) and Alfred Vail (1807–1859).** Morse, a portrait
painter by profession, promoted and financed the American electric telegraph; Vail,
his mechanically gifted partner, did much of the practical design. The code that
bears Morse's name — and which Vail is generally credited with shaping — assigns
short symbols to frequent letters and long ones to rare letters, a decision made by
counting the type in a printer's font case. This is variable-length source coding,
arrived at empirically in 1838, and it anticipates Shannon's source coding theorem
by 110 years. Nobody involved could have said why it worked.

**Harry Nyquist (1889–1976).** Swedish-born American engineer at Bell Labs whose
1924 paper *Certain Factors Affecting Telegraph Speed* established the relationship
between a channel's bandwidth and the maximum symbol rate it can carry without
intersymbol interference. Chapter 4 develops the result; it is half of the answer
to "how fast can this channel go," and Shannon supplied the other half
twenty-four years later. Nyquist also gave his name to the sampling theorem and,
with John Johnson, to the analysis of thermal noise.

**Ralph Hartley (1888–1970).** Bell Labs researcher whose 1928 paper *Transmission
of Information* proposed measuring information as the logarithm of the number of
distinguishable symbol sequences — the direct predecessor of Shannon's entropy and
the origin of the "hartley," the base-10 unit of information. Shannon cited both
Nyquist and Hartley in his opening paragraph as the work he was building on.
