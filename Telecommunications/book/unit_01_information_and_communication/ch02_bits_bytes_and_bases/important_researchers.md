# Chapter 2 — The People

**George Boole (1815–1864).** English mathematician, largely self-taught, who
became professor at Queen's College Cork without ever holding a degree. His *An
Investigation of the Laws of Thought* (1854) developed an algebra of two values in
which logical propositions could be manipulated symbolically. He believed he was
formalising human reasoning. He was, incidentally, providing the mathematics of
every digital circuit ever built, eighty-three years before anyone noticed.

**Claude Shannon (1916–2001).** His 1937 MIT master's thesis connected Boole's
algebra to relay switching circuits, making digital design a matter of calculation
rather than intuition. His 1948 paper adopted "bit" as the unit of information and
established the logarithmic measure. See Chapter 1's notes for the fuller
biography.

**John Wilder Tukey (1915–2000).** American statistician at Bell Labs and
Princeton, who coined "bit" in a 1947 internal memorandum — Shannon credits him in
a footnote. Tukey also coined "software," in 1958, and invented the box plot, the
stem-and-leaf display, and (with James Cooley) the Fast Fourier Transform
algorithm that Chapter 5 relies on. Few people have contributed useful vocabulary
and useful algorithms in comparable measure.

**Ralph Hartley (1888–1970).** Established in 1928 that information should be
measured logarithmically, because a logarithmic measure makes information additive
under composition of independent choices. Shannon's entropy is Hartley's measure
generalised to unequal probabilities.

**Rolf Landauer (1927–1999).** German-American physicist at IBM. His 1961 paper
"Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing Process" established that
erasing information has an unavoidable thermodynamic cost. The principle was
experimentally confirmed in 2012 by a group at ENS Lyon using a single colloidal
particle in an optical trap. It is the tightest known link between information
theory and physics.

**Émile Baudot (1845–1903).** French telegraph engineer whose 1870 five-bit code
and synchronous multiplexing system allowed several operators to share one
telegraph line — a direct ancestor of the time-division multiplexing in
Chapter 9. His code introduced shift states to extend a 32-symbol alphabet, and
his name became the unit of symbol rate. He worked for the French postal
telegraph service for most of his career and received almost none of the money his
patents generated.

**Bob Bemer (1920–2004).** American computer scientist who chaired much of the
ASCII standardisation effort at ANSI and who is credited with introducing the
escape sequence, the backslash, and the concept of registered code pages. He also
spent decades, from 1971 onward, publicly warning about the two-digit year problem
that became Y2K, and was largely ignored until the late 1990s.

**Ken Thompson (b. 1943) and Rob Pike (b. 1956).** Bell Labs researchers, both
central to Unix and Plan 9. In September 1992 they designed UTF-8 — according to
Pike's account, on a placemat in a New Jersey diner, in response to an
unsatisfactory proposal circulating in the X/Open committee — and had it
implemented in Plan 9 within days. Thompson had earlier co-created Unix, B (the
predecessor of C), and the regular-expression engine; Pike later co-designed the
Go programming language. UTF-8 is arguably the most widely deployed piece of
protocol design either of them did, and it took an evening.

**Danny Cohen (1937–2019).** Israeli-American computer scientist. His 1980 IEN 137
memo *On Holy Wars and a Plea for Peace* introduced "big-endian" and
"little-endian" from *Gulliver's Travels*, argued that the choice of byte order
was arbitrary but that standardising one was essential, and settled network byte
order for the Internet. He also did foundational work on real-time voice over
packet networks in the 1970s — packet voice on the ARPANET in 1973, two decades
before VoIP became a product category.

**Jonathan Swift (1667–1745).** Anglo-Irish satirist. *Gulliver's Travels* (1726)
contains the war between Lilliput and Blefuscu over which end of an egg to break,
which Cohen used to name the byte-order dispute. Included here because the joke is
load-bearing: Swift's point was that the parties were fighting over something
arbitrary, which is precisely Cohen's point about byte order, and the reason the
memo persuaded people where a purely technical argument would not have.
