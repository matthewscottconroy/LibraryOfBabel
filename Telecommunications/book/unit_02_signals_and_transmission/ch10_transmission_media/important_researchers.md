# Chapter 10 — The People

**Charles Kao (1933–2018).** Shanghai-born, educated in Hong Kong and London, and
working at Standard Telecommunication Laboratories in Harlow when he and George
Hockham published the 1966 paper arguing that optical fibre's catastrophic
attenuation was caused by **impurities rather than by glass**. The best optical
glass of the day lost about 1,000 dB/km; Kao argued that pure silica should manage
20 dB/km, which would be workable.

The insight was only half the work. He then spent several years travelling to glass
manufacturers, arguing that they should attempt a purity nobody had asked for, for a
market that did not exist. Corning succeeded in 1970. He received the Nobel Prize in
Physics in 2009, by which time he had been diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease; his
wife Gwen accepted much of the public role. Modern fibre achieves 0.17 dB/km — some
six thousand times better than the glass he was arguing against.

**George Hockham (1938–2013).** Kao's co-author, whose contribution was the
waveguide analysis showing that a fibre of the required dimensions would actually
guide light in a single mode with tolerable loss from imperfections. The paper needed
both halves and Hockham is usually omitted from the story.

**Robert Maurer (b. 1924), Donald Keck (b. 1941) and Peter Schultz (b. 1942).**
The Corning team who made the glass. In 1970 they produced fibre with under
20 dB/km using a fused-silica core doped with titanium, then improved it with
germanium doping. Keck's laboratory notebook entry for the successful measurement
reportedly reads, in full, "Eureka!" — one of the more justified uses of the word.
Their patent on low-loss fibre became one of the most valuable in the industry.

**John Tyndall (1820–1893).** Irish physicist who demonstrated in 1854 that light
could be guided along a curved stream of water pouring from a tank — total internal
reflection made visible to a Royal Institution audience. He was demonstrating a
principle already described by Daniel Colladon in Geneva in 1841, and the
demonstration is usually credited to Tyndall because he was better at publicity. The
principle sat unused for communications for over a century.

**Narinder Singh Kapany (1926–2020).** Indian-American physicist who coined the
term **fibre optics** in 1956 and did much of the early work on bundled fibres for
image transmission — the ancestor of the endoscope rather than of the
telecommunications cable. He is frequently described as the father of fibre optics
and was notably not included in the 2009 Nobel award, which caused some comment.

**Oliver Heaviside (1850–1925).** The coaxial cable is his patent, granted in 1880,
and the transmission-line theory in §10.2's impedance discussion is his. See
Chapter 6's notes for the fuller account of a remarkable and badly treated career.

**Herbert Frisch and the Bell Labs coaxial teams.** The characteristic impedance
optima that produce 50 Ω and 75 Ω were worked out at Bell Labs in the 1920s and
1930s, and the 50 Ω compromise between minimum attenuation (77 Ω) and maximum power
handling (30 Ω) is documented in their internal work. The fact that two standards
exist rather than one is a genuine engineering result rather than an accident.

**Robert Metcalfe (b. 1946) and the 10BASE-T working group.** Metcalfe's Ethernet
began on coax; the move to twisted pair in 10BASE-T (1990) was driven by people who
had operated coaxial segments and wanted fault isolation and reuse of existing
telephone cabling. §10.2's account of why coax lost the LAN is fundamentally their
argument, and it is worth noting that the medium that won was electrically worse.
Chapter 16 covers Metcalfe properly.

**David Payne (b. 1944).** The erbium-doped fibre amplifier, without which
long-haul fibre would need electrical regeneration every 40 km per wavelength. See
Chapter 9's notes; his work is what turned Kao's medium into an economic
proposition.

**Michael Marcus (b. 1946).** The FCC engineer who drove the 1985 decision opening
the ISM bands to unlicensed spread-spectrum use, described in Chapter 43. He has
said that the bands were regarded internally as spectral wasteland and that the
proposal met considerable resistance. What was built on that wasteland is most of
§10.4's subject and a large fraction of the modern world's local connectivity.
