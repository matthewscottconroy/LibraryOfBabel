# 37.3 Retransmission and Timers

§37.2 established *what* to retransmit. This section is *when* — and it is a harder
question than it appears, because the answer must adapt to a network whose delay TCP
cannot know in advance and which changes while the connection runs.

## The two recovery mechanisms

**TCP recovers from loss in two ways, and the difference in cost is enormous.**

| Mechanism | Triggered by | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Fast retransmit** | **3 duplicate ACKs** (§37.2) | **~1 RTT** |
| **Retransmission timeout (RTO)** | **a timer expiring** | **at least 200 ms, often ~1 s, sometimes minutes** |

**Fast retransmit is the good case** and requires that **more data was in flight behind the
lost segment** — the duplicate ACKs are generated by later segments arriving.

**The RTO is the fallback**, and it fires when there is nothing behind the loss to generate
duplicates: the last segment of a transfer, a small request, or a window's worth of data
all lost at once.

> **A single loss at the end of a transfer costs an RTO. The same loss in the middle costs
> one RTT.** This asymmetry explains a great deal of otherwise mysterious latency.

## Estimating the round-trip time

**The RTO must be longer than the RTT** — otherwise TCP retransmits data that is merely in
flight, wasting bandwidth and, worse, confusing its own congestion control (Chapter 38).

**And it must not be much longer**, or recovery is slow.

**So TCP measures.**

### The original estimator

**RFC 793's smoothed RTT**, an exponentially weighted moving average:

$$\text{SRTT} \leftarrow \alpha \cdot \text{SRTT} + (1-\alpha) \cdot \text{RTT}_{\text{sample}}$$

with α ≈ 0.9, so each new sample moves the estimate by a tenth.

$$\text{RTO} = \beta \cdot \text{SRTT}, \quad \beta = 2$$

**And it was not good enough.** The problem is that **it tracks the mean and ignores the
variance.** On a path whose RTT varies between 50 ms and 500 ms, an RTO of twice the mean
fires constantly on the slow samples — and each spurious retransmission is treated as
congestion, so the connection slows down for no reason.

### Jacobson's estimator

**Van Jacobson, 1988**, and it is the version every stack uses.

**Track the mean deviation as well as the mean:**

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Err} &= \text{RTT}_{\text{sample}} - \text{SRTT} \\
\text{SRTT} &\leftarrow \text{SRTT} + g \cdot \text{Err} & (g = 1/8) \\
\text{RTTVAR} &\leftarrow \text{RTTVAR} + h \cdot (|\text{Err}| - \text{RTTVAR}) & (h = 1/4) \\
\text{RTO} &= \text{SRTT} + 4 \cdot \text{RTTVAR}
\end{aligned}$$

**The RTO now adapts to the path's *stability*, not merely its speed.**

| Path | SRTT | RTTVAR | RTO |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stable fibre | 20 ms | 2 ms | **28 ms** |
| Congested link | 20 ms | 40 ms | **180 ms** |
| Satellite | 600 ms | 20 ms | **680 ms** |

**Row 2 is the point.** Same mean as row 1, and a much longer RTO — because the path is
erratic and a short timeout would fire spuriously.

**The gains are all in the fractions and shifts.** Jacobson chose *g* = 1/8 and *h* = 1/4
so the arithmetic is **shifts and adds with no multiplication or floating point** — which
mattered enormously on 1988 hardware and still means the calculation costs nothing.

**RFC 6298 mandates a minimum RTO of 1 second**, which is conservative and is the reason a
timeout-based recovery is so expensive. Linux uses 200 ms, deviating deliberately.

## Karn's algorithm

**A subtle problem, with an elegantly blunt solution.**

**When a segment is retransmitted and an ACK arrives, which transmission is it for?**

```
   Send seq 5000  ──────────▶  (lost? or slow?)
   ...RTO fires...
   Retransmit 5000 ─────────▶
                   ◀────────── ACK 6000
```

**Was that ACK triggered by the original or the retransmission?** TCP cannot tell — the ACK
looks identical either way.

**And guessing wrong corrupts the estimator:**

- **Assume the retransmission**, when it was actually the original: the measured RTT is far
  too short, so the RTO shrinks, so more spurious retransmissions follow
- **Assume the original**, when it was the retransmission: the RTT is far too long, so the
  RTO grows and recovery becomes sluggish

**Karn's algorithm (1987): do not measure the RTT of any retransmitted segment at all.**

**Simply refuse to take an ambiguous sample.** Measure only segments sent once.

**With one addition — exponential backoff:**

$$\text{RTO} \leftarrow 2 \times \text{RTO} \quad \text{on each successive timeout}$$

**Because without it, refusing to measure means the RTO never adapts during a period of
loss** — which is exactly when it needs to. Doubling on each timeout provides the
adaptation that measurement cannot.

**The backoff sequence:** 1 s, 2 s, 4 s, 8 s, 16 s, 32 s, 64 s… **This is why a connection
to an unreachable host takes minutes to fail** rather than seconds, and why the retries in
a capture are so distinctively spaced.

**Timestamps remove the ambiguity.** With the timestamp option (RFC 7323), an ACK echoes
the timestamp of the segment it acknowledges, **so the sender knows exactly which
transmission it refers to** and can measure retransmitted segments safely. **Modern stacks
use timestamps and fall back to Karn's algorithm when the option is unavailable.**

## Fast retransmit and fast recovery

**The good path**, and the two halves are distinct.

**Fast retransmit** (§37.2): three duplicate ACKs → retransmit the missing segment
immediately, without waiting for the RTO.

**Fast recovery**: **do not drop back to slow start.** The duplicate ACKs prove that
segments are still arriving, so the path is not broken — it is merely lossy. **Halve the
congestion window and continue** (Chapter 38 §38.2), rather than restarting from one
segment.

**Together they are "Reno"**, and the improvement over "Tahoe" — which did drop to slow
start — is substantial on any path where a single loss is not a sign of collapse.

**NewReno** (RFC 6582) fixes Reno's behaviour when *several* segments are lost in one
window, where Reno would exit recovery too early and take an RTO for the second loss.
**SACK-based recovery** (RFC 6675) does better still, because it knows precisely what is
missing (§37.2).

## Tail loss — and the modern fixes

**The case fast retransmit cannot help**, and it matters more than its name suggests.

**If the *last* segments of a transfer are lost, there is nothing behind them to generate
duplicate ACKs.** The sender waits for the RTO — **hundreds of milliseconds, for a transfer
that was otherwise complete.**

**And short transfers are almost all tail.** A web request of three segments that loses the
third has no fast-retransmit path at all.

**This is why page-load latency is dominated by loss far more than bandwidth would
suggest**, and two mechanisms address it:

**Tail Loss Probe (TLP)** — after a short timer (~2 × SRTT, much less than the RTO), send
one probe segment. **If data was lost, the probe generates a duplicate ACK and triggers
fast retransmit.** Recovery in ~2 RTTs instead of an RTO.

**RACK — Recent ACKnowledgement** (RFC 8985) — **a fundamental rethink**: instead of
counting duplicate ACKs, declare a segment lost if a segment sent *later* has been
acknowledged and enough time has passed.

> **RACK replaces "three duplicates" with "time-based reasoning about what should have
> arrived by now."**

**It handles reordering better** (a reordered segment is not immediately declared lost) and
**tail loss better** (no duplicates are needed), and it is now the default in Linux and in
QUIC.

## Reading retransmissions

```
$ tcpdump -nn -S
10.0.0.5.51234 > 203.0.113.10.443: Flags [P.], seq 1000:2460, length 1460
10.0.0.5.51234 > 203.0.113.10.443: Flags [P.], seq 1000:2460, length 1460   ← same seq
```

**The same sequence number sent twice is a retransmission.** Wireshark flags them:

| Wireshark label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **TCP Retransmission** | sent again after a timeout |
| **TCP Fast Retransmission** | sent again after duplicate ACKs |
| **TCP Dup ACK** | the receiver is signalling a gap |
| **TCP Out-Of-Order** | arrived out of sequence — **not necessarily loss** |
| **TCP Spurious Retransmission** | retransmitted data the receiver already had |

**"Spurious retransmission" is the interesting one.** It means the RTO fired too early, or
an ACK was lost — **the network is fine and TCP is wasting bandwidth on it.** Frequent
spurious retransmissions suggest an RTO that is too aggressive or a path with high
variance.

**And per-connection, live, without a capture** (Chapter 35 §35.4):

```
$ ss -tni
    cubic wscale:7,7 rto:236 rtt:35.5/2.1 mss:1448 cwnd:24
    bytes_sent:14200000 bytes_retrans:18000 retrans:0/12
```

**`rtt:35.5/2.1`** is **SRTT and RTTVAR** — Jacobson's two estimators, visible per
connection.

**`rto:236` is not simply SRTT + 4×RTTVAR**, which would be 43.9 ms here. **Linux clamps
the RTO to a minimum of 200 ms** (`TCP_RTO_MIN`) and rounds to its clock granularity, so
on any low-latency path the reported RTO is the floor rather than the computed value.

**This is worth knowing when reading the field:** a stable LAN connection will show
`rto:2xx` regardless of how good its RTT is, and that is the minimum asserting itself, not
a sign of instability. **The formula only becomes visible when SRTT + 4×RTTVAR exceeds the
floor** — on a satellite path, or a congested one, where the numbers become interesting.

**`retrans:0/12`** — twelve retransmissions over the connection's life, none outstanding.

## What breaks here

**A transfer that stalls for exactly a second, repeatedly.** RTO-based recovery, probably
tail loss. Check for TLP and RACK support.

**Connections taking minutes to fail.** Exponential backoff. Normal, and it is why
application-level timeouts are needed.

**Frequent spurious retransmissions.** The RTO is too aggressive, or ACKs are being lost.

**Terrible throughput at low loss rates.** Recovery is falling back to RTO rather than fast
retransmit — check SACK and check whether losses are at the tail.

**Retransmissions in a capture with no actual loss.** Look for duplicate ACKs; if there are
none, the sender's timer is firing early.

> **Network+ note.** Objective 1.4 expects TCP reliability. Over-learn: **TCP retransmits
> unacknowledged data**; **three duplicate ACKs trigger fast retransmit**; **the RTO is
> derived from a measured RTT and its variance**; and **the RTO doubles on each successive
> timeout.** The exponential backoff explains the "why does it take so long to fail"
> question that appears in troubleshooting scenarios.
