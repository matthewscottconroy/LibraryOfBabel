# Chapter 43 — The People

**Michael Marcus (b. 1946).** The FCC engineer who drove the **1985 decision permitting
unlicensed spread-spectrum use of the ISM bands** — §43.1's accident.

**The reasoning was technical and the consequence was economic.** Spread-spectrum
techniques, developed for military anti-jamming, could tolerate the noise sources already in
those bands and could coexist with each other, **in a way narrowband signals could not.** So
a band nobody wanted for communication could be opened to it.

**He has said the proposal met substantial internal resistance**, on the reasonable ground
that nobody could predict what would be built there — **which is exactly why it worked.** A
band with no licensing, no coordination and no permission required is a band anyone can
experiment in.

> **Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Zigbee, and most of the Internet of Things exist because of a
> regulatory decision made about spectrum considered worthless.**

**Marcus has argued since that the lesson has not been fully learned** — that regulators
still allocate by predicted use rather than by opening space for unpredicted use. Chapter 72
§72.3 returns to the argument.

**Hedy Lamarr (1914–2000) and George Antheil (1900–1959).** Frequency-hopping spread
spectrum — Chapter 42's notes give the story, and it belongs here because **spread spectrum
is what made §43.1's decision technically defensible.**

**Paul Baran (1926–2011).** Chapter 13's subject, and relevant here for the same reason: his
distributed-network work at RAND was motivated by survivability, and **spread spectrum's
resistance to jamming is the radio equivalent** of packet switching's resistance to node
loss.

**Vic Hayes (b. 1941).** **Chair of the IEEE 802.11 working group from its formation in 1990
until 2000**, and reasonably called "the father of Wi-Fi".

**The contribution was standardisation rather than invention.** Several incompatible wireless
LAN products existed by 1990 — NCR's WaveLAN, Proxim's, Symbol's — **and each was a
proprietary island.** Hayes's committee produced a specification that let them
interoperate.

**And the choice to build on the ISM bands** (§43.1) was the committee's, following Marcus's
decision — **which is why Wi-Fi is free to deploy and why it has the interference problems it
has.** A licensed-band wireless LAN would have been cleaner and would not have been
deployable by anyone without a licence.

**The NCR / AT&T Nieuwegein team**, including **Bruce Tuch** and **Cees Links**, built
WaveLAN in the late 1980s and much of it became 802.11's basis.

**Cees Links** has written honestly about the commercial history — including that **the
market for wireless LANs was widely believed not to exist** until Apple shipped AirPort in
1999 at a price point that made it ordinary rather than exotic.

**Steve Jobs and the 1999 AirPort launch** deserve a mention for that reason. **The
technology existed and was expensive and niche**; a laptop with it built in, at a consumer
price, created the demand. **Chapter 28's lesson about deployability inverted — sometimes the
barrier is not incentive but packaging.**

**The IEEE 802.11 working group's channel planners.** The 1/6/11 arrangement of §43.2 is not
in any standard as a recommendation — **it is a consequence of the channel spacing and width
the standard specifies**, worked out by operators.

**Which is worth noting:** the most repeated piece of advice in wireless networking is
**folklore in the precise sense** — knowledge transmitted by practice rather than by
specification, and correct.

**The ITU-R and the World Radiocommunication Conferences.** Unglamorous and load-bearing.
**WRC-19 and WRC-23 are where the 6 GHz allocations of §43.1 were argued out**, between
incumbent licensed users, mobile operators wanting more licensed spectrum, and the
unlicensed community.

**The outcome differed by region** — 1,200 MHz in the US, 480 in Europe — **because the
national positions differed**, which is why §43.3's tables have two columns and why a
multinational wireless design cannot assume uniformity.

**The AFC designers.** Automated Frequency Coordination — §43.1's database-mediated sharing —
is the genuinely new idea in recent spectrum policy.

**Neither exclusivity nor free-for-all**, but a service that knows where incumbents operate
and tells a device what it may do where. **It is the model that makes higher-power
unlicensed use of occupied bands possible at all**, and it is being extended to other bands.

**Its weakness is that it introduces a dependency:** a device that cannot reach the AFC
service cannot use standard power. **Which is a coordination requirement in a regime whose
whole value was requiring none** — and the tension is unresolved.

**Jim Zyren, Al Petrick, and the many engineers of the 802.11 amendment process.** Chapter 44
covers the standards; what belongs here is that **each amendment had to work in a band
already occupied by everything that came before.**

**Backward compatibility is why 2.4 GHz carries the legacy burden of §43.3** — an 802.11b
device from 2000 still associates, and the protection mechanisms that make it possible cost
every other client on the radio. **The alternative was breaking existing installations**, and
the committee consistently chose compatibility.

**Chapter 21 §21.1's argument about independent evolution has a cost, and this is where it
is paid.**
