# Chapter 54 — Monitoring and Telemetry

"Is the network slow?"

You cannot answer that question. Not because you lack tools, but because the question
is comparative and you have nothing to compare against. Slow relative to what?
Yesterday? Last month? The day it was installed? Some notion of what it ought to do?

Without a **baseline**, every performance question is unanswerable and every
performance discussion is an exchange of impressions. This is the chapter's first and
most important point, and it has an inconvenient corollary: **the baseline must be
collected before you need it**, during the period when everything is fine and nobody
is asking. The moment you need one is the moment it is too late to start.

## What a baseline actually is

Not a single number. A distribution, over time, of the quantities that matter,
captured with enough granularity to show the shape of a day and a week:

- Interface utilisation, in and out, per link
- Error and discard counters
- Latency and jitter to a set of reference destinations
- CPU and memory on network devices
- Wireless channel utilisation and client counts
- Application response times where you can measure them

The value is not in the absolute figures but in the **shape**. A link at 60% at
14:00 on a Tuesday means nothing until you know it is normally at 25% at 14:00 on a
Tuesday. Deviation from the expected shape is the signal; absolute thresholds are a
crude proxy for it.

Two details that separate a useful baseline from a decorative one.

**Averaging interval matters enormously.** A link averaging 40% over five minutes may
be at 100% for twenty seconds of every minute — and those twenty seconds are where
the user's video call lives (Chapter 3 §3.2's queueing curve). Five-minute averages
hide microbursts completely. Where it matters, sample faster.

**Percentiles beat averages.** The 95th percentile of latency tells you what your
unluckiest users experience; the mean tells you almost nothing about them. Almost
every complaint is generated by the tail.

## SNMP, and its awkward truths

§54.2 covers the protocol that still does most of the world's device monitoring.

The model is a **MIB** — a hierarchical namespace of numbered objects — from which a
manager retrieves values by **OID**, either by polling (the manager asks) or by trap
(the device volunteers). Standard MIBs cover interfaces, system information and much
else; vendors add their own.

The awkward truths, stated plainly because they affect deployment decisions:

**v1 and v2c have no security.** Authentication is a "community string" — a shared
password — sent in **cleartext**. Anyone who can capture a packet can read your
device inventory and, if the string is read-write, reconfigure your equipment. The
default strings are `public` and `private` and they remain in use on production
equipment in 2026 with dispiriting frequency. **v3** adds authentication and
encryption and should be used; the reason it often is not is that it is more work to
configure, which is not a good reason.

**Polling has a resolution floor.** Polling every five minutes cannot see a
thirty-second event. Polling every ten seconds across ten thousand interfaces is a
substantial load on the devices and the network. This tension is what streaming
telemetry was invented to resolve.

**Counters wrap.** A 32-bit octet counter on a 10 Gb/s interface wraps in about three
seconds at full rate. Monitoring systems must use the 64-bit high-capacity counters,
and a system that silently uses the 32-bit ones produces nonsense on fast links —
which appears as impossible traffic spikes and is a genuinely confusing artefact.

## Logs, and correlation

§54.3 covers syslog: its eight severity levels from Emergency (0) to Debug (7), its
facilities, and — the operationally important part — **centralisation**.

Logs stored on the device that generated them are useless in exactly two situations:
when the device has failed, and when you need to correlate its behaviour with another
device's. Both are the situations you have logging for. Centralise.

Correlation requires accurate time, which requires NTP, which is why Chapter 41 §41.3
insisted that clock skew is a networking problem. Log entries from two devices whose
clocks differ by four minutes cannot be sequenced, and sequencing is the entire point.

§54.3 also covers the discipline of **log levels in production**: debug-level logging
generates volume that overwhelms storage and, on some platforms, the device's own CPU,
and there are documented outages caused by logging configuration alone.

## Flow records: the question counters cannot answer

An interface counter tells you a link is at 90%. It does not tell you **what is on
it**, and that is almost always the next question.

**NetFlow** (and its standardised successor **IPFIX**, and the sampling-based
**sFlow**) records metadata per flow: the five-tuple, byte and packet counts, start
and end times, and interface and next-hop information. From that you can answer: which
hosts, which applications, which conversations, which direction, and how the mix has
changed.

This is the tool that turns "the circuit is full" into "the circuit is full because
one host is uploading to a cloud storage service," which is a statement someone can
act on. §54.4 covers the collection architecture, the sampling tradeoff, and the
privacy considerations, which are real — flow data is a detailed record of who
communicated with whom, and it should be governed accordingly.

**Streaming telemetry** is the modern successor: instead of the manager polling, the
device pushes structured data continuously, modelled in YANG and transported by gNMI
(Chapter 70 §70.2). Sub-second granularity without polling load. It is where the
industry is going and adoption is uneven.

## Alerting, which is where monitoring succeeds or fails

The most important page in the chapter, and the one most often absent from treatments
of this subject.

**An alert nobody acts on is worse than no alert.** It consumes attention, it trains
people to ignore the channel, and it provides false assurance that the system is being
watched. Alert fatigue is not a soft problem; it is the mechanism by which real
incidents are missed, and it has a documented role in several serious outages.

The rules §54.4 develops:

- **Alert on symptoms users experience**, not on every threshold. "Users cannot reach
  the application" beats "CPU is 81%."
- **Every alert must have an action.** If the answer to "what do I do about this" is
  "nothing, it clears itself," it is not an alert. It is a graph.
- **Alert on trend and duration**, not on instantaneous values. A link at 95% for five
  minutes is worth knowing; a link at 95% for two seconds is a backup starting.
- **Review alerts that fired and were ignored**, monthly, and delete or fix them. This
  is the single practice that keeps a monitoring system trustworthy.
