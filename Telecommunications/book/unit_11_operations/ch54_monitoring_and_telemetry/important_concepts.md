# Chapter 54 — Important Concepts

**"Is the network slow?" is unanswerable without a baseline** *(§54.1)* — The question is
comparative. **Without a reference, every performance discussion is an exchange of
impressions.**

**A baseline must be collected before you need it** *(§54.1)* — **During the period when
everything is fine and nobody is asking.** Which makes it an investment against a future
incident, **made by people who cannot demonstrate its value until the incident happens** — and
that is why so few organisations have one.

**The value is in the shape, not the figures** *(§54.1)* — **A link at 60% at 14:00 on a Tuesday
means nothing until you know it is normally 25% at 14:00 on a Tuesday.** **Deviation from the
expected shape is the signal; absolute thresholds are a crude proxy for it.**

**A five-minute average of 40% is consistent with line rate a third of the time** *(§54.1)* —
**And those seconds are where the user's video call lives.** Five-minute averages hide
microbursts completely, and **microbursts are the commonest cause of "the graph looks fine and
it isn't."**

**Discards on an interface that is not congested on average is the microburst signature**
*(§54.1)* — **The device already counts it**, so you can detect bursts without changing the
polling rate. **One of the most useful single observations in operational networking.**

**Percentiles beat averages for anything a person waits for** *(§54.1)* — **Almost every
complaint is generated by the tail.** A mean that has not moved is entirely consistent with a
p99 that has tripled. **And percentiles do not compose** — you cannot compute a day's p95 from
its hours' p95s.

**A baseline has several periods** *(§54.1)* — Daily, weekly, **monthly (month-end reporting)**,
**annual (the retail peak, the academic year)**, and event-driven. **A system that alerts on
"unusually high" without knowing about month-end will alert every month, and within three
months nobody will read it.** **The annual cycle needs a year of data to see.**

**Three uses of a baseline want different things from it** *(§54.1)* — **Anomaly detection**
compares against the same time last week; **capacity planning** fits a trend to the busy hour
and **produces a date, not a percentage**; **change validation** compares the hour after against
previous days — **the most under-used and nearly free.**

**Downsample rather than delete** *(§54.1)* — Five-minute data for a month, hourly for a year,
daily forever. **Capacity trends need months and seasonality needs a year**, and the storage is
trivial.

**INFORM is a trap that is acknowledged** *(§54.2)* — **A trap is UDP with no acknowledgement,
so a trap lost in transit is simply lost** — and it is most likely to be lost precisely when the
network is in trouble. **Use INFORM for anything you care about.**

**A community string is a cleartext password in every request** *(§54.2)* — Anyone capturing a
packet reads your **interface names, addresses, topology, serial numbers and software
versions**, and with a read-write string can reconfigure the device. **The defaults are `public`
and `private` and they are still in production use.** **There is essentially no legitimate use
for SNMP read-write; disable SET.**

**A 32-bit octet counter wraps in 3.4 seconds at 10 Gb/s** *(§54.2)* — 34 seconds at 1 Gb/s.
**Polling every five minutes therefore produces a number with no relationship to reality**, and
**the symptom is impossible spikes appearing intermittently on fast links.** **Use `ifXTable`'s
64-bit counters** — which require v2c or above, **a practical reason to abandon v1.**

**Polling has a resolution floor, and the load falls on the control-plane CPU** *(§54.2)* —
10,000 interfaces at 10-second intervals is 1,000 requests per second. **Aggressive polling has
caused outages** — a device spending its CPU answering the monitoring system rather than
processing routing updates. **That tension is what streaming telemetry was invented to
resolve**, and it is the honest reason SNMP is being replaced.

**SNMP is universal, simple and mature** *(§54.2)* — **Everything supports it**, a GET is one
packet with no session state, and every failure mode is known. **It will still be in service in
twenty years alongside its replacement**, exactly as Chapter 50 §50.1's T1 circuits are.

**Syslog severities are inverted: lower is worse** *(§54.3)* — **0 Emergency to 7 Debug**, and
**"level 6" means informational and above, i.e. everything except debug.** The priority field is
$\text{facility} \times 8 + \text{severity}$.

**Centralise, for three independent reasons** *(§54.3)* — **The device may be gone** and took
its last messages with it; **correlation requires one place**; and **local buffers are a few
thousand lines that wrap in seconds during the event you care about.**

**Log entries from devices whose clocks differ cannot be sequenced, and sequencing is the
point** *(§54.3)* — **You cannot tell whether the firewall event caused the router event or
followed it, and causality is what an investigation establishes.** **Log in UTC, always** — local
time produces an hour that occurs twice a year, or not at all, **and an estate spanning
timezones produces logs that cannot be merged.** **Monitor the clock offset itself, because NTP
failing silently is the normal failure mode.**

**There are documented outages caused by logging configuration alone** *(§54.3)* — Debug output
left enabled, or an unrate-limited ACL log on a busy rule, **consuming the control-plane CPU
until the device stopped forwarding.** **And console logging is synchronous on many platforms**
— at 9,600 baud a burst of messages can stall forwarding. **`logging console` off; buffered plus
a collector instead.**

**The commonest investigation error is filtering too early** *(§54.3)* — **Searching for `error`
finds errors; the event that caused the incident may have been logged as informational.**
**Start from a timestamp, widen, and read the sequence.** And **a message that appears every
five minutes and then does not** is frequently more informative than a new one.

**Alert on the absence of an expected message** *(§54.3)* — A device that logs a successful
backup nightly and stops is telling you something, **and no threshold will catch it.**

**A flow record answers what a counter cannot** *(§54.4)* — **"The circuit is full" becomes "the
circuit is full because one host is uploading 40 GB to a cloud storage service"** — which is a
statement someone can act on.

**NetFlow/IPFIX keep a flow cache; sFlow samples packets** *(§54.4)* — **Exact and costly in
memory and CPU**, against **stateless, ASIC-implementable and statistical.** **NetFlow answers
"exactly how much did this conversation transfer?"; sFlow answers "what is the mix,
approximately?"** They are not substitutes. **IPFIX is NetFlow v9 standardised and extensible;
new deployments should use it.**

**Sampling is accurate for elephants and blind to mice** *(§54.4)* — At 1:2000 an 8 GB transfer
yields thousands of samples and **a 300-packet reconnaissance scan yields 0.15.** **Capacity
analysis is fine sampled; security analysis is not**, because the scan, the exfiltration and the
beacon are all small flows. **And record the sampling rate with the data** — a collector scaling
counts without knowing it produces confident, wrong numbers.

**Flow export is UDP, template-dependent and voluminous** *(§54.4)* — **Records lost in
congestion are lost silently**, and congestion is when you want them. **A restarted collector
cannot decode anything until the next template arrives.** **3,500 flows/s is 300 million records
and about 15 GB a day** before indexing.

**Flow data survives encryption, and that is why it matters both ways** *(§54.4)* — **TLS hides
the content; it does not hide that the conversation occurred.** **Metadata alone establishes
that a device contacted a medical, legal or political service, with timestamps** — which is
personal data in many jurisdictions, and **is why flow analysis became more valuable as
encryption spread.**

**Streaming telemetry: the device pushes structured data continuously** *(§54.4)* — **Sub-second
granularity without polling load, and YANG models that are typed and named** rather than numeric
OIDs — **which is what makes automated analysis practical.** **Adoption is uneven and honestly
so**; the practical position is telemetry where it exists and SNMP everywhere else, for years
yet.

**An alert nobody acts on is worse than no alert** *(§54.4)* — It consumes attention, **trains
people to ignore the channel**, and provides false assurance. **Alert fatigue is the mechanism
by which real incidents are missed.**

**The four rules** *(§54.4)* — **Alert on symptoms users experience**, not thresholds. **Every
alert must have an action** — if the answer is "nothing, it clears itself", it is a graph, not
an alert. **Alert on trend and duration**, because a link at 95% for two seconds is a backup
starting. **Review alerts that fired and were ignored, monthly, and delete or fix them** — the
single practice that keeps a monitoring system trustworthy, and the one that is never done.

**"Page" must be a small list** *(§54.4)* — **Adding to it requires justifying that a human must
act within minutes.** Most things do not meet that bar, **and treating them as if they do is how
the bar stops being respected.**

**A silent monitoring system looks identical to a healthy network** *(§54.4)* — **Dead-man's-
switch alerting** is the answer. And **alert on certificate and contract expiry** — the most
predictable outages in this book, which still happen because nobody is watching a date.
