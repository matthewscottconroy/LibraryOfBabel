# Proof vs. Persuasion

## Overview
Rhetoric persuades by appealing to emotions, authority, and plausibility. Proof compels by
showing that denial leads to contradiction. This distinction — between psychological
convincingness and logical necessity — is one of the great intellectual achievements of
ancient Greece and remains the foundation of mathematics and formal science.

## Learning Objectives
- Articulate the difference between proof and persuasion
- Identify cases where plausible arguments fail
- Explain why mathematical proof is independent of the prover's authority

## The Poverty of Intuition
Intuition fails repeatedly in mathematics:
- The Banach-Tarski paradox: a ball can be decomposed and reassembled into two balls of
  the same size (using the Axiom of Choice)
- The Monty Hall problem: most people's strong intuition is wrong
- Space-filling curves: a 1D path can cover all of 2D space

Proof is necessary precisely because intuition cannot be trusted.

## Authority vs. Proof
In mathematics, *who* says something is irrelevant to whether it is true. Euler made errors.
Cauchy published a "proof" of a false theorem. The only arbiter is the proof itself.
This is the objectivity of mathematics: a valid proof compels assent from any rational agent.

## Proof and Explanation
There is a distinction between proofs that *establish* and proofs that *explain*.
A proof by exhaustive case checking may establish truth without revealing why.
An elegant proof reveals the underlying structure. Both are valid; both have value.
