# Higher-Order Plurals and the Semantics of Mathematics

Plural logic began as a way to hear the Geach–Kaplan sentence correctly; it has become a standard instrument in the foundations of mathematics. This closing section surveys the developments after Boolos — the systematic logics of Oliver–Smiley, the superplural hierarchy of Rayo and Linnebo, and the applications where plural quantification does foundational work: Lewis's reduction of set theory to mereology plus plurals, the plural reading of the axioms of set theory, and the defense of absolutely unrestricted generality.

## Systematizing Plural Logic: Oliver and Smiley

Alex Oliver and Timothy Smiley's *Plural Logic* (2001 sequence of papers; book 2013, 2nd ed. 2016) is the most thorough formal treatment. Where Boolos used $\prec$ and plural variables, they build a full plural language: genuine **plural terms** (proper plural names like "Whitehead and Russell," plural descriptions, plural function values), a distinction between **distributive** and **collective** predicates, and — controversially — an empty plural term **"zilch"** together with a treatment of *empty* and *co-partial* plural terms so that plural definite descriptions never fail to denote. Their logic makes precise the collective/distributive ambiguity of Section 1 ("the students lifted the piano": together, or each?) and supplies a semantics on which plural predication is basic rather than reduced to singular predication. Byeong-uk Yi (*The Logic and Meaning of Plurals*, 2005–06) and Thomas McKay (*Plural Predication*, 2006) develop parallel systems, McKay emphasizing a plural metalanguage that keeps the whole apparatus set-free.

## Superplurals and the Plural Hierarchy

If we can quantify plurally over individuals, can we quantify *plurally over pluralities* — refer to some pluralities at once, superplurally? Natural-language candidates are debated: "these people, those people, and the others" seems to group pluralities; the rows of a stadium, or "the Kaufmann and the Neumann buildings" as two campuses each a plurality of buildings. **Superplural variables** $XX, YY$ range over pluralities-of-pluralities, with a new inclusion $xx \prec\!\!\prec XX$ ("the $xx$ are among the $XX$"), respecting the same sortal discipline that blocked paradox before.

- **Rayo, *Beyond Plurals* (2006)** and **Linnebo & Rayo, *Hierarchies Ontological and Ideological* (Mind 2012)** develop the hierarchy systematically, arguing that just as plurals interpret monadic second-order logic, superplurals interpret *third*-order logic, super-superplurals fourth-order, and so on up a **type hierarchy** that runs in step with the orders of higher-order logic — but, the authors argue, without ascending an ontological hierarchy of ever-more-abstract objects. The ideology grows; the ontology need not.
- **Skeptics** (Gabriel Uzquiano weighs both sides; Ben-Yami and others doubt genuine superplurals in English) question whether natural language really exhibits the higher levels, making the higher hierarchy a formal extrapolation more than a datum. The formal systems are consistent regardless (the type discipline blocks the paradoxes at every level); what is contested is their linguistic and philosophical warrant.

Rayo and Yablo's earlier *Nominalism Through De-Nominalization* (2001) and Rayo's *Word and Objects* (2002) supply the semantic underpinning: a compositional semantics for plural (and higher plural) languages that treats plural quantification as primitive, so that "there are some things such that…" is interpreted directly rather than paraphrased into set talk — the semantic counterpart of the innocence thesis.

## Megethology: Set Theory from Mereology plus Plurals

David Lewis's *Parts of Classes* (1991) and *Mathematics is Megethology* (1993) put plural logic to its most ambitious foundational use, and forge the link to Chapter 16. Lewis showed that the parthood relation of classes coincides with the subclass relation, so that a class *is* the mereological fusion of its members' singletons and the only irreducibly set-theoretic primitive is the **singleton function** $x \mapsto \{x\}$. Combining **mereology** (Chapter 16) with **plural quantification**, Lewis then reconstructs the singleton function *structurally*: quantifying plurally over the atoms and using fusion to build wholes, one can simulate the full apparatus of set theory — indeed of full second-order logic — given only hypotheses about *how many* atoms there are. Hence the name: mathematics reduces to **megethology**, the theory of size. The two ingredients are exactly this chapter and the next; neither alone suffices (mereology forgets cardinality, Section 2 of Chapter 16; singular first-order logic cannot express infinity), but together they recover the strength of set theory with a nominalist-friendly base.

## The Plural Reading of Set Theory and Absolute Generality

Plural quantification also illuminates set theory from *within*. Boolos, in *Iteration Again* (1989), reads the ZF axioms plurally: Separation becomes "for any set $a$ and condition $\phi$, there are some things — the members of $a$ satisfying $\phi$ — and they form a set," making the existential force of comprehension explicit and separating the *plurality* (which always exists, by P-Comp) from the *set* (which exists only when the plurality is "not too big"). This is the natural home of the **limitation-of-size** doctrine: every plurality that is not *too large* forms a set; the Russell plurality and the universal plurality exist as pluralities but form no set, which is why the paradoxes do not arise.

That in turn is the modern tool for defending **absolute generality** — the coherence of quantifying over *absolutely everything*, sets included (Cartwright; Williamson; the Rayo–Uzquiano volume *Absolute Generality*, 2006). The objection to absolute generality runs: to interpret "everything" one needs a domain, a domain is a set, and there is no set of all sets, so unrestricted quantification is incoherent. The plural reply: a domain need not be a *set*; it can be given *plurally* — the domain just *is* all the objects, taken together, and "$a$ is in the domain" is "$a$ is one of them," $a \prec dd$, with no set of everything required. Plural quantification thus rehabilitates the absolutely general quantifier that both set theory and semantics seemed to forbid. It is also the setting in which second-order reflection principles and category-theoretic "large" collections (Chapter 21) can be stated without a super-universe.

## Paradox Avoidance, Restated

The recurring dividend is structural. Naïve set comprehension is inconsistent; the standard fixes are the **cumulative hierarchy** (Chapter 6) and the **type discipline** of type theory (Chapter 11). Plural comprehension is a third route: it is *consistent as it stands*, because a plurality is not an object and the paradox-generating formula $xx \prec xx$ is ungrammatical (Section 3, Derivation 4). The plurality of all non-self-membered *sets* exists — it is exactly the sets, plurally — and generates no contradiction, because it is not itself a set to be tested for membership in itself. Where type theory stratifies objects and the iterative conception stages their construction, plural logic keeps a single flat domain and stratifies *reference*: singular, plural, superplural. The same discipline that made the Geach–Kaplan sentence sayable makes the foundations safe.

## The Thread of the Chapter

Plural logic runs a single idea through logic, mathematics, and language. Linguistically, it is the correct semantics for collective predication no singular paraphrase captures. Logically, it matches second-order logic (Section 4) while claiming first-order ontology (Section 2). Foundationally, it revives Frege's logicism, reduces set theory to mereology-plus-size (Chapter 16), reads the ZF axioms transparently, and licenses absolutely general quantification. Whether the innocence thesis fully holds remains open — Linnebo's verdict is "cheaper than sets, but not free" — but the reach is not in doubt: with one new predicate, $x \prec xx$, a logic of *many* does the work that seemed to require a Platonic heaven of *ones*.
