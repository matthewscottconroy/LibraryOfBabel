# Algorithmic Randomness and Chaitin's Theorem

Section 2 showed that *most finite strings are incompressible*. But what does it mean for an *infinite* sequence to be random? Classical probability cannot say: it assigns the fair-coin measure to sets of sequences, and every individual sequence — the all-zero one included — has probability zero, so probability theory never calls one particular sequence "random." Algorithmic information theory does, in three equivalent ways, and the third, Chaitin's, delivers an information-theoretic form of Gödel's incompleteness theorem.

## Martin-Löf Randomness

The idea (Per Martin-Löf, 1966) is that a sequence is random iff it passes *every effective statistical test* — it lands in no small, effectively describable set. "Small" means measure-zero; "effectively describable" means computably enumerable.

**Definition (Martin-Löf test).** A **Martin-Löf test** is a uniformly computably enumerable sequence $(U_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ of open subsets of Cantor space $2^\omega$ with $\mu(U_n) \le 2^{-n}$, where $\mu$ is the uniform (fair-coin) measure. The set $\bigcap_n U_n$ is an **effective null set**. A sequence $X \in 2^\omega$ **fails** the test if $X \in \bigcap_n U_n$, and **passes** it otherwise.

**Definition (ML-randomness).** $X$ is **Martin-Löf random** if it passes every Martin-Löf test — equivalently, $X$ belongs to no effective null set.

Each test captures one "effective rarity": non-normality, a biased limiting frequency, a computable regularity in the digits. Requiring $X$ to avoid *all* of them makes $X$ satisfy every effective law of large numbers at once. The decisive structural fact:

**Theorem (Universal test; Martin-Löf 1966).** There is a single Martin-Löf test $(V_n)$ that is **universal**: $X$ is ML-random iff $X$ passes $(V_n)$. Equivalently, there is a largest effective null set, containing all others, and the ML-random sequences are exactly its complement — a set of measure $1$.

So "random" is not a moving target across infinitely many tests; one test decides it, and randomness has measure one, matching the intuition that a fair coin produces a random sequence with probability $1$.

## Schnorr's Theorem: Randomness Is Incompressibility

Martin-Löf's definition is measure-theoretic. The Levin–Schnorr theorem identifies it with complexity, closing the loop with Section 2.

**Theorem (Levin–Schnorr).** $X \in 2^\omega$ is Martin-Löf random iff there is a constant $c$ such that
$$K(X {\restriction} n) \;\ge\; n - c \qquad \text{for all } n,$$
where $X{\restriction}n$ is the length-$n$ prefix and $K$ is *prefix* complexity.

A sequence is random exactly when every one of its prefixes is incompressible. This is why the [prefix variant $K$](../02_kolmogorov/01_descriptive_complexity.md), not plain $C$, is the right tool: Martin-Löf's *oscillation theorem* says that for **every** infinite sequence — random or not — the plain complexity $C(X{\restriction}n)$ dips below $n - k$ infinitely often (a length-$n$ prefix cannot self-delimit, so $\log n$ bits leak). No sequence is plain-incompressible on all prefixes; only the prefix-free measure $K$ supports a clean characterization. The technical distinction of Section 2 earns its keep here: randomness of an infinite object is *prefix*-incompressibility.

## Chaitin's Number Ω

There is a canonical ML-random real, and it is definable in one line. Fix a universal prefix-free machine $U$.

**Definition (Halting probability).** $\displaystyle \Omega = \sum_{p\,:\,U(p)\downarrow} 2^{-|p|}$ — the probability that $U$ halts when fed a program generated by fair coin flips.

Prefix-freeness and Kraft's inequality give $0 < \Omega < 1$. Its properties are remarkable:

- **Left-c.e. but uncomputable.** $\Omega$ is the limit of the increasing computable sequence $\Omega_s = \sum \{2^{-|p|} : U(p)\text{ halts within } s \text{ steps}\}$, so it is *lower semicomputable*. But no computable function bounds how far $\Omega_s$ still is from $\Omega$, and $\Omega$ itself is not computable.
- **Martin-Löf random.** By Levin–Schnorr, $K(\Omega{\restriction}n) \ge n - O(1)$: $\Omega$'s bits are algorithmically incompressible. It is the concrete random real the finite theory only gestured at.
- **It compresses the halting problem.** The first $n$ bits of $\Omega$ decide halting for *every* program of length $\le n$: run all programs, accumulate $\Omega_s$, and once $\Omega_s$ agrees with $\Omega{\restriction}n$ to within $2^{-n}$, every program of length $\le n$ that has not yet halted never will. So $n$ bits of $\Omega$ answer all size-$n$ halting questions at once — the halting problem (Chapter 10) squeezed into a random real. Its uncomputability is thereby inherited from the halting problem.

$\Omega$ is thus a single object binding this chapter to Chapter 10: definable, perfectly determinate bit by bit, yet random and uncomputable.

## Chaitin's Incompleteness Theorem

The uncomputability of $K$ (Section 2, Berry's paradox) becomes a limitation on *provability* — an incompleteness theorem measured in bits.

**Theorem (Chaitin 1974).** Let $T$ be a sound, recursively axiomatized theory that can express statements of the form "$K(x) > n$." There is a constant $L_T$ such that $T$ proves "$K(x) > n$" for **no** $x$ and no $n > L_T$. Moreover $L_T$ equals, up to an additive constant, the Kolmogorov complexity of $T$ itself.

*Proof.* Build a program $B$: on input $n$, enumerate the theorems of $T$ (possible since $T$ is r.e.) and halt, outputting the first string $x$ for which $T$ proves "$K(x) > n$"; if none is ever found, $B$ runs forever. The description of $B$ needs the enumerator for $T$ — a fixed program of some length $c_T$ — plus the input $n$, so any string $B$ outputs has
$$K\big(B(n)\big) \;\le\; c_T + 2\log_2 n + O(1).$$
Suppose, for contradiction, that for some $n > L_T$ (to be fixed) $T$ proves "$K(x) > n$" for some $x$. Then $B(n)$ halts with output $x = B(n)$, and by **soundness** the proven statement is true: $K(x) > n$. Combining,
$$n \;<\; K(x) \;\le\; c_T + 2\log_2 n + O(1).$$
For all $n$ larger than some $L_T = c_T + O(\log c_T)$, the inequality $n < c_T + 2\log_2 n + O(1)$ is false. Contradiction. So $T$ proves no such statement for $n > L_T$. $\square$

Read the conclusion against reality: by the counting theorem, "$K(x) > L_T$" is **true for all but finitely many strings** $x$. Yet $T$ can prove it for *none* of them. A formal system with $L_T$ bits of axiomatic information can certify no object to be more than $L_T$-and-a-bit complex — "you cannot prove a 20-pound theorem with 10 pounds of axioms" (Chaitin). Incompleteness appears here as a **conservation law for information**: proof cannot manufacture complexity beyond what the axioms already contain. The same bound limits how much of $\Omega$ any theory can settle — $T$ pins down at most $L_T + O(1)$ bits of $\Omega$.

## Relation to Gödel, and a Caution

Chaitin's theorem and Gödel's (Chapter 10) are both genuine incompleteness results, reached by different self-referential engines. Gödel's sentence is a *single* unprovable truth constructed by the diagonal lemma, encoding "I am not provable" — the formalized **Liar**. Chaitin's gives *infinitely many* unprovable truths, the statements "$K(x) > L_T$," characterized not by self-reference but by **information content**, and its engine is the formalized **Berry paradox** ("the least number not describable in few bits"). The halting problem underlies both the uncomputability of $K$ and the randomness of $\Omega$, tying the whole picture to Chapter 10's undecidability.

A caution on interpretation. It is tempting to say Chaitin *explains* Gödel — that incompleteness happens "because mathematical truth is random." The theorem does not license so strong a claim: as Torkel Franzén and others stress, $L_T$ is roughly the size of $T$'s axioms, and richer theories (say ZFC extended by large-cardinal axioms) prove more and larger complexity lower bounds, so incompleteness here reflects the *finiteness of a chosen axiom set*, not an inherent randomness of arithmetic truth. What is beyond dispute is the theorem itself and its moral: information is conserved across proof, and every formal system has an horizon of complexity it cannot see past.

The remaining distinction is between two kinds of complexity we have so far run together — the *randomness* of $\Omega$ and the *organized content* of a theorem — which the [final section](../04_depth/01_logical_depth.md) separates with Bennett's notion of logical depth.
