# Universal Algebra and Equational Logic

Groups, rings, lattices, Boolean algebras: each was a set with operations satisfying equations, and each time we proved the same theorems — homomorphisms, quotients, a first isomorphism theorem. Universal algebra studies *all* such structures at once, and its central results are logic theorems: a completeness theorem for equational reasoning (Birkhoff) that is the exact analogue of Gödel's completeness theorem for first-order logic, and a characterization of which classes of structures equations can define at all.

## Signatures, Algebras, and the Three Constructions

**Definition (Signature).** A signature $\Sigma$ is a set of operation symbols, each with an arity $n \ge 0$. Examples: $\Sigma_{\mathrm{grp}} = (\cdot, {}^{-1}, e)$, $\Sigma_{\mathrm{ring}}$, $\Sigma_{\mathrm{lat}} = (\wedge, \vee)$.

**Definition (Algebra).** A $\Sigma$-algebra is a set $A$ with an operation $f^A : A^n \to A$ for each $n$-ary $f \in \Sigma$.

**Definition (Subalgebra, homomorphism, product).** A **subalgebra** is a subset closed under all operations. A **homomorphism** $\varphi : A \to B$ satisfies $\varphi(f^A(a_1, \dots, a_n)) = f^B(\varphi(a_1), \dots, \varphi(a_n))$ for every $f$. The **direct product** $\prod_{i} A_i$ carries the componentwise operations.

## Congruences and Quotients

**Definition (Congruence).** An equivalence relation $\theta$ on $A$ compatible with the operations: if $a_i \mathbin{\theta} b_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$, then $f^A(\bar a) \mathbin{\theta} f^A(\bar b)$. The **quotient algebra** $A/\theta$ has the classes as elements, with well-defined induced operations.

Congruences unify the chapter's quotient constructions. On a group, a congruence is determined by the class of $e$, which is a normal subgroup; on a ring, by the class of $0$, which is an ideal. The three "first isomorphism theorems" are one theorem: for any homomorphism $\varphi : A \to B$, the relation $\ker \varphi = \{(a, a') : \varphi(a) = \varphi(a')\}$ is a congruence and $A/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$.

The set $\mathrm{Con}(A)$ of all congruences on $A$, ordered by inclusion, is a **complete lattice** (Knaster–Tarski territory, Section 3): intersections of congruences are congruences, and joins are generated. The shape of $\mathrm{Con}(A)$ encodes deep structural facts — for groups and rings it is always modular.

## Terms, Term Algebras, and Free Algebras

**Definition (Term).** Terms over a variable set $X$ are built inductively: each $x \in X$ is a term, and if $t_1, \dots, t_n$ are terms and $f \in \Sigma$ is $n$-ary, $f(t_1, \dots, t_n)$ is a term. The **term algebra** $T_\Sigma(X)$ has terms as elements and purely formal operations.

$T_\Sigma(X)$ is *absolutely free*: any map $X \to A$ into any $\Sigma$-algebra extends uniquely to a homomorphism $T_\Sigma(X) \to A$ (evaluation). Free algebras relative to a theory refine this:

**Definition (Free algebra).** Let $E$ be a set of equations. Define $s \mathbin{\theta_E} t \iff E \vdash s = t$ (provability defined below). The **free algebra** $F_E(X) = T_\Sigma(X)/\theta_E$ satisfies the universal property: for every algebra $A \vDash E$, every map $X \to A$ extends uniquely to a homomorphism $F_E(X) \to A$.

Familiar objects fall out: the free monoid on $X$ is the set of strings over $X$; the free group is the reduced words; the free commutative ring on $x_1, \dots, x_n$ is the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[x_1, \dots, x_n]$; the free Boolean algebra on $n$ generators has $2^{2^n}$ elements.

## Equational Logic and Birkhoff's Completeness Theorem

Equational logic derives equations from equations. Its judgments are $E \vdash s = t$, generated by the axioms in $E$ and five rules:

1. **Reflexivity:** $\vdash t = t$.
2. **Symmetry:** from $s = t$ infer $t = s$.
3. **Transitivity:** from $r = s$ and $s = t$ infer $r = t$.
4. **Congruence (replacement):** from $s_1 = t_1, \dots, s_n = t_n$ infer $f(s_1, \dots, s_n) = f(t_1, \dots, t_n)$.
5. **Substitution:** from $s = t$ infer $s\sigma = t\sigma$ for any substitution $\sigma$ of terms for variables.

The labeled group derivations of Section 1 are exactly proofs in this calculus. Write $E \vDash s = t$ when every algebra satisfying $E$ satisfies $s = t$.

**Theorem (Birkhoff, 1935 — completeness of equational logic).** $E \vDash s = t$ if and only if $E \vdash s = t$.

*Proof sketch.* Soundness: each rule preserves validity (routine induction). Completeness: take $X$ countably infinite and consider $F = T_\Sigma(X)/\theta_E$. Rules 1–4 make $\theta_E$ a congruence, and rule 5 makes it substitution-invariant, from which $F \vDash E$: any valuation of variables in $F$ is represented by a substitution, and $E \vdash e\sigma$ for each $e \in E$. Now suppose $E \vDash s = t$. Then $F$ satisfies $s = t$ under the particular valuation $x \mapsto [x]$, which says precisely $[s] = [t]$, i.e., $E \vdash s = t$. $\square$

The proof pattern — build a canonical model out of syntax — is Henkin's construction (Chapter 9) in miniature, and the Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra of Section 3 is the same move for propositional logic.

## Birkhoff's HSP Theorem

**Definition (Variety).** A class of $\Sigma$-algebras is a **variety** if it is the class of all models of some set of equations.

**Theorem (Birkhoff, HSP).** A class $K$ is a variety if and only if it is closed under **H**omomorphic images, **S**ubalgebras, and direct **P**roducts.

*Proof idea.* ($\Rightarrow$) Three easy inductions show equations survive quotients, subalgebras, and products. ($\Leftarrow$) Let $E = \{ \text{equations valid in all of } K \}$ and let $A \vDash E$; one shows $A$ is a homomorphic image of a $K$-free algebra on enough generators, which is itself a subalgebra of a product of members of $K$. Hence $A \in \mathrm{HSP}(K) = K$. $\square$

Groups, abelian groups, rings, $R$-modules, lattices, and Boolean algebras are all varieties — we exhibited the equations.

**Fields are not a variety.** Take any field $k$ and form $k \times k$. Then $(1,0) \cdot (0,1) = (0,0)$, so the product has zero divisors, and $(1,0)$ has no inverse: $(1,0) \cdot (a,b) = (a, 0) \neq (1,1)$. So the class of fields is not closed under P (nor under S: $\mathbb{Z}$ is a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$), and by HSP *no equational axiomatization of fields can exist* — no matter how ingeniously one chooses a signature. This cashes out the promissory note from Section 2: the field axiom $\forall x (x \neq 0 \to \exists y\, xy = 1)$ is unavoidably non-equational.

## Term Rewriting and Knuth–Bendix Completion

The **word problem** for an equational theory $E$ asks: given terms $s, t$, decide whether $E \vdash s = t$. In general this is undecidable — the word problem for finitely presented groups (Novikov–Boone, Section 1; Chapter 10) is a special case. But there is a powerful positive strategy: orient each equation $l = r$ into a **rewrite rule** $l \to r$ that decreases terms in a well-founded order. If the resulting system is **terminating** (no infinite rewrite sequence) and **confluent** (any two rewrites of a term can be rejoined), then every term has a unique normal form, and

$$E \vdash s = t \iff \mathrm{nf}(s) = \mathrm{nf}(t),$$

which is decidable. Confluence of a terminating system reduces to checking **critical pairs** — the finitely many ways left-hand sides overlap (the Knuth–Bendix critical pair lemma). **Knuth–Bendix completion** repeatedly turns non-joinable critical pairs into new rules, aiming for a convergent system.

**Worked micro-example (completing the group axioms).** Orient
$$(1)\; (x \cdot y) \cdot z \to x \cdot (y \cdot z), \qquad (2)\; e \cdot x \to x, \qquad (3)\; x^{-1} \cdot x \to e.$$
Superpose (3) into (1): the term $(x^{-1} \cdot x) \cdot z$ rewrites two ways:
- by (1): $\;x^{-1} \cdot (x \cdot z)$;
- by (3) then (2): $\;e \cdot z \to z$.

The critical pair $\langle x^{-1} \cdot (x \cdot z),\; z \rangle$ does not rejoin, so completion adds the rule $(4)\; x^{-1} \cdot (x \cdot z) \to z$. Iterating, the process terminates in a classic ten-rule convergent system including $x \cdot e \to x$, $x \cdot x^{-1} \to e$, $e^{-1} \to e$, $(x^{-1})^{-1} \to x$, $x \cdot (x^{-1} \cdot z) \to z$, and $(x \cdot y)^{-1} \to y^{-1} \cdot x^{-1}$ — a decision procedure for the word problem of free groups. (Note that socks–shoes, proved by hand in Section 1, is *discovered automatically* as a completion rule.)

Completion is a semi-decision procedure in general — it may loop forever — but it is the ancestor of modern equational theorem provers (unfailing completion, the superposition calculus) and the conceptual model for `simp` in Lean: rewriting with a curated rule set intended to be terminating and confluent (Section 5).
