# Mathematical Realism and Anti-Realism

Do mathematical objects — numbers, sets, functions, groups — *exist independently of us*? Or are they human constructions, useful fictions, or patterns in concrete reality?

## Platonism (Mathematical Realism)

The standard view among working mathematicians: mathematical objects exist in an abstract realm, independent of minds and physical reality. Mathematical truths are *discovered*, not invented.

**Arguments for**:
- *Indispensability* (Quine-Putnam): Mathematics is indispensable to physics. Physics is our best account of what exists. So mathematical objects exist.
- *Phenomenology*: Mathematical experience feels like discovery, not invention. The Mandelbrot set "was always there" before we found it.
- *Objectivity*: Mathematical truths are not subject to human choice or convention. The Riemann Hypothesis is either true or false, independently of us.
- *Wigner's puzzle*: The "unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics" in physics — why does pure mathematics developed without application end up describing physical reality, unless mathematical structures are real?

**Problems**:
- *Epistemology* (Benacerraf 1973): How do we have knowledge of abstract objects? Knowledge requires causal connection; abstract objects are causally inert. How can we know about them?
- *Multiple reductions*: Natural numbers can be set-theoretically encoded in many ways (von Neumann ordinals, Zermelo numerals, ...). Which encoding represents the "real" numbers? If the question has no answer, Platonism seems undermined.

## Nominalism

Mathematical objects do not exist. Mathematics is about concrete things (inscriptions, structures) or is a useful fiction with no ontological commitment.

**Hartry Field** (1980): Mathematical physics can be *nominalistically reformulated* — eliminating quantification over abstract objects. Mathematics is a useful device for drawing inferences about the concrete world but has no literal content.

**Problems**: The reformulation is extremely cumbersome and has not been carried through for all of physics. "Useful fiction" must explain *why* the fiction is so reliably useful.

## Structuralism

Mathematical objects are *positions in structures*, not independent entities. "2" means the second position in the natural number structure — there is no further fact about what 2 *is*.

**Eliminative structuralism**: Mathematical assertions are disguised universal claims about all structures satisfying the axioms.
**Non-eliminative structuralism** (Shapiro): Structures are abstract objects, but objects within them have no properties beyond their structural roles.

## Fictionalism

Mathematics is like fiction — "Sherlock Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street" is part of the Sherlock Holmes story, and "7 is prime" is part of the mathematics story. Neither commits us to the literal existence of Sherlock or 7.

Unlike outright nominalism, fictionalism acknowledges that mathematical claims are made *as if* mathematical objects exist — and the pragmatic success of this fiction is explained without positing actual abstract objects.

## Why It Matters for Foundations

The philosophy affects what we consider a *satisfying* foundation:
- Platonists may be content with ZFC (it describes the real mathematical universe).
- Nominalists need a foundation that avoids abstract objects.
- Structuralists want *categorical* axiomatizations (describing structures uniquely up to isomorphism).
- Intuitionists (Brouwer) deny that mathematical objects exist independently of mental construction — requiring constructive foundations.

These aren't merely philosophical positions; they have formal manifestations in type theory, proof theory, and the choice of foundational system.
