# Formal Ontology: Properties, Modality, and Essence

The preceding sections read existence, identity, and abstractness off the apparatus of first-order logic. But the richest metaphysical questions — what a *property* is, what *necessity* comes to, what belongs to a thing's *essence* — outrun first-order resources and call for higher-order and modal logic. **Formal ontology** is the enterprise of building axiomatic theories of these categories and reading their commitments off the formalism. Its thesis, pursued here, is that "the nature of objects" done rigorously is a chapter of applied logic: the disputes about universals, worlds, and essence are disputes about the right logical regime.

## Properties and Universals in Higher-Order Logic

The oldest problem in ontology is the **problem of universals**: when $a$ and $b$ are both red, is there a *property* redness they share, over and above the two red things? **Realism** about universals says yes — one entity, wholly present in each instance (Plato's Forms; Armstrong's sparse, scientifically discovered universals). **Nominalism** says no: there are only particulars, and "redness" is at most the *class* of red things (class nominalism), a *resemblance* structure among them (resemblance nominalism), or a bundle of particularized **tropes**, the redness-of-*this*-cup being numerically distinct from the redness-of-*that*-one.

Second-order logic is the natural regimentation. Where first-order quantifiers range over objects, second-order quantifiers range over **properties and relations**: "$a$ and $b$ have a common property" becomes $\exists X\,(X a \land X b)$. The engine is the **comprehension schema**
$$\exists X\,\forall x\,\bigl(X x \leftrightarrow \varphi(x)\bigr),$$
which posits, for each formula $\varphi$, a property holding of exactly the $\varphi$-things — the higher-order analogue of ontological commitment, now to *universals*. Two fault lines run through it. **Predicative** comprehension restricts $\varphi$ to first-order formulas; **impredicative** comprehension lets $\varphi$ quantify over *all* properties, including the very $X$ being defined — needed for classical mathematics but the locus of Russell's paradox when a property is allowed to be an object it can apply to (Frege's Basic Law V, [§3](../03_abstract/01_abstract_objects.md)). And the **interpretation** of $\exists X$ is itself the realism question: Quine read second-order logic as "set theory in sheep's clothing," its property-quantifiers covertly committed to classes; Boolos replied that they may be read as **plural** quantification (Chapter 15) — "there are some things" — ontologically innocent of any one-over-many. Whether ascending to higher order commits us to universals is not a technical detail but the problem of universals restated.

## Type Theory as a Formal Ontology

A **typed** universe is itself an ontology of categories. Russell's ramified theory of types stratified entities into individuals, properties of individuals, properties of properties, and so on, precisely to block the paradoxes; simple type theory and the modern dependent-type universes $\mathsf{Type}\,0 : \mathsf{Type}\,1 : \cdots$ (Chapter 11) carry the same idea. The type hierarchy *is* a formal category scheme: what can be predicated of what, and at which level, is fixed by the typing discipline rather than left to metaphysical intuition. Intensional and Montague-style type theories go further, modelling properties as functions from possible worlds to extensions — which brings modality into the ontology.

## Possible Worlds as Formal Ontology

Modal claims — that something is **necessary** or merely **possible** — are regimented by quantification over **possible worlds** (Kripke semantics, Chapter 12): $\Box\varphi$ is true at a world $w$ iff $\varphi$ holds at every world accessible from $w$, and $\Diamond\varphi$ iff at some accessible world. This is formally powerful, but it relocates rather than removes the metaphysics: *what are possible worlds?* Applying Quine's criterion ([§1](../01_ontology/01_what_exists.md)) to the semantics of Chapter 12 forces an ontological choice.

- **Modal realism** (David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds*, 1986): possible worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated universes, as real as the actual one, which is merely the world *we* inhabit. This makes the semantics literally true but at an "incredulous stare" — a vast ontology of talking donkeys and blue swans.
- **Ersatzism** (Plantinga, Adams, Stalnaker): worlds are *abstract* objects — maximal consistent sets of propositions, or maximal states of affairs — so only one concrete universe exists, and the others are ways it *could have been*. This tames the ontology but must explain modality without circularly presupposing it.

The choice between them is a metaphysics of modality settled by what one's possible-world semantics is prepared to be committed to.

## Essence and *De Re* Modality

Essentialism holds that a thing has some of its properties **necessarily** — Socrates is necessarily human but only accidentally snub-nosed. Formalizing this requires **de re** modality, where the modal operator governs an open position bound from outside:
$$\exists x\,\Box F x \quad(\textit{de re})\qquad\text{versus}\qquad \Box\,\exists x\,F x \quad(\textit{de dicto}).$$
Quine was skeptical of the *de re*: essence seemed to him "invidious," relative to description — a cycling mathematician is necessarily rational *qua* mathematician but necessarily two-legged *qua* cyclist, with no absolute fact of the matter. Kripke's rigid designation and a-posteriori necessity ([§2](../02_identity/01_identity.md)) rehabilitated essentialism: because a name tracks the *same object* across worlds, "necessarily $F$" of that object is well-defined independently of how it is picked out. Kripkean essentialist theses became respectable — the **necessity of origin** (this table could not have been made from a different hunk of wood), the essentiality of kind and of material constitution.

Such theses are governed, in quantified modal logic, by the interaction of $\exists$ and $\Box$ — the **Barcan formula** $\Diamond\exists x\,\varphi \to \exists x\,\Diamond\varphi$ and its converse. The Barcan formula corresponds to **constant domains**: every world has the same individuals, so nothing could have failed to exist and there are no merely possible objects. Its failure corresponds to **varying domains** — contingent existence, requiring the existence predicate and free logic of [§1](../01_ontology/01_what_exists.md) to handle terms for objects absent from a given world. The metaphysics of what *could* have existed is thus encoded in a choice of modal axiom.

## Fine: Essence Outruns Modality

The Kripkean orthodoxy identified essence with de re necessity: $x$ is essentially $F$ iff $\Box(x \text{ exists} \to F x)$. Kit Fine's "Essence and Modality" (1994) argues this is **extensionally wrong**, and the argument is a clean formal counterexample. Socrates is necessarily a member of his **singleton** $\{\text{Socrates}\}$: in every world where Socrates exists, $\text{Socrates} \in \{\text{Socrates}\}$. So the modal account entails that *being a member of that set* is part of Socrates's essence. But it plainly is not: nothing in what Socrates *is* — his nature as a man — makes reference to that set; the dependence runs the other way, the singleton depending on Socrates. Likewise Socrates is necessarily such that $2 + 2 = 4$, yet arithmetic is no part of his essence.

The moral: **essence is finer-grained than necessity.** Essence is *real definition* — what it is to be the thing — and modal facts should be **grounded in** essences (it is necessary that $\varphi$ *because* $\varphi$ flows from the natures of things), reversing the Kripkean reduction. Fine develops a **logic of essence** with a primitive operator $\Box_x\,\varphi$, "it is true in virtue of the nature of $x$ that $\varphi$," which is *hyperintensional*: it distinguishes necessarily equivalent claims (membership in $\{\text{Socrates}\}$ vs. being self-identical) that the modal box conflates. This launched the contemporary program of **grounding** and ontological dependence — a further hyperintensional relation of non-causal metaphysical explanation ($\{\text{Socrates}\}$ exists *because* Socrates does; the mental *because* the physical), formalized as a sentential operator or a relation on facts with its own emerging logic (Fine, Schaffer).

## Metaphysics as Applied Logic

These programs vindicate the chapter's thesis. The problem of universals is a question about the interpretation of higher-order quantifiers; the nature of modality is a question about the ontology of possible-world semantics; essentialism is a schema in quantified modal logic; and the frontier — essence and grounding — demands hyperintensional operators finer than the modal box. The instruments are precisely those of the preceding chapters: first- and second-order logic, plural quantification, modal logic, type theory, mereology. Metaphysics, regimented, does not float free of logic. It *is* logic, turned upon the most general features of what there is.
