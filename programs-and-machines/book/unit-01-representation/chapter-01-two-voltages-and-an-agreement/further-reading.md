# Further Reading

## Start here

Petzold, C. (1999). *Code: The Hidden Language of Computer Hardware and
Software*. Microsoft Press. (Second edition, 2022.)

If you read one book alongside this unit, read this one. Petzold builds a working
computer from flashlights and telegraph relays, one step at a time, assuming
nothing. It covers the same ground as Chapters 1 and 2 at a gentler pace and with
more historical detail, and the overlap is a feature — hearing an explanation
twice in two voices is how most understanding actually happens.

Kernighan, B. W. (2017). *Understanding the Digital World*. Princeton University
Press.

Written for a Princeton course aimed at non-specialists, by one of the authors of
the original C book. Broader and shallower than Petzold; useful for seeing where
representation sits in the wider landscape.

## Primary sources

Shannon, C. E. (1937). *A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits*.
Master's thesis, Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

Shannon, C. E. (1948). "A Mathematical Theory of Communication." *Bell System
Technical Journal*, 27(3), 379–423 and 27(4), 623–656.

The 1948 paper is more readable than its reputation suggests, particularly the
first ten pages. It is where "bit" enters the literature. You will not follow all
of it now; you will follow considerably more of it after Unit VIII, and coming
back to it then is a good way to measure how far you have moved.

Leibniz, G. W. (1703). "Explication de l'Arithmétique Binaire." *Mémoires de
l'Académie Royale des Sciences*, Paris.

Cohen, D. (1981). "On Holy Wars and a Plea for Peace." *IEEE Computer*, 14(10),
48–54. (Originally circulated as Internet Experiment Note 137, 1980.)

Short, funny, and still the clearest statement of why an arbitrary convention
must nevertheless be agreed.

## Hardware and history

Patterson, D. A., & Hennessy, J. L. *Computer Organization and Design: The
Hardware/Software Interface*. Morgan Kaufmann. (Available in MIPS, ARM, and
RISC-V editions; any of them serves.)

The standard undergraduate text on what is actually happening underneath. Chapter
2 of this book overlaps its Chapter 3. More than you need right now, and the
right place to go when you want more.

Haigh, T., Priestley, M., & Rope, C. (2016). *ENIAC in Action: Making and
Remaking the Modern Computer*. MIT Press.

A careful history that corrects a good deal of received folklore, including about
ENIAC's decimal architecture.

Brusentsov, N. P., & Ramil Alvarez, J. (2011). "Ternary Computers: The Setun and
the Setun 70." In Impagliazzo, J., & Proydakov, E. (eds.), *Perspectives on
Soviet and Russian Computing*, IFIP AICT 357, Springer, 74–80.

A first-hand account of the ternary machine, by the person who built it.

## For later

Goldberg, D. (1991). "What Every Computer Scientist Should Know About
Floating-Point Arithmetic." *ACM Computing Surveys*, 23(1), 5–48.

Do not read this yet. Read it after Chapter 3, when you have been bitten once and
the title has become a promise rather than a warning.
