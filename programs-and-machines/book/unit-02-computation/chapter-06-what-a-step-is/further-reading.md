# Further Reading

## The primary source

Turing, A. M. (1936). "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the
Entscheidungsproblem." *Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society*, Series
2, 42, 230–265. (Correction, 43, 544–546, 1937.)

Harder than the profiles make it sound, and worth attempting anyway. The first
few sections, where Turing describes the machine by analogy with a human computer
working with pencil and paper, are readable with no special background and are
the clearest statement of the idea anyone has managed.

Petzold, C. (2008). *The Annotated Turing*. Wiley.

The 1936 paper, reproduced in full, with Petzold explaining every paragraph.
If the original defeats you — it defeats most people — this is the way in. It is
also a good history of the mathematical context that made the question urgent.

## Computation theory

Sipser, M. (2012). *Introduction to the Theory of Computation* (3rd ed.). Cengage.

The standard undergraduate text. Chapter 1 covers finite automata at more depth
than Section 6.1, and Chapter 3 covers Turing machines properly. Well written and
unusually humane for the genre; the natural next step if this chapter interested
you more than the Java chapters do.

Hopcroft, J. E., Motwani, R., & Ullman, J. D. (2006). *Introduction to Automata
Theory, Languages, and Computation* (3rd ed.). Pearson.

The other standard text. Denser than Sipser and stronger on the language-theory
side, which connects directly to Chapter 24.

## Architecture

von Neumann, J. (1945). *First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC*. Moore School of
Electrical Engineering, University of Pennsylvania.

Read Sections 1 through 3 for the argument, keeping the authorship question in
mind.

Patterson, D. A., & Hennessy, J. L. *Computer Organization and Design*. Morgan
Kaufmann. Chapter 4, "The Processor".

Builds a working processor — datapath, control, program counter — from the
components of Chapter 1. This is what "a transition rule implemented in hardware"
actually looks like.

## History

Haigh, T., Priestley, M., & Rope, C. (2016). *ENIAC in Action: Making and
Remaking the Modern Computer*. MIT Press.

Careful, well-sourced, and corrective. Includes a serious treatment of the
programmers' contribution and of the stored-program authorship dispute.

Isaacson, W. (2014). *The Innovators*. Simon & Schuster.

Popular rather than scholarly, and occasionally too tidy, but a readable
narrative of the whole period and a reasonable place to start if the history is
new to you.

Hodges, A. (1983). *Alan Turing: The Enigma*. Burnett Books.

The standard biography, and unusually good at explaining the mathematics
alongside the life.
