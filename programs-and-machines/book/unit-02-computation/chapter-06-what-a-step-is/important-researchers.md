# People

## Alan Turing (1912–1954)

Turing's 1936 paper *On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the
Entscheidungsproblem* did three things at once: it defined mechanical procedure
precisely, it proved that the halting problem is undecidable, and it described
the universal machine — one that reads another machine's description and
simulates it. That last construction is the theoretical ancestor of every
general-purpose computer.

He was 23, and had not intended to found a discipline. The target was Hilbert's
*Entscheidungsproblem*, a question in mathematical logic about whether validity in
first-order logic could be decided mechanically. Answering it required a
definition of "mechanically", and the machine was built to supply one.

During the Second World War he worked at Bletchley Park on the cryptanalysis of
German ciphers, including the design of the bombe used against Enigma. After the
war he worked on early computer design at the National Physical Laboratory and
Manchester, and on the question of whether machines could be said to think — the
1950 paper *Computing Machinery and Intelligence* proposes what is now called the
Turing test.

In 1952 he was prosecuted for homosexual acts, then criminal in the United
Kingdom, and accepted chemical castration in place of prison. He died in 1954 of
cyanide poisoning; the inquest found suicide. He received a posthumous pardon in
2013.

I include this because the history of a field includes what it did to the people
in it, and because the distance between "founded the theory of computation" and
"was prosecuted by the state he had served" is not a detail.

## Alonzo Church (1903–1995)

A logician at Princeton who, in the same year as Turing and independently,
answered the *Entscheidungsproblem* using an entirely different formalism: the
**lambda calculus**, a system in which everything is a function and computation is
the substitution of arguments into function bodies.

The two models look nothing alike. Turing's is a machine with a tape; Church's has
no machine at all, only rules for rewriting expressions. That they compute exactly
the same functions — proved shortly afterwards — is the main evidence for the
Church–Turing thesis. Two people, aiming at the same target from opposite
directions, hit the same point.

Turing became Church's doctoral student at Princeton in 1936. The lambda calculus
is not a historical curiosity: it is the theoretical basis of functional
programming, and Java's lambda expressions in Chapter 26 are named for it and
descend from it directly.

## The ENIAC programmers

Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings, Betty Snyder, Marlyn Wescoff, Fran Bilas, and Ruth
Lichterman were the six women who programmed ENIAC, which in 1945 meant physically
configuring the machine with cables and switches for each new calculation.

The work required understanding the machine in complete detail — there were no
manuals, and they learned it from wiring diagrams — and included developing
techniques for breaking a problem down, checking intermediate results, and
locating faults. That is programming, and debugging, before either word meant
what it means now.

They were classified as "subprofessional" operators, were not invited to the
machine's public unveiling dinner, and for decades were described in photographs
as models posed with the equipment. Recognition came largely after 1986, when
Kathryn Kleiman began locating and interviewing them.

Betty Snyder, later Betty Holberton, went on to write the first sort-merge
generator and to contribute to COBOL and FORTRAN standards.

## John Mauchly (1907–1980) and J. Presper Eckert (1919–1995)

The designers and builders of ENIAC, and afterwards of UNIVAC, the first
commercial computer produced in the United States.

They appear here because of the authorship dispute mentioned in Chapter 2. The
stored-program concept was under active discussion in their group before von
Neumann joined it, and the *First Draft* circulated with his name alone as author.
The document's circulation had the effect of placing the ideas in the public
domain, which arguably accelerated the field and certainly cost Eckert and Mauchly
patents they believed were theirs.

Historians continue to disagree about the allocation of credit. The useful lesson
is not who deserves it but that a foundational idea usually has a messier
provenance than the textbook version suggests, and that "von Neumann
architecture" is a convenient label rather than an attribution.
