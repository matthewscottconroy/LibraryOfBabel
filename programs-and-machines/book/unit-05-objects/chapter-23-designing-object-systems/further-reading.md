# Further Reading

**Joshua Bloch, *Effective Java*, third edition.** Item 18, "Favor composition
over inheritance," is Section 23.1.2 with the counting-set example in the author's
own words. Item 19 on designing for inheritance is its necessary companion. If you
read one thing after this chapter, read those two items together.

**David Parnas, "On the Criteria to Be Used in Decomposing Systems into Modules"
(1972).** Five pages, no code, and it contains the whole of modern software
design. Parnas takes one small program, decomposes it two ways, and shows that the
decomposition by processing steps loses to the decomposition by hidden decisions
on every change he tries. It has not dated at all.

**Rebecca Wirfs-Brock and Alan McKean, *Object Design: Roles, Responsibilities,
and Collaborations* (2002).** The long form of Section 23.1.1. More useful than
the patterns literature for the specific skill of deciding what a class is for,
and written without the doctrinal edge that the 1990s gave the subject.

**Martin Fowler, *UML Distilled*, third edition.** Under 200 pages, and honest
about which parts of UML matter. Read the first two chapters and the class-diagram
chapter; skip the rest until you meet a diagram you cannot read.

**Martin Fowler, *Refactoring*, second edition.** A design book disguised as a
catalogue. The value is in the smell names — feature envy, data class, shotgun
surgery — which give you words for design problems you can already feel but
cannot articulate. Naming a problem is most of fixing it.

**Gamma, Helm, Johnson, and Vlissides, *Design Patterns* (1994).** Read pages 1
through 30, which contain both principles, and then Strategy, Observer, and
Decorator. Approach the remaining twenty patterns as a reference: they are
solutions to problems, and reading them before you have the problem is how the
cargo cult started.

**Kent Beck and Ward Cunningham, "A Laboratory for Teaching Object-Oriented
Thinking" (1989).** The CRC card paper. Three pages, freely available, and worth
the twenty minutes for the argument about why the physical smallness of a card is
doing real work.
