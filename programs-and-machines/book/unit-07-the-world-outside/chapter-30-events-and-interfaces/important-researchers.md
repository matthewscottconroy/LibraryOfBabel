# Important Researchers

**Ivan Sutherland** (born 1938) wrote Sketchpad in 1963, for his doctoral work at
MIT, and invented interactive computer graphics in the process. It had a light
pen, direct manipulation of objects on a screen, constraints that the system
solved automatically, and a notion of master drawings with instances — which is
object orientation arriving several years before anyone named it. Films of
Sketchpad running are worth twenty minutes: essentially every idea in modern
graphical interfaces is visible in them, on a machine with less memory than a
modern keyboard. Turing Award, 1988.

**Douglas Engelbart** (1925–2013) demonstrated in 1968 the mouse, hypertext,
windows, shared-screen collaboration, and video conferencing, in a ninety-minute
session now called "the mother of all demos". His argument was not about
convenience but about *augmenting human intellect* — that the point of interactive
computing is to make people better at thinking, which is a considerably more
ambitious framing than the industry that followed generally adopted. Turing Award,
1997.

**Alan Kay** (born 1940) returns for the third time, and this is where his work
had the most visible effect. At Xerox PARC in the 1970s his group built Smalltalk
and with it the overlapping-window interface, the idea of a personal computer for
non-specialists, and MVC. Kay's framing that objects are things that receive
messages is precisely the event model in this chapter — a component gets a click
and decides what to do — and it is closer to Chapter 22's interfaces than to
Chapter 21's hierarchies, as promised there.

**Trygve Reenskaug** (born 1930) invented Model-View-Controller at Xerox PARC in
1979, while visiting from Norway. His description is worth reading because it is
more modest than the doctrine it became: the aim was to let a user manipulate a
mental model of their problem through several simultaneous representations, and
the separation followed from wanting several views of one thing. Every later
variant — MVP, MVVM, the state management in modern web frameworks — is his
observation with different plumbing.

**Adele Goldberg** (born 1945) co-developed Smalltalk with Kay and wrote the books
that let anyone else use it, which is a distinct and undervalued contribution. She
also ran the demonstration at which Steve Jobs saw PARC's interface, and has been
notably clear over the years about how much of what became the personal computer
industry was taken from work she was part of.

**Bill Atkinson** (born 1951) wrote QuickDraw for the original Macintosh, which
made overlapping windows fast enough to be usable on hardware that had no business
managing it, and then HyperCard, which let people with no programming background
build interactive applications. HyperCard is the most direct ancestor of both the
web and the modern low-code tool, and it is a good example of an interface idea
mattering more than the technology under it.
