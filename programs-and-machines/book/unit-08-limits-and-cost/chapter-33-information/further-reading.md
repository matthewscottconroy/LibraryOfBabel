# Further Reading

**Claude Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948).** Read it. It is
long for a paper and unusually readable for a foundational one, and the first ten
pages contain everything in Section 33.1. Seeing entropy derived from three
reasonable requirements — rather than presented as a formula — is worth the time.

**Claude Shannon, "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English" (1951).** The
guessing experiment, the one-bit-per-character estimate, and the method. Short, and
Exercise 33.14 is a reproduction of it.

**James Gleick, *The Information* (2011).** A history rather than a textbook, and
the best popular account of how the idea developed — from African talking drums
through the telegraph to Shannon and after. Read it if this chapter interested you
and you do not want another book of formulas.

**David MacKay, *Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms* (2003).**
Free online. Considerably more demanding than this chapter and the best single
source for going further, particularly on the connection between compression,
prediction and inference that Section 33.1.2 gestures at. Chapters 1 through 6
cover this material properly.

**Cover and Thomas, *Elements of Information Theory*.** The standard graduate
text. Rigorous, and the place to go for the source coding theorem's actual proof
and for channel capacity, which this chapter did not touch at all.

**The comp.compression FAQ**, still findable, particularly its section on the
recurring claims of universal compression. A useful demonstration of Section
33.2.2's practical value: a standing refutation that never had to examine a single
proposed method.

**Mark Nelson and Jean-Loup Gailly, *The Data Compression Book*.** Practical and
implementation-focused, with working code for Huffman, arithmetic coding, and LZ77.
The right book if you want to build the things this chapter describes.
