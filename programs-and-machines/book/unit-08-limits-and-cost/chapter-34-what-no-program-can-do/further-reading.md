# Further Reading

**Alan Turing, "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the
Entscheidungsproblem" (1936).** The paper. It is thirty-six pages, the notation is
dated, and sections 1 through 6 are readable with what this book has given you.
Seeing the machine invented for the purpose of a proof — rather than as a model of
a computer — reframes the whole subject.

**Charles Petzold, *The Annotated Turing* (2008).** Turing's paper, printed in
full, with a paragraph-by-paragraph commentary supplying the mathematical
background. The best way to read the original if the original alone is too much,
and Petzold is a patient explainer.

**Douglas Hofstadter, *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979).** On self-reference, in
mathematics, art and music, at considerable length. Uneven and worth it; the
chapters on Gödel's proof and on formal systems are the clearest informal
treatment there is, and the book is why a great many people became interested in
this material.

**Michael Sipser, *Introduction to the Theory of Computation*, third edition.** The
standard undergraduate text, and unusually well written for one. Chapters 3 through
5 are this chapter properly, with the reductions done carefully. If you take a
theory course this is likely the book.

**Ming Li and Paul Vitányi, *An Introduction to Kolmogorov Complexity and Its
Applications*.** The reference for Section 34.2, and considerably deeper than there
was room for. The applications chapters — the incompressibility method, minimum
description length, normalized compression distance — are the reason to read it.

**Scott Aaronson, "Why Philosophers Should Care About Computational Complexity"
(2011)** and his blog *Shtetl-Optimized*. Aaronson writes about what these results
mean better than almost anyone, and he is reliably careful about the difference
between what they say and what they are claimed to say.

**Andrew Hodges, *Alan Turing: The Enigma*.** The biography, and it takes the
mathematics seriously rather than treating it as background. Long, and the best
account of what Turing was actually trying to do in 1936.
