# Exercises

These are not exercises in the earlier sense. There is nothing to submit and no
answer to check. They are the things worth doing next, and each will take longer
than a chapter's worth of problems.

**35.1** Reread the preface. Note every claim it made about what the book would
do, and mark each one as kept, partly kept, or not kept. Being able to hold a text
to its own stated intentions is a skill, and it is more useful practised on a book
that mostly delivered than on one that did not.

**35.2** Take the list of eight bugs from Section 35.1.1 — the unsigned `int`, the
`double` comparison, the wrong charset, the `Integer` cache, the mutable key, the
polluted list, the CSV comma, the missing framing. For each, write two sentences:
what the two parties assumed, and how you would detect it.

**35.3** Pick the three chapters you found hardest. For each, write a paragraph
explaining its central idea to somebody who has not read the book. If you cannot,
you have found what to reread, which is the point.

**35.4** [carries forward] Finish the interpreter. At minimum, add closures, a second value type,
and a static checker for undefined variables — Exercises 25.9, 25.11 and 25.12.
Then give it a garbage collector.

**35.5** Take a program you wrote in the first three units and rewrite it with what
you know now. Then write a paragraph on what changed and which chapter is
responsible for each change.

**35.6** Read a small open-source project — under ten thousand lines — until you
could explain its design to someone else. Then write down one decision you would
have made differently and why. Being able to disagree with real code, specifically,
is the skill.

**35.7** Build something you will use. Not a demonstration; a tool for a problem
you actually have. Note every place where a chapter of this book turned out to
apply, and every place where something you needed was not covered.

**35.8** Contribute one small thing to a project you did not write: a documentation
fix, a test, or a bug report with a minimal reproduction. The process is the
exercise.

**35.9** Find a claim in this book you think is wrong or overstated. Reconstruct the
argument, find the case where it fails, and write it down. Section 35.2.2 named
four candidates and there are others.

**35.10** In six months, come back and reread Section 35.2.2's list of what you do
not know. Mark what has changed. That list is the honest map of the distance
between a first course and competence, and watching it shorten is the most reliable
encouragement available.
