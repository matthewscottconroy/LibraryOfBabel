# Further Reading

Section 35.2.1 is this chapter's reading list, organized by where you might be
going. What follows is the shorter thing: seven books that are worth reading for
reasons other than the technical content.

**Frederick Brooks, *The Mythical Man-Month* (1975, anniversary edition 1995).**
On why software projects fail, from the person who managed IBM's System/360. Fifty
years old and the observations have not dated, which is either encouraging or
alarming. The essay on the second-system effect will describe something you are
about to do.

**Peter Naur, "Programming as Theory Building" (1985).** Recommended in Chapter 24
and worth repeating here. The argument that the real product of programming is a
theory held in the programmers' heads, of which the code is a lossy record, is the
best explanation anyone has given for why a codebase decays when the people leave.

**Richard Gabriel, "The Rise of Worse Is Better" (1991).** A short essay on why the
simpler, less correct design frequently wins. It will complicate your view of good
design in a useful way, and Gabriel spent years afterward arguing with himself
about whether he was right.

**Ellen Ullman, *Close to the Machine* (1997).** A memoir about what it is actually
like to write software for a living: the absorption, the disconnection from
everything else, the clients, the money. There is very little honest writing about
the experience of this work and this is the best of it.

**Gerald Weinberg, *The Psychology of Computer Programming* (1971).** The first
book to take seriously that programming is done by people, and the source of
"egoless programming" — the idea that your code is not you, which is the
precondition for being able to have it reviewed.

**Douglas Hofstadter, *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979).** Recommended in Chapter 34.
It is the book that made a great many people want to understand this subject, and
its central preoccupation — self-reference, and what happens when a system can
describe itself — is Unit VI's preoccupation at greater length and with more
music.

**Donald Knuth, *Literate Programming* (1992).** The essays, not the system. Knuth's
argument that a program should be written to be read by a person, with the code
arranged for exposition rather than for the compiler, is more radical than anything
in Chapter 14 and is worth encountering even if you never adopt it.
