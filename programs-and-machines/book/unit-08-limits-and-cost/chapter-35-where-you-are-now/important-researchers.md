# Important Researchers

This chapter names no new researchers. Instead, the people who appeared most, and
what each of them was ultimately arguing.

**Barbara Liskov** appeared more than anyone else — abstract data types, the case
for restricted visibility, the substitution principle, and the argument behind
composition over inheritance. Her single claim, made in different rooms for forty
years: **a type is its behavior, and a convention anyone can reach around is not a
guarantee.** Everything this book said about boundaries is downstream of it.

**Alan Turing** framed the outer limit, and did it in 1936 with a machine he
invented to make a proof possible. The computer is a side effect. His result is
the reason Chapter 34 could say that the boundary of the computable is sharp,
knowable, and not where intuition puts it.

**Claude Shannon** did two things ten years apart, and this book rests on both: a
master's thesis showing that Boolean algebra describes switching circuits, which
is Chapter 8's foundation, and the 1948 paper that made "how much information" a
question with a number for an answer.

**John McCarthy** wrote `eval` as a specification and had it implemented against
his expectations, and with it established that a program is data. Unit VI is his
idea, and Chapter 25's interpreter is his function with types on it.

**Edsger Dijkstra** appeared for structured programming, for loop invariants, for
mutual exclusion, and for the general position that programs should be reasoned
about rather than tested into submission. Uncompromising, frequently right, and
the source of Chapter 9's insistence that you can prove a loop correct.

**Donald Knuth** established that algorithms could be analyzed rather than timed,
and then said the thing everyone quotes half of. Chapter 32 is his subject and
Chapter 18 is his warning.

**Joshua Bloch** is the one whose advice you will use most this year. Not a
theorist: the person who wrote the collections framework and then wrote down what
he had learned, item by item, with the reasoning attached.

**David Parnas** wrote five pages in 1972 saying that modules should be organized
around what they hide, and that what to hide is whatever is most likely to change.
Chapter 23 is a special case of that sentence and so, on reflection, is most of
Units III through VII.

**Grace Hopper, Frances Allen, Margaret Hamilton, Adele Goldberg, Jean Sammet, and
the six women who programmed ENIAC** appeared in various chapters, and it is worth
noting together that the early history of this field was substantially their work
and that the standard telling of it has not always said so.

**And Abelson and Sussman**, whose argument that a first course should treat
programs as objects of study — and should culminate in writing an evaluator for the
language you are learning — is the reason this book has the shape it has. Chapter
25 exists because of them, and Section 35.2.1 sends you to read the original.
